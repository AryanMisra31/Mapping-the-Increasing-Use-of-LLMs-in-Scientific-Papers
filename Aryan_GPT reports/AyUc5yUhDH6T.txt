Introduction

Binaural beats are an auditory phenomenon that arises when two tones of slightly different frequencies are presented separately to each ear. The auditory system integrates these two tones, and listeners perceive a periodic fluctuation or beat at the difference between the two frequencies. Researchers and commercial products have proposed that these beats can entrain neural oscillations at corresponding frequencies, a mechanism often described as neural entrainment. Alpha binaural beats, commonly targeted at approximately 8–13 Hz, are intended to promote alpha band activity in the cortex. Alpha band oscillations are one of the most studied neural rhythms in cognitive neuroscience and are broadly associated with states of relaxed wakefulness, attention, and inhibitory control. The rationale for using alpha binaural beats to influence cognition rests on the premise that increasing alpha activity will enhance cognitive processes such as selective attention and perhaps memory encoding. The underlying physiological model asserts that binaural beats are processed in subcortical auditory pathways, including the superior olivary complex and inferior colliculus, and that this processing yields phase-locked responses that can drive thalamocortical oscillatory circuits. If external auditory beats can modulate thalamocortical coupling, then they could theoretically bias cortex-wide oscillatory dynamics in a way that benefits cognitive functions that rely on coordinated oscillations. This line of reasoning has motivated a growing literature testing whether listening to binaural beats at alpha frequencies can improve performance on tasks that rely on attention, working memory, or long-term memory.

Despite a plausible neurophysiological account, empirical evidence for the cognitive benefits of alpha binaural beats (ABB) is mixed. Some studies have reported that exposure to ABB increases spectral power in the alpha band and that these increases correlate with improved performance on attention tasks. Other research has failed to replicate alpha power increases and found no behavioral benefits. The literature is further complicated by heterogeneity in experimental designs, such as differences in carrier frequencies, beat frequencies, duration of exposure, and the use of silent, white noise, or sham-beat controls. Some investigations have used pure behavioral measures, whereas others have simultaneously recorded electroencephalography (EEG) to evaluate whether ABB produces measurable entrainment. Where EEG has been recorded, findings vary; some reports find modest increases in alpha power localized to occipital sites, and others find no reliable entrainment or only transient effects during stimulation. The inconsistency of findings raises questions about boundary conditions under which ABB might influence cognition, and it highlights the need for well-controlled experimental studies.

Memory, in particular long-term verbal memory, is a cognitive domain of interest when assessing the potential benefits of ABB. Long-term memory encoding and consolidation are complex processes that involve interactions between cortical and hippocampal circuits and are associated with multiple oscillatory rhythms, including theta and gamma bands. The role of alpha oscillations in memory is nuanced; elevated alpha is sometimes taken as an index of cortical idling or inhibition, but more recent work suggests that alpha rhythms support the suppression of task-irrelevant information and thus can indirectly facilitate cognitive processes by improving signal-to-noise ratios. If ABB could selectively enhance alpha-mediated inhibitory control during encoding, it might reduce interference and thereby improve the fidelity of long-term memory traces. Conversely, if alpha increases reflect disengagement or drowsiness, ABB exposure might detrimentally affect encoding. Hence, the directionality of any ABB effect on long-term memory cannot be assumed a priori and requires empirical testing.

Several experimental studies have tested the effect of ABB on memory with a range of methodologies and outcomes. Some studies using short retention intervals and recognition tasks have reported small improvements in memory performance after ABB exposure. Other studies using free recall or longer retention intervals have reported null effects. Differences in sample characteristics, such as age and baseline cognitive ability, may moderate ABB effects. Individual differences in intrinsic alpha frequency and susceptibility to auditory stimulation also likely influence whether ABB produces measurable entrainment and behavioral consequences. In addition, expectation effects and placebo responses are potential confounds; participants who expect cognitive benefits from ABB may put forth greater effort or adopt different encoding strategies. Blinding and the use of active placebos are therefore important methodological considerations in this field.

The methodological variability across studies extends to properties of the binaural beats themselves. For instance, the choice of carrier frequencies is not trivial because the auditory system responds differently depending on the spectral content of stimuli. Many studies use a carrier tone in the 200–600 Hz range and create a beat frequency by offsetting the carrier by the target beat frequency, typically set in the alpha range. However, the amplitude of the beat perception and the resulting neural response can depend on the relative loudness and phase relationships of the binaural inputs. Some commercial offerings do not disclose their acoustic parameters, which further complicates replication. Moreover, duration of exposure varies widely from a few minutes to over half an hour. It remains unclear whether longer exposures are necessary to produce stable entrainment or whether intermittent exposures are sufficient to induce transient cognitive benefits.

Another important factor is the specificity of frequency bands that relate to memory processes. While alpha has been implicated in attention and inhibitory control, hippocampal theta rhythms (approximately 4–8 Hz in humans) are more consistently implicated in episodic memory encoding and retrieval. Studies that have attempted to enhance memory via external rhythmic stimulation have sometimes targeted theta frequencies using transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) and reported enhanced associative memory performance in some cases. By contrast, auditory binaural beats at theta frequencies have produced less consistent results, perhaps because the binaural beat mechanism engages different circuits than electrical brain stimulation. Given this landscape, it is plausible that alpha binaural beats would preferentially influence attentional gating rather than the hippocampal dynamics that underpin long-term consolidation of episodic memories.

The current study was designed to evaluate whether listening to alpha binaural beats during a study phase would improve later free recall of visually presented words after a brief distraction task. Free recall is a demanding test of long-term memory that typically relies on robust encoding and retrieval processes and often shows sensitivity to encoding strategies and attentional state. We chose a free recall paradigm to probe whether any ABB-induced enhancement in encoding quality would translate into measurable increases in the number of correctly recalled items. The study manipulated the presence of ABB during the encoding phase in a between-subjects design, with participants randomized to either an ABB condition or a silence control condition. We selected a beat frequency in the middle of the alpha band to target a domain of frequencies where previous reports have claimed cognitive benefits. In addition to the primary behavioral outcome, we collected self-reports of attention and expectation to explore whether subjective factors might mediate any observed effects.

We hypothesized that participants who listened to alpha binaural beats during the encoding phase would recall more words in a subsequent free recall test than participants who encoded in silence. This hypothesis is grounded in the proposal that ABB can enhance alpha oscillatory activity and thereby improve selective attention and inhibition of distractors during encoding. Enhanced attentional focus during the study phase was expected to produce stronger memory traces and thus better free recall performance. Given the mixed prior literature, however, we also reasoned that effect sizes might be small and that methodological factors such as individual variability and the absence of EEG monitoring could limit observable behavioral differences.

To strengthen internal validity, the experimental protocol included several controls. The same visual word stimuli, presentation timing, and distraction tasks were used for both groups to isolate the effect of ABB during encoding. Participants were asked to use headphones to ensure adequate binaural presentation and were instructed to keep their eyes on the screen during the study phase. We included a brief practice block to familiarize participants with the task and to reduce variability in strategy use. Despite these precautions, we also recognized that silence is a passive control and does not match ABB in sensory stimulation; this choice was made to isolate the effect of ABB relative to a baseline without ongoing auditory input. We anticipated that this approach would provide a conservative test of the hypothesized benefit.

We also considered potential confounding variables such as sleepiness, baseline cognitive ability, and mood, which can influence memory performance. To monitor these variables, participants completed brief self-report scales before the task. These measures allowed us to test whether any group differences in memory could be attributed to non-specific factors. Finally, we planned statistical analyses that would compare the number of correctly recalled words between the ABB and silence groups using appropriate parametric tests, and we intended to report effect sizes and confidence intervals to convey the magnitude and precision of any observed effects.

The present work contributes to an active area of inquiry regarding whether non-invasive auditory stimulation can modulate neural oscillations in a behaviorally meaningful way. The design balances ecological validity—using audible binaural beats delivered over headphones under normal listening conditions—with experimental control. In light of prior mixed results, the study offers an opportunity to add rigorous data to the question of whether ABB can enhance long-term verbal memory when presented during encoding. If ABB were found to improve memory, it would suggest a readily implementable cognitive enhancement technique. If no effect were observed, the results would still be informative by constraining theoretical accounts of how external rhythmic stimulation interacts with cognition and by pointing to methodological factors that future research should address.

Method

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from the local community. All participants were adults aged between 18 and 35 years. Exclusion criteria included self-reported hearing impairment, current use of medications that affect alertness, and a history of neurological or psychiatric illness. The initial sample consisted of 68 participants who provided informed consent. Data from eight participants were excluded prior to analysis: four participants failed an attention check included in the task, and four reported equipment malfunction or non-compliance with headphone instructions. The final dataset included 60 participants, with 30 randomly assigned to the ABB condition and 30 to the silence control condition. Randomization was implemented using a computerized random number generator and was performed in blocks of four to maintain balanced group sizes throughout data collection.

The primary stimuli consisted of 40 monosyllabic and bisyllabic English nouns selected from a standardized word pool to have moderate frequency and concreteness. Words were randomized for each participant and presented in a visually centered format on a computer display. Each word was displayed for two seconds with an interstimulus interval of one second during which a fixation cross appeared. The entire study list presentation thus lasted approximately three minutes. During the study phase, participants in the ABB condition listened to alpha binaural beats through circumaural headphones calibrated to a comfortable listening level. The binaural beat stimulus was generated by presenting a carrier tone of 440 Hz to theleft ear and a carrier tone of 450 Hz to the right ear, resulting in a perceived beat frequency of 10 Hz. The tones were sine waves and were presented continuously from two seconds prior to the first word onset until two seconds after the last word offset. The sound level was set to 65 dB SPL as measured by a sound level meter with the headphones on a coupler; volume was adjusted slightly for comfort but maintained within a narrow range across participants. Participants in the silence condition wore the same headphones but did not receive any auditory stimulation during the study phase; they were instructed to keep the headphones on to control for any tactile or pressure sensations.

Before the main study phase, all participants completed a short practice block consisting of five words to familiarize them with the procedure. Participants were instructed to remember as many words as they could for a later memory test and were not informed about the specific nature of the cognitive hypotheses regarding auditory stimulation. After the study phase, participants completed a five-minute distractor task designed to prevent rehearsal and to mimic standard short-delay intervals used in memory research. The distractor task comprised simple arithmetic problems presented on screen, and participants typed their responses. After the distractor task, participants were given two minutes to freely recall and type as many words from the study list as they could in any order. Participants were told to be as thorough as possible and to write down any words they believed were from the list.

In addition to the behavioral task, participants completed brief questionnaires before the study phase and immediately after the recall test. The pre-task questionnaire assessed current sleepiness using the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale (a single-item numeric scale), recent consumption of caffeine, and mood on a simple five-point scale. The post-test questionnaire asked participants whether they noticed any sounds during the study phase, whether they thought the sounds influenced their concentration or memory, and whether they had any prior experience or beliefs about binaural beats. These subjective reports were used in exploratory analyses to assess potential expectancy effects and sensory awareness.

All procedures were conducted in a quiet, dimly lit laboratory room. Participants were tested individually and were monitored by an experimenter who remained in the room but out of the participant's line of sight. Experimenters followed a standardized script to provide instructions and to minimize variability across sessions. The session lasted approximately 25 minutes per participant, including consent, questionnaires, practice, study phase, distractor task, recall test, and debriefing.

Scoring of the free recall output was performed by two independent raters who were blind to experimental condition. Raters scored exact matches and allowable minor orthographic variants as correct recalls; compound responses or semantically related intrusions were scored separately as intrusions. Inter-rater reliability was high, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.98; disagreements were resolved by consensus. The primary dependent variable was the number of correctly recalled words out of the 40 presented.

Statistical analysis was conducted using standard parametric methods. The primary test compared mean recall scores between the ABB and silence groups using an independent-samples t-test. Prior to conducting the t-test, assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were examined. Skewness and kurtosis were inspected and histograms plotted; Levene's test was used to assess equality of variances. In addition to the t-test, we computed Cohen's d as a measure of effect size and reported 95% confidence intervals for the mean difference. Given the mixed findings in the literature, we also conducted a Bayesian t-test to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis relative to the alternative. Secondary analyses included Pearson correlations between recall scores and subjective measures such as reported sleepiness and expectation, and exploratory subgroup comparisons for participants who reported hearing the beats versus those who did not in the ABB condition.

A power analysis conducted prior to data collection targeted a medium effect size (d = 0.50), with alpha set at .05 and power of .80, yielding a target sample size of 64 participants. Practical constraints limited recruitment to 60 analyzable participants, which provided slightly reduced power to detect medium effects and considerably reduced power to detect small effects. All data processing and analyses were carried out using the statistical software package commonly used in behavioral research. Data and analysis scripts are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary dependent variable are presented first. Participants in the ABB condition recalled a mean of 10.8 words (SD = 3.4) out of 40. Participants in the silence condition recalled a mean of 10.2 words (SD = 3.1). The raw data showed a range from 3 to 17 recalled words across the entire sample. Both groups exhibited similar distributions, with no extreme outliers identified by standardized z-scores greater than 3.29. Visual inspection of histograms suggested approximate normality for both groups, although slight right skew was present given the floor at zero and the moderate range of scores. Skewness and kurtosis values for each group were within acceptable limits for parametric testing.

Levene’s test for equality of variances was non-significant, indicating that the assumption of homogeneity of variance was met (p = .57). An independent-samples t-test comparing recall in the ABB and silence groups yielded t(58) = 0.76, p = .45. The mean difference in recall between the ABB and silence groups was 0.6 words, with a 95% confidence interval for the difference of [-1.1, 2.3]. Cohen’s d for the group difference was 0.15, indicating a small effect size. Given these results, the primary analysis did not support the hypothesis that listening to alpha binaural beats during encoding enhances subsequent free recall relative to silence.

To quantify evidence in favor of the null hypothesis, we conducted a Bayesian independent-samples t-test. The Bayes factor in favor of the null hypothesis over the alternative hypothesis (BF01) was 3.2. This value indicates moderate evidence supporting the null model relative to the alternative model that ABB would increase recall. The Bayes factor provides a continuous measure of evidence and complements the null-hypothesis significance test by indicating that the observed data are more consistent with no effect than with a positive effect of ABB on recall.

We conducted several secondary analyses to probe potential moderators and to ensure robustness of the null finding. First, we examined whether participants’ self-reported sleepiness prior to the task correlated with recall outcomes. Across the full sample, the Pearson correlation between sleepiness and recall was r = -0.18, p = .16, indicating a small and non-significant negative relationship. When examined separately by group, correlations were similar and non-significant. Second, we tested whether participants’ belief that they had experienced sounds during the study phase influenced recall. In the ABB group, 26 of 30 participants reported hearing sounds. Recall means for those who reported hearing the beats (M = 10.9, SD = 3.5) were not significantly different from those who did not report hearing the beats (M = 10.0, SD = 2.0), t(28) = 0.56, p = .58. This exploratory comparison did not indicate that subjective awareness of the auditory stimulus moderated the behavioral outcome.

Third, we examined whether expectation regarding binaural beats influenced recall. On the post-task questionnaire, only eight participants overall reported having prior beliefs that binaural beats could enhance cognition. These participants did not differ systematically in recall from participants without such beliefs (t(58) = 0.34, p = .74). Correlations between self-reported perceived influence of the sounds on concentration and recall in the ABB group were small and non-significant (r = 0.12, p = .53). These exploratory analyses suggest that overt expectation effects or subjective awareness did not account for the lack of a group-level benefit.

We also conducted robustness checks by re-running the primary t-test after removing the four participants in the ABB group with the lowest recall scores (below the 5th percentile). Removing these participants did not materially change the result; the t-statistic remained non-significant (t(54) = 0.92, p = .36). A corresponding re-analysis removing four participants from the silence group with the lowest scores likewise did not produce a significant difference. These sensitivity analyses provide evidence that the null finding was not driven by a few extreme low scorers.

To examine whether the effect of ABB might vary across the distribution of recall performance, we performed a quantile comparison at the median. At the 50th percentile, the median recall in the ABB group was 11 words, and in the silence group it was 10 words. A Mood’s median test did not show a significant group difference (chi-square = 0.72, p = .40). We also compared the number of intrusions produced by participants in the two groups and found no significant difference (ABB M = 1.6, SD = 1.3; silence M = 1.4, SD = 1.2; t(58) = 0.58, p = .56), indicating that ABB did not increase false memories or intrusive errors.

Given that the study was slightly underpowered relative to pre-registered targets for medium effects, we computed the minimal detectable effect size with the current sample size (n = 60) at alpha = .05 and power = .80. The detectable Cohen’s d was approximately 0.72 under these parameters, indicating that our design was not well powered to detect small effects. The observed effect size of d = 0.15 would require a sample size of approximately 870 participants to achieve 80% power, suggesting that any true effect, should it exist, is likely to be small.

Finally, we explored potential learning strategies reported by participants on the post-task questionnaire to see if strategy use differed by condition. Participants commonly reported using rehearsal and imagery, and the proportion of participants reporting the use of a mnemonic strategy did not differ significantly between groups (chi-square = 0.10, p = .75). There were no significant group differences in the amount of time participants reported spending thinking about the words during the distractor task, suggesting similar compliance with the distractor instructions.

Overall, the pattern of results across primary and secondary analyses indicates no evidence that alpha binaural beats delivered during encoding improved subsequent free recall relative to silence. Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence favoring the null hypothesis, and sensitivity checks did not reveal moderator effects that could have obscured a true ABB benefit.

Discussion

This study tested whether listening to alpha binaural beats during encoding would enhance subsequent free recall of visually presented words. Contrary to our hypothesis, we observed no statistically significant difference in the number of correctly recalled words between participants who listened to 10 Hz binaural beats during the study phase and those who studied in silence. The observed mean difference was small and favored the ABB group by only 0.6 words, and Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence in support of the null hypothesis. Exploratory analyses of subjective awareness, expectation, and reported encoding strategies likewise failed to reveal moderating effects. Taken together, these results suggest that under the present experimental conditions, exposure to alpha binaural beats during encoding does not produce meaningful enhancements in long-term verbal free recall.

The null finding contributes to an emerging literature characterized by inconsistency in reported effects of ABB on cognition. Several factors may explain why ABB failed to improve memory in the current study, and these factors illuminate theoretical and methodological considerations for future research. First, the mechanistic assumption that ABB leads to reliable cortical entrainment in the alpha band may not hold in typical behavioral settings without direct neurophysiological confirmation. Many claims about the cognitive benefits of binaural beats rely on inferred entrainment rather than direct measurement. Without concurrent EEG recordings, it is not possible to confirm whether ABB in this study elicited increases in alpha power or phase-locking. If entrainment did not occur, then the null result is unsurprising. Future studies that combine behavioral testing with EEG can determine whether ABB produces the hypothesized neural modulation and whether any neural changes mediate behavioral outcomes.

Second, even if ABB did induce some degree of alpha modulation, the functional role of alpha oscillations in memory encoding is complex and context-dependent. Alpha rhythms are implicated in inhibitory gating and the suppression of task-irrelevant processes. Enhanced alpha may therefore benefit tasks in which suppression of external or internal distraction during encoding is crucial. In the present paradigm, participants encoded a relatively short list of words under controlled laboratory conditions with minimal distractors. Under such circumstances, there may have been limited room for ABB to further reduce interference, thus attenuating potential benefits. In contrast, tasks that induce greater distractor interference or require sustained attention might be more amenable to improvement via alpha-mediated inhibitory mechanisms. This suggests that ABB effects may be context-sensitive and more likely to emerge in ecologically noisy or attentionally demanding environments.

Third, memory encoding and consolidation are supported by a repertoire of oscillatory mechanisms beyond alpha. Hippocampal theta rhythms have a well-documented connection to episodic memory processes, and cross-frequency coupling between theta and gamma bands is thought to orchestrate the temporal binding of memory traces. If the critical frequency for enhancing long-term encoding is in the theta range, then targeting alpha frequencies might be suboptimal. Some non-invasive stimulation studies that have targeted theta frequencies using transcranial alternating current stimulation have reported memory benefits, which raises the possibility that the choice of beat frequency is crucial. Future work could systematically compare multiple beat frequencies, including individualized alpha frequency and theta frequencies, to determine which band, if any, yields reliable improvements in long-term memory.

Fourth, individual differences likely play an important role in responsiveness to ABB. Intrinsic alpha frequency varies across individuals, and aligning external stimulation with an individual's intrinsic frequency may be necessary for effective entrainment. The present study used a fixed 10 Hz beat for all participants, which may not have matched many participants’ intrinsic alpha frequency. Personalizing beat frequency to each participant’s peak alpha could increase the likelihood of entrainment and behavioral effects. Additionally, personality traits, baseline attentional capacity, and susceptibility to auditory illusions may all moderate responses to binaural beats. Future studies should measure individual EEG characteristics and trait variables and consider stratified or within-subjects designs to better delineate responder profiles.

Fifth, methodological details of the auditory stimulus can influence whether binaural beats are perceived and whether they influence neural activity. Carrier frequency selection, stimulus amplitude, and the ratio of binaural beat amplitude to background noise can affect beat salience. In the present study, we used a 440 Hz left and 450 Hz right carrier tones at a comfortable listening level. While these parameters are common in the field, they might not have been optimal for maximizing neural entrainment. Some research suggests that lower carrier frequencies may produce stronger binaural beat perception, and others report that the presence of masking noise influences beat efficacy. Systematic parametric manipulation of these acoustic properties would be informative.

Sixth, the control condition in this study was silence. While silence isolates the presence versus absence of auditory stimulation, it does not control for non-specific auditory factors such as general arousal induced by sound or expectancy effects associated with receiving a sensory stimulus. An active control, such as white noise or monaural beats that do not produce a binaural percept, could serve as a more stringent test of whether the unique interaural frequency difference drives any observed effects. Additionally, double-blind designs in which neither participants nor experimenters know condition assignments would reduce potential biases. The present design attempted to minimize expectancy effects by withholding details about hypotheses, but more robust blinding would strengthen causal inferences.

Seventh, the size of the present sample offered reasonable power to detect medium-sized effects but limited power to detect small effects. The observed effect size was small, and a much larger sample would be required to detect such an effect with adequate power. It is possible that ABB confers a very small benefit to memory which accumulates across repeated exposures and real-world learning contexts but is difficult to detect in a single-session laboratory experiment. Pre-registration of hypotheses and adequately powered replication attempts are essential to determine whether small effects persist across samples.

Eighth, the timing and duration of ABB exposure relative to the memory process may be important. In this study, ABB was present only during the initial encoding phase and for a relatively brief period. Memory consolidation occurs over extended time scales and may depend on sleep and offline processing. Some research has explored auditory stimulation during sleep and its effects on consolidation, with mixed results. It is conceivable that ABB could influence consolidation processes if presented during post-encoding intervals or sleep, rather than during encoding. Future experiments might manipulate the temporal placement of ABB, including presentation during wakeful rest periods after encoding or during specific sleep stages, to test these possibilities.

Ninth, the choice of outcome measure—free recall—reflects a particular aspect of long-term memory that is sensitive to encoding strength and retrieval organization. ABB might influence other aspects of memory, such as recognition sensitivity, associative binding, or source memory, which were not assessed here. It is important for future work to examine a wider range of memory measures, including recognition tests with signal detection analyses, associative memory tasks that tax hippocampal binding, and measures of recollection versus familiarity.

Finally, the practical implications of the null result should be considered. Many commercial products advertise binaural beats for cognitive enhancement, often without rigorous evidence. The present findings caution against broad claims that alpha binaural beats will reliably improve long-term verbal memory in typical learning situations. That said, the null result does not prove that ABB can never have beneficial effects; rather, it establishes boundary conditions under which no effect was observed and highlights the need for a more nuanced and mechanistically grounded research agenda.

To advance the field, several concrete directions for future research emerge. First, studies should couple behavioral testing with EEG to verify whether ABB produces the intended neural entrainment and whether any neural changes mediate behavioral outcomes. Second, researchers should compare multiple beat frequencies, including personalized alpha and theta frequencies, to identify which bands are most relevant for specific cognitive functions. Third, active control conditions and double-blind procedures should be standard to minimize expectancy and non-specific auditory effects. Fourth, individual differences in intrinsic oscillatory properties and susceptibility to auditory stimulation should be measured and leveraged to identify responder subgroups. Fifth, longer-term and ecologically valid learning paradigms, potentially including repeated ABB exposures and retention intervals that include sleep, could reveal cumulative or consolidation-related benefits not evident in single-session laboratory studies. Sixth, studies with larger sample sizes are needed to detect small effects and to provide precise estimates of effect sizes.

In conclusion, the present randomized controlled study found no evidence that listening to 10 Hz binaural beats during encoding enhances subsequent free recall of a list of words compared to silence. The findings add to a mixed literature on the cognitive effects of binaural beats and underscore the importance of rigorous methodology, direct measurement of neural effects, and careful attention to individual and task-related boundary conditions. While ABB remains an intriguing tool for probing the interaction between sensory stimulation and brain rhythms, claims of robust memory enhancement from listening to alpha binaural beats should be treated with caution until systematic, well-powered, and neurophysiologically informed studies establish the circumstances under which such effects occur.
