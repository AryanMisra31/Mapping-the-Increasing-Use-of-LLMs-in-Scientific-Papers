Introduction

Tattoos have moved from the margins of many societies into visible positions within mainstream Western culture. Once associated with specific subcultures, criminality, or deviance, tattoos are now commonly seen among a wide range of occupational, social, and age groups. The increasing prevalence of tattooing has generated interest in the psychological correlates of body art and in the processes by which tattoos may interact with aspects of personality and identity. Researchers and commentators have offered differing views on how tattoos relate to personality, suggesting both that tattoos may reflect preexisting personality characteristics and that the act of tattooing may itself influence a person’s psychological state or self-perception. Sanders proposed that tattoos can change a person’s personality through their symbolic and social effects. According to that perspective, the experience of acquiring a tattoo and the social responses elicited by visible body art can contribute to shifts in self-concept and social behavior over time. In contrast, another, selection-focused perspective emphasizes that individuals with certain personality traits are more likely to choose to get tattoos in the first place. Under this selection view, personality differences precede tattooing and are not causally affected by the tattooing process. Beyond the question of causality, there is a further possibility that anticipatory processes—namely, the idea of getting a tattoo—could be associated with intermediate personality profiles. That is, people who are considering getting a tattoo may differ from both established tattooed individuals and people who are not considering tattoos, either because they already have personality profiles closer to eventual tattooed individuals or because the contemplation itself alters self-concept in measurable ways. Extraversion, as a major dimension of personality, has been of particular interest in research on visible self-expression. Extraversion encompasses sociability, assertiveness, and a tendency to seek and enjoy social stimulation. Because tattoos are visible and potentially communicative, a theoretical link between extraversion and tattooing is plausible. More extraverted individuals might be more inclined to use visible body modifications as a means of social expression, or they might be less inhibited about marking their bodies in ways that invite social attention. Conversely, having a tattoo—or contemplating one—could encourage greater sociability or confidence as a function of identity consolidation or social feedback. The measures used to assess extraversion vary across studies, but they generally aim to capture tendencies toward sociability, activity, and positive affect. In the present study, the Francis, Lewis and Ziebertz (2006) measure of extraversion was used to provide a concise and validated assessment of extraverted tendencies within a general population sample. Prior empirical work has yielded mixed findings regarding the relationship between tattoos and extraversion. Some studies have reported higher extraversion among tattooed individuals, consistent with the idea that tattoos serve as forms of social signaling and self-expression that may be more attractive to sociable, outgoing people. Other studies have failed to find consistent differences, or have identified associations with other personality dimensions such as openness to experience, sensation seeking, or lower conscientiousness. The literature also reveals methodological heterogeneity, with cross-sectional designs dominating the field and with varied operationalizations of tattoo status, such as any lifetime tattooing, number of tattoos, visibility of tattoos, and recency of tattoo acquisition. Very few studies have explicitly examined those who are considering tattoos as a distinct group, and the psychological status of "considerers" remains underexplored. The idea that the prospect of getting a tattoo might shape current personality measures draws on broader literature on self-fulfilling identity processes and the impact of anticipated identity changes on present behavior. If considering a tattoo functions as a form of identity experimentation or a rehearsal for a future self, it could be associated with intermediate levels of traits such as extraversion relative to those who have tattoos and those who are not considering them. Given these conceptual possibilities, an empirical examination that compares tattooed individuals, non-tattooed individuals who are considering tattoos, and non-tattooed individuals who are not considering tattoos can shed light on whether extraversion is merely correlated with tattoo status or whether anticipatory states are also associated with variation in extraversion. Another aspect of the relationship between tattoos and personality that warrants attention is the potential dose–response relation between the number of tattoos and trait levels. If tattoos reinforce or reflect personality tendencies, one might predict that a greater number of tattoos would be associated with stronger endorsements of traits such as extraversion. Conversely, if tattooing is primarily a categorical choice, the number of tattoos may not be meaningfully related to trait levels. Prior research offers limited and inconsistent evidence on this point, and empirical clarification is needed. The current study was designed to address these issues by comparing extraversion levels across three groups defined by tattoo status and intentions: tattooed individuals, non-tattooed individuals who were actively considering getting a tattoo, and non-tattooed individuals who were not considering a tattoo. The study also examined the association between the number of tattoos and extraversion among tattooed participants. We used a well-validated self-report measure of extraversion to obtain reliable trait estimates. A cross-sectional design was used to assess group differences, and additional analyses considered potential demographic covariates. We expected, based on prior findings and theoretical considerations, that tattooed participants would show higher levels of extraversion than non-tattooed participants who were not considering tattoos. We further anticipated that participants considering tattoos might display extraversion levels intermediate between tattooed and non-considering non-tattooed participants, reflecting either partial alignment with the personality profile of tattooed individuals or transient effects of contemplating a personal change. Regarding the number of tattoos, we had no strong directional prediction given the mixed prior literature, but we examined the correlation between tattoo count and extraversion to test for any dose–response pattern. By distinguishing between current tattoo status and the consideration of future tattooing, this study aimed to contribute to a more nuanced understanding of how visible body modification relates to core personality dimensions. The examination of contemplators as a discrete category responds to a clear gap in existing research and may illuminate whether identity-related anticipation is associated with measurable differences in personality traits. In addition, by investigating the relationship between the number of tattoos and extraversion, the study explored whether cumulative engagement with tattooing is associated with stronger trait-level correlates. The study's approach and analyses were designed to be transparent and replicable, providing clear operational definitions and statistical methods to test the stated hypotheses. The findings have the potential to refine theoretical models in which tattoos are positioned either as reflections of stable personality traits, as instruments of personality change, or as part of an anticipatory process that influences current psychology. Finally, the study aimed to generate avenues for future longitudinal and experimental work that could disentangle selection from socialization effects in the tattoo–personality relationship.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a general adult population using convenience sampling methods including online advertisements and flyers distributed in community venues. The final sample included 106 participants who completed all study measures and met inclusion criteria. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 54 years, with a mean age of 27.9 years. The sample included both self-identified women and men and a small number who identified with other gender labels; the majority identified as female. Ethnic and educational backgrounds were varied, reflecting a cross-section of urban and suburban community members. Inclusion criteria required that participants be at least 18 years of age and able to provide informed consent; there were no restrictions based on tattoo status. Exclusion criteria were minimal and included inability to complete the survey in English or reporting a history of severe cognitive impairment that would preclude informed consent. The study received approval from an institutional ethics committee, and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Measures included the Francis, Lewis and Ziebertz (2006) extraversion scale, a brief demographic questionnaire, and a body-art questionnaire developed for the study. The extraversion measure is a brief self-report inventory that yields a composite score intended to reflect individual differences in sociability, social confidence, and preference for social stimulation. Participants responded to items using a Likert-style format, and item responses were summed to create an extraversion score for each participant. Higher scores indicated greater extraversion. The demographic questionnaire collected age, gender, educational level, and occupational status. The body-art questionnaire assessed current tattoo status (whether the participant had any tattoos), the number of tattoos among those with tattoos, and whether non-tattooed participants were currently considering getting a tattoo. For the purposes of group comparisons, participants were categorized into three mutually exclusive groups: Tattooed (participants reporting at least one tattoo), Considering (participants reporting no current tattoos but indicating that they were considering getting a tattoo in the near future), and Non-considering Non-tattooed (participants reporting no tattoos and not considering getting one). The distribution of participants across the three groups was as follows: Tattooed n = 42, Considering n = 26, and Non-considering Non-tattooed n = 38. This grouping approach was chosen to directly test the hypothesis that extraversion would vary across tattoo status and intention categories. Data collection was conducted via an online survey platform during a single assessment session for each participant. The survey was presented in a fixed order beginning with informed consent, followed by demographic questions, the body-art questionnaire, and concluding with the extraversion measure. The fixed order minimized the possibility that completing the personality items would influence responses about tattoo intentions, although the cross-sectional nature of the data limits causal inference. Participants were debriefed at the end of the survey and provided with contact information for the research team. Statistical analyses were planned to include descriptive statistics for all study variables, checks of assumptions required for parametric tests, group comparisons of extraversion scores, and correlation analyses examining the relationship between number of tattoos and extraversion among tattooed participants. Specifically, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was chosen as the primary method to compare extraversion across the three groups, with follow-up post-hoc pairwise comparisons conducted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference test to control for familywise error. Prior to conducting the ANOVA, continuous variables were inspected for skewness and kurtosis, and the homogeneity of variances assumption was tested using Levene’s test. Where assumptions were violated, nonparametric alternatives or robust procedures were considered. Effect sizes were calculated for primary comparisons to provide an estimate of the magnitude of observed differences. For the correlation between number of tattoos and extraversion, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was planned if the assumptions of linearity and normality were reasonable; otherwise, Spearman’s rank-order correlation would be used. Additional exploratory analyses were planned to examine whether age or gender might account for observed group differences by conducting ANCOVA models with these variables as covariates. All analyses were conducted using commonly available statistical software, and alpha was set at .05 for inferential tests. Missing data were minimal in this sample, and cases with missing values on key variables were excluded listwise from the relevant analyses. No imputation procedures were necessary given the small proportion of missing responses. Sample size considerations were pragmatic rather than based on formal power analysis, as recruitment was constrained by resource and time limits; nonetheless, the sample size of 106 provided reasonable power to detect medium-sized effects in group comparisons. Care was taken to report both statistical significance and effect size estimates to aid interpretation. The methods were documented in sufficient detail to permit replication: the recruitment methods, screening criteria, measures used, grouping definitions, data collection procedures, and primary planned analyses were all specified a priori. Participants received modest compensation in the form of entry into a prize draw or a small electronic voucher for their participation. Ethical considerations included ensuring confidentiality, providing informed consent materials that described the voluntary nature of participation, and alerting participants to the minimal risks associated with completing self-report questionnaires about tattoos and personality.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the full sample and for each tattoo-status group were calculated first. For the entire sample (N = 106), the mean extraversion score was 16.1 with a standard deviation of 4.0. Group-specific means and standard deviations were as follows: Tattooed participants (n = 42) had a mean extraversion score of 18.1 (SD = 3.2); Considering participants (n = 26) had a mean extraversion score of 16.0 (SD = 3.9); Non-considering Non-tattooed participants (n = 38) had a mean extraversion score of 14.3 (SD = 4.1). The group means suggested a pattern in which tattooed participants were highest in extraversion, non-considering non-tattooed participants were lowest, and considerers were intermediate. Prior to inferential testing, the distributional assumptions underlying parametric analyses were examined. Visual inspection of histograms and quantile–quantile plots indicated that extraversion scores approximated normality within the full sample and within each group. Skewness and kurtosis values for the extraversion variable were within acceptable ranges. Levene’s test for equality of variances across groups was nonsignificant, indicating homogeneity of variances for the ANOVA. Given that assumptions were adequately met, a one-way ANOVA was conducted to test whether mean extraversion differed across the three tattoo-status groups. The ANOVA yielded a statistically significant effect of group on extraversion, F(2, 103) = 8.51, p < .001. The omnibus test indicated that at least two of the group means differed from one another. An estimate of effect size for the omnibus test was computed as eta-squared and indicated a medium-sized effect, eta^2 = .14, suggesting that approximately 14% of the variance in extraversion scores could be attributed to tattoo-status grouping. To further investigate the pattern of group differences, pairwise comparisons were performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference test. The post-hoc comparisons showed that tattooed participants had significantly higher extraversion scores than non-considering non-tattooed participants (mean difference = 3.8, 95% CI [1.6, 6.0], p < .001). The considering group’s mean did not differ significantly from the tattooed group (mean difference = 2.1, 95% CI [-0.3, 4.5], p = .09), and the considering group also did not differ significantly from the non-considering non-tattooed group (mean difference = 1.7, 95% CI [-0.6, 4.0], p = .12). These post-hoc results suggested that the significant omnibus effect was primarily driven by the contrast between tattooed and non-considering non-tattooed participants, while the considering group occupied an intermediate position that did not reach statistical separation from either of the other groups. Given the theoretical interest in whether the number of tattoos relates to extraversion, correlations were examined among tattooed participants. The number of tattoos reported by tattooed participants ranged from 1 to 12, with a median of 2. A Pearson correlation between number of tattoos and extraversion among tattooed participants was small and nonsignificant, r = .08, p = .41. This indicated no evidence of a linear dose–response association between number of tattoos and extraversion in the tattooed subgroup. To assess the robustness of the main group comparison results, additional exploratory analyses were conducted that incorporated demographic covariates. Age was modestly negatively correlated with extraversion in the full sample, r = -.18, p = .06, but this association did not reach conventional significance. Gender differences in extraversion were examined; descriptive statistics suggested small differences by gender, but an independent-samples t-test did not reveal a significant mean difference in extraversion between self-identified women and men in the sample, t(104) = 1.12, p = .27. An analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) including age and gender as covariates yielded a significant effect of tattoo-status group on extraversion, F(2, 101) = 7.12, p = .001, indicating that the group effect persisted after adjusting for these demographic variables. The adjusted means from the ANCOVA preserved the same pattern: tattooed participants remained highest in extraversion, non-considering non-tattooed participants remained lowest, and considerers were intermediate. Effect size estimates from the ANCOVA were similar to those from the unadjusted ANOVA. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted using nonparametric procedures to ensure that the main results were not dependent on distributional assumptions. A Kruskal–Wallis test comparing extraversion ranks across the three groups was significant, chi-square(2) = 11.6, p = .003. Pairwise Mann–Whitney U comparisons with Bonferroni-adjusted alpha levels supported the conclusion that tattooed participants differed from non-considering non-tattooed participants, while the considering group again occupied an intermediate, nonsignificantly distinct position. Finally, exploratory examination of the association between tattoo intention among the non-tattooed group and demographic characteristics was performed. Among non-tattooed participants, those considering tattoos tended to be slightly younger than those not considering tattoos, although the difference in mean age did not reach statistical significance in this sample. Gender distributions were comparable across the three groups. In summary, the results supported the hypothesis that tattooed individuals report greater extraversion than non-tattooed individuals who are not considering tattoos, with the considering group showing intermediate scores that did not differ significantly from either extreme. The number of tattoos did not correlate with extraversion among the tattooed subgroup.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between tattoo status, consideration of tattooing, and extraversion in a general adult sample. The principal finding was that individuals with tattoos reported significantly higher extraversion than non-tattooed individuals who were not considering tattoos. Individuals who were considering tattoos but did not yet have them had extraversion scores that were intermediate between the tattooed and non-considering non-tattooed groups and did not differ significantly from either group. Additionally, the number of tattoos among tattooed participants was not correlated with extraversion. These findings contribute to ongoing debates about whether tattoos serve primarily as expressions of preexisting personality characteristics or whether tattooing may play a role in shaping personality. The elevated extraversion among tattooed participants is consistent with the view that tattooing is associated with traits linked to sociability and outward expression. This pattern aligns with the notion that tattoos are visible markers that may be particularly appealing to individuals who are socially oriented and comfortable with attention. The result also resonates with previous studies that have reported higher extraversion among tattooed individuals, although prior literature has been mixed and variable in effect sizes. The absence of a significant correlation between number of tattoos and extraversion suggests that the relationship may be categorical rather than dose-dependent. That is, the choice to get a tattoo at all may be associated with a personality profile that is distinct from not choosing to get one, but accumulating more tattoos does not necessarily map onto stronger endorsements of extraversion. This pattern argues against a simple cumulative socialization effect in which each additional tattoo leads to incrementally greater extraversion. Instead, it supports a threshold or selection model in which the decision to tattoo at least once is associated with trait differences but further tattooing is influenced more by other factors such as aesthetic preference, symbolic meaning, or cultural and social contexts. The intermediate extraversion scores observed among considering participants are theoretically interesting and merit careful interpretation. The considering group’s mean did not significantly differ from either tattooed or non-considering groups, suggesting that this group may contain a heterogeneous mix of individuals. Some considerers may possess personality profiles similar to tattooed individuals and are in the early stages of deciding to obtain a tattoo, whereas others may be exploring the idea without a stable inclination toward the trait profile associated with tattooed individuals. Alternatively, contemplating a tattoo might have transient psychological effects, such as increasing openness to social expression or alter self-image in ways that bring extraversion scores closer to those of tattooed individuals. The cross-sectional design precludes definitive conclusions about causality or temporal sequencing, so both selection and anticipatory-change explanations remain plausible. The ANCOVA findings indicating that the group effect persisted after adjusting for age and gender suggest that the observed differences in extraversion are not merely artifacts of demographic composition. Although the sample was modest in size and convenience-based, and despite modest age associations with extraversion, the persistence of group differences after covariate adjustment strengthens confidence in the robustness of the main effect. The nonparametric sensitivity analyses further supported the primary conclusion that tattooed individuals differ from non-considering non-tattooed participants in extraversion. These converging analytic approaches increase the credibility of the findings within the study’s design constraints. Several mechanisms could account for the pattern observed. One account is selection: individuals who are more extraverted may be more likely to choose to get tattoos because tattoos are a form of visible self-expression that aligns with sociable and assertive presentation styles. Extraverted people might also be more comfortable with piercing social norms or with publicly signaling aspects of their identity. A second account is socialization or social feedback: acquiring a tattoo could produce changes in social interactions that reinforce extraverted behaviors, for example through increased compliments, conversations about the tattoo, or inclusion in social groups with shared body-art norms. A third account involves anticipatory identity processes: considering a tattoo might instigate cognitive and affective shifts—such as imagining oneself with a new visible identity—that are reflected in modest changes in trait self-reports. The current cross-sectional data cannot adjudicate among these accounts, and longitudinal and experimental research would be required to disentangle these mechanisms. The absence of a relationship between number of tattoos and extraversion could reflect several realities. First, after an initial decision to tattoo, subsequent tattooing may be influenced by factors unrelated to personality traits traditionally measured by extraversion scales, such as personal narratives, artistic preferences, or memorialization motives. Second, individuals with high extraversion may choose a single salient tattoo that satisfies their expressive goals, whereas others may seek multiple tattoos for reasons unrelated to sociability. Third, the number-of-tattoos metric may lack sensitivity to meaningful dimensions of tattoo engagement, such as size, visibility, personal significance, or recency, which could be more relevant to psychological associations than raw count. Methodological limitations of the study constrain the scope of inference and point to directions for future research. The cross-sectional design prevents causal conclusions about whether tattoos cause increases in extraversion or whether extraverted personalities predispose individuals to tattooing. A longitudinal design that tracks personality before and after tattoo acquisition would be valuable for testing causal hypotheses. Experimental or quasi-experimental designs that manipulate the salience of tattoo-related identity or examine social feedback following visible cues could also help disentangle causal processes. The sample was recruited using convenience methods and may not be representative of the broader population; replication in larger and more diverse samples, including different cultural contexts where tattoos carry different social meanings, is essential. The reliance on self-report measures introduces potential biases, including social desirability and shared-method variance; incorporating peer reports and behavioral measures of extraversion would help validate findings. The body-art questionnaire used in this study was brief and focused on basic indicators; more fine-grained assessments of tattoo characteristics—such as visibility, thematic content, placement, recency, and subjective importance—could refine understanding of the tattoo–personality relationship. Considering motivations for tattooing, such as aesthetic preference, commemoration, group membership, or rebellion, might also elucidate heterogeneity within tattooed populations. Another limitation is the modest sample size in subgroup analyses, particularly for the considering group, which may have limited statistical power to detect small to medium effects. The nonsignificant differences involving the considering group might reflect insufficient power rather than true absence of effects; therefore, future studies should aim for larger samples to reliably detect subtler distinctions. Despite these limitations, the study provides several contributions to the literature. It demonstrates that tattooed individuals report higher extraversion relative to non-tattooed individuals who are not considering tattoos, supporting a link between visible body modification and sociability-related personality traits. It also introduces consideration status as a potentially meaningful category that occupies an intermediate position on extraversion, suggesting a complex relationship between identity intent and trait measures. Finally, it shows that simple counts of tattoos are not associated with extraversion, arguing against a straightforward dose–response interpretation. Future work should pursue longitudinal investigations that measure personality prior to, shortly after, and long after tattoo acquisition to examine potential personality change processes. Studies that include experimental manipulations of identity salience or that assess social consequences of visible tattoos in naturalistic interactions could provide direct tests of proposed mechanisms. Research that considers cultural context is particularly important, given that the social meanings of tattoos vary dramatically across societies and social groups. Qualitative studies exploring personal narratives and motivations for tattooing could complement quantitative approaches by revealing the subjective processes through which tattoos intersect with identity and social life. Clinically and practically, understanding the relationship between tattoos and personality can inform practitioners who work with clients for whom body modification is salient to self-concept or coping. It may also inform public discourse by highlighting that tattoos are one among many forms of identity expression linked to personality tendencies rather than simple markers of deviance. In conclusion, the present study adds to evidence that tattooed individuals tend to score higher on extraversion than non-tattooed individuals who are not considering tattoos, with those considering tattoos occupying an intermediate position. These findings underscore the importance of considering both current body-art status and intentions when investigating personality correlates of tattooing. They also point to the need for longitudinal, culturally sensitive, and multidimensional research to disentangle selection, socialization, and anticipatory effects. By clarifying how visible body modifications relate to personality, future studies can deepen understanding of the complex interactions among self-expression, social feedback, and enduring trait tendencies.
