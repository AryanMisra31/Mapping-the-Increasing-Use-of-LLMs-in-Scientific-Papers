Introduction

Human memory undergoes changes across the lifespan that have been robustly documented in behavioral, neuroimaging, and clinical literatures. One of the most commonly observed features of cognitive aging is a decline in episodic memory, a form of memory that supports the recollection of specific events, contexts, and temporal sequences. Episodic memory can be decomposed into different processes, and one important distinction is between memory for individual items (item memory) and memory for the associations among those items or the binding of features into a coherent episode (associative memory). The associative deficit hypothesis proposes that age-related deficits in episodic memory are driven in large part by a diminished ability to form and retrieve associations among components of an event, rather than by a uniform decline in all aspects of memory. Under this hypothesis, older adults are expected to show relatively preserved memory for single items but disproportionate impairments when asked to remember arbitrary pairings or contextual bindings. This hypothesis has attracted considerable attention because it offers a mechanistic account of why certain memory tasks are more sensitive to aging than others.

A substantial body of work supports the associative deficit hypothesis, showing that older adults frequently perform more poorly than younger adults on tests that require binding two or more elements together, such as name–face associations, object–location pairings, or word–word associations. These findings have been linked to age-related changes in neural systems that support associative binding, particularly the hippocampus and its interactions with frontal and parietal control regions. The hippocampus is thought to play a key role in the formation of relational representations by linking disparate cortical representations into unified memory traces. Aging is associated with structural and functional changes in the hippocampus, including reduced volume, altered functional connectivity, and reduced pattern separation capabilities, all of which could plausibly underlie associative memory impairments. Prefrontal cortical regions, which are involved in strategic encoding and retrieval operations such as elaboration, organization, and monitoring, also exhibit age-related alterations in activity and connectivity, and these changes likely contribute to reduced ability to generate and use effective encoding strategies that support associative binding.

Despite the strong empirical support for the associative deficit hypothesis, the literature also contains heterogeneity in outcomes across tasks, stimulus modalities, and individual differences. For example, the magnitude of associative deficits can vary with the meaningfulness of the items, the presence of semantic relationships between paired elements, the depth of encoding instructions, and the time allowed for study and retrieval. When pairs have inherent semantic links or when elaborative encoding is promoted, older adults' associative memory performance is sometimes bolstered, suggesting that the associative deficit is not immutable but is modulated by task characteristics and encoding strategies. In addition, individual difference variables such as education, cognitive reserve, working memory capacity, and lifestyle factors have been shown to moderate age-related declines in associative memory. These moderators suggest that the associative deficit hypothesis must be understood in the context of both neurobiological aging and psychological and environmental influences that shape encoding and retrieval.

One individual difference that has received comparatively less attention in the context of associative memory and aging is handedness. Handedness, as an index of hemispheric lateralization, has been associated with differences in cognitive processing, particularly in domains that rely on interhemispheric integration or specialized processing in one hemisphere. The standard model of hemispheric specialization posits that language processing is generally left-lateralized, while certain aspects of spatial and pitch processing can show right-hemisphere dominance. Left-handed individuals, however, exhibit a more variable pattern of lateralization, and some left-handers exhibit more symmetric or even right-lateralized language representations. This variability in lateralization can have consequences for how information is stored, processed, and retrieved.

Specific lines of research have suggested that handedness may affect certain types of auditory and pitch-related memory tasks. For example, studies that examine pitch discrimination or absolute pitch recognition have sometimes found that left-handed individuals perform differently from right-handers, which has been attributed to differences in the organization or lateralization of auditory processing networks. The putative advantage for left-handers in some auditory tasks has been tied to the possibility that a less strongly left-lateralized language system may allow for more distributed or bilateral processing of auditory features, thereby facilitating certain types of auditory memory. However, the generalizability of handedness effects from auditory pitch tasks to other memory domains, such as verbal associative memory for words, remains unclear.

Very few studies have directly examined whether handedness interacts with age to influence associative memory performance. It is plausible that if left-handers differ in lateralization patterns that affect binding processes, then handedness could modulate the extent of associative deficits observed in older adults. For example, if left-handed individuals maintain a more bilateral representation of verbal and contextual information, they might be better able to compensate for age-related hippocampal or unilateral cortical decline by recruiting alternative networks. Conversely, if handedness-related differences are primarily domain-specific (e.g., only affecting pitch or auditory processing) then little to no effect would be expected for verbal associative memory tasks that rely heavily on semantic and hippocampal binding processes.

Beyond handedness, age-related associative difficulties might also interact with the nature of the memory test itself. Associative memory tests that require recognition of intact versus recombined pairs (associative recognition) can be contrasted with item recognition tests that probe memory for single words regardless of their pairings. The associative deficit hypothesis typically predicts a larger age decrement on associative recognition than on item recognition. Nonetheless, whether this pattern emerges consistently depends on task parameters including study time, the number of items, and the presence of distractors. An absence of an age by test-type interaction could indicate that the specific task lacked sensitivity to associative deficits or that older adults were able to use compensatory strategies during encoding or retrieval.

In the present study, we aimed to investigate the joint effects of age group and handedness on item and associative recognition performance for verbal materials. We recruited a sample of younger adults and a sample of older adults and assessed both item recognition (memory for single words) and associative recognition (memory for word pairs). Handedness was measured using a standardized inventory to classify participants as left- or right-handed. We designed the task to be straightforward and well-controlled, with careful counterbalancing of stimuli across test types and age groups, and with encoding instructions that emphasized deliberate study of both items and their pairings.

We had several specific hypotheses. First, consistent with the broader literature on aging and episodic memory, we predicted that younger adults would outperform older adults across memory tests. Second, consistent with the associative deficit hypothesis, we predicted that older adults would show a larger decrement in associative recognition relative to item recognition. Third, based on previous findings that have linked left-handedness to certain auditory memory advantages, we explored whether handedness would be associated with better associative or item recognition performance, and whether any handedness-related differences would interact with age. Given that this area of inquiry is exploratory, we did not make a strong directional prediction for the handedness effects in the verbal associative domain, but we expected that if handedness affected the lateralization of verbal and associative processing, an interaction might be detectable.

To foreshadow our findings, we observed a robust age effect such that younger adults exhibited higher accuracy overall than older adults. Contrary to our second hypothesis, however, the type of memory test (item vs associative) did not interact with age group; both younger and older participants showed similar profiles across test types. Finally, we did not find reliable main effects or interactions involving handedness, suggesting no straightforward advantage for left- or right-handed participants in this verbal memory task. In the remainder of the paper, we provide a detailed account of the methods used to obtain these results, present the analyses that support these conclusions, and discuss how the findings inform the associative deficit hypothesis and the broader consideration of handedness as a moderator of age-related memory change.

Methods

Participants were recruited from university participant pools, community centers, and local advertisements targeted to older adults. The final sample included 79 participants in total, comprising 40 younger adults and 39 older adults. Younger adults ranged in age from 18 to 30 years, with a mean age of 22.4 years (SD = 3.1). Older adults ranged in age from 62 to 79 years, with a mean age of 68.7 years (SD = 4.6). Both age groups included men and women; the younger group included 24 women and 16 men, and the older group included 23 women and 16 men. All participants were native speakers of the language used in the task, reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and reported no history of neurological or psychiatric illness that might affect cognitive functioning.

Handedness was assessed using a standardized handedness inventory administered at the beginning of the testing session. Participants completed a ten-item inventory that asked about hand preference for common actions, and responses were used to derive a laterality quotient. Participants were classified as left-handed if their laterality quotient indicated a predominant left-hand preference and as right-handed if their laterality quotient indicated a predominant right-hand preference. In the sample, 12 participants were classified as left-handed and 67 as right-handed. The distribution of left- and right-handed participants was similar across age groups, with seven left-handed individuals in the younger group and five in the older group.

Before testing began, participants provided informed consent in accordance with institutional review board protocols. Participants were compensated for their time with either course credit (for younger adults) or a modest monetary payment (for older adults). The study consisted of a single laboratory session lasting approximately 70 minutes, during which participants completed background questionnaires, the handedness inventory, and the memory task described below. Demographic information and self-reported education levels were collected, and the older adult sample also completed a brief cognitive screening questionnaire to ensure the absence of significant cognitive impairment.

Stimuli consisted of 160 concrete nouns selected from a standardized list of words matched on word frequency and familiarity. The word list was divided into four blocks of 40 words each to facilitate counterbalancing. For each participant, the words were randomly assigned to serve as study items or lures at test, and word pairs for the associative task were constructed by pairing words randomly within a block. Care was taken to avoid pairing words that were strongly semantically related in order to promote the need for associative encoding rather than relying on preexisting semantic links.

The experimental design included two main test types: an item recognition test and an associative recognition test. The item recognition test probed memory for individual words that had been presented during study, whereas the associative recognition test probed memory for the specific pairings of words that had been presented together. Each participant completed both test types in separate phases, and the order of the test phases was counterbalanced across participants to control for order effects. Within each test type, half of the trials contained previously studied materials (targets) and half contained new materials or recombined pairings (foils).

The study phase for both test types involved an intentional encoding task. Participants were presented with 80 words in total, displayed one at a time on a computer screen for 3.5 seconds each, with a 500 ms interstimulus interval. For words that would later be tested in the associative recognition condition, words were presented in pairs at encoding, with each pair displayed together for 4.5 seconds per pair; for words that would later be tested in the item recognition condition, words were presented singly. The study list was organized so that each participant encountered 40 item-only study trials and 40 associative study trials. Participants were given specific encoding instructions: for item trials they were asked to form a vivid image of the object or concept denoted by the word, and for associative trials they were instructed to form a link between the two words in the pair by generating a brief interactive image or sentence that connected the two concepts. The encoding instructions were intentionally designed to be supportive of associative formation to reduce floor effects in the older group.

After a 10-minute filler task consisting of simple arithmetic problems to prevent rehearsal, participants proceeded to the test phases. For the item recognition test, participants were presented with 80 words one at a time and were asked to indicate whether each word was "old" (presented during the study phase) or "new" (not presented during the study phase). For the associative recognition test, participants were shown either intact pairs (two words that had been studied together) or recombined pairs (two words that had been studied but not together) and were asked to indicate whether the pair was the same as at study or was recombined. Responses were recorded via keyboard presses, and participants were instructed to balance speed and accuracy. Reaction times were recorded from stimulus onset to response.

Accuracy for each participant was computed separately for the item recognition and associative recognition tests. Hit rates were defined as the proportion of targets correctly identified, and false alarm rates were defined as the proportion of lures incorrectly endorsed as old or intact. Using these rates, we computed corrected recognition scores (hits minus false alarms) for both test types to provide a bias-corrected measure of memory performance. In addition, signal detection sensitivity (d’) was computed for supplementary analyses to assess perceptual sensitivity independent of response bias.

Data were screened for outliers and response patterns that suggested noncompliance. Two participants were excluded from analysis because their overall accuracy was at or below chance levels, and one participant was excluded for excessively long response times and a high proportion of missed trials. The final analytic sample therefore consisted of 79 participants, as reported above. All analyses reported below reflect this final sample.

To examine the main effects and interactions of interest, we conducted a mixed-model analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Test Type (item, associative) as a within-subjects factor and Age Group (younger, older) and Handedness (left, right) as between-subjects factors. Supplementary analyses included independent-samples t-tests to compare groups on demographic variables, paired-samples t-tests to compare item and associative performance within groups, and correlational analyses to examine associations between continuous age and memory performance. We also conducted analyses controlling for education level to ensure that group differences were not attributable to educational disparities.

Results

Descriptive statistics for corrected recognition scores are presented first to provide an overview of the data. Younger adults’ mean corrected recognition score across both test types was .805 (SD = .093), whereas older adults’ mean corrected recognition score across both test types was .715 (SD = .118). For the item recognition test specifically, younger adults had a mean corrected score of .838 (SD = .080) and older adults had a mean corrected score of .732 (SD = .116). For the associative recognition test, younger adults had a mean corrected score of .772 (SD = .101) and older adults had a mean corrected score of .698 (SD = .130). These descriptive values indicate that younger adults outperformed older adults on both test types, and that associative scores were numerically lower than item scores in both groups.

We first examined overall age group differences using a mixed ANOVA with Test Type (item, associative) as the within-subjects factor and Age Group (younger, older) as the between-subjects factor. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of Age Group, F(1,77) = 18.45, p < .001, partial η2 = .19, confirming that younger adults performed better than older adults across test types. The main effect of Test Type was not statistically significant, F(1,77) = 1.82, p = .18, partial η2 = .02, indicating that there was no reliable overall difference in corrected recognition between item and associative tests across the combined sample. The interaction between Age Group and Test Type was not significant, F(1,77) = 1.04, p = .31, partial η2 = .01, suggesting that age-related performance differences did not differ substantially between item and associative recognition.

Because handedness was a variable of interest, we extended the primary analysis to include Handedness as a between-subjects factor. A mixed ANOVA with Test Type (within), Age Group (between), and Handedness (between) showed a main effect of Age Group, F(1,75) = 18.45, p < .001, partial η2 = .20, consistent with the previous analysis. The main effect of Handedness was not significant, F(1,75) = 0.32, p = .57, partial η2 = .00, indicating no overall difference between left- and right-handed participants in corrected recognition. The main effect of Test Type remained non-significant, F(1,75) = 2.11, p = .15, partial η2 = .03. Importantly, none of the interactions involving Handedness were significant: Handedness by Test Type, F(1,75) = 0.23, p = .64; Age Group by Handedness, F(1,75) = 0.07, p = .80; and the three-way interaction Age Group by Test Type by Handedness, F(1,75) = 0.12, p = .73. These findings indicate that handedness did not modulate the pattern of item versus associative recognition in younger or older adults.

To probe the possibility that small sample sizes for left-handed participants reduced power to detect handedness effects, we conducted follow-up analyses comparing mean scores of left- and right-handed participants within each age group. In the younger group, left-handed participants had mean corrected recognition of .812 (SD = .088) and right-handed participants had mean corrected recognition of .803 (SD = .096); the difference was not significant, t(38) = 0.23, p = .82. In the older group, left-handed participants had mean corrected recognition of .708 (SD = .126) and right-handed participants had mean corrected recognition of .718 (SD = .117); this difference was likewise not significant, t(37) = -0.20, p = .84. These within-group comparisons reinforce the conclusion that handedness was not associated with meaningful differences in recognition performance.

We also examined item and associative performance within each age group using paired-samples t-tests to determine whether there were within-group differences between test types. For younger adults, the difference between item (M = .838, SD = .080) and associative (M = .772, SD = .101) corrected recognition was statistically significant at a conventional alpha level, t(39) = 2.26, p = .03, Cohen’s d = 0.36, indicating a small-to-moderate advantage for item recognition. For older adults, the difference between item (M = .732, SD = .116) and associative (M = .698, SD = .130) corrected recognition was not statistically significant, t(38) = 1.17, p = .25, Cohen’s d = 0.19. Although younger adults showed a modest item advantage, the Age Group by Test Type interaction was not significant in the ANOVA, indicating that the magnitude of the item-associative differential did not differ reliably across age groups when taking between-subject variance into account.

Signal detection analyses using d’ produced results that paralleled the corrected recognition analyses. Across participants, younger adults exhibited higher d’ values than older adults for both item and associative tests. The mean d’ for younger adults was 2.10 (SD = 0.41) for item recognition and 1.92 (SD = 0.50) for associative recognition. For older adults, the mean d’ was 1.67 (SD = 0.58) for item recognition and 1.55 (SD = 0.61) for associative recognition. A mixed ANOVA on d’ scores again revealed a significant main effect of Age Group, F(1,75) = 16.38, p < .001, partial η2 = .18, and non-significant effects for Test Type, F(1,75) = 2.36, p = .13, partial η2 = .03, and Age Group by Test Type interaction, F(1,75) = 0.94, p = .34, partial η2 = .01. Handedness effects on d’ were not significant, and no interactions involving handedness were observed in the d’ analyses.

We conducted analyses of reaction time to assess potential speed-accuracy tradeoffs. Mean correct response times for younger adults were 972 ms (SD = 182) for item recognition and 1,038 ms (SD = 205) for associative recognition. For older adults, mean correct response times were 1,213 ms (SD = 262) for item recognition and 1,287 ms (SD = 281) for associative recognition. A mixed ANOVA on correct response times revealed a significant main effect of Age Group, F(1,75) = 44.10, p < .001, partial η2 = .37, with older adults responding more slowly than younger adults. There was also a significant main effect of Test Type on reaction time, F(1,75) = 9.22, p = .003, partial η2 = .11, with associative recognition yielding slower responses than item recognition across groups. No main effect of Handedness on reaction time was observed, F(1,75) = 0.06, p = .81, and no interactions involving Handedness were significant.

Because education could potentially confound age group comparisons, we repeated the primary mixed ANOVA on corrected recognition scores with years of education entered as a covariate. Education did not significantly predict corrected recognition in this sample, F(1,74) = 2.07, p = .15, and the main effect of Age Group remained robust, F(1,74) = 16.81, p < .001, partial η2 = .19. The inclusion of education did not alter the nonsignificant main effects or interactions involving Test Type or Handedness.

To further assess evidence for the absence of handedness effects, we conducted Bayesian analyses on the main effect of Handedness and on the Age Group by Test Type by Handedness three-way interaction. These Bayesian analyses yielded Bayes factors in favor of the null model over models including Handedness, with BF01 ≈ 4.0 for the main effect of Handedness and BF01 ≈ 3.5 for the three-way interaction. These Bayes factors provide moderate evidence supporting the null hypothesis that handedness does not influence corrected recognition performance in this verbal item and associative task.

Finally, exploratory correlations examined the relation between continuous age (within each age group) and memory performance. Within the older adult group, higher age was modestly associated with lower corrected recognition scores, r = -0.28, p = .06, a trend that did not reach conventional significance. Within the younger group, age was unrelated to corrected recognition, r = -0.05, p = .73. No significant correlations were observed between handedness laterality quotient (treated continuously) and corrected recognition scores within either age group, further supporting the conclusion that handedness was not a meaningful predictor of performance in this sample.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether handedness moderates age-related differences in item and associative recognition for verbal materials. Our primary findings can be summarized succinctly. First, younger adults outperformed older adults in corrected recognition and sensitivity (d’) across both item and associative tests, consistent with well-established age-related declines in episodic memory. Second, contrary to the associative deficit hypothesis as it is often operationalized, we did not observe a reliable Age Group by Test Type interaction; the relative difference between item and associative recognition did not differ significantly across younger and older adults. Third, handedness did not exert a significant main effect nor did it interact with age group or test type; left-handed participants did not perform better than right-handed participants on item or associative recognition. Bayesian analyses provided moderate support for the null hypothesis regarding handedness effects. Taken together, these results suggest that in the present task and sample, age-related memory decline was evident but did not selectively target associative binding in a way that interacted with handedness.

The robust main effect of age that we observed aligns with a large literature documenting declines in episodic memory performance with advancing age. The current results replicate the often-reported pattern that older adults show lower accuracy and slower response times in recognition paradigms. These effects were evident in both item and associative tests and were observed across both corrected recognition and d’ measures, indicating that the disadvantage for older adults was not simply due to response bias but reflected reduced sensitivity. Reaction time analyses also revealed slower responding in older adults, which is consistent with generalized slowing models of cognitive aging and could reflect both slower perceptual-motor processes and slower mnemonic retrieval operations.

However, the absence of a significant Age Group by Test Type interaction is notable because it runs counter to a straightforward interpretation of the associative deficit hypothesis, which would predict a larger relative impairment on associative recognition for older adults. Several factors may account for the lack of a differential associative deficit in our sample. One possibility concerns task demands and encoding instructions. We explicitly instructed participants to form interactive images or sentences for paired items, which encouraged elaborative associative encoding strategies that can mitigate associative deficits. When older adults are provided with effective strategies or when task conditions allow for deeper encoding, associative memory performance can improve. Therefore, the supportive encoding environment used here may have reduced the expected age-related associative impairment.

A related consideration is that our associative stimuli were composed of concrete nouns paired in a manner that avoided highly unusual or unrelated combinations but that nevertheless may have afforded semantic linking. If participants were able to impose meaningful connections between words even when they were arbitrarily paired, such semantic scaffolding could facilitate binding and thereby attenuate age differences. Future studies that vary the degree of semantic relatedness and that manipulate strategy instructions could clarify the boundary conditions under which associative deficits emerge.

Another possibility is that our sample characteristics affected sensitivity to associative deficits. The older adults tested here were community-dwelling and screened for obvious cognitive impairment, and they had relatively high levels of education. Cognitive reserve associated with education and lifestyle factors can attenuate age-related cognitive decline and might have helped older participants compensate for associative demands. Although we controlled for education statistically and found that it did not account for the age effect, unmeasured aspects of cognitive reserve or lifestyle may still have contributed to preserved associative performance.

Moreover, measurement issues concerning the associative test may have played a role. Corrected recognition scores and d’ are useful summary measures, but they may not capture more nuanced differences in retrieval processes, such as recollection versus familiarity contributions. Older adults often show reduced recollection but preserved familiarity, and associative recognition probes are thought to rely more on recollection. If participants used familiarity-based judgments or if the associative test could be solved to some extent via familiarity with individual items, that could diminish the sensitivity of the associative measure to binding deficits. A more fine-grained approach, such as obtaining remember/know judgments or using source memory paradigms, might reveal age-related associative deficits that the current measures did not detect.

The absence of a handedness effect in our verbal memory task is informative and suggests that previously reported left-handed advantages in certain auditory pitch tasks do not readily generalize to verbal item and associative recognition for words. There are several plausible reasons for this lack of generalization. First, handedness-related differences in lateralization may be domain-specific: left-handed individuals might indeed exhibit advantages in auditory pitch processing due to differences in the organization of auditory cortex or interhemispheric connectivity, yet these neural organizational differences may not confer advantages for hippocampal-dependent verbal associative binding. Second, the handedness measure itself may be insufficiently sensitive. We used a standard laterality inventory to classify participants dichotomously as left- or right-handed, but the neural correlates of handedness are more continuous and multifaceted. A more detailed characterization of lateralization using functional imaging or dichotic listening tasks might better capture individual differences that relate to memory performance.

Third, statistical power to detect handedness effects may have been limited. Left-handedness is relatively uncommon in the population, and our sample included only 12 left-handed individuals. Although Bayesian analyses suggested moderate evidence for the null, the small number of left-handers means that small to moderate effects could have gone undetected. Larger samples that oversample left-handed participants would be better positioned to detect subtle effects or interactions involving handedness.

Fourth, handedness-related advantages reported in the literature may be specific to tasks that involve tonal or pitch discrimination and may depend on fine-grained auditory processing that is not central to the verbal associative tasks used here. If the neural substrates supporting pitch memory are largely distinct from those supporting verbal associative binding, then an effect of handedness in the former domain would not be expected to transfer to the latter. Future work that includes both pitch and verbal associative tasks within the same participants could directly test the domain specificity of handedness effects.

The present findings have implications for the associative deficit hypothesis. The hypothesis remains a valuable framework for understanding certain age-related memory changes, but our results suggest that its predictions are context-dependent. Specifically, when encoding conditions encourage elaboration, when stimuli permit semantic linking, or when participants have sufficient cognitive reserve or strategy use, associative deficits may be attenuated or absent. This underscores the idea that associative memory outcomes result from an interaction between neurobiological aging processes and task demands and strategies. The current study therefore supports a more nuanced view of associative deficits, one that recognizes the importance of encoding supports, participant characteristics, and stimulus properties.

Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. As noted, the number of left-handed participants was relatively small, limiting the power to detect handedness effects; future work should recruit larger and more balanced samples of left- and right-handers. In addition, our use of corrected recognition and d’ as primary dependent measures, while standard, may have insufficiently discriminated between recollection and familiarity; incorporating measures that directly index recollection, such as source memory tasks or remember/know judgments, would provide a clearer test of associative binding deficits. Furthermore, our sample of older adults was relatively high functioning and well educated, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to older cohorts with broader ranges of cognitive status and educational backgrounds. Finally, the cross-sectional design precludes inferences about within-individual changes across time; longitudinal designs would be necessary to chart trajectories of associative and item memory and to examine whether handedness moderates the rate of decline.

Several avenues for future research follow naturally from our findings. One promising direction is to manipulate encoding instructions more systematically to assess how much elaborative strategies can offset associative deficits at different ages. Experimental manipulations could compare incidental encoding, shallow encoding, and deep elaborative encoding to quantify the degree of compensation that instruction can produce. Another approach is to include neuroimaging measures, such as functional MRI or diffusion-weighted imaging, to examine how handedness relates to patterns of hemispheric activation and interhemispheric connectivity during item and associative encoding and retrieval. Such neuroimaging data could clarify whether handedness-related organizational differences in the brain are linked to differences in memory networks and whether these relationships change with age.

Extending the assessment of handedness beyond self-report inventories to direct measures of lateralization would also strengthen future studies. For example, including dichotic listening tasks, transcranial magnetic stimulation probes, or functional lateralization tasks during neuroimaging could provide richer, physiologically grounded indices of hemispheric specialization. These measures could then be related to behavioral performance in verbal and auditory memory domains to test domain-specific or domain-general effects of lateralization.

Another valuable extension would be to include a broader range of associative paradigms, such as object–location, face–name, and cross-modal pairings, to determine whether the absence of an associative deficit in our verbal task generalizes to other associative domains. Comparing associative performance across modalities could reveal whether certain types of associations are more vulnerable to age or more sensitive to handedness-related differences. In addition, incorporating measures of cognitive reserve, such as lifetime cognitive activities, occupational attainment, and bilingualism, could help elucidate the protective factors that modulate associative memory performance in older adults.

Finally, longitudinal work that tracks individuals over time would be instrumental in determining whether handedness predicts differential trajectories of memory decline. If handedness were to moderate longitudinal decline, this would have implications for early identification of individuals at risk for accelerated memory impairment and might inform targeted intervention strategies.

In sum, the present study demonstrates a clear age-related decline in recognition memory for verbal materials but does not find evidence that associative deficits differentially affect older adults in the context of supportive encoding instructions or that handedness predicts item or associative recognition performance. These findings suggest that associative memory deficits in aging are modulated by task and participant factors and that handedness, at least as measured here, does not offer a simple explanatory variable for individual differences in verbal associative binding. Future work employing larger samples of left-handers, more sensitive measures of lateralization, neural imaging, and diverse associative paradigms will be necessary to fully characterize the interplay among handedness, hemispheric organization, and age-related memory change.
