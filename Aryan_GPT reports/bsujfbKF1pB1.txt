Introduction

Social media use has become a central feature of daily life for many young adults. Platforms that emphasize images and lifestyle presentation have grown especially popular. Among these platforms, visual-focused apps have drawn attention for their influence on appearance norms. Visual social media allows rapid dissemination of idealized body images. Users are exposed frequently to curated and edited content from peers and influencers. Exposure to such content can shape norms about what bodies are desirable and attainable. These norms can influence individual attitudes toward exercise and diet. There is a growing public interest in using social media as a resource for health and weight management. Some users report following accounts that depict fitness routines and healthy eating. Others follow accounts that primarily showcase idealized body shapes and progress photos. Both types of content can serve as models for behavior change. The mechanisms by which social media affects behavior include social learning and social comparison. Observing others perform exercise and share meals can provide practical ideas for one's own routine. Observing idealized bodies can create aspirational goals that may motivate behavior. Social support embedded in online communities may also play a role in sustaining health behavior. The literature on social media and health behavior has grown rapidly in recent years. Many studies focus on outcomes such as body dissatisfaction and disordered eating. Other studies examine positive outcomes including motivation to exercise and improved diet awareness. Findings in the literature are mixed and context dependent. Some research indicates that exposure to idealized images undermines self-esteem and can reduce motivation. Other research suggests that seeing peers engage in healthy behaviors can encourage similar actions. The content characteristics matter for whether exposure is motivating or harmful. For example, educational posts about exercise technique may be more beneficial than images that emphasize appearance alone. The source of the content also influences outcomes. Content from close peers tends to have different effects than content from distant influencers. The degree of identification with the content creator shapes the impact on the viewer. Young adults are particularly likely to engage with visual social media as part of identity formation. University students often use such platforms to find role models and lifestyle tips. This developmental context makes them an important population for study. Behavioral self-monitoring is another pathway through which social media can affect diet and exercise. Platforms that encourage posting progress photos or workouts can increase accountability. Accountability, in turn, is known to promote maintenance of exercise habits. The interactive nature of social media also allows for reinforcement through likes and comments. Positive feedback can strengthen intentions to continue health-promoting behaviors. Negative feedback or harsh comparison may have the opposite effect. Understanding the net effect of social media exposure requires attention to user motivations and behaviors. Some users actively seek out content that supports health goals. Others passively scroll through feeds without clear intentions. Intentional following of fitness-related bloggers may differ from incidental exposure to idealized images. The present study focuses on intentional following of bloggers who post their considered ideal bodies and health-related content. It seeks to examine the relationship between such following behavior and self-reported diet and exercise. This area of inquiry has practical implications for interventions that leverage social media for weight management. If following idealized and health-focused bloggers is associated with increased exercise and diet consciousness, platforms could be designed to encourage such exposure. Conversely, if such exposure is associated with harmful outcomes, caution would be warranted in promoting content-based interventions. Prior studies that link social media use to behavior change have varied in their operational definitions of use. Some studies measure general time spent on platforms. Other studies measure content-specific engagement such as following fitness accounts. Content-specific measures are likely to have greater predictive validity for health behaviors. Therefore, assessing the extent to which individuals follow bloggers with idealized bodies provides a focused measure of exposure. The current research uses such a focused measure to examine its correlation with composite measures of diet and exercise behavior. The hypothesis is that greater use of the platform to follow these bloggers is positively correlated with more frequent exercise and greater diet consciousness. The study was designed to test this hypothesis in a sample of university students aged 18 to 24. A cross-sectional correlational design was chosen to provide an initial empirical assessment. This design allows for identification of relationships that can inform subsequent experimental work. The study contributes to the literature by linking content-specific social media use with concrete behavioral indicators. It also addresses a gap related to the potential for social media to act as a supportive tool for weight management. The following sections describe the methods used, the statistical tests conducted, and the results obtained. The implications of the findings are then discussed in light of theoretical frameworks and practical applications. The paper concludes by outlining limitations and proposing directions for future research. Taken together, this work aims to clarify whether intentional engagement with health and appearance content on image-based social media is associated with self-reported health behaviors.

Methods

One hundred undergraduate students from a single university participated in the study. All participants were aged between 18 and 24 years. Recruitment occurred through campus emails and posters advertising a short online survey. Participation was voluntary and no payment was provided beyond the option to enter a prize draw. Ethical approval was obtained from the university's ethics committee prior to data collection. Participants provided informed consent electronically at the start of the online survey. Demographic information was collected including age, gender, year of study, and nationality. Participants also provided self-reported height and weight, which were used to compute body mass index. The main predictor variable was the extent of Instagram use to follow bloggers who posted idealized body images and health-related content. This construct was measured using a newly developed Instagram Use for Ideal Bodies scale. The scale included eight items that tapped frequency and intensity of following such bloggers. Sample items included "I regularly follow accounts that show what I consider an ideal body" and "I often look to fitness blogs on Instagram for inspiration about exercise and diet". Respondents rated each item on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Scores on these items were summed to produce an Instagram Use score with a possible range from 8 to 40. The dependent variable was a composite Diet and Exercise Behavior score. This score combined self-reported exercise frequency, exercise duration, and dietary consciousness. Exercise frequency was assessed with an item asking how many days per week the participant engaged in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity. Exercise duration was assessed with an item asking for the typical number of hours per week spent exercising. Dietary consciousness was measured using six items adapted from existing measures of healthy eating awareness. Example dietary items included "I plan my meals to meet health goals" and "I am careful about portion sizes". Each dietary item used the same five-point Likert scale as the Instagram Use scale. The exercise frequency and duration items were standardized and combined with the dietary items to form the composite Diet and Exercise Behavior score. Higher scores indicated greater engagement in exercise and greater awareness or control of diet. Internal reliability for the Instagram Use scale and the Dietary items was assessed using Cronbach's alpha. Prior to main analyses, data were screened for missing values and outliers. Cases with more than 10% missing data were excluded from analyses. Remaining missing data points were handled using mean imputation within scale items. Descriptive statistics including means, standard deviations, and ranges were calculated for all variables. The primary analysis used Pearson's correlation to estimate the strength and direction of the relationship between Instagram Use and the Diet and Exercise Behavior score. Prior to running correlations, assumptions of normality and linearity were checked using skewness, kurtosis, and scatterplots. Homoscedasticity was examined by inspecting residual plots. Where assumptions were questionable, Spearman's rank correlation was computed as a robustness check. Additional analyses included partial correlations controlling for gender and BMI to assess whether the observed relationship was robust to these covariates. All statistical tests were conducted using standard statistical software with a two-tailed alpha level of .05. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional benchmarks and confidence intervals were reported where applicable. The full survey took participants on average approximately fifteen minutes to complete. Participants were debriefed at the end of the survey and provided with links to university wellbeing resources. Data were stored on secure university servers and handled in accordance with data protection regulations. No identifying information was linked to the responses during analysis.

Results

Data from one hundred participants were included in the analysis. The sample consisted of 62 female and 38 male students. The mean age of participants was 20.6 years with a standard deviation of 1.7 years. The computed body mass index had a mean of 22.5 and a standard deviation of 3.1. The Instagram Use score ranged from 10 to 39 with a mean of 24.7 and a standard deviation of 6.2. The Diet and Exercise Behavior composite score had a range from 2.1 to 4.8 on its standardized metric. The mean Diet and Exercise score was 3.2 with a standard deviation of 0.7. Internal consistency for the Instagram Use scale was acceptable with Cronbach's alpha of .83. Internal consistency for the dietary items was satisfactory with Cronbach's alpha of .79. Examination of skewness and kurtosis indicated that both the Instagram Use score and the Diet and Exercise score were within tolerable limits for parametric testing. Visual inspection of scatterplots suggested a positive linear relationship between the two variables. Inspection for outliers identified two cases with extreme Instagram Use scores. Sensitivity analyses excluding these two cases produced very similar results to the full sample and were therefore not excluded. The primary Pearson correlation between Instagram Use and the Diet and Exercise composite was r = .569. This correlation was statistically significant at p < .001 using a two-tailed test. The observed t statistic for this correlation was approximately 6.85 with 98 degrees of freedom. The effect size indicates a moderate to strong positive association between following ideal-body bloggers on Instagram and self-reported diet and exercise behaviors. To assess robustness, a Spearman rank-order correlation was also computed. The Spearman correlation coefficient was rho = .542 and remained significant at p < .001. A partial correlation controlling for gender yielded r_partial = .522 and remained significant at p < .001. A partial correlation controlling for BMI produced r_partial = .506 and was significant at p < .001. A further partial correlation controlling for both gender and BMI produced r_partial = .489 and remained significant at p < .001. These partial correlations indicate that the observed relationship is not fully explained by gender or body mass index. Subgroup analyses indicated that the correlation held in both female and male subsamples, although it was stronger among female participants. Among females, the correlation was r = .61 and significant at p < .001. Among males, the correlation was r = .42 and significant at p = .006. Mean Instagram Use scores differed by gender, with females reporting a slightly higher mean than males. Mean Diet and Exercise scores did not differ significantly by gender. Regression analyses were conducted to obtain an estimate of the predictive relationship. A simple linear regression with Instagram Use predicting Diet and Exercise score yielded a standardized beta of .57. The model explained approximately 32% of the variance in the Diet and Exercise composite (R^2 = .324). Adding gender and BMI as covariates increased the model R^2 slightly to .35. In the adjusted model Instagram Use remained a significant predictor with p < .001. Confidence intervals for the primary correlation did not include zero, further supporting the reliability of the association. Overall, the results indicate a robust positive relationship between the targeted Instagram use measure and self-reported diet and exercise behaviors in this sample.

Discussion

The present study found a statistically significant positive correlation between self-reported Instagram use to follow idealized body and health bloggers and a composite measure of diet and exercise behavior among university students. The strength of the association was moderate to strong and remained robust after controlling for gender and BMI. These findings suggest that intentional engagement with fitness and ideal-body related content on visual social media is associated with higher levels of exercise and greater dietary consciousness. One plausible interpretation is that following bloggers who model exercise and healthy eating provides inspiration and practical guidance that facilitates behavior change. Social learning theory offers a useful framework to understand this association. Observing models who perform exercises and share meal ideas can increase self-efficacy and provide actionable strategies. When users identify with content creators, they may be more likely to adopt similar routines and habits. The positive correlation observed here aligns with perspectives that view social media as a potential resource for health promotion. At the same time, the correlational design of the study precludes causal conclusions. It is possible that individuals who are already motivated to exercise and to be conscious of their diet are more likely to seek out and follow such content. In other words, reverse causation and self-selection remain viable alternative explanations. The partial correlations controlling for gender and BMI reduced but did not eliminate the association, indicating that these factors only partially account for the observed relationship. The gender differences observed in subgroup analyses, with a stronger correlation among female participants, are noteworthy. Females in this sample reported slightly higher Instagram Use scores. This pattern suggests that gender may moderate the strength of the link between social media content and health behavior. Previous work has frequently observed gendered differences in social media engagement related to body image and health. Women often experience stronger pressures related to appearance and may be more likely to use social media in ways that connect to diet and exercise. However, the association evident among male participants indicates that the phenomenon is not limited to women. The finding that the Instagram Use scale had good internal reliability supports the validity of the measure used in this investigation. The dietary items also showed acceptable consistency, suggesting that the composite score represented a coherent construct. Nonetheless, the measures relied on self-report and are therefore subject to biases such as social desirability and retrospective inaccuracies. Participants may overreport healthy behaviors or the degree to which they follow health-focused accounts. Future research would benefit from including objective measures of behavior such as wearable activity trackers and food logs verified by technology. Another limitation is the cross-sectional nature of the data. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether following ideal-body bloggers leads to increases in exercise and diet consciousness over time. Experimental designs, such as randomized exposure to different types of content, would help disentangle causality. Such designs can also test for potential harmful effects, including increases in body dissatisfaction or disordered eating behaviors. The present results do not address the qualitative nature of the content being followed. Some bloggers may emphasize balanced nutrition and safe exercise practices, while others may promote extreme dieting or unrealistic body ideals. The net effect of following such accounts likely depends on content characteristics such as focus on appearance versus functionality. Future studies should employ content analysis to categorize the types of posts and to examine which categories are most strongly associated with positive behavioral outcomes. The social context of following is also important to consider. Engagement in comments, direct messages, and reciprocal social ties may amplify the effects of content exposure. Social reinforcement through likes and comments could serve as motivators or could increase social comparison and pressure. In addition to content and social context, individual differences in susceptibility to social comparison should be examined as potential moderators. For instance, baseline body dissatisfaction or trait social comparison orientation may influence how users respond to idealized images. Interventions could be personalized based on such moderating characteristics to maximize benefits and minimize harm. The sample in this study consisted of university students and therefore may not generalize to other age groups or to populations with different socioeconomic or cultural backgrounds. Young adults are heavy users of visual social platforms, which made them a logical focus for this initial study. However, generalization to older adults, adolescents, or clinical populations should be made with caution. The observed effect size, explaining roughly one third of the variance in the Diet and Exercise composite, is noteworthy in the context of behavioral research. It suggests that social media engagement with specific content could be an influential factor in health-related behavior among young adults. From an applied perspective, these findings point to the potential of leveraging visual social media platforms in weight loss and health promotion programs. Practitioners and program designers might partner with influencers or create curated content streams that model sustainable exercise routines and balanced eating. However, such efforts must be implemented carefully to avoid reinforcing harmful appearance-focused messaging. Ethical considerations arise when promoting content that emphasizes body ideals, especially for vulnerable individuals. Clear guidelines and collaborations with trained health professionals may mitigate potential harms. Platforms themselves could offer features that prioritize evidence-based, health-promoting content and flag content that may be misleading or extreme. Future intervention studies can test the efficacy of platform-based programs that combine educational material with community support and modeling. For example, randomized controlled trials might compare an Instagram-based intervention to standard educational programs in terms of increasing physical activity and improving diet. It would also be valuable to examine dose-response relationships between the amount and type of exposure and behavioral outcomes. Moderation analyses can reveal thresholds beyond which exposure becomes less beneficial or even detrimental. Qualitative methods could enrich understanding by revealing how users interpret and integrate social media content into their daily routines. Interviews and diary studies can capture nuanced processes such as motivation, identity shifts, and emotional responses to content. The present study adds to a growing literature that recognizes the complex role of social media in shaping health behaviors. It underscores the importance of distinguishing between general platform use and specific content engagement when studying behavioral outcomes. By focusing on following behavior related to idealized bodies and health content, the study provides a more fine-grained perspective than measures of overall screen time. Nevertheless, additional work is needed to map the boundary conditions of the observed association. Researchers should also consider potential unintended consequences such as disordered eating, excessive exercise, or body image disturbance in future investigations. In sum, the positive correlation observed here suggests that content-specific engagement with visual social media may be associated with greater exercise and diet consciousness among university students. The results are promising for the idea that social media can be harnessed to support healthy behavior change, but caution is required. Longitudinal and experimental research will be essential to determine causality and to design safe, effective interventions. Scholars, practitioners, and platform designers should collaborate to explore how best to leverage the motivational potential of social media while minimizing harms. The current findings provide a foundation for such collaborative efforts and point to a rich agenda for future research.
