Introduction

Decisions under uncertainty are a central concern across psychology, economics, and related disciplines because many real-world choices involve outcomes that are not known with certainty at the time a decision is made. Researchers have therefore devoted considerable effort to understanding how people represent probabilities and how different presentation formats affect choices. One well-established empirical phenomenon in this literature is the decision-from-description versus decision-from-experience distinction, which captures systematic differences in how people treat rare and extreme events depending on whether probabilities are explicitly stated or must be inferred from samples. The phenomenon is important because it links cognitive processes of learning and representation with behavioral outcomes that have implications for health, finance, and public policy. Early studies documented that when people are given numerical descriptions of risks, they tend to overweight small probabilities, consistent with prospect-theoretic predictions of probability weighting functions that are inverse-S shaped. In contrast, when people learn about the same probability distributions through sampling experience, a counterintuitive reversal often occurs: rare events tend to be underweighted relative to their objective occurrence. This difference between description and experience has been termed the description–experience gap and has been reported in a range of contexts and paradigms. The gap is theoretically interesting because it suggests that different cognitive and memory mechanisms might be engaged when probabilities are presented explicitly versus when they must be learned through sampling, and that these mechanisms have systematic biases that alter decision making.

A prominent line of work has emphasized that sampling-based learning imposes limitations on the representation of rare events because sampling variability makes them less salient or less frequently represented in memory. When sampling from the true underlying distribution, rare events occur infrequently, and participants who draw only a modest number of samples are likely to underrepresent such events in their mental model. This underrepresentation can then lead to underweighting of rare-event probabilities in subsequent choices. Complementary explanations have pointed to memory retrieval processes that favor more frequent or recent events, and to intuitive heuristics that rely on the subjective availability of outcomes. By contrast, description presents probabilities explicitly and thus bypasses the sampling limitation; when small probabilities are salient as numerical quantities, they may be subject to cognitive transformations that amplify their impact on valuation, producing overweighting instead of underweighting.

Work exploring the description–experience gap has used a variety of experimental paradigms, including computerized sampling tasks in which participants can draw repeatedly from stochastic outcome-generating devices, and description tasks in which probabilities and payoffs are given in numerical form. One influential set of findings used computerized “sampling with replacement” procedures in which participants drew from virtual urns many times to learn the distribution; in parallel description trials, participants were shown the exact probabilities. These studies consistently reported that participants overweighted rare outcomes in described choices but underweighted the same outcomes in experienced choices. The robustness of these findings across multiple samples and laboratories has contributed to the gap being treated as a core empirical regularity that any account of risky choice must address.

However, subsequent work has also shown that the presence and magnitude of the description–experience gap can be sensitive to task features and to participant-level variables. For example, the number of samples allowed or taken in experience situations modulates the gap: with very large samples, experienced probabilities converge on the true frequency and the gap can shrink. Other manipulations such as the presence of feedback about the objective probabilities, or variations in payoff magnitudes and framing, alter the pattern of results. Differences in the presentation medium, such as tangible objects versus screen-based displays, may also influence salient aspects of experience and memory. Furthermore, individual differences such as cognitive ability, numeracy, and personality traits have been investigated as moderators of description–experience effects, with mixed results. Some studies report that higher numeracy attenuates the gap by improving learning from experience, while others find no reliable associations. These mixed findings point to a complex interaction among sampling, memory, cognition, and task format, leaving open the conditions under which description and experience yield divergent choices.

One domain of emerging interest is the role of dispositional optimism in risky decision making. Optimism, broadly defined as the tendency to expect favorable outcomes in the future, has been linked to differences in risk perception, probability judgments, and approach-avoidance behavior. Individuals higher in optimism may systematically estimate probabilities differently, favoring optimistic outcomes, or they may weigh potential gains and losses in a biased manner. Several studies have explored optimism’s relationship to risk taking, finding varied patterns: some report that optimism predicts greater risk taking in gain domains but more risk aversion in loss domains, while others find weak or null associations. Yet the extent to which optimism moderates the description–experience gap has been little explored. One might hypothesize that optimistic individuals would underweight low-probability negative events even more when they learn through experience, due to selective attention and memory processes that support favorable expectations. Alternatively, optimism might not systematically alter the cognitive sampling and memory processes that drive the gap, in which case no association would be observed.

Another methodological factor that has received relatively little direct attention is the physicality of the sampling medium used in experience-based tasks. Much of the experimental work on the description–experience gap has relied on computerized sampling procedures, where participants click to draw outcomes that are visually displayed on a screen. Such displays are convenient and allow tight experimental control, but they lack some of the sensory richness and salience of physical sampling. Other researchers have used tangible objects—such as cards, balls, or counters in bags—to create an experiential sampling procedure that is more concrete and possibly more memorable. The use of tangible objects introduces subtle differences: sampling may be without replacement if objects are not returned to the container, tactile cues and visual inspection can create stronger encoding, and participants may perceive the sampling as more “real,” which could alter attention and subsequent choice. Given that description–experience effects are argued to arise from cognitive sampling limitations and memory retrieval biases, it is plausible that making the sampling experience more tangible could mitigate underweighting of rare events by increasing the salience and memorability of each drawn outcome. To date, relatively few studies have directly compared tactile versus computerized sampling in the context of the description–experience gap, leaving an important empirical gap.

The present study was designed to test two interrelated questions. First, we sought to examine whether the description–experience gap observed in prior work replicates when the experience condition is implemented using a tangible sampling medium—specifically, counters in physical bags—rather than computer-generated draws. Second, we aimed to investigate whether individual differences in dispositional optimism are associated with the propensity to choose risky options, and whether optimism moderates any observed description–experience differences. We selected these questions because they address both theoretical mechanisms and practical concerns. If the gap is robust to changes in sampling modality, it would strengthen process-based accounts that attribute the effect to intrinsic properties of sampling and memory that are independent of medium. If, instead, the gap attenuates or disappears when participants sample physical objects, this would suggest that previously reported underweighting in experience might be partly an artifact of the computerized sampling context.

Our approach was modeled on earlier experiments that contrasted described and experienced choices using simple gambling problems with rare events. In description trials, participants were presented with textual and numerical descriptions of choice options, including explicit probabilities for different outcomes. In experience trials, participants were allowed to sample outcomes from a distribution by drawing counters from a bag. To minimize other differences across conditions, payoff magnitudes and nominal probabilities were matched closely between description and experience conditions, and the same choice options were offered. Participants were randomly assigned to either a description-first or experience-first order in order to control for potential order effects, and within-subjects comparisons were not used in order to avoid contamination between described and experienced formats. We also included a validated self-report measure of dispositional optimism to examine individual differences.

We had several pre-registered expectations. First, based on the prior literature, we expected to observe the classic description–experience gap, such that participants in the description condition would overweight small probabilities relative to participants in the experience condition. Specifically, when faced with a choice between a risky option with a low-probability large payoff and a safer option with smaller but more certain payoffs, we predicted a higher proportion of risky choices in the description condition. Second, regarding optimism, we anticipated that higher dispositional optimism might predict a greater tendency to select options that favor favorable outcomes, though we left open whether this would manifest as increased risk taking overall or as a moderation of the description–experience relationship.

We also planned several exploratory analyses to probe sampling behavior and subjective probability estimates. In the experience condition, we recorded the number of samples participants chose to draw and examined whether sampling depth correlated with choice. We also elicited post-decision probability estimates from a subset of participants to assess how well sampled frequencies translated into explicit beliefs. These exploratory measures were intended to illuminate the cognitive processes by which tangible sampling might influence valuation. Finally, we included manipulation checks designed to verify whether participants understood the task and whether they perceived the sampling procedure as realistic.

The present study thus combines replication intent with methodological innovation by substituting a tangible sampling medium for the computer-based draw. It contributes to debates about the generality of the description–experience gap and about the role of individual differences such as optimism in shaping risky choices. By carefully matching payoffs and probabilities across conditions and by measuring sampling behavior directly, the study aims to isolate the impact of presentation medium from other task features. Given the practical implications for how risk information is communicated—whether via numbers, narratives, or experience-based demonstrations—the results bear on domains that range from public health communication to consumer choice.

We also underscored the importance of sample size and statistical power in detecting the description–experience gap, especially because prior effect size estimates vary across studies. We therefore collected a sample of moderate size, balancing feasibility and sensitivity, and we analyzed the data using both frequentist and Bayesian approaches to quantify evidence both for and against the presence of the gap. The combination of preregistered hypotheses and exploratory probes was intended to promote transparent interpretation.

Finally, we noted several boundary conditions that could limit the generality of the findings. For example, physical sampling might itself introduce cues that differ from both classic description and classic experience formats, such as visual inspection of the bag or social context of handling objects. Additionally, participants’ prior beliefs and familiarity with physical counters could interact with task features. We therefore designed the procedure to minimize extraneous cues and to standardize instructions while acknowledging that tangible sampling is inherently different from computerized draws.

In sum, the present study examined whether the description–experience gap replicates when sampling experience is made tangible, and whether dispositional optimism predicts or moderates risky choice in these formats. The study’s results have implications for theoretical accounts of the gap, for methodological choices in experimental research on risky choice, and for practical decisions about how to present risk information in applied contexts.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from the surrounding community to obtain a sample of adults aged 18 and older. In total, 120 participants were enrolled in the study and completed the experimental session. We aimed for this sample size to provide adequate power to detect a medium effect size in between-group comparisons, while also remaining feasible given resources and logistics. Participants were compensated with a small monetary payment for their time, and they were additionally informed that some choices would be incentivized with real payouts determined by one randomly selected trial. All participants provided informed consent in accordance with institutional guidelines and ethical approval was obtained prior to data collection.

Design

The study employed a between-subjects design with one primary experimental factor: decision format (description versus experience). Participants were randomly assigned to one of the two format conditions. Within each condition participants completed a series of risky choice trials that were nominally identical in terms of payoff structure and objective probabilities; the only intended difference was whether probabilities and outcomes were described numerically or experienced through sampling. Optimism was measured as a continuous individual difference variable and treated as a moderator in secondary analyses.

Materials

Choice tasks. The main task consisted of six binary choices between a risky option featuring a low-probability large outcome and a safer option with a smaller but higher-probability outcome. The specific payoff structures were selected to mirror those used in prior description–experience research: three “gain” domain problems in which the risky option offered a small chance of a large monetary gain, and three “loss” domain problems in which the risky option offered a small chance of a large monetary loss. For example, one gain problem presented a choice between Option A: 10% chance to win $10 and 90% chance to win $0 (risky), and Option B: 100% chance to win $1 (safe). The probabilities and payoffs were matched across the description and experience conditions. The objective rare-event probability was set at 10% for the risky options to ensure a clear “rare” category while allowing a manageable number of samples to potentially reveal rare events.

Experience materials. For participants in the experience condition, physical bags containing plastic counters were used to simulate the outcome distributions. Following a practice phase, each bag was constructed to contain 10 counters, one of which produced the rare outcome and nine of which produced the common outcome. Counters were visually identical on the exterior and were mixed in the bag before sampling. Participants drew counters sequentially by reaching into the bag and pulling a single counter, then observed the outcome and returned the counter to the bag or, in some trials, did not return it depending on the block specification (see below). Instructions emphasized that draws were random and that the contents matched the described distributions, but participants were not given explicit numerical probabilities in the experience condition.

Description materials. In the description condition, participants were presented with written descriptions of the same choice options, including explicit numerical probabilities and payoffs. The descriptions were presented on paper sheets in a standardized format similar to those used in previous work. To maximize comparability to the experience condition, lists of outcomes and payoffs were structured similarly and the numerical probabilities matched the underlying compositions of the bags used in the experience condition.

Optimism measure. Dispositional optimism was measured using a standardized self-report questionnaire widely used in behavioral research. The scale consists of six items that assess generalized expectancies for positive outcomes, with responses provided on a five-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Scores were summed to yield an overall optimism index that ranged from low to high optimism.

Procedure

Participants arrived at the laboratory and completed informed consent. They were then randomly assigned to either the description condition or the experience condition using a pre-generated randomization list. All participants first completed a brief practice phase designed to familiarize them with the procedure and to reduce the influence of initial confusion. In the practice phase, participants in the experience condition sampled a practice bag with an equal split of counters, while participants in the description condition were presented with a practice descriptive problem.

Experimental trials were administered one at a time in a fixed sequence. In the description condition, for each trial participants read the written description of the two options and recorded their choice on a response sheet. They then proceeded to the next trial with a brief inter-trial interval. Descriptions were presented without any additional sampling opportunity and participants were encouraged to make choices based on the provided information.

In the experience condition, participants were instructed that they could draw as many samples as they wished from the bag prior to making a choice. They were told that the bag contained counters with two possible outcomes and that drawing a counter would reveal an outcome representative of the underlying composition. To standardize the procedure while allowing naturalistic sampling, participants were told that they could draw up to 20 samples per trial and that they could stop sampling whenever they felt they had enough information. After sampling, participants made their choice between the two options. For practical reasons, we implemented sampling with replacement by instructing participants to return the drawn counter to the bag and to mix the bag between draws; this approach maintained the underlying probability distribution across draws and aligned more closely with common computerized sampling procedures. The number of draws per trial was recorded automatically by the experimenter.

After participants completed all six choice trials, they completed the optimism questionnaire and a simple manipulation check assessing comprehension and perceptions of realism. In the experience condition, they were also asked to estimate the probability of the rare event for each choice to allow assessment of subjective probability calibration. A subset of participants in the description condition also provided probability estimates for exploratory comparability.

Incentives were implemented by randomly selecting one of the six trials for payment at the end of the session. For selected trials, payouts were resolved according to the participant’s chosen option and the probabilistic payoffs were implemented by drawing a counter from the appropriate bag for experience participants or by using a random number generator for description participants. This incentivization approach is standard practice to align choices with real monetary consequences while keeping overall payment costs manageable.

Counterbalancing and checks. To control for potential order effects across gain and loss frames, the order of the six trials was randomized for each participant. Additional checks included questions probing whether participants believed the draws were random and whether they found the physical sampling realistic. The experimenter stayed in the room to observe the procedure and to ensure adherence to instructions, but did not interact with participants except to provide clarification if requested.

Data preparation and analysis plan

The primary dependent variable was the binary choice on each trial (risky versus safe). For the main analyses, we computed the proportion of risky choices for each participant across the six trials and compared these proportions between the description and experience groups. Because some trials involved gain framing and others loss framing, we also computed separate proportions for gain and loss trials to examine potential framing effects. Optimism scores were centered and treated as continuous covariates in regression analyses.

We planned to test the primary hypothesis using both frequentist tests (independent-samples t-tests and logistic regression) and Bayesian analyses to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis. Secondary analyses included correlational tests between number of samples drawn and choice behavior in the experience group, and comparisons of subjective probability estimates and objective frequencies. All statistical analyses were performed using standard statistical software, and significance thresholds were set at p < .05 for inferential tests.

Results

Descriptive statistics and data screening

Data from all 120 participants were included in the primary analyses. There were 60 participants assigned to the description condition and 60 participants assigned to the experience condition. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 63 years, with a mean age of 26.4 years and a standard deviation of 8.2 years. The sample included 68 female participants, 50 male participants, and 2 participants who indicated a nonbinary response. Mean optimism score across the sample was 18.9 on the scale used, with a standard deviation of 4.2; the distribution of optimism scores approximated normality with only modest skew. No participants were excluded for failure to follow instructions, and all participants completed the random trial payout at the end of the session.

We first examined whether baseline characteristics differed between the description and experience groups. Age distributions did not differ significantly between groups, t(118) = 0.68, p = .50. Gender composition was similar across groups, χ2(2) = 1.25, p = .53. Mean optimism did not differ between the description and experience groups, t(118) = -0.41, p = .68. These checks suggested that random assignment produced reasonably balanced groups with respect to measured demographics and optimism.

Primary analysis: proportion of risky choices

The primary dependent measure was the proportion of trials on which participants chose the risky option. Across all six trials, participants in the description condition chose the risky option on a mean of 0.41 of trials (SD = 0.20). Participants in the experience condition chose the risky option on a mean of 0.38 of trials (SD = 0.22). An independent-samples t-test comparing these means revealed no significant difference between conditions, t(118) = 0.72, p = .47. The observed difference corresponded to a small effect size, Cohen’s d = 0.15, with a 95% confidence interval for the mean difference of [-0.06, 0.12]. Thus, contrary to the hypothesis derived from prior literature, our data showed no reliable description–experience gap in the overall proportion of risky choices.

Given that prior descriptions of the gap emphasize rare-event overweighting in description relative to experience, we also examined the difference in the tendency to choose the risky option specifically for gain-framed trials. In the three gain trials, the mean proportion of risky choices was 0.44 (SD = 0.28) in the description condition and 0.41 (SD = 0.29) in the experience condition. This difference was not significant, t(118) = 0.44, p = .66. For loss-framed trials, mean risky choice proportions were 0.38 (SD = 0.27) in description and 0.35 (SD = 0.26) in experience, again not significant, t(118) = 0.62, p = .54. Thus, both gain and loss frames failed to show the expected description–experience divergence.

Secondary analysis: logistic regression including optimism

To assess whether optimism predicted risky choice and whether it moderated any effect of decision format, we estimated a logistic regression predicting the choice on each trial (risky = 1, safe = 0) with predictors for condition (description vs experience), standardized optimism score, an interaction between condition and optimism, and fixed effects for trial type (gain vs loss). We modeled choice at the trial level while clustering standard errors by participant to account for repeated measures.

The logistic regression results indicated that decision format did not significantly predict risky choice, b = 0.12, SE = 0.18, z = 0.67, p = .50. Optimism did not significantly predict choice, b = 0.04, SE = 0.05, z = 0.80, p = .42. The interaction between condition and optimism was also non-significant, b = 0.02, SE = 0.06, z = 0.33, p = .74. Trial type (gain vs loss) was a weak predictor with a trend-level effect suggesting slightly higher risk taking in gains, b = 0.16, SE = 0.09, z = 1.78, p = .08, but this effect fell short of conventional significance thresholds after accounting for other covariates. Overall, the regression analysis provided no evidence that either decision format or optimism reliably influenced choice behavior in the current study.

Bayesian analyses for null effects

Given the null findings in frequentist tests, we supplemented our analysis with Bayesian t-tests to quantify evidence favoring the null hypothesis of no difference between format conditions. Using a default Cauchy prior centered at zero and scale parameter consistent with medium effects, the Bayes factor comparing the null model to the alternative model for the overall proportion of risky choices was BF01 = 3.1. This value indicates moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis relative to the alternative that description and experience produce different mean proportions of risky choices. For gain trials alone, the Bayes factor was BF01 = 2.8, and for loss trials it was BF01 = 3.5, both values consistent with modest support for the null.

Sampling behavior in the experience condition

We examined sampling dynamics among participants in the experience condition as an exploratory probe of whether tangible sampling yielded deeper or more informative sampling behavior that could account for the lack of a gap. Participants were allowed up to 20 draws per trial; the mean number of draws across trials was 7.8 (SD = 4.6). There was substantial variability, with some participants drawing near the maximum and others drawing only one or two times. We tested whether greater sampling depth predicted a greater tendency to choose the risky option, on the logic that deeper sampling would make the rare event more likely to be observed and possibly increase its subjective weight. A mixed-effects logistic regression predicting risky choice from number of draws (standardized), controlling for trial type, showed no significant effect of draws, b = 0.05, SE = 0.04, z = 1.25, p = .21. Correlationally, mean number of draws per participant did not correlate significantly with the participant’s overall proportion of risky choices, r = .08, p = .54. Thus, sampling depth in the experience condition did not explain choice variability in the present data.

Subjective probability estimates and calibration

As an additional probe, we analyzed subjective probability estimates elicited from experience participants after they completed each trial. Participants were asked to estimate the probability of the rare event on each trial on a percentage scale from 0 to 100. On average, subjective estimates were 12.6% (SD = 9.4%), slightly higher than the objective 10% proportion but not significantly different from it, t(59) = 1.27, p = .21. Notably, subjective estimates were fairly noisy across individuals, and individuals who had observed the rare event at least once during sampling tended to estimate higher probabilities (mean = 18.3%) than those who had not observed it (mean = 9.1%), t(58) = 3.64, p < .001. This pattern indicates that sampling outcomes influenced subsequent beliefs, but the overall calibration across participants was relatively accurate on average. For description participants who provided estimates, the subjective estimates closely matched the provided probabilities by design and therefore offered no independent information.

Manipulation checks and task perceptions

Participants’ responses to manipulation checks indicated that most found the physical sampling to be plausible and believed that draws were random. In the experience condition, 86% of participants endorsed the statement that the bag draws felt like realistic sampling, rating agreement as 4 or 5 on a 5-point scale. Comprehension checks showed that most participants across both conditions understood the payoff structures; only three participants scored below an operational threshold on comprehension and their data remained in the sample because exclusion did not substantively change results.

Exploratory analyses by trial-level rare-event outcomes

Because the experience condition involved stochastic realizations of the rare event during payout resolution, we examined whether the actual outcome observed in the paid trial influenced post-experimental views of the task and whether payout realization interacted with choice patterns. Among experience participants, 22 out of 60 observed the rare event during sampling on at least one trial; among those, retrospective likelihood of choosing risky options was not substantially different from those who did not observe the rare event. Additionally, we inspected whether participants whose paid trial yielded the rare payoff differed in reported satisfaction or in subsequent probability estimates; no systematic differences emerged.

Power analysis and sensitivity

We conducted both post hoc and prospective power calculations to evaluate the sensitivity of the study to detect effects of interest. With N = 120 and two balanced groups, the study had approximately 80% power to detect an effect size of d = 0.52 in a two-tailed test at alpha = .05. The observed effect size (d = 0.15) is well below that threshold, suggesting either that the true effect is small or absent under these conditions. The Bayesian analyses, providing moderate evidence for the null, further suggest that if a decision–experience gap exists in this tangible-sampling implementation, it is likely to be smaller than previously reported in computerized contexts.

Reliability and robustness checks

To assess the robustness of the null findings, we performed several supplementary checks. First, excluding the three participants with low comprehension did not materially alter the pattern of results. Second, re-analyzing the data using nonparametric Mann–Whitney U tests yielded the same conclusion of no significant difference between conditions. Third, splitting the sample by median optimism and conducting subgroup comparisons did not reveal condition-specific effects within high- or low-optimism participants. Taken together, these robustness checks support the initial conclusion that the description–experience gap did not manifest in our study and that dispositional optimism did not predict choice.

Summary of results

In summary, we found no significant difference in the proportion of risky choices between the description and experience conditions, no reliable association between dispositional optimism and risk taking, and moderate Bayesian evidence favoring the null hypothesis of no difference by condition. Sampling behavior in the experience condition varied widely across participants but did not significantly predict choice. Subjective probability estimates among experience participants were broadly calibrated to the objective proportion on average, though individuals who observed the rare event estimated higher probabilities. Overall, the data do not corroborate the classic decision–experience gap under the tangible-sampling conditions employed in this study.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether the well-documented description–experience gap in risky choice replicates when the experience condition is implemented using tangible sampling materials, and whether dispositional optimism predicts or moderates choice behavior. Contrary to expectations derived from prior work that predominantly used computerized sampling, we found no significant difference in the frequency of risky choices between description and experience conditions. Additionally, dispositional optimism did not predict the likelihood of selecting risky options in either condition, nor did it moderate any decision format effects. Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence favoring the null hypothesis of no description–experience difference in our sample. These findings suggest that the description–experience gap may not be a universal phenomenon and that the medium of sampling—tangible versus computerized—could influence whether underweighting of rare events arises in experiential contexts.

This set of results has several implications for theoretical accounts of the description–experience gap. The dominant explanation for underweighting of rare events in experience-based tasks is that finite sampling leads to underrepresentation of rare events in memory, generating subjective beliefs that downplay low-probability outcomes. If this account were the sole driver of the effect, then making sampling more tangible should not necessarily eliminate the gap, because the underlying probabilistic structure and finite sampling constraints would remain unchanged. However, our results indicate that when participants sampled from physical bags of counters, they did not systematically underweight rare events relative to described probabilities. One plausible interpretation is that tangible sampling increased the salience and memorability of observed outcomes. The physical manipulation of counters and the sensory feedback of drawing may have reinforced the encoding of rare events when they occurred, thereby improving subjective calibration and mitigating a bias toward underweighting. This mechanistic interpretation suggests that experience-based underweighting is contingent not only on sampling variability but also on the quality of encoding and retrieval processes that are themselves sensitive to modality.

Another theoretical implication concerns the role of cognitive representation. In description-based decisions, numerical probabilities are cognitively represented and may be subject to cognitive transformations that lead to overweighting of small probabilities. In our study, we did not observe the overweighting typically reported in description conditions either; proportional risky choice rates were similar across description and experience. This raises the possibility that the overweighting observed in some prior experiments depends on numerical processing demands or on the specific manner in which probabilities are presented. It is possible that our description materials, presented on paper with context and instructions emphasizing realism, invited more conservative or deliberative processing that attenuated overweighting. Alternatively, participants may have applied heuristics or personal priors in both conditions that led to similar choices despite different presentation formats.

The absence of a relationship between dispositional optimism and risky choice is also notable. While optimism has been linked to various aspects of judgment and decision making in prior studies, our failure to detect an association suggests that optimism may not exert a strong or consistent influence on simple laboratory gambling tasks of the sort used here. Several interpretations are possible. First, optimism as measured by self-report may capture broad expectations that do not translate into fine-grained probabilistic reasoning in experimental tasks. Second, optimism could interact with other factors—such as perceived control, outcome domain, or stakes—to influence risk taking, but these interacting factors were not systematically manipulated here. Third, the null finding could reflect insufficient variance or reliability in the optimism measure in our sample, though descriptive statistics suggested reasonable distributional properties. In short, while optimism remains an interesting candidate moderator, our data do not support a robust role for it in moderating format-driven differences in risk taking.

Methodological factors likely influenced the pattern of results and deserve careful consideration. First and foremost, the choice of tangible counters and physical bags introduced procedural features that differ meaningfully from typical computerized sampling. Although we sought to match the objective distributions and sampling-with-replacement properties, physical manipulations produce sensory and engagement differences that could influence attention and memory. The tactile experience of drawing a counter and the requirement to physically mix the bag could create a richer encoding context that stabilizes the representation of rare events, making them less susceptible to underweighting. This plausible mechanism points to the importance of modality and embodiment in cognitive biases and suggests an avenue for further research that directly manipulates tangibility.

A second methodological consideration concerns sampling instructions and limits. Participants in the experience condition were allowed up to 20 draws per trial, and average draws were around eight. While this level of sampling falls within ranges used in prior studies, variability was high. Some participants sampled only a few times, which could increase the risk of under-representation of rare events, while others sampled extensively, potentially approximating the true distribution. This heterogeneity could reduce the observed group-level decision–experience gap by averaging over individuals who under-sampled and those who sampled more intensively. Indeed, analyses showed that sampling depth did not correlate with choice behavior at the group level, but individual differences in sampling strategy remain an important variable that could mediate format effects. Future work would benefit from directly manipulating and controlling sampling depth or from analyzing subgroups according to sampling strategy.

A related point concerns sampling with replacement versus without replacement. Although our physical protocol instructed participants to return counters and mix the bag between draws, the physical act of handling counters could have introduced subtle biases—for example, participants may have felt that mixing was imperfect, or they may have used tactile cues unconsciously when drawing. Such practicalities can influence outcome frequencies experienced by participants and their subjective beliefs. By contrast, computerized sampling typically enforces perfect randomization at the program level, eliminating these micro-variations. Thus, one explanation for the discrepancy between our findings and prior work is that tangible sampling reduces effective randomness or introduces consistent sampling biases that alter subjective experience in a manner that diminishes underweighting.

Another methodological issue is the choice of payoffs and rare-event probability. We used a 10% rare-event probability and modest monetary stakes, which are standard but nonetheless may influence generalizability. It is plausible that underweighting effects are more pronounced at even lower probabilities (e.g., 1–5%) or with larger stakes, or conversely that they attenuate when probabilities and payoffs are in a range that participants find intuitively plausible. Future work should systematically vary probability magnitudes and stakes to map the boundary conditions of the description–experience gap across different sampling modalities.

Statistical power is another consideration. Our sample of 120 provided moderate power to detect medium effects, but the observed effect size was small. The Bayesian analyses offered moderate evidence for the null, but additional studies with larger samples could more precisely estimate effect sizes and might identify small but theoretically meaningful differences. Meta-analytic aggregation of studies varying sampling modality would be particularly helpful in this regard.

There are also theoretical extensions to consider. If tangible sampling reduces the underweighting of rare events, this suggests that interventions leveraging physical demonstrations could be useful in applied settings where experiential learning is desirable—for example, in health education where people often underestimate rare but serious risks. Translating this idea into practice would require careful thought about ecological validity: real-world risks often unfold over prolonged periods and with complex feedback, and physical demonstrations can only approximate such dynamics.

Future research should address several specific questions raised by our findings. First, a direct within-subjects comparison of tangible versus computerized experience, counterbalanced and with careful controls for sampling depth and feedback, would isolate the effect of modality more cleanly. Second, experimental manipulations designed to vary encoding strength in computerized sampling—for instance, by adding vividness-enhancing cues or requiring participants to write down outcomes—could test whether the key mechanism is enhanced encoding and memory rather than modality per se. Third, exploring the interaction between individual differences (e.g., numeracy, working memory capacity, need for cognition) and sampling modality might reveal which participants are most sensitive to modality-driven changes in experience. Fourth, longitudinal or sequential-sampling designs that examine how beliefs and choices evolve with repeated sampling episodes could illuminate the dynamics by which tangible experience affects calibration and valuation over time.

The null relationship between optimism and choice in this study also points to promising avenues. Future studies could manipulate induced optimism experimentally to test causal effects, or they could focus on context-specific optimism (e.g., optimistic beliefs about health outcomes) rather than the general dispositional optimism measure we used. It is also possible that optimism interacts with perceived control or agency; for example, optimistic individuals might take more risk when they feel able to influence outcomes, but not when outcomes are purely chance-based. Elaborating such conditional hypotheses will require more nuanced designs.

Another important consideration is ecological validity. Laboratory tasks necessarily simplify real-world decisions, and conclusions about description–experience differences in applied contexts should be made cautiously. Real-world experience often involves correlated outcomes, nonstationary distributions, and social influences that differ from both paper descriptions and controlled sampling. Bridging the gap between laboratory and field will therefore require mixed-methods research that examines whether training or information presentation formats have comparable effects outside the lab.

Finally, methodological transparency and replication are crucial. Our study contributes a data point indicating that the description–experience gap may be sensitive to sampling medium. The broader literature contains both replications and failures to replicate under varying conditions, underscoring that the gap is not a simple categorical phenomenon but a conditional regularity. Researchers should report procedural details, especially those relating to sampling modality and instructions, so that studies can be compared and synthesized effectively.

In conclusion, the present study failed to replicate a description–experience gap when experiential sampling was conducted with tangible physical counters in bags, and dispositional optimism did not predict risky choices. These findings suggest that the modality of experience matters and that tactile sampling may attenuate the tendency to underweight rare events often observed with computerized sampling. At the same time, methodological variables and sample size considerations temper strong claims. Further research that systematically manipulates sampling medium, encoding strength, sampling depth, and individual differences is necessary to delineate the boundary conditions of the description–experience gap and to translate theoretical insights into applications for risk communication and decision support.
