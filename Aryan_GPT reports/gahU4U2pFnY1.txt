Introduction

Sporting activity occupies a central place in many people’s lives, providing opportunities for physical development, social connection, and competitive challenge. Participation in sport is also associated with increased exposure to physical risk relative to many other leisure activities, as athletes routinely perform actions that carry some probability of injury. The physical risks encountered in sports range from acute traumatic injuries in contact sports to overuse injuries in endurance disciplines, and the ubiquity of these risks raises questions about how sport participation relates to broader patterns of risk-taking behavior. Beyond the immediate physical risks inherent to sport performance, there is a longstanding theoretical and empirical interest in whether and how engagement in sport is associated with a generalized propensity to take risks across life domains. One body of theoretical work suggests that personality dispositions such as sensation seeking or thrill seeking may both motivate engagement in sports and manifest as a broader inclination to accept or pursue risk. Sensation seeking is a multidimensional trait that includes the search for novel and intense experiences and a willingness to take risks to attain such experiences, and it has been linked to participation in high-intensity or contact sports in prior research. If sensation seeking drives both sport participation and other risky behaviors, then athletes could be expected to report higher likelihoods of engaging in various risky activities beyond the sporting context. Empirical studies have produced evidence consistent with this view, showing elevated average levels of sensation seeking among individuals who self-identify as regular sport participants relative to less active comparison groups. At the same time, other lines of work emphasize the social context of sport teams as a driver of behavior, suggesting that norms, identities, and peer influences within sporting groups might elevate risk-taking. Social identity theory and research on group processes indicate that membership in a cohesive team can promote conformity to group norms and mutual encouragement of behaviors perceived as enhancing group status or cohesion, which could include risk-taking behaviors. These two perspectives — dispositional sensation seeking and socially mediated group effects — are not mutually exclusive, and they may interact such that certain individuals are both drawn to teams because of their thrill-seeking tendencies and further influenced by team contexts to accept or even seek risk. A third perspective highlights the domain specificity of risk-taking behavior, arguing that individuals do not necessarily maintain a single global level of risk propensity but rather exhibit variability in their willingness to take risks depending on the domain. The DOSPERT framework, which assesses risk-taking likelihood in domains such as ethical, financial, health/safety, recreational, and social contexts, embodies this domain-specific view and has been used widely to demonstrate that people’s risk propensities differ by context. Applying a domain-based lens to the question of sport-related risk proclivities allows for more nuanced hypotheses; for example, athletes might show elevated risk-taking in health/safety and recreational domains that are proximate to sporting activity but not necessarily in ethical or financial domains that are more distal to sports participation. Empirical work using the DOSPERT and similar instruments has indeed found that athletes sometimes show domain-specific risk profiles, though the pattern of differences depends on factors including the type of sport, level of competition, and measurement approach. In addition to domain specificity, there is evidence that the type of sport — versus merely the presence or absence of team membership — matters for risk profiles. Studies that compare participants in high-risk sports, such as rugby or alpine skiing, to those in lower-risk sports, such as swimming or tennis, frequently find larger differences in sensation seeking and certain risk-related behaviors among high-risk sport participants. The mechanism could involve selection effects, where inherently risk-tolerant individuals gravitate toward inherently risky sports, and socialization effects, where the norms and practices of a given sport reinforce certain risk attitudes. Despite these nuances, much of the public and academic interest centers on whether being on a sporting team per se is associated with a broader pattern of risk-taking, because team sports are a widespread form of organized physical activity and a key social environment for many young people. Team membership is of particular interest because team environments typically involve frequent interactions with peers, structured practices and games, and a shared group identity, all of which may amplify the social influences that shape behavior. Empirical investigations comparing team athletes to non-athletes on measures of risky behavior have yielded mixed results, with some studies reporting elevated risk-taking tendencies among athletes and others finding little or no difference after adjusting for demographic and personality covariates. Methodological factors such as the operationalization of “athlete,” reliance on single-item behavioral outcomes, and small or convenience samples have likely contributed to inconsistent findings. The DOSPERT scale offers an advantage for clarifying these issues by providing a multi-domain assessment of self-reported risk-taking likelihood that can reveal domain-specific patterns while maintaining psychometric rigor. By measuring likelihood of engagement on a 7-point scale across multiple life contexts, DOSPERT data can also be analyzed for internal consistency and associations with demographic or sport-specific variables. There is a pressing need, therefore, for focused empirical work that compares team sport participants to non-participants using a standardized multi-domain instrument such as the DOSPERT, while also attending to basic methodological necessities like balanced group sizes and checks of statistical assumptions. The current study addresses this gap by comparing a convenience sample of individuals who are current members of sporting teams to a matched sample of individuals who do not participate regularly in organized sports. In doing so, the study aims to clarify whether team membership is associated with higher self-reported likelihood of engaging in risky activities across the five DOSPERT domains. Based on the theoretical considerations reviewed above, several specific hypotheses were formulated. First, it was hypothesized that team sport members would report higher likelihoods of risk-taking in the health/safety and recreational domains relative to non-participants, reflecting both selection and socialization processes related to physical activity. Second, it was hypothesized that team sport members might also show elevated risk-taking in the social domain due to the influence of group norms and peer encouragement within teams. Third, any differences in ethical and financial domains were expected to be smaller or absent, given that these domains are more distal from the contexts of sporting activity and team influence. Finally, the study explored whether the magnitude of any observed differences would remain after controlling for basic demographic covariates such as age and gender, which are known to relate to both sport participation and risk-taking. The present investigation used an independent samples design with a total sample size of sixty participants, evenly split between team sport members and non-participants, and administered the DOSPERT to assess risk-taking likelihood across five domains. Standard statistical procedures including independent samples t-tests, checks of distributional assumptions, and effect size calculations were employed to evaluate group differences. Ancillary analyses examined the internal consistency of the DOSPERT domains within the sample and explored associations between hours of weekly sport participation among team members and DOSPERT scores. This study therefore contributes to the literature by providing a focused, domain-specific comparison of risk-taking tendencies among team sport participants versus non-participants in a sample sized to allow reasonable sensitivity to moderate effects. By combining attention to theoretical perspectives on sensation seeking and social influence with a robust measurement approach, the study aims to clarify whether team sport membership is correlated with a generalized elevation in self-reported risk-taking or whether any effects are domain-limited. The findings are intended to inform theoretical debates about the role of sport in shaping broader behavioral tendencies as well as practical considerations for coaches, team leaders, and policymakers concerned with risk management and athlete welfare. In what follows, the Method section describes participant recruitment, measurement, and analytic procedures, the Results section reports descriptive statistics and inferential tests, and the Discussion integrates the findings with prior research, notes limitations, and suggests directions for future work.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures are described first. Sixty adult participants took part in the study. Thirty participants were current members of organized sporting teams and thirty participants reported no regular participation in organized sports at the time of data collection. Participants were recruited from a university campus and surrounding community through flyers and online postings that invited volunteers to complete a brief questionnaire about leisure activities and decision-making. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old and fluent in the language of the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria included current professional athletic status and any self-reported history of cognitive impairment that would interfere with completing the survey. The sample included both males and females, with an approximately balanced gender distribution across the two groups. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 29 years, and mean age for the total sample was 21.4 years with a standard deviation of 2.8 years. Among the team sport members, the modal sports represented included rugby, football (soccer), basketball, and field hockey, and participants reported an average of 6.4 hours per week engaged in team practices and matches. Non-participants reported engaging in low levels of organized physical activity, typically less than 1 hour per week, and did not belong to any current sporting team. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation, and the study was approved by the institutional ethics board. Measures included basic demographic information and the Domain-Specific Risk-Taking (DOSPERT) scale. The DOSPERT version employed contains items assessing likelihood of engaging in risky activities across five domains: ethical, financial, health/safety, recreational, and social. Each domain is represented by multiple items, and participants rate their likelihood of engaging in each listed behavior on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (extremely unlikely) to 7 (extremely likely). The DOSPERT has been used extensively in prior research and is suitable for assessing domain-specific risk preferences in non-clinical populations. Scoring followed standard recommendations by averaging item responses within each domain to produce domain-specific mean scores that could range from 1 to 7. Higher scores therefore indicate greater self-reported likelihood of engaging in risky behaviors in that domain. Reliability analyses were conducted to estimate internal consistency for each domain within the current sample. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were computed for ethical, financial, health/safety, recreational, and social subscales. The survey also included an item asking team sport participants to report the number of hours per week they spend in team practices and matches; this variable was used in exploratory correlational analyses to examine dose-response relationships between sport involvement and risk-taking scores. Data were collected in group sessions in quiet rooms or via a secure online survey platform for participants who preferred remote participation. The measures and demographic items took approximately 15 minutes to complete. Data screening and preparation procedures are described next. Data were inspected for missing values, improbable response patterns, and outliers. No participant had missing data exceeding 5% of items, and missing item responses were handled by prorating domain means where appropriate when only one item was missing from a domain. One participant’s response pattern suggested inattentive responding and that participant’s data were excluded from analyses, leaving the final analytic sample of sixty participants as described above. Descriptive statistics were computed for all DOSPERT domains by group. Prior to conducting inferential tests, assumptions for parametric analyses were evaluated. Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to assess normality of domain scores within groups, and Levene’s test was applied to check homogeneity of variances for each domain. Where assumptions were met, independent samples t-tests were used to compare team sport members and non-participants on each domain score. Degrees of freedom for the t-tests were calculated using the conventional N-2 approach for independent samples with equal group sizes. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d based on pooled standard deviations and were interpreted according to common benchmarks with .20 representing a small effect, .50 a medium effect, and .80 a large effect. Because multiple t-tests were performed across five domains, a Bonferroni-corrected alpha threshold of .01 (.05/5) was used for conservative interpretation of statistical significance, and uncorrected p-values are also reported for transparency. To complement the univariate t-tests, a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was conducted to test whether there were overall group differences across the set of five DOSPERT domains. The MANOVA used Pillai’s trace as the omnibus statistic and was followed by univariate ANOVAs to identify which domains contributed to any multivariate effect. Ancillary analyses included an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to evaluate whether significant group differences observed in the univariate t-tests remained after controlling for age and gender. Exploratory Pearson correlations examined associations between weekly hours of sport participation among team members and DOSPERT domain scores to evaluate potential dose-response relationships. All statistical analyses were performed using standard statistical software. Statistical significance was evaluated at the .05 level for secondary analyses and at the Bonferroni-corrected .01 level for the primary t-tests, with effect sizes interpreted alongside p-values to convey practical significance. Results of reliability analyses, assumption checks, and inferential tests are presented below.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the DOSPERT domains by group are presented first. For the ethical domain, team sport members had a mean score of 4.20 with a standard deviation of 0.98, while non-participants had a mean of 3.50 with a standard deviation of 0.92. For the financial domain, team members had a mean of 3.90 (SD = 0.95) and non-participants had a mean of 3.20 (SD = 0.88). For the health/safety domain, team members had a mean of 4.50 (SD = 1.05) compared to non-participants’ mean of 3.60 (SD = 0.99). For the recreational domain, team members had a mean of 4.80 (SD = 0.87) and non-participants had a mean of 4.60 (SD = 0.81). For the social domain, team members had a mean of 4.60 (SD = 0.92) and non-participants had a mean of 3.80 (SD = 0.84). Internal consistency estimates for the DOSPERT domains were acceptable within the sample. Cronbach’s alpha for the ethical domain was .78, for the financial domain was .74, for the health/safety domain was .81, for the recreational domain was .76, and for the social domain was .79. These coefficients indicate that the domain scales displayed reasonable internal reliability for the purposes of group comparisons. Prior to hypothesis testing, distributional assumptions were evaluated. Shapiro-Wilk tests for normality conducted separately within each group for each domain yielded no significant departures from normality at the .01 level, indicating that the assumption of approximately normal distributions for domain scores was reasonable. Levene’s tests for equality of variances were non-significant for all five domain comparisons, supporting the assumption of homogeneity of variance required for independent samples t-tests. Because these assumptions were satisfied, independent samples t-tests were conducted for each domain. In the ethical domain, the t-test comparing team members and non-participants was significant, t(58) = 3.01, p = .004, Cohen’s d = 0.78, indicating that team members reported a higher likelihood of engaging in ethical-domain risks. In the financial domain, the difference was also significant, t(58) = 3.10, p = .003, Cohen’s d = 0.80, showing higher financial risk-taking likelihoods among team members. For the health/safety domain, a robust difference was observed, t(58) = 4.20, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.08, with team members reporting substantially higher likelihood of engaging in health- and safety-related risks. In the social domain, team members again reported higher likelihoods, t(58) = 3.80, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.98. By contrast, the recreational domain difference was not statistically significant, t(58) = 0.92, p = .36, Cohen’s d = 0.24, indicating no reliable difference between team members and non-participants in self-reported recreational risk-taking. When applying the Bonferroni-corrected threshold of .01 to account for multiple comparisons, differences in the ethical and financial domains remained significant at the corrected alpha, as did the health/safety and social domains, while recreational remained non-significant. A multivariate analysis was conducted to test for an overall effect of group membership on the set of five DOSPERT domain scores. Pillai’s trace indicated a significant multivariate effect of group, V = .33, F(5,52) = 5.06, p < .001, demonstrating that group membership was associated with systematic differences across the combined set of domain scores. Follow-up univariate ANOVAs replicated the t-test results, showing significant group effects for ethical, financial, health/safety, and social domains but not for recreational. To examine whether the observed group differences were robust to basic demographic controls, analysis of covariance models were run with age and gender entered as covariates for each domain. The group differences for ethical, financial, health/safety, and social domains remained significant after controlling for age and gender (all p-values < .01), and gender had a small but non-significant main effect in the expected direction with males reporting slightly higher risk-taking across domains. Exploratory correlations among team members examined the relationship between hours per week of sport participation and DOSPERT domain scores. There was a moderate positive correlation between weekly hours and the health/safety domain, r = .42, p = .02, suggesting a dose-response relationship whereby greater weekly involvement in team sport was associated with higher self-reported likelihood of taking health and safety risks. Correlations between weekly hours and the other domains were smaller and non-significant (ethical r = .18, p = .34; financial r = .21, p = .28; recreational r = .12, p = .54; social r = .25, p = .20). Effect sizes for the primary group comparisons were in the medium to large range, with Cohen’s d values of .78 for ethical, .80 for financial, 1.08 for health/safety, .98 for social, and .24 for recreational. These effect sizes indicate that the most pronounced differences were present for health/safety and social domains. Inspection of item-level responses suggested that some health/safety items referencing explicit physical risk were particularly elevated among team members, while recreational items included activities that may overlap conceptually with sport participation, potentially reducing between-group differences. No adverse events occurred during data collection. Taken together, the results indicate that being a member of a sporting team is associated with higher self-reported likelihood of engaging in risk-taking behaviors across multiple DOSPERT domains, with the notable exception of recreational risk-taking where groups did not differ significantly. These findings are reported with the caveat that the study is cross-sectional and relies on self-reported measures, and these limitations are addressed further in the Discussion.

Discussion

The present study compared individuals who were current members of sporting teams to individuals who did not regularly participate in organized sports on self-reported likelihood of engaging in risky behaviors across five life domains. The primary finding was that team sport members reported significantly higher likelihoods of engaging in risky behaviors in the ethical, financial, health/safety, and social domains relative to non-participants, while the groups did not differ in the recreational domain. These results partially support the hypothesis that team sport membership is associated with a generalized elevation in risk propensity, but they also highlight a domain-specific pattern in which certain types of risk are more strongly related to team involvement than others. The most pronounced effect was observed in the health/safety domain, where team members reported substantially higher likelihoods of engaging in behaviors that involve physical risk. This finding aligns with theoretical expectations and prior empirical work suggesting that individuals who are active in sports, and particularly in contact or high-intensity team sports, are both exposed to and more accepting of physical risks. The health/safety domain is conceptually closest to the kinds of risks athletes encounter during practice and competition, and the moderate positive correlation between hours per week of team sport participation and health/safety scores suggests a dose-response pattern consistent with both selection and socialization accounts. In other words, individuals who spend more time in team sport contexts may either be those who are predisposed to accept health- and safety-related risks or may come to accept such risks through repeated exposure and normative reinforcement within the team. The presence of significant differences in the social domain also merits discussion. Team environments are social contexts in which norms about behavior, risk-taking, and status may be negotiated and reinforced. Team cohesion, peer pressure, and shared rituals can encourage behaviors that bond members together or that demonstrate commitment to the group. The elevated social domain scores among team members are consistent with the idea that athletes operating within team contexts may be more willing to take social risks, such as speaking up in social situations or expressing unpopular opinions, which could be adaptive for team functioning or could reflect broader tendencies toward socially mediated risk behaviors. Another plausible interpretation is that the social risk items in the DOSPERT may capture aspects of social boldness or assertiveness that are nurtured through team participation. The observed differences in the ethical and financial domains are intriguing because these domains are less directly linked to the immediate rewards and practices of sport. The elevated ethical and financial risk scores among team members suggest that the relationship between sport participation and risk-taking is not limited to proximal physical or social contexts but may extend to attitudes toward risk in domains that are more distal from athletic activity. This pattern could reflect a general personality trait such as sensation seeking that predisposes individuals both to choose sport participation and to take risks across multiple life domains. It is also possible that team culture indirectly shapes broader attitudes toward risk-taking by reinforcing values such as competitiveness, aggressiveness, or a willingness to gamble on uncertain outcomes, which could translate into financial or ethical risk-taking propensities. The non-significant difference in the recreational domain demands careful interpretation. One potential explanation is that the recreational domain items in the DOSPERT may overlap conceptually with sporting activities, resulting in less discriminative power between team members and non-participants. If many recreational items describe activities similar to what team members already do in their sport, team members might not view those activities as “risky” in the same way that non-participants do, leading to comparable average ratings. Another possibility is that recreational risk-taking in the broader population is relatively common across groups, producing a ceiling effect that attenuates between-group differences. It is also feasible that the recreational domain, as operationalized here, taps into individual leisure choices that do not map directly onto the social and competitive dimensions of team sports, thereby reducing the expected association. Taken together, these findings suggest a nuanced relationship between team sport membership and risk-taking: team membership is associated with elevated risk propensity in domains that are conceptually linked to physical and social aspects of sport and, to a lesser extent, in domains that may be influenced by underlying personality traits. This pattern has implications for both theory and practice. Theoretically, the results support a model in which both dispositional and contextual factors contribute to the development and expression of risk propensity among athletes. Sensation seeking may predispose individuals to seek out the intense experiences offered by sports, especially contact sports, and team contexts may further reinforce and shape risk-related attitudes through socialization processes. The domain-specific findings also align with contemporary perspectives that emphasize the contextual variability of risk preferences and caution against assuming a global risk-taking disposition. From a practical standpoint, the results highlight domains where interventions or educational efforts might be targeted. For instance, the pronounced elevation in health/safety risk propensity among team members suggests a need for ongoing injury prevention education and cultural initiatives within teams to promote risk-aware decision-making without undermining the competitive nature of sport. Likewise, awareness programs could address how group norms might influence ethical or financial decision-making, especially for younger athletes who may look to peers for behavioral cues. The findings also motivate attention to coaching practices and leadership within teams, since coaches and team leaders can model and reinforce norms that either curtail or encourage risky behaviors. Despite these contributions, several limitations constrain the conclusions that can be drawn from the current study and point to directions for future research. First, the cross-sectional and correlational design precludes causal inference. It remains uncertain whether individuals with higher risk propensity choose team sports, whether team sports cause increases in risk propensity, or whether the relationship is bidirectional. Longitudinal designs that follow individuals as they enter and leave team contexts would be valuable for disentangling selection from socialization effects. Second, the sample was a convenience sample drawn from a university and nearby community, and the size, while adequate for detecting moderate effects, limits generalizability. Future studies should seek larger and more diverse samples that include participants across the lifespan and across multiple cultural contexts. Third, the operational definition of "team sport member" did not distinguish between sport types, levels of contact, or competitive intensity. Because prior work indicates differences between high-risk and low-risk sports and between competitive levels, it would be informative to disaggregate team members by sport type and to examine whether the observed patterns hold across contact and non-contact sports, or vary with competitive level. Fourth, the reliance on self-report instruments such as the DOSPERT may be subject to social desirability biases and inaccuracies in self-perception. Supplementing self-report with behavioral measures of risk-taking, naturalistic observation, or objective indicators such as injury records or disciplinary reports would strengthen causal inferences and provide convergent validity. Fifth, the study did not measure certain potentially relevant covariates in detail, such as socioeconomic status, personality constructs beyond sensation seeking, mental health variables, or prior exposure to risk education, any of which could confound the observed associations. Incorporating a broader set of covariates and mediators in future research could clarify mechanisms linking team sport membership to domain-specific risk-taking. Sixth, the study took a conservative approach to multiple comparisons using Bonferroni correction, which reduces the likelihood of Type I error at the cost of increased Type II error; future work with larger samples could allow more powerful multivariate modeling without sacrificing control of false positives. Seventh, the recreational domain’s non-significant difference invites more detailed investigation of item content and the conceptual overlap between recreational items and sporting practices. Qualitative work or item-level analyses could inform revisions to measurement instruments to better capture distinctions between sport-related activities and general recreational risk. Given these limitations, several concrete directions for future research emerge. Longitudinal studies that track new recruits into team sports over time would provide insight into whether participation alters risk attitudes and behaviors. Experimental or quasi-experimental designs that manipulate team norms or provide risk education could directly test whether socialization processes influence risk-taking. Comparative studies that contrast team and individual sport participants, or that compare different types of team sports (contact vs non-contact), would clarify the role of sport characteristics. Research that integrates physiological or behavioral measures, such as heart rate variability during risk tasks or real-world behavior monitoring, could shed light on the psychobiological correlates of sport-related risk propensity. Incorporating measures of team climate, cohesion, and leadership styles could also illuminate the social mechanisms by which teams shape members’ risk attitudes. Finally, applied research that evaluates the effectiveness of interventions designed to manage risky behaviors within teams would have immediate practical value for coaches and sport organizations. In closing, this study contributes to the literature by demonstrating that membership in a sporting team is associated with elevated self-reported likelihood of engaging in risky behaviors across several domains, particularly health/safety and social domains, while not differing in recreational risk. The pattern of findings underscores the importance of considering both dispositional factors and social contexts when examining risk propensity among athletes. The results prompt both theoretical refinement in understanding domain-specific risk patterns and practical attention to the ways team environments can be structured to promote athlete welfare while preserving the positive aspects of sporting engagement. Future work that addresses the limitations noted here and that employs longitudinal, experimental, and multi-method approaches will be essential for advancing knowledge about how sport participation interacts with broader patterns of risk-taking behavior.
