Introduction

The relationship between physical exercise and sleep quality has been a subject of scientific and public interest for many decades. People frequently report that being active helps them sleep better, and many health guidelines include moderate regular exercise as a recommendation to promote sleep. Despite the commonality of this recommendation, the mechanisms linking exercise and sleep are complex and multifactorial, and empirical findings have not always been uniform. From a theoretical perspective, multiple frameworks have been proposed to explain why exercise might influence sleep. One influential perspective emphasizes restorative and energy conservation functions of sleep, proposing that greater daytime energy expenditure leads to compensatory changes in sleep architecture and duration. Within that framework, higher physical exertion is predicted to increase the homeostatic drive for sleep, manifesting as increased slow-wave sleep and potentially longer total sleep time. Another biologically oriented model focuses on cerebral glycogen depletion and replenishment, suggesting that neural energy stores decline during wakefulness and are restored during non-rapid eye movement sleep; greater daytime cerebral energy use should therefore increase the need for replenishment and could lead to deeper or more consolidated sleep. Beyond purely metabolic accounts, exercise influences a range of physiological systems that are relevant to sleep regulation, including thermoregulation, autonomic nervous system balance, hormonal rhythms, and mood regulation. For example, exercise-induced transient rises in core body temperature are followed by a compensatory decline that may facilitate the onset of sleep, and regular exercise is associated with reduced anxiety and depressive symptoms, which in turn are linked to improved sleep. These multiple, partly overlapping mechanisms make it plausible that exercise can have beneficial effects on sleep, but they also imply that the direction and magnitude of effects could depend on the timing, intensity, and chronicity of activity as well as individual factors such as fitness, age, and chronotype.

Empirical work examining the link between exercise and sleep has produced heterogeneous results. Several studies have documented associations between regular moderate exercise and improved subjective sleep quality, increased sleep efficiency, and reductions in sleep latency. At the same time, some studies have found limited or inconsistent effects, particularly when considering the intensity of exercise or the timing relative to bedtime. Vigorous late-evening exercise has sometimes been associated with difficulty initiating sleep, which has been attributed to lingering physiological arousal, elevated sympathetic tone, or disrupted circadian signals. In contrast, moderate-intensity activity earlier in the day often shows more reliable associations with improved sleep. These mixed empirical patterns suggest that exercise intensity alone may not be the only relevant variable, and that intensity may interact with timing and individual differences.

One area that has been relatively underexplored is whether the relationship between exercise intensity and sleep quality differs systematically across populations with distinct training patterns and demands. Two groups that exemplify different patterns of physical activity are dancers and athletes. Dancers typically engage in activities that combine technical skill, aesthetic concerns, frequent rehearsals, and often late-day or evening performance schedules. Their training may emphasize repetitive practice, flexibility, and artistic expression, and it is common for dancers to have irregular schedules tied to classes, rehearsals, and performances. Athletes, by contrast, commonly engage in sport-specific training that may include structured strength and conditioning, high-intensity interval work, and scheduled practice sessions that are designed to optimize physical performance in competitions. Athletes often follow periodized training regimens with clearer off-season and competitive-season distinctions and may have different recovery protocols compared to dancers. Given these differences, it is plausible that the relationship between exercise intensity and sleep quality could vary between dancers and athletes.

In dancers, nontraining factors that influence sleep may include performance anxiety, irregular performance schedules, and aesthetic or weight-related concerns that influence eating and sleep patterns. Late-night performances and rehearsals can push activity into evening hours and potentially interact with the timing of sleep, while the dual demands of maintaining technique and body composition may increase stress and influence sleep negatively even when physical activity is high. In athletes, structured training and recovery strategies, greater emphasis on physical conditioning, and potentially more consistent training times could produce different relationships between intensity and sleep. Athletes may also use specific recovery modalities, such as scheduled napping, active recovery sessions, or nutrition plans, that influence sleep independently of exercise intensity. These differences motivate direct comparisons between dancers and athletes when studying exercise–sleep associations.

Beyond population differences, methodological heterogeneity in prior studies has likely contributed to inconsistent findings. Studies vary widely in how they quantify exercise: some rely on global frequency and duration metrics, some use objective measures such as accelerometry or heart rate monitoring, and others employ subjective measures such as perceived exertion or self-rated intensity. Sleep measures are similarly varied, ranging from subjective sleep questionnaires to actigraphy and polysomnography. The timing of assessment (e.g., habitual weekly patterns versus day-to-day diaries) also varies. Cross-sectional surveys, which are common, are limited in their ability to disambiguate causal directions; for example, poor sleep could reduce daytime activity or make intense exercise more difficult, producing inverse or null correlations in cross-sectional data. Thus, studies that test the same basic question but use different operational definitions of exercise and sleep are liable to produce different conclusions.

Given the theoretical plausibility that greater energy expenditure should promote deeper or better quality sleep according to restorative models, but also given the empirical inconsistencies and potential moderating role of training context, there is a need to examine whether the intensity of typical exercise in a given week relates to habitual sleep quality, and whether that relationship differs between dancers and athletes. A focused investigation that uses consistent questionnaires to quantify both exercise intensity and sleep quality in a sample that includes both dancers and athletes can help clarify whether intensity per se is associated with sleep and whether group membership moderates that association.

In the present work, we conducted a cross-sectional survey study in a sample of individuals who identified as dancers or athletes. We collected information about typical weekly exercise habits and sleep patterns using structured questionnaires and coded responses to produce continuous summary scores for exercise intensity and sleep quality for each participant. Our main analytic approach was to examine the correlation between exercise intensity and sleep quality overall, and then to test the association separately within dancers and within athletes. We also tested whether the relationship between intensity and sleep quality was moderated by group membership by estimating an interaction in a regression model. Prior to analysis we considered several plausible outcomes. One possibility, consistent with restorative models and a simple energy expenditure argument, is that higher exercise intensity would be associated with better sleep quality. Another possibility is that the relationship would be more nuanced, with high-intensity exercise having neutral or even negative associations with sleep when performed late in the day, and beneficial effects when performed earlier. Because our questionnaire captured habitual weekly patterns but did not systematically measure exercise timing relative to sleep, we anticipated that an overall correlation might be attenuated if timing was a key moderator. We also considered that dancers and athletes might show different patterns, given differences in training schedules, performance demands, and recovery practices. Therefore we planned to test for an overall relationship and for interactions with group membership.

Given the mixed prior literature and the limitations inherent to cross-sectional designs, the present study aimed to provide a clear descriptive answer to a straightforward question: in a mixed sample of dancers and athletes using a consistent set of questionnaires, is there a relationship between exercise intensity and habitual sleep quality, and does that relationship differ between the two groups? The study was intended to be an initial step toward understanding how training context might influence exercise–sleep associations and to highlight directions for more mechanistic or experimental work. We therefore preregistered analytic choices before unblinding data, including the use of Pearson correlations as the primary test, supplementary nonparametric correlations where assumptions were violated, and regression analyses to test group interactions. Given the theoretical rationale and mixed empirical findings reported above, we hypothesized that there would be a modest positive association between exercise intensity and sleep quality overall, but we also anticipated that the association could be attenuated or absent within subgroups depending on heterogeneity in training timing and recovery practices. We also hypothesized that athletes might show a clearer positive association than dancers due to potentially more structured training and recovery routines that align activity with sleep-promoting behaviors. Finally, we recognized that null results would be informative in this context because they would suggest that intensity alone may not be a reliable predictor of sleep quality across varied training contexts.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures are described in detail below. We recruited 100 participants for the study, with the explicit aim of enrolling individuals who identified primarily as either dancers or athletes. Of these 100 participants, 33 self-identified as dancers and 67 self-identified as athletes. Participants were recruited through university bulletin boards, community dance and sport organizations, social media postings targeting performance and recreational groups, and word-of-mouth. Inclusion criteria required that participants were aged 18 to 40, engaged in regular dance or athletic training at least twice per week over the preceding three months, and willing to complete two questionnaires about their typical weekly exercise and sleep patterns. Exclusion criteria included self-reported diagnosis of a primary sleep disorder (e.g., obstructive sleep apnea, narcolepsy) that was currently untreated, current use of prescribed sleep medications for chronic sleep difficulties, or any medical condition that made exercise unsafe. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation, and the study protocol followed standard ethical principles for behavioral research.

Participants completed an online survey that consisted of demographic items, a structured exercise questionnaire assessing weekly activity patterns, and a structured sleep questionnaire assessing habitual sleep quality and patterns during a typical week. Demographic items included age, sex, self-reported height and weight, years of experience in dance or sport, typical weekly training hours, and classification as dancer or athlete. Participants who reported involvement in both dance and sport were asked to indicate which identity best described their primary activity; only those who identified primarily as dancer or athlete were included in the sample.

The exercise questionnaire was designed to capture habitual weekly exercise characteristics and was composed of items tapping frequency, duration, and perceived intensity of the typical training sessions. Frequency items asked participants to indicate the number of training sessions per week; duration items asked for the typical duration of each session in minutes; and intensity items asked participants to rate the typical overall intensity of their sessions using a standardized 0–10 perceived exertion-like scale where 0 corresponded to no exertion and 10 corresponded to maximal effort. The questionnaire also contained items about the types of activities performed (e.g., classical dance technique, contemporary choreography, resistance training, endurance training), whether training commonly occurred in the morning, afternoon, or evening, and whether participants engaged in frequent late-night performances or competitions. The exercise intensity score used in primary analyses was computed as a composite metric: frequency (sessions per week) multiplied by duration (minutes per session) multiplied by perceived intensity (0–10 rating), divided by 10 to maintain a practical range. This composite score provided a continuous index of typical weekly exercise intensity that combined frequency, duration, and perceived exertion components.

The sleep questionnaire assessed habitual sleep quality and patterns during a typical week. The questionnaire included items on typical bedtime and wake time on weekdays and weekends, perceived sleep latency, number of awakenings, total sleep time, daytime sleepiness, and overall subjective sleep quality. These items were combined into a single sleep quality score designed such that higher values indicated better sleep quality. Specifically, we reversed and scaled items where necessary so that the composite score ranged approximately from 0 to 100, with higher scores reflecting shorter latency, fewer awakenings, longer sleep duration, lower daytime sleepiness, and higher subjective sleep satisfaction. The composite approach allowed us to capture multiple facets of habitual sleep quality while maintaining a single outcome variable for correlational analyses.

We conducted initial data screening to identify implausible values and outliers. Responses with impossible values (e.g., reported training durations exceeding 24 hours per day) were checked with participants when possible and excluded if clearly erroneous. No participants were excluded for such errors in the current data set; all 100 participants provided plausible responses and were retained in the primary analyses. Missing data for individual questionnaire items were minimal; in cases with a single missing item necessary for composite scoring, we imputed the missing value using the participant's mean for the other items comprising that subscale if fewer than 10% of items were missing. No participant had more than one imputed value. Demographic and composite score summaries were inspected for normality.

The primary analyses tested the association between the exercise intensity composite score and the sleep quality composite score using Pearson correlation coefficients for the full sample. We supplemented the primary analysis with nonparametric Spearman correlations to test robustness to potential non-normality. We also conducted the primary correlational analysis separately within the dancers (n = 33) and athletes (n = 67) subgroups to evaluate whether the association differed by group. To formally test whether group membership moderated the intensity–sleep relationship, we estimated a linear regression model with sleep quality as the dependent variable and exercise intensity, group (coded 0 for athlete, 1 for dancer), and an interaction term (exercise intensity × group) as predictors. We included age and sex as covariates in sensitivity analyses because these variables are known to relate to sleep characteristics and could confound the relationship between exercise and sleep.

Before running inferential tests, we examined the distribution of the key variables for skewness and kurtosis and inspected scatterplots for linearity and potential influential cases. Where violations of normality or linearity were identified, we reran nonparametric tests and examined whether transformations affected results. Because sample sizes for subgroups were modest, particularly the dancer group with 33 participants, we refrained from conducting highly parameterized models within subgroups that would be underpowered. Instead, we focused on simple correlational tests and a parsimonious interaction model for moderation testing.

We reported Pearson r values, 95% confidence intervals for correlations, and associated p-values. For the regression models, we reported unstandardized coefficients, t-values, and p-values for each predictor, along with model R-squared values. All tests used a two-tailed alpha of .05. To aid interpretation of null findings, we also conducted sensitivity analyses estimating the smallest effect size detectable with 80% power given our sample sizes, and we reported whether observed correlations exceeded that threshold. We used widely accepted statistical tools for these power and sensitivity calculations.

Results

The sample consisted of 100 participants who met inclusion criteria and completed both questionnaires. The overall sample had a mean age of 23.8 years with a standard deviation of 4.6 years. The sample included 64 females and 36 males; of the 33 dancers, 26 were female and 7 were male, whereas among the 67 athletes 38 were female and 29 were male. Participants reported a mean of 6.1 years (SD = 4.0) of experience in their primary activity domain. Typical weekly training hours averaged 7.4 hours (SD = 3.9) across the entire sample; dancers reported a mean of 7.9 hours per week (SD = 4.1) and athletes reported 7.2 hours per week (SD = 3.8), a difference that was not statistically significant (t(98) = 0.87, p = .39). These descriptive statistics indicate that the two groups were broadly comparable in terms of training commitment, although sample sizes differ.

Exercise intensity composite scores had a mean of 45.6 with a standard deviation of 12.3 across the full sample and ranged from 8.0 to 86.0. The dancers had a mean exercise intensity score of 47.1 (SD = 11.8), while athletes had a mean of 44.9 (SD = 12.5). A two-sample t-test comparing intensity scores between groups yielded t(98) = 0.84, p = .40, indicating that the groups did not differ significantly in their composite exercise intensity as operationalized here. Sleep quality composite scores had a mean of 72.3 (SD = 10.6) across the sample and ranged from 42.0 to 96.0. Dancers had a mean sleep quality of 71.8 (SD = 11.2) and athletes had a mean of 72.6 (SD = 10.3). The difference in sleep quality between groups was not significant (t(98) = 0.37, p = .71). These descriptive results suggested similar central tendencies in both variables across groups, which made it less likely that between-group differences in means accounted for association patterns.

We inspected distributions for skewness and kurtosis for both composite scores. Exercise intensity scores had a skewness of 0.42 and kurtosis of -0.18, while sleep quality scores had a skewness of -0.15 and kurtosis of -0.44. Visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots did not reveal substantial departures from normality. Scatterplots of exercise intensity versus sleep quality were examined for linearity; no clear nonlinear pattern was evident. We also examined potential influential cases using standardized residuals and Cook's distance in regression diagnostics; no participant exceeded conventional thresholds for undue influence.

The primary analysis tested the Pearson correlation between exercise intensity composite scores and sleep quality composite scores in the full sample. The observed Pearson correlation was r = .06 with a 95% confidence interval of [-.12, .24], and the associated two-tailed p-value was p = .54. This result indicates no statistically significant linear association between exercise intensity and sleep quality across the whole sample. Given that the composite scales were oriented such that higher sleep quality scores represented better sleep, the small positive correlation, while not significant, points to no meaningful relationship in this data set.

To assess robustness, we computed the Spearman rank-order correlation, which yielded rho = .05, p = .60, consistent with the Pearson result and suggesting that non-normality did not obscure a monotonic association. We also performed a bootstrapped correlation analysis with 5,000 resamples to obtain empirical confidence intervals; the bootstrapped 95% confidence interval for Pearson r was [-.13, .25], consistent with the parametric confidence interval and reinforcing the conclusion of no reliable association.

We next tested the correlation separately within each subgroup. Among the 33 dancers, Pearson r between exercise intensity and sleep quality was r = -.02, 95% CI = [-.34, .30], p = .91. In the athlete subgroup (n = 67), Pearson r was r = .09, 95% CI = [-.12, .29], p = .48. Neither subgroup yielded a statistically significant correlation, and the direction of association differed slightly between groups (a very small negative correlation in dancers and a small positive correlation in athletes), though both were close to zero in magnitude. Spearman rho values paralleled these findings (dancers rho = -.01, p = .95; athletes rho = .08, p = .52). These subgroup analyses suggested that if an intensity–sleep relationship exists, it is not detectable with our sample sizes and measurement strategy within either group.

To formally assess whether the relationship between exercise intensity and sleep quality depended on group membership, we ran a linear regression model predicting sleep quality from exercise intensity, group (dummy-coded with athletes as 0 and dancers as 1), and the interaction between exercise intensity and group. Age and sex were included as covariates in a sensitivity analysis but their inclusion did not materially change the findings; results reported here are from models both with and without those covariates where appropriate. In the primary interaction model without covariates, the main effect of exercise intensity was b = 0.04 (SE = 0.07), t = 0.57, p = .57, indicating no effect. The main effect of group was b = -0.86 (SE = 1.34), t = -0.64, p = .52, indicating no mean difference in sleep quality between groups in the regression framework. The interaction term (exercise intensity × group) had b = -0.07 (SE = 0.10), t = -0.70, p = .49, showing no evidence that the slope of the intensity–sleep relationship differed by group. The overall model explained a trivial amount of variance, R2 = .007, adjusted R2 = -.023.

When age and sex were added as covariates, the pattern was unchanged: exercise intensity b = 0.03 (SE = 0.07), p = .66; group b = -1.02 (SE = 1.32), p = .44; interaction b = -0.06 (SE = 0.09), p = .55. Age had a nonsignificant association with sleep quality in these models and sex did not predict sleep quality either. These adjusted models therefore provided no additional evidence for relationships or moderation and suggested that demographic covariates did not mask a real association.

Given that timing of exercise relative to bedtime could be a key moderator but was not the primary focus of the composite intensity score, we conducted exploratory analyses using the questionnaire item that asked participants whether they commonly trained in the evening or late at night. We compared participants who reported frequent late-evening training to those who did not to see whether the intensity–sleep association differed. Among the 29 participants who reported frequent late-evening training, the correlation between intensity and sleep quality was r = -.12 (p = .54). Among those not reporting late-evening training (n = 71), the correlation was r = .10 (p = .39). These exploratory subgroup correlations did not provide evidence that evening timing produced a marked difference in the intensity–sleep relationship in our sample.

We also categorized the exercise intensity composite into three roughly equal groups (low, medium, high intensity tertiles) and performed a one-way ANOVA on sleep quality across these tertiles. The ANOVA yielded F(2,97) = 0.37, p = .69, indicating no significant differences in mean sleep quality across low, medium, and high intensity categories. Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction also showed no significant contrasts.

Because null results can reflect limited power rather than true absence of effect, we computed sensitivity estimates for detectable effect sizes. With N = 100, and alpha = .05 two-tailed, the minimum Pearson r detectable with 80% power is approximately r = .28. Our observed correlations (r = .06 overall, r = -.02 in dancers, r = .09 in athletes) are well below that threshold, suggesting the study was underpowered to detect small effects but adequately powered to detect medium effects. Thus, we can be relatively confident in concluding that medium or larger effect sizes are unlikely in this sample, although small effects cannot be ruled out.

Finally, we conducted supplementary checks for potential measurement artifacts. We recalculated the exercise intensity composite using an alternate scaling in which the perceived exertion score was dichotomized into low-to-moderate (0–5) versus high (6–10) and then multiplied by frequency and duration; the resulting intensity metric correlated highly with the primary composite (r = .92) and yielded the same pattern of null correlations with sleep quality. We also recalculated the sleep quality composite using only the subjective global sleep satisfaction item and found that global satisfaction correlated with the composite at r = .84; its correlation with exercise intensity was r = .04, p = .69, again nonsignificant. In summary, multiple analytic approaches converged on the same conclusion: no reliable association between habitual exercise intensity and subjective sleep quality was detectable in this cross-sectional sample of dancers and athletes, and the relationship did not differ by group.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether habitual exercise intensity is associated with self-reported sleep quality in a sample of individuals identifying as dancers or athletes. Using structured questionnaires to derive composite indices of weekly exercise intensity and habitual sleep quality, we found no statistically significant correlation between exercise intensity and sleep quality across the full sample of 100 participants. Subgroup analyses yielded similarly null results: dancers (n = 33) showed a negligible correlation near zero and athletes (n = 67) showed a small but nonsignificant positive correlation. A regression model testing the interaction between exercise intensity and group indicated no moderation by dancer versus athlete status. Supplementary analyses using nonparametric correlations, bootstrapping, categorical intensity comparisons, and alternate composite constructions all produced consistent null findings. Taken together, these results suggest that, at least in this sample and with the instruments employed, exercise intensity as captured by a composite of frequency, duration, and perceived exertion is not a robust predictor of habitual subjective sleep quality, and that the relationship does not differ substantively between dancers and athletes.

The lack of a detectable association in this study has several possible interpretations. One possibility is that there is genuinely no meaningful cross-sectional relationship between habitual exercise intensity and overall subjective sleep quality once other variables and heterogeneity in training contexts are considered. This interpretation would imply that simple aggregate measures of intensity averaged over a week are insufficient to capture the relevant aspects of exercise that influence sleep. Another possibility is that exercise affects specific components of sleep rather than global subjective quality. For instance, restorative models predict changes in slow-wave sleep or sleep architecture that may not be readily reflected in participants' global subjective reports of sleep. Objective measures such as polysomnography or actigraphy are required to detect these architecture-related changes, which we did not collect here. A third possibility is that the effects of exercise on sleep depend heavily on moderators that were not fully assessed or controlled in this study, including timing of exercise relative to bedtime, individual chronotype, cumulative training load and recovery practices, psychological stress, and nutritional factors. Our exploratory analyses of evening training did not reveal a striking moderating effect, but the dichotomous evening variable derived from our questionnaire likely lacked the precision needed to capture nuanced timing effects. A final interpretive possibility is that measurement error and limited reliability attenuated the observable correlation. Self-report measures are subject to recall bias and imprecision, particularly when asking participants to summarize a typical week; such noise can substantially reduce correlations between constructs even when a true association exists.

When the current findings are considered in the context of prior literature, they highlight the complexity and conditional nature of exercise–sleep relationships. Some studies have reported beneficial effects of regular moderate exercise on subjective and objective sleep indices. Such findings align with restorative and metabolic theories that predict increased homeostatic sleep drive following higher energy expenditure. Other studies have found no association, or have identified conditions under which vigorous activity close to bedtime can transiently impair sleep onset or subjective sleep quality. The present null results therefore do not stand alone but instead reinforce the notion that the aggregate intensity of weekly activity alone is an insufficiently specific predictor of habitual sleep quality across heterogeneous populations. The divergence of findings across studies may partly reflect differences in measurement, population characteristics, and crucially the timing and context of training.

The comparison between dancers and athletes in this study was motivated by the thought that these groups might differ in ways that could moderate the exercise–sleep relationship. Dancers may be exposed to irregular schedules, late-night performances, and aesthetic pressures that influence stress and sleep independent of physical intensity. Athletes may engage in more structured training and recovery practices that could synergize with intensity to influence sleep positively. Despite these plausible differences, group membership did not moderate the intensity–sleep association in our data. This could indicate that the aspects of dance and sport that differ meaningfully in their impact on sleep were not captured by our measures, or that the two groups are more similar in practice than the labels suggest. For example, many dancers in contemporary settings also follow rigorous, structured training regimens that resemble athletic training, and many athletes experience irregular schedules around competitions that can disrupt sleep. Alternatively, it may be that factors other than intensity—such as psychological stress, injury status, cognitive load associated with performance, or use of stimulants or alcohol—are more important in distinguishing sleep outcomes between dancers and athletes.

The null findings have implications for both research and practice. For researchers, the results underline the need for more nuanced and mechanistic studies that go beyond aggregate intensity indices. Future research would benefit from incorporating objective measures of physical activity (e.g., accelerometry with heart-rate monitoring to capture intensity and timing precisely) and objective sleep metrics (e.g., actigraphy or polysomnography to capture sleep architecture, sleep efficiency, and latency). Longitudinal or within-subject designs in which exercise intensity and timing are manipulated experimentally would provide stronger causal tests; for example, randomized crossover trials comparing morning versus evening high-intensity exercise, or varying intensity while holding volume constant, could disentangle the specific influence of intensity. Such designs should also measure potential mediators and moderators, including core body temperature trajectories, autonomic nervous system indices, mood and arousal, and biomarkers of metabolic demand, such as lactate or glucose dynamics, to test hypothesized mechanisms like glycogen depletion and restoration.

For practitioners working with dancers and athletes, the present study suggests caution in assuming that increasing training intensity will automatically improve subjective sleep quality. Instead, practitioners should consider a broader assessment of factors that influence sleep, including timing of sessions, performance schedules, psychological stress, nutrition, and intentional recovery strategies. Structured recovery practices such as consistent sleep schedules, sleep hygiene education, scheduled naps, and targeted recovery modalities may be more effective levers for improving sleep than simply increasing exercise intensity. For individuals who report sleep difficulties, attention to the timing of high-intensity sessions relative to bedtime may be warranted, particularly if subjective arousal or difficulty initiating sleep is a primary complaint.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; even if a correlation had been observed, directionality could not be firmly established. Second, our reliance on self-report questionnaires to assess both exercise intensity and sleep quality introduces measurement error and potential shared-method variance, which can either inflate or obscure associations. Although the composites used attempted to capture multiple dimensions of each construct, objective measures would provide greater precision. Third, the dancer subgroup was relatively small (n = 33), which limited power for subgroup-specific inferences; while the sensitivity analyses indicated adequate power to detect medium-sized effects, small effects could have been missed. Fourth, our exercise composite did not fully capture timing nuances, intensity spikes within sessions, or other aspects of training load such as eccentric muscle damage or metabolic stress, all of which could be relevant to sleep. Fifth, participant heterogeneity in age, sex, training discipline, and other lifestyle factors could have introduced noise that attenuated associations; although we examined covariates, more granular stratification might be informative in larger samples.

Given these limitations, several clear directions for future work emerge. A first priority is to replicate these analyses with objective measures: wearable accelerometers and heart rate monitors to quantify intensity and timing precisely, paired with actigraphy or polysomnography to capture sleep parameters and architecture. A second priority is to employ longitudinal or experimental designs that manipulate intensity and timing while measuring sleep outcomes and relevant physiological mediators. A third priority is to examine potential moderators in larger and more diverse samples, including chronotype, baseline fitness level, sex, age, and psychological stress, to map the boundary conditions under which exercise affects sleep. For dancer-specific research, studies could examine the unique demands of performance season, rehearsal timing, and aesthetic pressures, and how these interact with training intensity to influence sleep and recovery. Similarly, athlete-specific research could investigate periodization, tapering, travel schedules, and competition-related stress as moderators of any exercise–sleep relationship.

In addition to these empirical advances, future research should consider constructing and validating more comprehensive composites of training load that combine objective intensity measures, sessional ratings of perceived exertion, and markers of neuromuscular and metabolic strain. Such multidimensional metrics may better capture the physiologically relevant aspects of exercise that influence sleep regulation. Finally, interdisciplinary approaches that integrate sleep science, exercise physiology, chronobiology, and psychology are likely to be fruitful in unraveling the complex interactions between activity and sleep.

In conclusion, this cross-sectional study of 100 individuals who identify as dancers or athletes found no evidence of a meaningful association between a composite measure of habitual exercise intensity and a composite measure of subjective sleep quality, either overall or within dancer and athlete subgroups. These null findings suggest that simple measures of weekly exercise intensity do not reliably predict habitual subjective sleep quality in heterogeneous samples, and that additional factors such as timing, individual differences, and recovery practices are likely important. While acknowledging limitations in measurement and design, the study contributes to the literature by highlighting the conditional nature of exercise–sleep associations and by suggesting specific directions for future research aimed at clarifying when and for whom exercise intensity influences sleep.
