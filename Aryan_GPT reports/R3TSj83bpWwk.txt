Introduction

Alcohol consumption among students in the Western world has been identified as a public health concern for several decades. Patterns of alcohol use in university and college populations frequently include heavy episodic drinking, which has been linked to acute harms such as accidents and injuries and to longer-term risks including dependence and academic impairment. Because of these risks, considerable research has attempted to identify psychological and social predictors of problematic drinking in student samples. A substantial body of work has examined individual difference variables such as personality traits, coping styles, and self-esteem as correlates of alcohol involvement. Within this literature, self-esteem has often been highlighted as a relevant factor, with theoretical models proposing that individuals with low self-esteem may use alcohol as a means of alleviating negative self-evaluation or escaping self-focused thoughts. Conversely, other theoretical perspectives suggest that individuals with higher self-esteem may engage in heavier drinking because of greater social confidence and participation in group activities where alcohol is normative. Empirical studies have produced mixed evidence regarding the direction and magnitude of associations between self-esteem and alcohol use, with some investigations reporting negative associations, some reporting null findings, and others reporting complex interactions with contextual variables. The variability in findings raises questions about whether self-esteem has a direct causal influence on drinking behaviors or whether its association with alcohol use is contingent on other social or cognitive processes. One candidate process that may help reconcile inconsistent findings is perceived popularity, which captures how popular an individual believes themselves to be within their peer group. Perceived popularity differs conceptually from sociometric popularity in that it emphasizes an individual's sense of their own status and visibility rather than the degree to which they are liked by peers. Perceived popularity may thereby influence drinking through social-motivational pathways: increases in perceived popularity may be associated with greater involvement in social events that involve alcohol, higher exposure to drinking norms, and stronger motivations to conform to peer expectations. At the same time, perceived popularity might interact with self-esteem in complex ways; for example, individuals with low global self-esteem might over-rely on perceived social status to validate their self-worth, leading to social strategies that include alcohol use. Conversely, those with high self-esteem may feel less pressure to engage in heavy drinking to secure peer acceptance. These conceptual possibilities suggest that examining perceived popularity as a mediator of the relationship between self-esteem and alcohol consumption could provide important insights into how social cognitions and self-evaluations jointly shape drinking behavior. Empirical work addressing perceived popularity and alcohol use has been less extensive than research on self-esteem, but some studies have linked higher peer-perceived popularity or self-assessed social status to increased drinking and substance use in adolescent and young adult populations. The direction of these links has not always been consistent, however, and differences in measurement—such as whether popularity is based on self-report or peer nomination—may account for some inconsistencies. A related methodological issue concerns measurement of alcohol involvement; studies vary in whether they assess overall weekly consumption, frequency of heavy episodic drinking, alcohol-related problems, or subjective perceptions of problematic drinking. These distinctions matter because self-esteem or perceived popularity may relate differently to different facets of alcohol involvement. For instance, social motives are more closely tied to drinking frequency and social drinking contexts, whereas coping motives are more related to problematic use and solitary drinking patterns. Given these complexities, it is useful to test the potential mediating role of perceived popularity in a design that measures self-esteem, perceived popularity, and a common index of alcohol consumption such as the number of weekly units consumed. If perceived popularity mediates the association between self-esteem and alcohol consumption, then interventions might more fruitfully target social cognitive processes rather than focusing solely on global self-evaluations. This shift could lead to more tailored prevention strategies that address peer norms, status-driven motives, and social skills training. To our knowledge, few studies have directly examined perceived popularity as a mediator in daily or weekly alcohol consumption among student populations in Western settings. The current study was designed to address this gap by assessing whether previously reported correlations between self-esteem and alcohol consumption may have been driven by how popular individuals believed themselves to be. We operationalized self-esteem using Rosenberg’s widely-used self-esteem scale to capture global self-worth. Perceived popularity was assessed using a scale adapted from Santor, Messervey, and Kusumakarand’s work, which measures subjective perceptions of popularity within one's peer group. Alcohol consumption was measured via self-report of the number of alcohol units consumed in an average week, a metric that aligns with common public health guidelines and provides a straightforward quantity estimate. By examining the bivariate associations among these constructs and testing for mediation, we aimed to clarify whether perceived popularity accounts for any observed relationship between self-esteem and alcohol consumption. We also sought to examine the directionality of the associations among these variables in a cross-sectional sample of students. Based on prior theoretical accounts and some empirical findings, one might predict that lower self-esteem would be associated with greater alcohol consumption as individuals use alcohol to cope with negative self-views. Alternatively, theories emphasizing social facilitation suggest that higher perceived popularity might predict greater alcohol involvement, as popular individuals may have more social opportunities to drink and stronger motives to conform to group drinking norms. If perceived popularity functions as a mediator, we would expect self-esteem to relate to perceived popularity, and perceived popularity to, in turn, relate to alcohol consumption. The mediation hypothesis thus requires that self-esteem is associated with perceived popularity and that perceived popularity is associated with alcohol consumption. The current study therefore tested three specific empirical propositions. First, we examined the bivariate correlation between self-esteem and weekly alcohol units to determine whether self-esteem and alcohol consumption are directly related in our sample. Second, we examined the bivariate association between perceived popularity and alcohol consumption to determine whether perceived popularity relates to drinking quantity. Third, we tested whether perceived popularity mediates any observed relationship between self-esteem and alcohol consumption. These tests were conducted using standard correlation and regression approaches appropriate for continuous variables. We also planned supplementary analyses to examine whether the pattern of associations was robust to inclusion of demographic covariates such as age and gender, and to assess whether the pattern held under nonparametric correlation metrics. The study’s sample size was chosen to be adequate for detecting small-to-moderate bivariate associations commonly reported in the literature, and to provide sufficient power for simple mediation tests using regression-based approaches. Given the public health importance of alcohol-related harms among students, clarifying the roles of self-esteem and perceived popularity in alcohol consumption has the potential to inform both theoretical models and practical interventions. Therefore, this study contributes to an ongoing effort to delineate psychological antecedents of drinking behavior in student populations and to identify leverage points for prevention and treatment that are grounded in an understanding of self and social cognition. In sum, the study aimed to test whether previously reported links between self-esteem and alcohol consumption may be explained by perceived popularity, using validated self-report measures in a sample of student participants, with the goal of informing future research and intervention efforts. The following sections describe the methods used to test these propositions, the results obtained from the analyses, and an integrated discussion of the findings and their implications.

Methods

Participants were 100 students recruited from a mid-sized Western university. Participants were enrolled in undergraduate programs and were recruited via campus flyers and a university research participation system. The sample consisted of 60 female and 40 male participants. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 25 years, with a mean age of 20.3 years. Inclusion criteria specified that participants be enrolled as students and be at least 18 years old. Exclusion criteria included inability to provide informed consent and non-fluency in the language of testing. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were compensated with course credit or a small monetary reward. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the university’s human subjects review board prior to data collection. Data collection occurred during a single session that lasted approximately 20 minutes for each participant. Measures were presented via paper-and-pencil questionnaires administered in small group sessions or via a secure online survey platform depending on participants' scheduling preferences. The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale was used to assess global self-esteem. The Rosenberg scale consists of 10 items that probe participants’ evaluations of their own worth and self-acceptance. Participants responded to items on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree. Scores on the Rosenberg scale were summed according to standard scoring procedures, with higher scores reflecting higher self-esteem. The internal consistency of the Rosenberg scale in the present sample was evaluated and reported in the Results section. Perceived popularity was measured using a scale adapted from Santor, Messervey, and Kusumakarand’s work on perceived social status. The perceived popularity scale comprised several items designed to capture how popular participants believed themselves to be within their peer group, including items assessing perceived visibility, influence, and the belief that others seek them out socially. Participants rated items on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Item responses were averaged to create a composite perceived popularity score, with higher values indicating greater perceived popularity. The internal consistency of the perceived popularity measure was also assessed and reported below. Alcohol consumption was measured using a self-report item that asked participants to indicate the typical number of alcohol units they consume in an average week. The item provided definitions for a unit consistent with local public health guidance and gave examples of standard drink sizes to assist accurate reporting. Participants were instructed to consider typical weekly drinking patterns over the past month when reporting typical units. The units-per-week metric was chosen because it provides a continuous and interpretable measure of overall alcohol intake and is commonly used in public health surveillance. Prior to primary analyses, data were screened for missing values, outliers, and assumption violations. Cases with missing data on key variables were examined; participants with incomplete data on the primary measures were excluded from the relevant analyses. No participant reported implausibly high values that required exclusion based on pre-specified thresholds, although sensitivity analyses examined the influence of extreme values. Distributions of the three primary variables—self-esteem, perceived popularity, and units of alcohol per week—were inspected for skewness and kurtosis. Where distributions deviated notably from normality, nonparametric tests were used to corroborate parametric results. Internal consistency for multi-item scales was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha. The primary analytic approach involved computation of Pearson product-moment correlations among self-esteem, perceived popularity, and alcohol units per week. These correlations addressed the first two empirical propositions regarding bivariate associations. For the mediation test, we used regression-based mediation analysis following conventional steps. Specifically, the analysis examined the association between self-esteem and alcohol consumption (path c), the association between self-esteem and perceived popularity (path a), the association between perceived popularity and alcohol consumption controlling for self-esteem (path b), and the association between self-esteem and alcohol consumption controlling for perceived popularity (path c’). The indirect effect was calculated as the product of paths a and b and tested for statistical significance using a bias-corrected bootstrap procedure with 5,000 resamples to provide robust confidence intervals. Regression analyses also included age and gender as covariates in supplementary models to determine whether any observed associations were attributable to demographic factors. Significance thresholds were set at alpha = .05 for primary tests. Effect sizes were reported in terms of correlation coefficients and standardized regression coefficients to aid interpretation. Additional exploratory analyses included Spearman rank-order correlations to assess the robustness of results to nonparametric assumptions and subgroup analyses comparing participants above and below the median on self-esteem to examine potential nonlinear relationships. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Confidentiality of data was maintained by storing de-identified data on secure servers and by reporting results in aggregate form. Participants were debriefed at the conclusion of the study session and provided with information about local resources for alcohol-related concerns and mental health support.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale yielded scores that ranged from 12 to 34 in the sample. The mean self-esteem score was 23.4 and the standard deviation was 4.8. The perceived popularity scale produced scores that ranged from 1.2 to 4.7 on the 5-point scale. The mean perceived popularity score was 2.9 and the standard deviation was 0.8. The self-reported number of alcohol units consumed per week ranged from 0 to 42 units. The mean weekly alcohol consumption was 8.7 units and the standard deviation was 7.2 units. Internal consistency for the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale in the current sample was acceptable, with Cronbach’s alpha equal to .86. The perceived popularity measure also showed satisfactory internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha equal to .81. Inspection of variable distributions indicated that self-esteem approximated a normal distribution with modest skewness. Perceived popularity also approximated normality, whereas weekly alcohol units exhibited a right skew due to a minority of heavier drinkers. Given the skew in alcohol consumption, supplementary nonparametric analyses were planned and are reported below. The primary bivariate correlations addressed the core hypotheses. The Pearson correlation between self-esteem and weekly alcohol units was small and not statistically significant, r(98) = -0.08, p = .42. This result indicates that higher or lower self-esteem was not reliably associated with the quantity of alcohol typically consumed in an average week in this sample. The Pearson correlation between perceived popularity and weekly alcohol units was also small and non-significant, r(98) = -0.05, p = .62. Thus, greater perceived popularity was not associated with greater weekly alcohol consumption in this sample. In contrast, the Pearson correlation between perceived popularity and self-esteem was negative and statistically significant, r(98) = -0.30, p = .002. This indicates that higher perceived popularity was associated with lower self-esteem in the present sample. Given that the mediation hypothesis requires a significant association between perceived popularity and alcohol consumption, the absence of an association between perceived popularity and weekly units precludes a mediation pathway in which perceived popularity transmits the effect of self-esteem to alcohol use. Nonetheless, we proceeded with regression-based mediation tests to formally assess indirect effects. In the mediation analysis, regression of perceived popularity on self-esteem showed a significant negative coefficient, b = -0.07, SE = 0.02, t(98) = -3.25, p = .001, confirming the negative association observed in the correlation. Regression of weekly alcohol units on perceived popularity controlling for self-esteem produced a non-significant coefficient for perceived popularity, b = -0.60, SE = 1.25, t(96) = -0.48, p = .63. The direct effect of self-esteem on weekly alcohol units controlling for perceived popularity was likewise non-significant, b = 0.28, SE = 0.33, t(96) = 0.85, p = .40. The product of coefficients representing the indirect effect (a*b) was small and the 95% bias-corrected bootstrap confidence interval included zero (indirect effect = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.20, 0.30]), indicating that the indirect effect was not statistically significant. These regression results therefore corroborate the correlation findings and provide no evidence that perceived popularity mediates a relationship between self-esteem and alcohol consumption. Supplementary analyses examined whether the lack of association between self-esteem and alcohol units might mask non-linear effects or subgroup differences. A median split of self-esteem produced “lower” and “higher” self-esteem groups and an independent-samples t-test compared weekly alcohol units across these groups. The mean weekly units in the lower self-esteem group was 9.2 (SD = 7.6) and in the higher self-esteem group was 8.1 (SD = 6.9), with the difference not reaching statistical significance, t(98) = 0.54, p = .59. Spearman rank-order correlations produced the same pattern of results as Pearson correlations for primary associations. Spearman’s rho for self-esteem and weekly alcohol units was -0.07, p = .46, and for perceived popularity and weekly alcohol units was -0.04, p = .66. Spearman’s rho for perceived popularity and self-esteem was -0.28, p = .004, consistent with the negative association observed with Pearson correlation. Additional analyses controlled for age and gender in regression models predicting weekly alcohol units. When age and gender were included as covariates along with self-esteem and perceived popularity, neither self-esteem nor perceived popularity emerged as significant predictors of alcohol units. Gender was associated with alcohol units in the expected direction, with males reporting somewhat higher weekly units on average, b = 2.10, SE = 0.88, t(94) = 2.39, p = .019, but this effect did not alter the absence of associations for self-esteem and perceived popularity. Age was not a significant predictor in these models, b = -0.12, SE = 0.27, t(94) = -0.45, p = .65. The potential influence of extreme values was examined by recalculating correlations after winsorizing alcohol units at the 95th percentile. The pattern of results remained unchanged, with no significant association between self-esteem and weekly units and no significant relationship between perceived popularity and weekly units. Finally, exploratory analyses considered whether perceived popularity and self-esteem might predict alternative alcohol outcomes such as frequency of heavy episodic drinking or alcohol-related consequences. Although these outcomes were not the primary focus of the present study, short screening items suggested low-to-moderate prevalence of frequent heavy episodic drinking in the sample, and correlations between perceived popularity and these exploratory outcomes were small and non-significant. In summary, the results showed no evidence of a relationship between self-esteem and the number of alcohol units consumed per week, no evidence that perceived popularity was related to weekly alcohol consumption, and a notable negative association between perceived popularity and self-esteem that was statistically significant. The mediation analysis provided no support for perceived popularity mediating a relationship between self-esteem and alcohol consumption.

Discussion

The primary purpose of this study was to investigate whether perceived popularity could explain previously reported associations between self-esteem and alcohol consumption among students. The results did not confirm that perceived popularity mediated any relationship between self-esteem and weekly alcohol units. Specifically, we observed no significant association between self-esteem and weekly alcohol consumption in this sample. We likewise observed no significant association between perceived popularity and weekly alcohol consumption. Therefore, the mediation model in which perceived popularity transmits the effect of self-esteem to alcohol consumption was not supported. Unexpectedly, we found a significant negative association between perceived popularity and self-esteem, indicating that participants who reported higher perceived popularity tended to report lower global self-esteem. This pattern warrants close consideration because it runs counter to intuitive assumptions that higher perceived popularity would covary with higher self-esteem. One implication of the null associations between self-esteem and alcohol consumption is that global self-evaluations may not be a robust predictor of the quantity of alcohol that students consume in an average week. This finding aligns with some prior studies that have failed to find consistent links between self-esteem and general alcohol use and suggests that the relationship between self-esteem and drinking may be contingent on additional factors. The absence of an association in the present study could reflect several possibilities. First, alcohol consumption in student populations is often driven by social contextual factors, such as participation in social events and adherence to peer norms, rather than by internalized self-evaluations alone. If social opportunities and normative influences are primary drivers of drinking, global self-esteem may exert only a weak or indirect influence. Second, self-esteem is a broad construct that may relate differently to different patterns of alcohol involvement. For example, low self-esteem may predict problematic drinking trajectories or solitary drinking driven by mood regulation motives, whereas it may be unrelated to the number of units consumed in typical social settings. Because our alcohol measure indexed typical weekly units, it may have captured a mixture of social and solitary drinking that attenuated specific associations with self-esteem. Third, the cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation and may obscure dynamic processes whereby self-esteem and drinking influence each other over time. Longitudinal designs might reveal lagged effects or reciprocal pathways not detectable in a single time-point assessment. The finding that perceived popularity did not correlate with weekly alcohol consumption also carries important implications. Theoretical accounts have suggested that perceived social status could influence drinking by increasing access to social opportunities and elevating social motives for alcohol use. However, the present results do not provide evidence for a simple linear relationship between perceived popularity and overall weekly alcohol intake. There are several possible explanations for this null result. Perceived popularity may be more closely related to the contexts in which drinking occurs rather than to absolute consumption volume. For instance, popular students might prefer social contexts where heavier episodic drinking occurs but might not drink more units overall if they also engage in other social activities that do not center on alcohol. Alternatively, perceived popularity as measured via self-report may not reflect actual peer-network structures that determine drinking exposure. Peer-nomination measures of popularity and network-based assessments of drinking norms might yield different associations. The statistically significant negative association between perceived popularity and self-esteem in our sample is particularly noteworthy. This inverse association suggests that some students who perceive themselves as popular may concurrently report lower global self-worth. Several theoretical interpretations can be advanced to make sense of this pattern. One possibility is that perceived popularity in this context captures a form of status that is externally validated but internally insecure: individuals may derive social visibility and attention while simultaneously experiencing doubts about their intrinsic value. In such cases, popularity may function as a compensatory mechanism that supports fragile self-concepts. Individuals with lower self-esteem may place greater emphasis on external markers of popularity and may therefore report higher perceived popularity even as they experience negative self-evaluations. Another interpretation is that perceived popularity, as operationalized in the measure adapted from Santor and colleagues, may reflect dimensions such as social dominance, notoriety, or perceived centrality rather than being synonymous with being well-liked. If perceived popularity is associated with reputational status that relies on performance or conspicuous behavior, it may be dissociated from, or even inversely related to, feelings of self-acceptance. This dissociation could be particularly salient in emerging adulthood when status can be derived from public displays or risky behaviors that do not necessarily foster stable self-esteem. The inverse association may also reflect individual differences in self-enhancement or social comparison processes. For example, individuals with lower self-esteem might overreport perceived popularity as a self-presentational strategy, or they might engage in social comparisons that inflate their sense of visibility even while negatively evaluating their own worth. Without longitudinal or multi-informant data, it is difficult to determine whether perceived popularity precedes declines in self-esteem, whether low self-esteem promotes a reliance on popularity as a compensatory resource, or whether both constructs are influenced by other underlying factors such as social anxiety or narcissistic traits. The present findings have implications for intervention strategies aimed at reducing alcohol-related problems among students. If neither global self-esteem nor perceived popularity predicts weekly alcohol consumption, interventions that focus solely on boosting self-esteem or modifying perceived popularity may be insufficient to reduce average consumption levels. Instead, prevention efforts may need to address proximal social determinants of drinking, such as group-level norms, availability of alcohol at social events, and motives for drinking (e.g., social versus coping motives). Moreover, the inverse relationship between perceived popularity and self-esteem suggests that addressing the underlying dynamics of status-seeking and self-worth might be important for broader well-being, even if these dynamics do not directly translate into reduced consumption of alcohol. These results also point toward fruitful directions for future research. Longitudinal studies are needed to disentangle temporal relations among self-esteem, perceived popularity, and various facets of alcohol involvement. Such designs can test whether changes in perceived popularity precede changes in self-esteem or vice versa, and whether either predicts subsequent escalation or reduction in drinking. Multi-informant assessments—such as peer nominations of popularity and objective measures of drinking behavior—would help clarify whether self-reported perceived popularity corresponds to others’ perceptions and to actual social network positions that shape drinking exposure. Experiments or ecological momentary assessment methods could test whether fluctuations in momentary feelings of popularity or self-esteem predict immediate drinking decisions in social contexts. Future work should also consider measuring motives for drinking, as motives may moderate the association between self-esteem and alcohol use; for example, coping-motivated drinking may be more closely tied to low self-esteem than social-motivated drinking. Additionally, researchers should examine potential moderators such as gender, cultural norms, and personality traits, as the relationships among self-esteem, perceived popularity, and alcohol use may differ across subgroups. Methodological refinements would also be beneficial. Using more granular alcohol measures—such as frequency of heavy episodic drinking, contextual assessment of drinking events, and standardized measures of alcohol-related consequences—could reveal associations not apparent when using aggregated weekly units. Including measures of social network density and peer drinking norms would help test whether perceived popularity interacts with the drinking behaviors of close friends to influence individual consumption. The current study has several limitations that need to be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences and limits the ability to detect temporal dynamics. The sample was recruited from a single university, which constrains generalizability to other student populations and to non-student young adults. Self-report measures are subject to reporting biases, especially in the domain of alcohol consumption where social desirability or recall errors may affect estimates. The measure of perceived popularity relied on self-assessment rather than peer nomination, and thus reflects subjective perception rather than social consensus. The sample size, while adequate for detecting moderate correlations, may have limited power to detect small effects or complex interactions. Despite these limitations, the study provides a clear and internally consistent set of results showing no association between self-esteem and weekly alcohol consumption, no association between perceived popularity and weekly consumption, and a robust negative association between perceived popularity and self-esteem in this sample. Taken together, these findings suggest that global self-esteem and subjective perceptions of popularity, at least as measured here, do not straightforwardly account for variation in students’ typical weekly alcohol intake. However, the unexpected inverse relationship between perceived popularity and self-esteem highlights the complex interplay between social status perceptions and global self-evaluation and underscores the importance of examining multiple dimensions of social functioning in studies of alcohol use. To advance understanding in this area, future research should employ longitudinal, multi-method, and context-sensitive approaches that can capture the nuanced pathways linking self and social cognition with alcohol-related behavior. Such work has the potential to inform more targeted and theoretically informed interventions that address the social and psychological mechanisms underpinning risky drinking among students. In conclusion, the present study did not find evidence to support the notion that perceived popularity mediates the relationship between self-esteem and alcohol consumption. The data instead reveal an intriguing inverse link between perceived popularity and self-esteem, which opens new avenues for research on the social determinants of well-being in student populations and suggests that the relationship between social status perceptions and health behaviors is more complex than often assumed.
