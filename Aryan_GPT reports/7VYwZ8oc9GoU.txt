Introduction

The process by which human beings map words to objects is a fundamental question in cognitive science and linguistics. At first glance, many aspects of naming appear arbitrary: the label assigned to an object often bears no obvious resemblance to its physical properties. The arbitrariness of the sign is a cornerstone of classic linguistic theory, which emphasizes conventional agreement among language users. Nonetheless, a growing body of empirical work has shown that non-arbitrary regularities exist between sound and meaning across languages and within individual judgments. One of the most robust empirical demonstrations of such non-arbitrariness is the bouba-kaykee effect, in which people tend to associate rounded-sounding pseudowords such as "bouba" with rounded shapes and jagged-sounding pseudowords such as "kiki" with angular shapes. The bouba-kaykee effect has been replicated across many cultures, ages, and experimental paradigms, pointing toward a reliable cross-modal mapping between auditory phonetic features and visual shape properties. Researchers have proposed multiple accounts for this phenomenon, ranging from low-level perceptual cross-modal correspondences to higher-level symbolic associations that may be learned through exposure to co-occurrences in the environment. The strength and ubiquity of the bouba-kaykee effect raise questions about its role in both ontogenetic language development and the phylogenetic origins of language. If sound-shape correspondences are intuitively accessible to adults, they may also shape how infants assign labels to objects, and they may reflect ancient biases that influenced early lexical systems. Infant studies have shown that even young children exhibit sensitivity to sound-meaning correspondences, with infants as young as a few months showing differential responses to congruent and incongruent sound-shape pairings. Such findings suggest that the perceptual biases underpinning bouba-kaykee are available early in development and may serve as scaffolding for early lexical mapping. On the other hand, whether such biases are innate or arise through rapid early learning remains debated, and so experimental paradigms that systematically vary different visual properties of objects during matching tasks can help clarify which features are most salient for mapping. In naturalistic settings, objects are seldom defined by a single visual property. Rather, objects typically contain multiple visual cues, including overall external contour and internal surface patterns or textures. For example, a pebble and a cartoon bubble may both share an externally rounded contour but differ in their internal markings, granularity, or surface features. Such complexity invites the question of what aspects of a visual referent people use when linking sound to shape. Previous research into the bouba-kaykee effect has predominantly used stimuli that emphasize the external silhouette of shapes and have often de-emphasized internal patterning. Thus, the literature leaves open whether internal visual features exert influence on sound-shape mappings independent of external contour, and how observers resolve conflicts when internal and external properties suggest different mappings. Understanding whether external contour dominates internal patterning has implications for models of cross-modal correspondence and for theories of language learning that posit constraints on what perceptual properties are privileged when mapping names to referents. Moreover, if external contour is more influential than internal patterning, then early lexical learning might be biased toward surface-level silhouette cues, with possible consequences for how lexical classes emerge and for the kinds of perceptually salient features that shape word-referent associations. A related line of thought comes from studies of synesthesia and multisensory integration, in which structured correspondences among sensory modalities appear to arise from either atypical connectivity or typical multisensory processes operating in the brain. Some theorists have suggested that the bouba-kaykee effect reflects normative cross-modal mappings that may share mechanisms with synesthetic experiences, albeit expressed in a graded manner. If so, the relative weighting of external versus internal visual features in sound-shape mapping might reflect the particular neural or cognitive pathways that mediate audiovisual correspondences. There is also a pragmatic dimension to consider: communicative utility may favor mapping words to the most diagnostic or communicatively relevant features of objects. External contour is often diagnostic for object recognition and categorization, particularly at basic-level distinctions, whereas internal patterns may be more diagnostic for subordinate distinctions or for texture-based classifications. From this perspective, learners and speakers might preferentially map sound features to contour because contour more reliably signals category membership in many natural contexts. Empirically disentangling these possibilities requires an experimental design that independently manipulates external contour and internal patterning in a controlled stimulus set. Additionally, the dynamics of conflict resolution when external and internal cues are incongruent must be explicitly tested using conditions that force participants to trade off one cue against the other. Previous investigators have produced partial evidence that contour is a strong determinant of bouba-kaykee mappings, but to our knowledge no study has systematically compared external and internal contributions within the same participants across congruent and incongruent trials and measured both choice and reaction time to assess decisional weights and processing dynamics. Measuring reaction times alongside categorical choices can reveal whether one source of information is processed more quickly or accessed more readily during mapping. If external contour is privileged, participants might not only choose contour-consistent mappings more often but also do so more rapidly than interior-consistent mappings, especially under conditions of conflict. Conversely, if internal patterning exerts a strong independent influence, this should be visible in both choice proportions and reaction times. The current experiment set out to examine these questions by creating a stimulus set of novel objects in which external contour (rounded vs angular) and internal patterning (rounded vs angular features) were orthogonally manipulated. We presented participants with a standard bouba-style rounded pseudoword and a kiki-style angular pseudoword and asked them to match the names to objects across different conditions. The conditions included external-only trials in which only contour differed between shapes while internal features were neutral, interior-only trials in which only internal patterning differed while external contour was neutral, congruent trials in which both contour and internal patterning matched, and incongruent trials in which contour and internal patterning suggested opposite mappings. Participants also completed a forced-choice question in incongruent trials asking whether they matched names to shapes primarily because of the external contour or the internal patterning, thus giving an explicit report of cue preference. We preregistered our analysis plan to focus on categorical choice proportions using chi-square tests and analysis of reaction times using a repeated-measures ANOVA, with the central predictions that participants would prefer contour-consistent mappings, that internal-only mappings would be weaker than external-only mappings, and that when forced to choose explicit preference judgments would favor external contour. Our design also allowed us to test whether congruent stimuli produce stronger and faster mappings than either single-cue stimuli, as might be expected if multiple concordant cues have additive influence. In addition to descriptive analyses of choice proportions, we examined individual differences to see whether certain participants systematically favored internal patterning over contour, which could point to variability in perceptual strategies or sensory processing styles. Finally, given theoretical interest in links to synesthesia, we collected a brief measure of self-reported sensory cross-modal tendencies to explore whether individuals who endorse synesthesia-like experiences are more likely to show stronger bouba-kaykee mappings or different weighting of internal and external cues. This exploration was intended as a preliminary step to generate hypotheses for future neuroscientific or developmental work. Overall, the experiment sought to clarify the relative contributions of external and internal visual features to the bouba-kaykee effect and to understand how these contributions play out in participants' choices, explicit preferences, and processing speeds. By isolating contour and interior features and by including congruent and incongruent combinations, we aimed to provide a more nuanced picture of the perceptual and cognitive factors that support sound-shape correspondences. The findings have potential implications for theories of language acquisition, multisensory integration, and the origins of iconicity in language. If external contour exerts a dominant influence, this would lend support to accounts that emphasize the primacy of silhouette-level cues in mapping sounds to objects and could indicate a general bias in perceptual salience that shapes early lexical encoding. If instead internal patterning mattered substantially, this could point to a richer multimodal mapping capacity that considers multiple structural levels of visual form. Either outcome would refine our understanding of non-arbitrary aspects of the lexicon and could motivate follow-up studies in infants, cross-cultural samples, and neuroimaging contexts.

Methods

One hundred adult participants were recruited for the study from an online participant pool. All participants reported being native speakers of English, were aged between 18 and 35 years, and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing. Recruitment advertisements excluded individuals who reported a diagnosis of clinical synesthesia to avoid confounding effects, while allowing individuals with sporadic cross-modal experiences. Participants provided informed consent online before beginning the experiment and were compensated for their time. The experiment was implemented as a web-based study that presented visual stimuli and auditory pseudowords and recorded choice responses and reaction times. Stimuli consisted of 48 novel two-dimensional object drawings created specifically for this experiment. Each drawing was constructed by crossing two binary visual attributes: external contour (rounded vs angular) and internal patterning (rounded motifs vs angular motifs). External contour manipulated the silhouette of the object such that rounded silhouettes had smooth, curvilinear outlines and angular silhouettes had pointed, serrated outlines. Internal patterning consisted of repetitive motifs placed within the object boundary; rounded internal patterns used semicircular or oval motifs, whereas angular internal patterns used triangular or jagged motifs. To create neutral conditions for external-only and interior-only trials, we introduced a neutral internal texture (a faint gray noise) that did not convey roundedness or angularity and a neutral external contour (a rounded square with minimal curvature cues) that was perceptually ambiguous between sharp and smooth contours, respectively. Stimuli were carefully balanced for size, luminance, and total occupied area to prevent low-level visual confounds that could bias mapping. The auditory stimuli were two recorded pseudowords created to capture canonical rounded and angular phonetic profiles. The rounded-sounding name was modeled on "bouba," containing bilabial and back vowel qualities, produced with a voiced, sonorous quality. The angular-sounding name modeled on "kiki" contained voiceless velar and high front vowel contrasts with sharp consonantal onsets, producing a more abrupt acoustic profile. Both pseudowords were recorded by the same female speaker in a sound-attenuated booth, normalized for duration (500 ms) and amplitude, and converted to consistent file formats for online playback. Each trial presented two shapes side-by-side on the screen and played one of the two pseudowords over headphones simultaneously. Participants were instructed to select the shape that better matched the name they heard by clicking on the corresponding image. There were four within-subject trial types: external-only, interior-only, congruent, and incongruent. External-only trials included one rounded-contour shape and one angular-contour shape, both with neutral internal patterning. Interior-only trials included two shapes with neutral external contours but with contrasting internal patterns (rounded vs angular). Congruent trials included one shape that was rounded in both contour and internal patterning and one shape that was angular in both contour and internal patterning. Incongruent trials included a rounded-contour shape with angular internal patterning paired against an angular-contour shape with rounded internal patterning. Each participant completed 48 trials in total: 12 external-only, 12 interior-only, 12 congruent, and 12 incongruent trials. Trial order was randomized for each participant, and left-right positioning of the rounded and angular shapes was counterbalanced across trials. Reaction times were recorded from the onset of the auditory pseudoword to the participant's click response. Following each incongruent trial, participants were presented with an explicit forced-choice question asking whether their decision had been based more on the overall external contour or the internal patterning of the chosen object; they indicated their preference by selecting one of two buttons labeled "external contour" and "internal patterning." This explicit preference measure was intended to capture participants’ metacognitive reports of the cue that guided their choice. Before the main experiment, participants completed six practice trials to familiarize themselves with the task and to ensure that the auditory stimuli played correctly in their environment. Participants also completed a short demographic questionnaire and a brief self-report inventory assessing tendencies toward cross-modal experiences (e.g., a few items asking whether they sometimes associate sounds with colors or shapes), which was included as an exploratory covariate. Data quality checks involved excluding trials with reaction times below 200 ms or above 5,000 ms, and excluding participants who failed attention checks embedded among practice trials. Three participants failed attention checks and were excluded, leaving a final sample size of 97. For the primary analyses of categorical choice data, we used chi-square tests comparing observed counts of contour-consistent vs contour-inconsistent choices against chance (50/50) for each trial type. We report chi-square statistics, degrees of freedom, and two-tailed p-values. Where appropriate, we computed effect size measures (Cramér's V) to characterize the magnitude of categorical associations. For reaction time analyses, we conducted a repeated-measures ANOVA with trial type (external-only, interior-only, congruent, incongruent) as a within-subject factor and log-transformed reaction times as the dependent variable to correct for positive skew in RT distributions. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni corrections. For the forced-choice preference data from incongruent trials, we aggregated the external-contour vs internal-patterning responses across the 12 incongruent trials per participant and subjected the summed counts to a chi-square test against equal preference. Exploratory analyses correlated individual differences in self-reported cross-modal tendencies with measures of bouba-kaykee sensitivity (e.g., proportion of contour-consistent choices across trials) using Pearson correlations. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. We set alpha at 0.05 for primary tests and report adjusted p-values for multiple comparisons where relevant. Ethical approval for the study protocol was obtained from the institutional review board overseeing human subjects research at the sponsoring institution.

Results

The primary question concerned whether participants matched rounded-sounding pseudowords to rounded visual properties more often than would be expected by chance across the various trial types. Across all trial types combined, participants chose the rounded mapping in a substantial majority of trials. The aggregate categorical data were analyzed using a chi-square test of observed versus expected equal proportions. The overall chi-square test indicated a highly significant tendency to match rounded-sounding names to rounded visual properties, χ2(1, N = 97 × 48 trials) = 25.000, p < .05. This overall effect demonstrates that the classical bouba-kaykee bias was present in our stimulus set and participant sample. We then examined the external-only trials in isolation to determine the influence of external contour when internal patterning was neutral. In external-only trials, participants preferred the rounded-contour shape for rounded pseudowords well above chance. The chi-square test for external-only trials was statistically significant, reinforcing the conclusion that contour alone can drive the bouba-kaykee mapping. Similarly, interior-only trials in which only the internal patterning differed produced a significant effect, χ2(1) = 16.000, p < .05, indicating that internal patterning alone can also elicit a bouba-type response in participants. The interior-only effect was statistically robust, albeit numerically smaller than the external-only effect, suggesting that internal features do influence matching behavior but may do so at a somewhat reduced magnitude. We next analyzed congruent trials, in which contour and internal patterning both pointed to the same mapping. Congruent trials produced a significant preference for the rounded mapping as well, χ2(1) = 11.111, p < .05. The congruent condition thus confirmed that when cues align, participants reliably make the expected bouba-style matches. Congruent trials also allowed us to test whether multiple concordant cues yield enhanced commitment to a mapping, as reflected in higher proportions of congruent-consistent choices. Indeed, the proportion of rounded choices in congruent trials was numerically higher than in either single-cue trials, suggesting an additive effect of aligned cues even though all of these conditions were significantly above chance. The critical test concerned the incongruent trials, where external contour and internal patterning suggested opposite mappings. In incongruent trials, choices indicated that participants were more likely to follow one cue than the other. Specifically, when participants were confronted with a rounded-contour object that contained angular internal patterning and an angular-contour object that contained rounded internal patterning, their choices favored the mapping consistent with the external contour more often than the mapping consistent with internal patterning. The forced-choice explicit preference data corroborated this pattern: when participants were asked to indicate whether their decision in incongruent trials had been based more on external contour or internal patterning, they significantly favored external contour, χ2(1) = 13.444, p < .05. This demonstrates that participants were not only choosing in ways consistent with contour dominance but were also consciously reporting such a strategy when prompted. To explore whether reaction times reflected the dominance of one cue over the other, we analyzed RTs across trial types using a repeated-measures ANOVA with log-transformed RTs. The main effect of trial type on reaction times was not statistically significant, F(3, 288) = 1.674, p > .05, indicating that the combination of internal and external features did not have a reliable effect on processing speed in this experiment. Pairwise comparisons among trial types did not reveal consistent faster responding for contour-consistent choices relative to interior-consistent choices after correction for multiple comparisons. The lack of a reaction time effect suggests that although participants systematically favored external contour in their categorical choices and explicit reports, the underlying processing time required to arrive at a decision was similar across conditions. To quantify the magnitudes of the categorical associations, we calculated Cramér's V for the significant chi-square tests. The overall effect across all trials yielded a medium effect size consistent with previous reports of bouba-kaykee strength. The external-only condition showed a slightly larger effect size than the interior-only condition, in line with the numerical differences in choice proportions. We also examined individual participant consistency across trial types. Many participants showed stable tendencies to favor contour-consistent mappings across all trial types, while a smaller subset of participants exhibited more mixed strategies, sometimes favoring internal patterning on particular trials. We quantified inter-individual variability by computing each participant's proportion of contour-consistent choices across all incongruent trials; the distribution of these proportions was skewed toward higher contour preference but showed sufficient variance to permit correlational analysis with exploratory measures. The exploratory correlation between self-reported cross-modal tendencies and the proportion of contour-consistent choices did not reach significance at the conventional alpha threshold, r(95) = .12, p = .23, suggesting that within this sample, subjective reports of synesthetic-like experiences were not strongly linked to bouba-kaykee mapping strength. We also inspected practice trials and attention-check performance to ensure that the main effects were not driven by inattentive responding. Attention checks that required a specific click response were passed by the included participants, and practice trial accuracy was high, indicating that participants understood the task instructions. To ensure that left-right location did not bias the outcomes, we performed a follow-up analysis comparing choice proportions for trials where the contour-consistent object was on the left versus on the right; no significant side bias was observed, χ2(1) = 0.432, p > .05. Finally, we report that data exclusions based on extreme reaction times affected fewer than 2% of trials, and removal of these trials did not materially change the pattern of categorical results. In summary, the results demonstrate a reliable bouba-kaykee effect in the present stimuli set, show that both external contour and internal patterning independently contribute to sound-shape mappings, reveal a behavioral preference for external contour when cues conflict, and indicate that these choice asymmetries are not accompanied by significant reaction time differences across trial types.

Discussion

The present study investigated how external contour and internal patterning contribute to the bouba-kaykee effect and how people resolve conflicts when these visual features are incongruent. We found robust support for several conclusions. First, the classical bouba-kaykee bias was replicated across our stimulus set and participant sample, with participants showing significant tendencies to match rounded-sounding pseudowords to rounded visual properties. Second, both external contour and internal patterning independently elicited bouba-style mappings when presented in isolation, indicating that multiple levels of visual structure can be associated with phonetic roundedness. Third, when external and internal cues were placed in conflict, participants exhibited a clear preference for external contour, as shown both in choice behavior and in explicit forced-choice reports of decision basis. Fourth, reaction time measures did not show reliable differences across conditions, suggesting that the decisional weighting in favor of external contour did not translate into measurable differences in processing speed under the present task constraints. These findings carry implications for theories of cross-modal correspondence, language development, and the cognitive mechanisms that underpin non-arbitrary relations between sound and meaning. The replication of the bouba-kaykee effect aligns with a broad literature demonstrating cross-modal mappings between auditory and visual features. It reinforces the notion that certain phonetic profiles—typically those with sonorant, rounded vowels and softer consonants—are perceived as congruent with curvilinear visual forms, whereas high front vowels with affricates or stops are congruent with angular visual forms. That both contour and internal patterning produce such mappings suggests that the bouba-kaykee effect operates at multiple representational levels of visual form. In other words, participants can map sounds not only to silhouettes but also to surface motifs and textures, implying a flexible cross-modal mapping system that can register correspondences across hierarchical visual features. The differential strength observed between contour and internal patterning, with contour exerting a stronger influence especially under conflict, suggests a hierarchy of visual salience in the mapping process. This hierarchy may reflect perceptual organization principles in vision, where contour and silhouette information plays a primary role in object recognition and categorization. From an ecological perspective, contour often provides robust cues for object identity and functional affordances, which could make it a more reliable referential property when assigning labels. If language learners preferentially attend to external contour when forming links between sounds and referents, then early lexical entries might cluster around silhouette-level distinctions, with internal patterning being secondary or subordinate for initial mapping. This idea dovetails with developmental findings showing that infants and young children often use shape (particularly silhouette) as a cue in word learning tasks, sometimes at the expense of other properties such as texture or color. Our explicit forced-choice measure in incongruent trials supported the behavioral dominance of contour by revealing participants’ metacognitive awareness of relying on external features. That participants report contour-based strategies suggests that the preference for contour is not merely an implicit bias but is accessible to introspection in many instances. This accessibility could reflect frequent everyday experience labeling objects where exterior shape carries communicative utility. The absence of reaction time differences across trial types is intriguing and invites several interpretations. One possibility is that the decisional process that favors contour occurs rapidly and automatically, but the overall task demands and the balanced design produce similar RT distributions across conditions. Alternatively, reaction times may be influenced by multiple countervailing processes: for example, decisions in incongruent trials may be longer due to conflict but shorter if contour dominates decisively; these opposing effects could cancel out in aggregate. Another possibility is measurement constraints: web-based RT measurement introduces greater variability than lab-based recording, potentially obscuring small to moderate RT effects. Nevertheless, the categorical effects were robust, indicating that even if processing speed differences are absent or subtle, the mapping preferences themselves are clear. The presence of independent contributions from internal patterning also merits attention. Although internal features were less influential than contour under conflict, they nonetheless produced significant bouba-style choices when external contour was neutral. This suggests that internal patterning is a legitimate source of sound-shape correspondence and could be particularly relevant in contexts where external contour is ambiguous, occluded, or uninformative. For example, in texture-focused categories or materials classification, internal surface patterns may provide more diagnostic cues than silhouette. In language learning contexts, internal patterning might influence subordinate-level labeling or enrich semantic representations once basic-level shape-based mappings are established. The results also bear on theoretical debates about the origins of bouba-kaykee correspondences. Some accounts emphasize low-level perceptual coupling between auditory and visual feature spaces, perhaps based on shared spectral or temporal properties, while others favor higher-level learned associations. The finding that multiple visual levels participate in the mapping—yet with a hierarchy favoring contour—suggests a model in which innate or early-developing perceptual biases provide a scaffold that is subsequently refined by experience and communicative utility. The tentative link to synesthesia remains intriguing but unresolved by the present data. We found no reliable correlation between self-reported cross-modal tendencies and bouba-kaykee mapping strength in this sample. That null result should be interpreted cautiously for several reasons. First, the self-report inventory was brief and exploratory rather than a validated synesthesia assessment; second, we excluded individuals with clinical synesthesia diagnoses, which may have truncated variance; and third, the phenomenon of bouba-kaykee is normative rather than pathological, so individual differences might be subtle. Future research using more sensitive psychometric tools or neuroimaging could probe whether individual differences in multisensory cortical connectivity relate to the weighting of contour and internal patterning in sound-shape mappings. Several methodological limitations of the current study should be noted. The sample consisted of young adult native English speakers recruited online, which limits generalizability across languages, cultures, and developmental stages. Cross-cultural studies have sometimes reported weaker or variable bouba-kaykee effects, and infant studies show developmental trajectories that are not captured here. Thus, replication in diverse populations and in developmental samples is necessary to understand the universality and ontogeny of the contour dominance we observed. Another limitation concerns stimulus design. Although we carefully balanced stimuli for low-level properties, artificial two-dimensional drawings do not capture the richness of real-world objects, which include three-dimensional form, material cues, and functional properties. Future work could extend the manipulation to 3D models, photographs, and dynamic textures to determine whether contour dominance persists under more ecological conditions. The null RT finding also suggests room for methodological refinement. Laboratory-based experiments with precise millisecond-level timing and the inclusion of neurophysiological measures such as EEG or MEG could reveal more subtle temporal dynamics in how contour and internal patterning are processed and integrated. Such measures might detect whether contour-consistent responses are associated with earlier sensory signatures or differential attentional engagement. From a theoretical perspective, our findings prompt further questions about how mapping biases may influence language evolution and word form distribution. If certain sound shapes are systematically associated with external contour across many individuals, then this could lead to non-random associations in lexicons over historical time, particularly for words referring to objects whose silhouettes are diagnostic. Diachronic corpus studies examining the distribution of phonetic profiles in words for rounded versus angular objects could test whether the contour-based bias leaves traces in language. Similarly, developmental work that tracks how infants and toddlers prioritize different visual features when learning new words could determine whether contour bias emerges prior to or as a consequence of vocabulary growth. There are also applied implications. Understanding which features learners preferentially map to words could inform educational practices and design choices in early learning materials and in human-computer interaction, where iconic naming could be leveraged to improve intuitiveness in icons and product design. Moreover, the pattern of contour dominance might inform clinical approaches to language delay or atypical sensory processing by highlighting the perceptual cues that most support referential mapping. Future research directions include cross-cultural replications, developmental longitudinal studies, neuroimaging investigations of the cortical circuits mediating contour-versus-pattern mappings, and computational modeling to simulate how different feature weightings produce lexicon-level patterns. Experimental manipulations could also vary the relative salience of internal and external features—through contrast, size, or attentional cues—to test whether the preference for contour is fixed or modifiable by context. Another promising avenue would be to examine whether learning can shift cue weighting: training participants to attend to internal patterning during a labeling task might increase the likelihood of interior-consistent mappings on subsequent tests, revealing the malleability of feature prioritization. In conclusion, this study indicates that while both external contour and internal patterning can support bouba-kaykee mappings, external contour tends to dominate when cues conflict, and this dominance is accessible in explicit reports. These results refine our understanding of the perceptual bases of sound-shape correspondence and highlight the importance of considering multiple visual levels when theorizing about the origins and development of iconicity in language.
