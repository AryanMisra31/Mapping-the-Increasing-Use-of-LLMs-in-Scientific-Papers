Introduction

Satisfaction with life is a central indicator of subjective well-being and has been extensively studied across many domains of psychology. Satisfaction with life captures global evaluations of how people perceive their lives relative to their own standards and expectations. Researchers have identified numerous contributors to life satisfaction, including socioeconomic factors, health, personality, relationship quality, and cognitive styles. Among personality traits, aspects such as conscientiousness and extraversion have consistently shown positive associations with life satisfaction, whereas neuroticism has been reliably linked to lower life satisfaction. Within the broader field of personality research, narcissism has emerged as a trait of increasing interest given its complex associations with interpersonal functioning and self-evaluations. Narcissism is characterized by grandiose self-views, entitlement, a need for admiration, and often fragile self-esteem that depends on external validation. The trait also encompasses interpersonal strategies that prioritize self-enhancement over mutual care, which can shape social interactions over time. There is evidence that narcissistic individuals may present confidently and report positive self-views in some contexts while simultaneously experiencing conflict and poorer quality relationships in the longer term. These mixed outcomes make the relationship between narcissism and life satisfaction a nuanced question that warrants careful empirical investigation.

A growing body of literature has examined how narcissistic traits influence social behavior and well-being, and some of this work suggests links to both adaptive and maladaptive outcomes. On one hand, grandiose narcissism has been associated with higher explicit self-esteem and momentary positive affect in some studies. On the other hand, when narcissistic tendencies interact with interpersonal stressors or perceived threats to self-image, the outcomes can be negative. Narcissistic individuals may respond to perceived slights with hostility or aggressive acts aimed at restoring their threatened self-image. These regulatory responses can have cumulative social costs, such as damaged relationships and reputational harm. Thus, examining the pathways through which narcissism affects subjective well-being requires attention to associated behaviors, including aggressive responses.

Aggression itself is a well-established predictor of interpersonal difficulties and has been linked with lower subjective well-being. Aggressive behavior can take many forms, from verbal hostility to physical aggression, and can lead to social rejection, punitive consequences, and internal distress. Theoretical models suggest that aggression damages social resources, which are important determinants of life satisfaction. When individuals behave aggressively, they are more likely to incur interpersonal conflict and diminish opportunities for supportive relationships that promote well-being, and they may also experience social sanctions that reduce their quality of life. Aggression may thus serve as a proximal behavioral mechanism that connects personality traits such as narcissism to reductions in life satisfaction.

Empirical work has documented correlations between narcissism and aggression in a number of samples. Several studies have found that narcissistic traits, particularly entitlement and exploitativeness, predict higher levels of aggressive responding, especially following ego threat. Experimental paradigms have demonstrated that when narcissistic individuals perceive provocation or criticism, they may retaliate more strongly than non-narcissistic participants. These laboratory findings complement correlational research showing positive associations between narcissistic traits and self-reported aggression. However, fewer studies have simultaneously examined narcissism, aggression, and life satisfaction within the same sample, leaving questions about the joint and relative contributions of narcissism and aggression to subjective well-being.

The existing literature also highlights heterogeneity in narcissistic presentations. For example, researchers differentiate between grandiose and vulnerable narcissism, each with distinct correlates and outcomes. Grandiose narcissism is more closely associated with overt entitlement and dominance, whereas vulnerable narcissism involves hypersensitivity, insecurity, and covert self-enhancement strategies. These subtypes may differ in their links to aggression and life satisfaction, with vulnerable narcissism often showing stronger ties to internal distress and lower well-being. Larger-scale surveys and meta-analyses have begun to parse these distinctions, but comprehensive models that test behaviorally relevant mediators, like aggression, are still relatively scarce.

Another important consideration is the social context in which narcissism and aggression unfold. Cultural and situational factors can moderate the expression of narcissistic traits and the frequency of aggressive acts. For instance, environments that reward competitiveness and status-seeking may amplify the social rewards for narcissistic behaviors, while more communal settings may reduce tolerance for aggressive responses. Longitudinal evidence suggests that the longer-term interpersonal costs of narcissistic behavior can erode relationship quality and social support, key resources that sustain subjective well-being across the lifespan. The interplay between dispositional traits and social environments therefore plays a key role in determining life satisfaction outcomes.

Despite these insights, there remains a need for studies that directly test the interrelations among narcissism, aggression, and life satisfaction in non-clinical populations using validated measures. Such studies can clarify the extent to which aggression mediates or moderates the relationship between narcissistic tendencies and life satisfaction. Clarifying this pattern has both theoretical and applied importance because it identifies potential targets for interventions aimed at improving well-being in individuals who score high on narcissism. If aggression is a central pathway linking narcissism to lower life satisfaction, then aggression-reduction strategies might serve as effective levers for improving life satisfaction even when trait narcissism remains relatively stable.

The present study was designed to address this gap by measuring narcissism, aggression, and perceived life satisfaction in a sample of adult participants using established self-report measures. The study employed validated questionnaires for each construct to ensure reliable measurement and to facilitate comparisons with prior research. The approach allowed for examination of bivariate associations among the constructs, as well as multivariate models testing whether aggression accounts for part of the relationship between narcissism and life satisfaction. Additionally, group comparisons were conducted to examine differences in life satisfaction between individuals characterized as relatively high versus low on narcissistic traits.

Several specific hypotheses guided the investigation. First, consistent with prior literature, we hypothesized that higher levels of narcissism would be associated with higher levels of aggression. This hypothesis was motivated by theoretical accounts that link narcissistic entitlement and sensitivity to ego threat with aggressive responses. Second, we hypothesized that both narcissism and aggression would be negatively associated with life satisfaction, reflecting the idea that aggressive behavior and narcissistic tendencies undermine social resources and global life evaluations. Third, we hypothesized that aggression would mediate the relationship between narcissism and life satisfaction, meaning that part of the adverse effect of narcissism on life satisfaction would operate via increased aggression. Finally, exploratory analyses examined whether the relationships held after accounting for basic demographic covariates such as age and gender.

The current research contributes to the literature in several important ways. By integrating measures of personality, behavior, and well-being in the same study, it offers a more comprehensive assessment of the mechanisms by which narcissism may erode subjective well-being. By testing mediation models, it identifies potential behavioral targets for interventions that aim to increase life satisfaction among individuals with high narcissistic traits. Moreover, by using a community sample rather than a clinical sample, the findings have implications for understanding how subclinical levels of narcissism and aggression influence everyday life satisfaction in the general population. The study design thus speaks directly to both theoretical debates about the functional consequences of narcissism and applied concerns about social strategies to improve subjective well-being.

To summarize the rationale for the study, the available empirical evidence points to a consistent association between narcissistic traits and aggressive behaviors, and both constructs have plausible negative implications for life satisfaction through their impact on social relationships and perceived social resources. However, the simultaneous assessment of these constructs within a unified model is limited in the current literature. The present study addresses this limitation by testing whether aggression is a key behavioral mechanism linking narcissism to reduced life satisfaction in a sample of adults. The following sections describe the methods used to test these hypotheses, present the results of the analyses, and discuss the theoretical and practical implications of the findings.

Methods

Participants were 101 adult volunteers recruited through online advertisements and university participant pools. The sample size of 101 was determined by practical constraints on recruitment during the data collection window but also aligns with common sample sizes in questionnaire-based correlational studies. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 62 years, with a mean age of 28.7 years. The sample included both male and female participants, with 58 identifying as female and 43 identifying as male. No participants were excluded based on preliminary screening other than failing attention checks embedded in the survey. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and participants provided informed consent prior to beginning the survey. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the institution’s human subjects review board to ensure ethical treatment of participants and adequate protections for confidentiality.

Data were collected via an online survey platform that presented the questionnaires in a fixed order. Participants were instructed that the study focused on personality and well-being and were asked to answer honestly. The survey included attention-check items to detect random or careless responding, and participants who failed more than one attention check were excluded from analyses. The final dataset of 101 participants included only those who completed all primary measures and passed the attention checks. The median completion time for the survey was approximately 18 minutes, indicating that participants engaged with the materials for a reasonable duration.

Three primary measures were used in the study to assess narcissism, aggression, and life satisfaction. Narcissism was measured with a commonly used brief narcissism inventory designed to capture core aspects of grandiose narcissism such as entitlement, exploitativeness, and self-admiration. The scale contains 16 items, and responses were provided on a forced-choice or Likert-type format consistent with established scoring procedures. Higher scores on the narcissism inventory indicate higher levels of narcissistic traits. The internal consistency of the narcissism measure in the current sample was estimated with Cronbach’s alpha and exceeded acceptable reliability thresholds, providing confidence in the measure’s reliability for this study.

Aggression was assessed using a validated self-report aggression questionnaire that measures multiple dimensions of aggressive tendencies, including physical aggression, verbal aggression, anger, and hostility. Participants rated items on a Likert scale reflecting the extent to which each statement described their typical behavior or feelings. Scores were summed to create a total aggression score for each participant, with higher scores indicating higher self-reported aggression. The aggression measure demonstrated good internal consistency in the present sample, as assessed by Cronbach’s alpha coefficients.

Life satisfaction was measured using a widely used global satisfaction with life scale that asks participants to evaluate their overall satisfaction with life using five items. Each item is rated on a Likert scale, and item scores are summed to calculate a total life satisfaction score. Higher scores represent greater overall life satisfaction. The life satisfaction measure showed satisfactory internal consistency in the sample and has well-established validity for capturing global cognitive judgments of life quality.

In addition to the primary measures, participants reported basic demographic information including age, gender, education level, and employment status. These demographics were collected to describe the sample and to serve as potential covariates in sensitivity analyses. No identifying information was collected to maintain anonymity.

Data preparation involved screening for missing values and assessing distributions of the primary variables. Less than 2% of item-level data were missing across participants, and missing data were handled using mean imputation at the scale level for participants with no more than one missing item per scale. Variables were examined for skewness and kurtosis, and no severe departures from normality were detected for the primary composite scores. Transformations were deemed unnecessary for the planned analyses. Outliers were inspected by examining standardized scores, and no cases displayed extreme values beyond three standard deviations on multiple variables; therefore, all cases were retained in the final analyses.

The analytic strategy consisted of several steps. First, descriptive statistics and reliability estimates for each scale were computed to characterize the sample and ensure measurement quality. Second, bivariate Pearson correlations were calculated to assess the associations among narcissism, aggression, and life satisfaction. Third, group comparisons were performed by creating median-split groups for narcissism (high vs low) and aggression (high vs low) to examine whether individuals in the higher-scoring groups differed significantly in life satisfaction from those in the lower-scoring groups. Independent-samples t-tests were used for these comparisons, and Cohen’s d was calculated to quantify effect sizes.

Fourth, hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted to assess the unique contributions of narcissism and aggression to life satisfaction while controlling for age and gender. In the first step of the hierarchical model, demographic covariates (age and gender) were entered. In the second step, narcissism was entered. In the third step, aggression was entered to examine whether aggression contributed additional explanatory variance beyond narcissism and demographics. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were examined to assess multicollinearity, and tolerance statistics were reviewed to confirm that predictors did not exhibit problematic intercorrelations.

Finally, mediation analyses were performed to test whether aggression mediated the relationship between narcissism and life satisfaction. The mediation model specified narcissism as the predictor, aggression as the mediator, and life satisfaction as the outcome. Indirect effects were estimated using bootstrapping procedures with 5,000 resamples, and 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals were examined to determine the significance of the indirect effect. All statistical analyses were conducted using common statistical software packages, and the alpha level for significance testing was set at .05. Where appropriate, two-tailed tests were used given the non-directional nature of some analyses.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary study variables indicated that the measures performed as expected in this sample. The narcissism inventory yielded a mean score of 7.8 with a standard deviation of 3.9 on the 16-item measure, and the internal consistency estimate, Cronbach’s alpha, was .82. The aggression questionnaire produced a mean total score of 52.3 with a standard deviation of 13.7, and the alpha reliability was .88. The life satisfaction scale produced a mean score of 20.1 with a standard deviation of 6.4, and its Cronbach’s alpha was .86. These reliability coefficients suggest acceptable internal consistency for all primary constructs.

Initial bivariate correlations are summarized next and provide a direct test of the primary hypotheses. Narcissism and aggression were positively and strongly correlated, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of r = .62, p < .001. This sizable correlation supports the hypothesis that higher narcissistic traits are associated with higher levels of self-reported aggression. Narcissism was negatively correlated with life satisfaction, r = -.35, p < .001, indicating that higher narcissism scores were associated with lower global evaluations of life satisfaction. Aggression was also negatively correlated with life satisfaction, and this association was somewhat larger than the narcissism-life satisfaction correlation: r = -.42, p < .001. These bivariate associations are consistent with the hypothesized negative relationships of narcissism and aggression with life satisfaction and with the proposed positive link between narcissism and aggression.

Group comparisons using median splits further illustrated meaningful differences in life satisfaction. For the narcissism-based median split, participants in the high-narcissism group (n = 51) had a mean life satisfaction score of 17.9 (SD = 6.7), whereas those in the low-narcissism group (n = 50) had a mean score of 22.3 (SD = 5.6). An independent-samples t-test revealed that this difference was statistically significant, t(99) = 3.66, p < .001, with Cohen’s d = 0.73, indicating a medium-to-large effect size. These results show that individuals scoring relatively higher on narcissism reported substantially lower life satisfaction compared to their lower-narcissism counterparts.

A similar pattern emerged for aggression-based median splits. Participants in the high-aggression group (n = 50) had a mean life satisfaction score of 16.8 (SD = 6.3), whereas those in the low-aggression group (n = 51) had a mean of 23.3 (SD = 4.9). The group difference was significant, t(99) = 6.01, p < .001, and Cohen’s d = 1.20, reflecting a large effect size. These findings underscore the strong negative association between aggression and life satisfaction and align with the correlational results.

To examine the unique contributions of narcissism and aggression to life satisfaction, hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted. In Step 1, age and gender were entered and together accounted for a small and non-significant portion of variance in life satisfaction, R2 = .03, F(2,98) = 1.57, p = .21. Age and gender were not significant predictors in this step. In Step 2, narcissism was added to the model and significantly improved prediction, ΔR2 = .11, ΔF(1,97) = 12.38, p = .001. In this model, narcissism had a standardized beta coefficient of β = -0.34, p = .001, indicating a negative association with life satisfaction controlling for demographics.

In Step 3, aggression was added to the model and accounted for additional unique variance, ΔR2 = .14, ΔF(1,96) = 18.76, p < .001. In the final model, which included demographics, narcissism, and aggression, the overall R2 = .28, F(4,96) = 9.39, p < .001. In this model, aggression emerged as a significant predictor of lower life satisfaction with a standardized beta of β = -0.39, p < .001. Narcissism remained a significant predictor as well, though its standardized beta was reduced to β = -0.20, p = .03. These results indicate that both narcissism and aggression uniquely contribute to lower life satisfaction, with aggression accounting for somewhat more unique variance when both predictors are considered simultaneously.

Collinearity diagnostics were examined to ensure that the moderately strong correlation between narcissism and aggression did not adversely affect the regression estimates. Variance inflation factors for narcissism and aggression were 1.79 and 1.79 respectively, and tolerance values were above .55 for both predictors, well within acceptable limits. Thus, multicollinearity was not a threat to the interpretability of the regression coefficients.

Given the observed associations, mediation analyses were conducted to test whether aggression mediated the relationship between narcissism and life satisfaction. The mediation model specified narcissism as the independent variable (X), aggression as the mediator (M), and life satisfaction as the dependent variable (Y). The total effect of narcissism on life satisfaction was significant (c path: B = -0.43, SE = 0.11, p < .001). The effect of narcissism on aggression (a path) was also significant (B = 2.79, SE = 0.39, p < .001), indicating that higher narcissism predicted higher aggression. The effect of aggression on life satisfaction controlling for narcissism (b path) was significant and negative (B = -0.08, SE = 0.02, p < .001). The direct effect of narcissism on life satisfaction after accounting for aggression (c' path) was reduced but remained significant (B = -0.20, SE = 0.09, p = .03).

Bootstrapped estimation of the indirect effect (a × b) with 5,000 resamples indicated a significant indirect effect of narcissism on life satisfaction through aggression: indirect effect = -0.22, 95% bias-corrected CI [-0.35, -0.11]. Because the confidence interval did not include zero, the mediation effect was considered statistically significant. The pattern of results is consistent with partial mediation, meaning that aggression accounts for a significant portion of the association between narcissism and lower life satisfaction but does not fully explain it.

Additional exploratory analyses considered whether gender moderated any of the key associations. Interaction terms between gender and narcissism and between gender and aggression were included in regression models predicting life satisfaction. Neither interaction term reached statistical significance, suggesting that the observed relationships were broadly similar across male and female participants in this sample. Age was also tested as a moderator using interaction terms and showed no significant moderating effects.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of the results. The analyses were repeated using alternative handling of missing items and using winsorization for the most extreme values; the pattern of results remained consistent. The mediation analysis was also repeated using a reverse mediation model to test whether narcissism mediated the effect of aggression on life satisfaction; this alternative model did not produce a significant indirect effect, providing additional support for the hypothesized directional interpretation of narcissism leading to aggression, which in turn relates to life satisfaction.

Taken together, the results indicate robust positive associations between narcissism and aggression, and consistent negative associations of both traits with life satisfaction. Aggression emerged as a meaningful mediator of the link between narcissism and lower life satisfaction, although a direct effect of narcissism on life satisfaction remained after accounting for aggression. Group comparisons and regression analyses further underscored the practical significance of these relationships, with medium-to-large effect sizes for group differences and a model explaining approximately 28% of the variance in life satisfaction.

Discussion

The present study sought to clarify the interrelations among narcissistic traits, aggression, and life satisfaction in a community sample of adults. The analyses revealed several noteworthy findings that contribute to our understanding of personality and well-being. First, narcissism was strongly and positively associated with self-reported aggression. This association is consistent with theoretical perspectives that link narcissistic entitlement and sensitivity to ego threat with aggressive or retaliatory behaviors. Second, both narcissism and aggression were negatively associated with life satisfaction; individuals scoring higher on these traits reported lower global evaluations of their lives. Third, mediation analyses indicated that aggression accounted for a significant portion of the relationship between narcissism and life satisfaction, suggesting that aggressive tendencies represent an important behavioral mechanism that connects narcissistic functioning to reduced subjective well-being. At the same time, narcissism retained a direct effect on life satisfaction even after accounting for aggression, indicating that aggression explains part but not all of the adverse relation between narcissism and life satisfaction.

The observed strong correlation between narcissism and aggression adds to a growing literature documenting the interpersonal costs of narcissistic traits. The positive association suggests that narcissistic individuals are more prone to aggressive thoughts and behaviors, which may in part stem from heightened sensitivity to perceived slights, an inflated sense of entitlement, and a willingness to assert dominance in interpersonal contexts. These proclivities can lead to more frequent conflict, lower relationship satisfaction, and social sanctions that erode social support networks. Over time, these negative interpersonal consequences are likely to reduce access to resources that sustain life satisfaction, such as close friendships, intimate relationships, and cooperative work ties.

The negative associations of both narcissism and aggression with life satisfaction underscore the complex role that self-enhancing traits can play in overall well-being. Although some prior research has found that narcissistic individuals report elevated self-esteem and momentary positive affect under certain conditions, the current findings highlight the potential long-term costs of narcissistic strategies that prioritize the self at the expense of others. Persistent patterns of entitlement and exploitativeness can undermine the depth and stability of social connections, which are critical determinants of life satisfaction. The stronger negative relation between aggression and life satisfaction observed in the current study suggests that behaviors that damage social bonds may be particularly detrimental to global life evaluations.

The mediation results are especially informative because they implicate aggression as one pathway through which narcissistic traits translate into lower life satisfaction. The partial mediation implies that narcissism exerts its deleterious effects on well-being through multiple channels. The behavioral route represented by aggression appears to play a substantial role: narcissism predicts higher aggression, and aggression, in turn, predicts lower life satisfaction. This pattern supports interventions that target aggressive behaviors as a viable approach to mitigating some of the negative consequences of narcissism on well-being. For instance, anger management programs, social skills training, and interventions aimed at increasing empathy could potentially reduce aggressive responses among individuals high in narcissism and thereby improve relationship quality and life satisfaction.

At the same time, the persistence of a direct effect of narcissism on life satisfaction after accounting for aggression suggests that other mechanisms are also operative. These mechanisms could include cognitive styles such as unrealistic self-expectations, perfectionism, social comparison tendencies, and fragile self-esteem that depends on external validation. Narcissistic individuals may also engage in impression management and self-deceptive processes that yield short-term boosts in self-evaluations but longer-term dissatisfaction when underlying needs for authentic connection are unmet. Future research should aim to identify these additional mediators, potentially including constructs such as social support, loneliness, depressive symptoms, and emotion regulation strategies.

The findings also have implications for conceptualizing the heterogeneity within narcissism. The current study utilized a measure geared primarily toward assessing grandiose narcissism. However, the broader literature suggests that vulnerable narcissism, which is characterized by hypersensitivity and internal distress, might display different patterns of association with aggression and life satisfaction. Vulnerable narcissism might more strongly predict internalizing symptoms and low life satisfaction regardless of aggression, whereas grandiose narcissism might be more behaviorally linked to externalizing outcomes such as aggression. Subsequent studies should include measures that distinguish subtypes of narcissism to unpack these differential pathways.

There are several practical implications of the present findings. Clinicians and practitioners who encounter individuals with elevated narcissistic traits might consider including behavioral strategies that reduce aggression and improve interpersonal functioning as part of holistic well-being interventions. Programs that foster perspective-taking, conflict resolution, and adaptive coping with criticism may help to reduce retaliatory behaviors that exact social costs. In organizational and educational settings, promoting norms that discourage entitlement and aggressive responses and that encourage cooperative problem-solving may decrease interpersonal friction and contribute to enhanced life satisfaction for members of those communities.

Despite these contributions, the study has several limitations that warrant consideration when interpreting the results. First, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inferences. Although the mediation analysis suggests a plausible pathway from narcissism to aggression to lower life satisfaction, the temporal ordering of these variables cannot be established with certainty from cross-sectional data. Longitudinal designs are needed to confirm whether increases in narcissistic traits precede increases in aggression and subsequent declines in life satisfaction over time. Second, the reliance on self-report measures raises concerns about shared method variance and response biases. Narcissistic individuals may be more or less likely to endorse certain items in ways that reflect self-enhancement or defensiveness, and self-reports of aggression may not fully capture actual aggressive behavior as observed by others. Future studies could incorporate multi-method assessments, including informant reports, behavioral tasks, and ecological momentary assessment to provide a more comprehensive picture.

Third, the sample size, while adequate for detecting medium-sized effects, limits the precision with which more subtle moderation effects and subgroup analyses can be examined. Replication in larger and more diverse samples would increase confidence in the generalizability of the findings. Fourth, the sample was a convenience sample drawn from online and university-related sources, and it may not be fully representative of the general population in terms of age, socioeconomic status, or cultural background. Cross-cultural studies could clarify whether the observed relationships hold across different societal contexts, given that cultural norms around self-enhancement and aggression vary.

Fifth, the study focused primarily on grandiose aspects of narcissism, and future research should incorporate measures of vulnerable narcissism and other related constructs to understand nuanced differences. Sixth, the interpretation of the negative relationship between narcissism and life satisfaction should be qualified by recognition that some contexts may temporarily reward narcissistic behaviors, leading to short-term gains in certain domains such as leadership or status. Long-term trajectories, however, may differ, and longitudinal work is needed to map these dynamics.

There are several promising avenues for future research that build on the present findings. Longitudinal studies could test the temporal sequencing implied by the mediation model and examine potential reciprocal relations among narcissism, aggression, and life satisfaction. Experimental studies could manipulate perceived ego threats or study responses to social provocation to observe whether reducing aggression in the moment alters subsequent well-being indicators. Intervention studies could test whether specific programs aimed at reducing aggression among narcissistic individuals lead to improvements in life satisfaction and social functioning. Research could also explore moderators that might buffer the negative effects of narcissism on life satisfaction, such as high-quality close relationships, adaptive coping strategies, or environments that channel status-seeking into socially constructive activities.

Another fruitful direction would be to examine biological and neurocognitive correlates of the observed associations. For example, research on physiological reactivity, stress responses, and neural systems involved in reward and social cognition could elucidate the mechanisms linking narcissism and aggressive responses to subjective well-being. Additionally, developmental work that tracks the emergence of narcissistic traits and aggressive behavior across adolescence and into adulthood could provide insight into critical periods for intervention.

In conclusion, the present study provides evidence that narcissistic traits are strongly associated with aggression and that both constructs are linked to lower life satisfaction. Aggression serves as a significant pathway through which narcissism relates to reduced subjective well-being, although narcissism has additional direct effects that merit further investigation. These findings highlight the importance of addressing aggressive behavior and related interpersonal strategies in efforts to improve the life satisfaction of individuals with elevated narcissistic traits. Continued research using longitudinal, multi-method, and intervention designs will be essential to deepen our understanding of these relationships and to guide the development of effective approaches to enhance well-being.
