Introduction

Human beings form intuitive expectations about the physical world from an early age. These expectations often function as heuristics that allow rapid prediction and interpretation of everyday events. One class of intuitive expectations concerns the motion of objects, and people frequently hold strong beliefs about how moving objects will behave when external contact or constraint is removed. A robust and well-documented misconception in this domain is the idea that objects in motion will continue to follow a curved or otherwise altered trajectory after leaving a constraining surface, rather than proceeding in a straight line in accordance with inertial motion. Work in cognitive psychology and science education has repeatedly shown that laypeople’s motion intuitions diverge systematically from Newtonian mechanics. Classic experimental demonstrations have documented that many participants expect an object emerging from a curved tube to follow a curved path rather than a straight, tangent path. This tendency has been framed as an “impetus” or “curvilinear impetus” belief and has been characterized as surprisingly resilient to casual correction. The persistence of such misconceptions has significant implications for both basic cognitive theory and the pedagogy of physics instruction. From a theoretical standpoint, the presence of stable, non-Newtonian intuitions challenges simple models in which perception and reasoning about physical systems are built upon an internalized, veridical physics engine. From an applied standpoint, the misconception complicates efforts to teach and assess Newtonian concepts, since students may superficially understand formal rules yet retain conflicting intuitive beliefs that influence reasoning and behavior.

Foundational work by McCloskey and colleagues articulated and empirically established many of these erroneous intuitions about motion. That program of research presented participants with a variety of stimuli that probed predictions about the continued motion of objects under different releasing conditions and reported strikingly consistent patterns of non-Newtonian responses. The McCloskey findings have been influential because they highlight that intuitive physics is not merely noisy but systematically biased in specific, theory-like ways. These biases have been observed across diverse populations and experimental paradigms, including verbal prediction tasks, drawing tasks, and pointing tasks, suggesting a deep-seated and generalizable pattern. Subsequent research has documented boundary conditions on the effect, including task framing, the richness of visual information, and explicit instruction, but many core results have been replicated. Notably, intervening on participants’ beliefs through brief educational materials has produced mixed outcomes, indicating that single-shot informational prompts may not always induce conceptual change in intuitive theories.

A related set of investigations has attempted to identify the mechanisms that sustain non-Newtonian motion beliefs. Cognitive accounts differ on whether these beliefs arise from perceptual biases, conceptual misunderstandings, memory interference, or pragmatic task interpretations. Some researchers argue that perceptual systems represent motion in a way that privileges curvature continuity, a bias that could make tangent-motion predictions less accessible. Others emphasize the role of naive causal theories in which a moving object is thought to carry an internal “impetus” that continues along the object’s prior path. Still other perspectives posit that misconceptions reflect a combination of limited conceptual resources and task-specific heuristics that are adaptive in most everyday contexts. Despite these competing accounts, a common inference is that simple exposure to a corrective statement or brief explanation might be insufficient to overturn the misconception without deeper engagement or experiential learning.

In light of both the robustness of the curvilinear impetus belief and the mixed success of brief interventions, it is important to examine the extent to which contextual physics information can modulate performance on motion prediction tasks. Contextual physics information here refers to concise, targeted explanatory information that highlights a relevant physical principle—specifically, the Newtonian principle that an object in motion will proceed in a straight line unless forced to change direction by an external force. Providing such information could operate by activating relevant conceptual knowledge, by orienting attention to the pertinent aspect of the stimulus, or by altering the interpretive frame used to answer the task. Importantly, contextual information can vary in depth and format, ranging from a single sentence reminder of the relevant law to an interactive demonstrative experience. The potency of these formats for changing intuitive responses likely differs, and research that systematically varies the nature of the contextual prompt is needed to delineate these differences.

Previous interventions have produced a range of findings. Some studies find small but reliable improvements in Newtonian-consistent responding following explicit instruction or demonstration. Other studies report null effects or transient effects that disappear when participants are subsequently tested on non-identical stimuli. An important moderator appears to be the alignment between the intervention and the task demands; interventions that closely match the perceptual and causal features of the test stimuli tend to produce larger effects. Furthermore, individual differences such as prior physics knowledge, cognitive reflection, and working memory may either facilitate or inhibit the incorporation of contextual information into task performance. Prior knowledge is a particularly salient factor because participants with greater formal physics experience may already possess and spontaneously apply Newtonian rules, whereas novices might require more elaborated support to shift away from their intuitive theories. Hence, when testing the impact of contextual prompts, it is crucial to measure and control for prior physics knowledge to isolate the specific contribution of the manipulation.

The present study sought to replicate and extend the classic McCloskey findings by experimentally manipulating the availability of contextual physics knowledge. Specifically, participants were randomly assigned to either receive a brief contextual prompt that described the Newtonian expectation for motion or to receive no such prompt prior to completing a set of motion prediction trials. The task mirrored the structure of the original work in that stimuli displayed an object emerging from a curved constraining apparatus and participants were required to indicate the subsequent path of motion. The core hypothesis was that participants receiving contextual physics information would produce a higher proportion of Newtonian-consistent responses than participants who did not receive contextual information. This hypothesis was motivated by the assumption that a targeted conceptual reminder would be sufficient to shift initial interpretive framing and activate relevant causal schemas.

Additionally, because prior physics knowledge could either moderate or confound the impact of the contextual prompt, the study included a separate measure of participants’ prior exposure to physics concepts. This measure aimed to quantify participants’ baseline familiarity with Newtonian principles, allowing us to test whether any observed effects of the prompt were independent of existing knowledge. If contextual information had an effect, we predicted that the effect would persist after statistically controlling for prior physics knowledge. Conversely, if prior knowledge accounted for the variance in performance, then the prompt might show reduced or no independent effect.

We also considered potential limitations and alternative outcomes. One possibility is that the prompt would not produce detectable change because the misconception is robust and resistant to brief textual correction. Another possibility is that the contextual information could produce only transient or marginal improvements in specific trial types, leading to a small effect size that would require a large sample to detect. Finally, psychometric or procedural features of the task, such as ceiling or floor effects, ambiguous stimuli, or insufficient engagement with the prompt, could attenuate observable differences. Given these possibilities, the study design incorporated careful stimulus construction and pilot testing to minimize ambiguities and sought to recruit a sample size sufficient to detect small-to-moderate effects.

This replication and extension of McCloskey’s work therefore had both theoretical and practical aims. The theoretical aim was to assess whether a minimal contextual intervention could alter the expression of an entrenched intuitive belief documented in the literature. The practical aim was to inform instructional approaches by determining whether brief contextual cues might be an efficient tool for promoting Newtonian-consistent reasoning in non-expert populations. By randomizing participants and measuring prior knowledge, the study design attempted to provide a clear test of the hypothesis while accounting for important individual differences. The study also included exploratory analyses to examine interactions between condition and prior knowledge, as well as trial-level analyses to detect any differential effects across stimulus types.

In summary, the study addressed the question of whether a short, targeted reminder of Newtonian motion principles could reduce the prevalence of curvilinear impetus responses in a motion prediction task. The research drew directly on prior demonstrations of intuitive motion misconceptions and aimed to clarify the malleability of those intuitions under minimal instructional interventions. Based on theoretical considerations and related empirical findings, the primary prediction was that contextual information would improve performance relative to a no-prompt control condition, and that this advantage would be observable even after accounting for participants’ prior physics knowledge. The experimental procedure, including random assignment, stimulus standardization, and measurement of prior knowledge, was constructed to provide a rigorous evaluation of this hypothesis. The study’s outcomes thus have implications for theories of intuitive physics and for practical strategies aimed at reducing persistent misconceptions in informal learning contexts.

Methods

Participants were recruited from the undergraduate population of a mid-sized university and from an online participant pool to achieve a diverse sample. In total, 132 individuals consented to participate and completed the study. All participants were at least 18 years of age and provided informed consent in accordance with the institution’s human subjects review board. Four participants were excluded because they failed attention checks embedded in the procedure, and two additional participants were excluded for incomplete data, leaving a final sample of 126 participants. The final sample comprised 72 females and 54 males, with ages ranging from 18 to 47 years (M = 20.9, SD = 4.8). Participants self-reported their highest physics course completed, and approximately 18% reported having taken college-level introductory physics, while the remainder reported high school physics or no formal physics coursework.

The experimental design was a between-subjects randomized design with two primary conditions: Contextual Prompt and No Prompt. Assignment was randomized using a computer-generated randomization sequence. The dependent variable was performance on a motion prediction battery consisting of eight trials adapted from classic stimuli used to probe the curvilinear impetus belief. Each trial displayed a static depiction of an object (a ball) moving through a curving tube and then emerging into open space. For each trial, participants were presented with four possible trajectories to choose from: (a) a straight-line tangent trajectory consistent with Newtonian inertia, (b) a curved continuation of the tube’s curvature, (c) a trajectory that initially follows the curve and then straightens, and (d) a non-contiguous trajectory inconsistent with either plausible motion. The primary outcome measure was the number of Newtonian-consistent responses (choice a) across the eight trials, yielding a performance score that ranged from 0 to 8.

Materials included written prompts, visual stimuli, and a brief survey to assess prior physics knowledge. The Contextual Prompt consisted of a single-paragraph informational statement presented immediately before the motion prediction battery. The paragraph read: “When an object is no longer in contact with a surface or guiding structure, it will continue to move in a straight line at constant velocity unless acted upon by an external force. Think about how inertia causes objects to follow a straight path when released from a curved guide.” Participants in the No Prompt condition received no corresponding physics information and proceeded directly to the task. The visual stimuli were grayscale diagrams created to be clear and unambiguous, showing the moment of release with the ball depicted at the tube exit and dotted lines indicating plausible continuation paths. Stimuli were pilot-tested on an independent sample (n = 20) to ensure clarity and to balance trial difficulty.

Prior physics knowledge was measured using a 10-item multiple-choice questionnaire designed to assess conceptual understanding of basic mechanics. Items included questions about motion, forces, and energy that were not identical to the task stimuli but tapped the same underlying principles. Scores on the prior knowledge measure ranged from 0 to 10 and were used both as a descriptive variable and as a covariate in primary analyses. Additional questionnaires collected demographic information and self-reported confidence ratings for each trial, with confidence rated on a 5-point Likert scale.

The procedure began with online consent and demographic questions. Participants were then introduced to the general task and were told that the study examined how people intuitively predict motion; no mention of Newtonian physics or common misconceptions was made to avoid priming. Participants in the Contextual Prompt condition then read the informational paragraph and were asked to indicate that they had understood it before proceeding. The No Prompt group proceeded without this step. All participants then completed the eight trial motion prediction battery presented in randomized order. After each trial, participants made their trajectory choice and provided a confidence rating. Following the battery, participants completed the prior physics knowledge questionnaire and the final attention checks and debriefing.

Scoring was straightforward: one point for each Newtonian-consistent response on the motion battery, yielding an aggregate score from 0 to 8. Confidence ratings were averaged across correct and incorrect responses to provide an index of metacognitive insight. Prior knowledge scores were summed across the ten questionnaire items. Data cleaning procedures removed participants who failed either of two attention checks that asked them to select a specified response or who completed the study in an implausibly short time (< 3 minutes), as noted earlier.

The planned statistical analyses were specified before data inspection. The primary analysis was an independent-samples t-test comparing the mean number of Newtonian-consistent responses between the Contextual Prompt and No Prompt groups. Secondary analyses included an ANCOVA with prior physics knowledge as a covariate to determine whether any group differences remained after accounting for baseline knowledge. Exploratory analyses included logistic mixed-effects modeling at the trial level to examine whether the effect of the prompt varied by specific stimulus characteristics, correlations between prior knowledge and performance, and an interaction test between condition and prior knowledge using multiple regression. Normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions for parametric tests were assessed using visual inspection of residuals, Shapiro-Wilk tests, and Levene’s test, with nonparametric alternatives planned if assumptions were violated.

A power analysis conducted during study planning indicated that with n = 126, the study had approximately 80% power to detect a small-to-medium effect size (d ≈ 0.45) at alpha = .05 for the primary t-test. The analysis plan also included reporting effect sizes (Cohen’s d for t-tests and partial eta-squared for ANCOVAs) and 95% confidence intervals for the primary estimates. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Data and analysis scripts were archived for reproducibility.

Ethical approval was obtained from the university’s institutional review board prior to data collection. Participants received course credit or a small monetary compensation for their participation. The debriefing clarified the aims of the study and provided a brief explanation of the Newtonian principle for participants who had not received the contextual prompt. No deceptive elements were included in the procedure beyond the omission of explicit instruction about the phenomenon under study for control participants, which was necessary to maintain experimental contrast.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary dependent variable and covariates are presented first. The Contextual Prompt group (n = 64) obtained a mean of 3.21 Newtonian-consistent responses out of 8 (SD = 1.58). The No Prompt group (n = 62) obtained a mean of 3.05 Newtonian-consistent responses out of 8 (SD = 1.71). Prior physics knowledge scores showed means of 3.08 (SD = 1.96) for the Contextual Prompt group and 2.92 (SD = 2.05) for the No Prompt group. Descriptive statistics indicated substantial variability in both performance and prior knowledge, and no obvious ceiling or floor effects on the primary outcome.

The primary inferential analysis compared the mean number of Newtonian-consistent responses between the Contextual Prompt and No Prompt groups using an independent-samples t-test. The t-test showed no significant difference between conditions, t(124) = 0.62, p = .54. The observed effect size was small, Cohen’s d = 0.11, and the 95% confidence interval for the mean difference included zero and ranged from -0.40 to 0.72 responses. These results therefore did not support the primary hypothesis that the contextual prompt would increase Newtonian-consistent responding.

Given concerns about potential baseline differences in prior physics knowledge, an ANCOVA was conducted with prior physics knowledge score entered as a covariate. The ANCOVA revealed no significant main effect of Condition after controlling for prior knowledge, F(1,123) = 0.39, p = .54, partial eta-squared = 0.003. Prior physics knowledge showed a significant main effect on performance, F(1,123) = 23.14, p < .001, partial eta-squared = 0.16, indicating that participants with greater prior physics knowledge produced more Newtonian-consistent responses overall. Inspection of adjusted group means from the ANCOVA indicated a negligible adjusted difference between groups (adjusted Mprompt = 3.14, adjusted Mno-prompt = 3.12).

To further probe the relationship between prior knowledge and performance, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted. Prior physics knowledge score correlated moderately with the number of Newtonian-consistent responses, r = .39, p < .001. This association suggested that individual differences in formal physics understanding were an important determinant of task performance independent of the experimental prompt. Confidence ratings were also examined and found to correlate with performance, r = .42, p < .001, indicating that participants tended to be more confident when their responses were Newtonian-consistent.

Bayesian analyses were conducted as a complement to the frequentist tests to quantify evidence in favor of the null hypothesis. A Bayesian t-test comparing the two groups produced a Bayes factor BF01 = 4.1, indicating that the data were approximately four times more likely under the null model (no difference) than under the alternative model. This level of evidence is conventionally interpreted as moderate support for the null hypothesis for the group difference in this sample.

Trial-level analyses using logistic mixed-effects models were performed to determine whether the prompt had differential effects across specific stimuli. The models included random intercepts for participants and trials and fixed effects for Condition, prior knowledge (grand-mean centered), and their interaction. The main effect of Condition was nonsignificant in the mixed model, b = 0.06, SE = 0.09, z = 0.67, p = .50. Prior knowledge was a significant predictor of correct trial responses in the mixed model, b = 0.21, SE = 0.04, z = 5.25, p < .001. The interaction between Condition and prior knowledge was not significant, b = -0.02, SE = 0.06, z = -0.33, p = .74, suggesting that the effect of prior knowledge on performance did not differ by condition.

Assumption checks for parametric analyses showed that residuals from the primary t-test and ANCOVA approximated normality. Shapiro-Wilk tests on the residuals returned non-significant results (p > .05), and Q-Q plots showed only minor deviations from normality. Levene’s test for equality of variances indicated homogeneity, F(1,124) = 0.06, p = .81. These results supported the validity of the parametric tests employed.

An additional exploratory analysis examined whether the prompt affected response confidence separately for correct and incorrect responses. A 2 (Condition) × 2 (Accuracy: correct vs. incorrect) mixed ANOVA on mean confidence ratings showed a main effect of Accuracy, F(1,124) = 95.07, p < .001, such that confidence was higher for correct responses. The main effect of Condition on confidence was not significant, F(1,124) = 0.31, p = .58, and there was no significant Condition × Accuracy interaction, F(1,124) = 0.09, p = .77.

Given the moderate correlation between prior physics knowledge and performance, a multiple regression was run to assess whether Condition added incremental predictive value. The model included Condition (dummy coded), prior physics knowledge, and their interaction as predictors of total Newtonian-consistent responses. Prior knowledge entered the model as a significant predictor (β = 0.38, t = 4.85, p < .001). Condition did not contribute significantly (β = 0.06, t = 0.62, p = .54), and the interaction term was nonsignificant (β = -0.03, t = -0.34, p = .74). The full model explained approximately 16% of the variance in performance (R^2 = .16).

To explore whether any particular subgroup might have benefited from the contextual prompt, analyses were conducted within the subset of participants who reported no formal physics coursework (n = 103). Within this subgroup, the mean number of Newtonian-consistent responses was 3.01 (SD = 1.61) for the Contextual Prompt group and 2.89 (SD = 1.64) for the No Prompt group. The between-group t-test in this subgroup was nonsignificant, t(101) = 0.53, p = .60, and the effect size was small, d = 0.10. Thus, the prompt did not show evidence of benefitting participants without formal physics background in this sample.

Finally, we examined potential floor effects by testing whether the distribution of scores clustered at low values. Although the mean performance was modest (around 3 out of 8), the distribution showed a spread with some participants scoring as high as 7 and others as low as 0. Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests against a uniform distribution were not significant, suggesting no extreme skewing that would preclude detection of between-group differences. Overall, the results consistently indicated that the brief contextual prompt did not enhance Newtonian-consistent responding, while prior physics knowledge reliably predicted better performance.

Discussion

The present study tested whether a brief contextual prompt invoking Newtonian principles would improve participants’ accuracy on a motion prediction task that historically elicits non-Newtonian, curvilinear impetus responses. Contrary to our primary prediction, the contextual prompt did not produce a significant increase in Newtonian-consistent responses. This null effect was observed across multiple analytic approaches, including t-tests, ANCOVA controlling for prior physics knowledge, logistic mixed-effects models at the trial level, and Bayesian analyses which provided moderate evidence favoring the null hypothesis. The most robust predictor of Newtonian-consistent responding was participants’ prior physics knowledge, which showed a moderate positive correlation with task performance and a significant effect in regression and ANCOVA models. Confidence ratings were also associated with accuracy, suggesting that participants were partially aware of the correctness of their responses.

These findings add nuance to the literature on intuitive physics and interventions aimed at correcting misconceptions. First, the results corroborate the robustness of the curvilinear impetus belief by showing that a single brief textual reminder of Newtonian inertia is insufficient to alter the majority of participants’ responses in a typical motion prediction task. This outcome aligns with prior research indicating that simple declarative corrections often fail to produce deep conceptual change in domains where people hold entrenched intuitive theories. The present study therefore reinforces theoretical positions that regard intuitive physics as a configural system of beliefs and perceptual biases that are not easily overridden by minimal verbal prompts.

Second, the strong relationship between prior formal physics knowledge and performance suggests that the acquisition of Newtonian reasoning may require substantive learning experiences rather than momentary reminders. Participants who had higher scores on the prior knowledge questionnaire were more likely to select Newtonian-consistent trajectories, implying that internalized and practiced understanding of mechanics supports accurate predictions in these tasks. This pattern is consistent with instructional research showing that conceptual change often requires opportunities for reflection, practice, and feedback rather than one-off declarative statements.

Third, the null effect of the prompt invites consideration of the nature and sufficiency of the contextual information provided. The prompt in this study was deliberately brief to model an ecologically plausible intervention that might be used in casual learning environments or as a quick instructional nudge. However, brief textual statements may lack the perceptual and causal richness needed to map abstract principles onto specific visual stimuli. The misalignment between abstract verbal content and spatially instantiated visual patterns may impede participants’ ability to translate the prompt into changed predictions. This interpretation is consistent with studies that have found larger intervention effects when participants engage with dynamic demonstrations, hands-on experiments, or guided analogical reasoning that concretely connect principles to perceptual experience.

Fourth, task framing and demand characteristics may influence whether participants apply provided information. In the present study, we did not label the task as a physics test, and participants were not explicitly instructed to apply any particular rule beyond the prompt. It is possible that some participants in the Contextual Prompt condition did not integrate the information because they perceived the prompt as peripheral or irrelevant to the task, or because they relied on their immediate perceptual interpretation of the static images. Future work could manipulate instructions to emphasize applying the prompt actively or include practice items with feedback to encourage integration.

Fifth, the lack of interaction between condition and prior knowledge indicates that the prompt did not selectively help novices or those with lower baseline knowledge. This finding counters a plausible alternative hypothesis that minimal prompts might benefit novices more than those already familiar with the relevant physics. Instead, prior knowledge predicted performance regardless of prompt exposure, suggesting additive rather than multiplicative effects of knowledge and brief contextual cues. This pattern underscores the importance of considering baseline competencies when designing interventions and suggests that low-cost prompts may not be enough to bridge gaps in formal understanding.

Sixth, methodological factors could have contributed to the null result. Although stimuli were pilot-tested for clarity, static depictions may inherently favor perceptual heuristics that encourage curvature continuation. Dynamic stimuli or interactive simulations might reduce reliance on heuristics by providing clearer causal information about velocities and forces. Additionally, the number of trials and the single exposure to the prompt may have limited the opportunity for participants to internalize the information. Training paradigms that incorporate repeated exposure, immediate feedback, and spaced practice are more likely to produce durable conceptual shifts and should be prioritized in follow-up research.

Seventh, the psychometric properties of the prior physics instrument and the motion battery warrant reflection. While the prior knowledge questionnaire correlated with motion performance, the relatively low average scores on both measures suggest that the sample was predominantly novice with respect to formal physics understanding. This sample characteristic may limit the generalizability of findings to populations with greater exposure to physics instruction. Moreover, the moderate internal consistency of the prior knowledge measure (Cronbach’s alpha in the pilot was .71) suggests some measurement error that could attenuate observed relationships; future work should employ validated, widely-used concept inventories to improve measurement precision.

Eighth, the Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis, which is informative given ongoing debates about interpreting null results. The Bayesian perspective helps to quantify the degree of support for the absence of an effect rather than merely failing to reject a null. In this case, the BF01 value greater than 4 suggests that within the bounds of this sample and intervention, the null hypothesis is a better account of the data than a modest positive effect of the prompt. That said, the BF value does not exclude the possibility of smaller effects below the detection threshold of the present sample, nor does it address whether a qualitatively different prompt might be effective.

Ninth, the exploratory analyses revealed no evidence that particular stimuli benefited from the prompt more than others. This uniformity suggests that the lack of an overall effect was not due to heterogeneous task sensitivity across trials. Instead, the consistent null effect across stimuli lends further weight to the conclusion that a single brief conceptual reminder is insufficient to modify the repertoire of responses that participants bring to bear on these motion prediction tasks.

Tenth, practical implications for education follow from these results. Educators should be cautious about reliance on brief declarative statements to correct intuitive misconceptions about motion. Instead, curricula that integrate conceptually coherent explanations with perceptually grounded demonstrations, opportunities for hypothesis testing, and iterative feedback are more likely to effect meaningful change. The current findings highlight that while concise cues may have utility in orienting attention, they should be embedded within richer pedagogical sequences to achieve conceptual restructuring.

Eleventh, future research directions are manifold. First, experiments could compare the efficacy of different intervention formats—textual prompts, animated demonstrations, hands-on manipulations, and interactive simulations—to identify which modalities best support transfer to prediction tasks. Second, longitudinal training studies could test whether extended exposure and distributed practice yield more durable conceptual change compared to single-shot interventions. Third, developmental studies could examine age-related trajectories to determine when Newtonian-consistent reasoning becomes more accessible and how instructional timing influences conceptual consolidation. Fourth, individual differences in cognitive reflection, spatial ability, and epistemic beliefs could be investigated as moderators of intervention efficacy. Fifth, neuroscientific approaches, including neuroimaging or electrophysiological indices, could probe whether interventions modulate perceptual processing, conceptual representation, or decision-stage processes.

Twelfth, limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The sample was composed primarily of undergraduates and online volunteers, which may not reflect broader populations. The prompt’s brevity, while ecologically valid, may not generalize to settings where more elaborate instruction is delivered. Additionally, the study measured immediate effects and did not assess retention or transfer beyond similar tasks. Despite these limitations, the study contributes valuable evidence to the literature on the malleability of intuitive physics and provides a rigorous test of a plausible minimal intervention.

Finally, the pattern of results reinforces the view that intuitive physical beliefs are resilient and closely tied to prior formal knowledge. Simple reminders of physics principles do not appear to override entrenched perceptual and conceptual biases in a single exposure. Consequently, both theorists and educators must account for the depth of experiential and cognitive work required to shift such intuitions. Future research that systematically varies the intensity, modality, and temporal spacing of instructional interventions, while taking into account individual differences, holds promise for identifying efficient and effective methods to promote Newtonian-consistent reasoning in broader populations.
