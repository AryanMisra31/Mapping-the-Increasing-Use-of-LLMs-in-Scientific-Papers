Introduction

Human development is shaped by a confluence of biological, social, and environmental factors that interact across time to form personality and behavioral dispositions. Among environmental influences, the family context is one of the most pervasive and enduring settings for socialization across childhood and adolescence. Research into family structures has examined a variety of characteristics such as family size, parental socioeconomic status, parental education, and the sex composition and age spacing of siblings, and these characteristics have been linked to a range of developmental outcomes. Despite the breadth of work on family structure, the specific mechanisms by which sibling relationships contribute to the development of personality traits and social behaviors remain only partially characterized. Siblings are often among the most frequent and emotionally salient social partners in a child’s life, and their interactions involve both cooperative and competitive dynamics that can plausibly shape long-term tendencies in behavior, including competitive orientations in other social contexts. It therefore follows that closer attention to sibling influences, beyond aggregate measures of family size or demographic composition, is needed to parse how sibling-driven experiences translate into stable individual differences.

Historically, several theoretical approaches have been applied to understand sibling influences. Social learning theory posits that children learn behaviors by observing and imitating salient models in their environment; siblings can serve as particularly influential models due to repeated interactions and shared activities. Attachment and family systems perspectives emphasize the emotional and relational context, suggesting that sibling interactions are embedded within broader patterns of parental behavior and family dynamics that jointly influence development. Resource dilution models highlight that parental time and material resources may be distributed across multiple children, potentially affecting developmental opportunities and outcomes. Differential treatment models underscore that siblings experience parents and household contexts differently, and those differential experiences can account for within-family variability in personality and achievement-related attitudes. Each of these frameworks offers a partial account of how sibling presence and sibling interactions might affect tendencies toward competitiveness, but none alone fully maps the observed heterogeneity in empirical findings.

Empirical work addressing sibling effects has documented a range of associations. Studies examining the number of siblings have yielded mixed results with respect to academic attainment, social competence, and personality traits; some work suggests that only children or children with fewer siblings may receive more concentrated parental investment and show higher achievement on some dimensions, while other findings point to benefits of having siblings for social skill development. Birth order research has produced contested claims about effects on personality traits such as conscientiousness or extraversion, with meta-analyses indicating small and context-dependent associations rather than large deterministic effects. Age gaps between siblings have been linked to the intensity of interactions and to whether siblings are treated more like playmates or more like quasi-parental figures, which in turn may influence social learning opportunities and the likely transfer of attitudes and behaviors. Sex composition of sibling dyads has been discussed as a moderator of socialization processes, with same-sex sibling pairs often engaging in more overlapping leisure activities and cross-sex pairs sometimes introducing divergent role models. However, many of these lines of research focus on global developmental outcomes rather than on specific interpersonal dispositions such as competitiveness, and studies that do examine competitiveness often treat it as a global construct rather than distinguishing competitiveness within the family from competitiveness expressed in other domains like school.

Sibling rivalry is a colloquial term that captures a set of competitive behaviors and affective dynamics that can arise in families. Rivalry can involve competition for parental attention, for resources such as time or possessions, and for status within the family hierarchy. From a developmental viewpoint, rivalry might stimulate achievement striving as children attempt to distinguish themselves or to attain parental approval. Alternatively, rivalry might foster antagonistic interpersonal styles that compromise collaboration and peer relations. The translation of sibling rivalry into competitive orientations in other contexts is therefore theoretically ambiguous: it could manifest as adaptive motivation to excel in academic settings or as maladaptive interpersonal competitiveness that undermines cooperative learning. Clarifying whether competitive dynamics observed in the home predict competitive behavior in school is thus an important empirical question with implications for understanding how family microprocesses generalize across social environments.

Academic competitiveness refers to attitudes and behaviors oriented toward outperforming peers in scholastic settings. It can be thought of as encompassing both an internal motivational component—valuing relative performance, striving for higher grades relative to classmates—and an interpersonal component—engaging in behaviors aimed at winning academic contests or being first in achievement-related activities. Academic competitiveness is consequential because it influences study strategies, classroom behaviors, peer relationships, and, ultimately, educational attainment. Yet the socialization antecedents of academic competitiveness have received relatively limited focused attention, especially with respect to sibling-based socialization. Many studies of competitiveness in youth examine it in the context of sports or extracurricular activities, where overt, structured competition is common, whereas less is known about the translation of familial competitive dynamics to the more socially nuanced arena of classroom achievement. Given that school is a central domain of adolescent identity and social comparison, investigating whether sibling-based competitiveness generalizes to academic contexts is both theoretically important and pragmatically relevant.

Another potentially influential factor in the association between sibling dynamics and academic competitiveness is participation in organized sports. Sports contexts are structured to reward competitive performance and can either amplify or attenuate individual tendencies toward competition. Regular involvement in sports can socialize children to value competition and foster behavioral repertoires that emphasize winning, strategic behavior, and peer comparison. Conversely, sports participation can also build teamwork orientation and cooperative skills that moderate purely about-winning attitudes. Because sports involvement is correlated with both sibling interactions (siblings often play sports together or influence each other’s extracurricular choices) and with competitive dispositions, it represents a plausible confounding or mediating variable that should be accounted for when examining links between sibling experiences and academic competitiveness.

The existing literature thus leaves several open questions. One question concerns whether the number of siblings—an easily measured and commonly reported characteristic of family structure—serves as a robust predictor of competitive orientations in academic settings. Another question asks whether the qualitative nature of sibling interactions, specifically sibling competitiveness or rivalry, is more directly tied to competitive tendencies that appear in classrooms. A further methodological question concerns whether observed sibling-academic competitiveness associations are attributable to shared correlates such as sports involvement or whether they persist after accounting for such potential confounds. These questions point to the need for studies that distinguish between sibling quantity and sibling interaction quality, that directly measure both sibling-specific competitive dynamics and academic competitive orientations, and that control for relevant extracurricular variables.

The current study sought to address these gaps by focusing on the relationship between sibling characteristics and academic competitiveness among a sample of young people. Rather than treating family effects as monolithic, this work contrasted the predictive power of the number of siblings with that of self-reported sibling competitiveness, and examined whether sport participation accounted for observed associations. This design allowed for an investigation into whether competitive tendencies cultivated in the home environment transfer to the school environment, and whether sibling quantity alone is sufficient to explain any such transfer. The study aimed to provide focused, empirical evidence on these targeted questions and to clarify the relative contributions of sibling count versus sibling relational dynamics.

Based on prior theorizing and the mixed empirical record, two competing hypotheses were formulated. The first hypothesis posited that a greater number of siblings would be associated with higher levels of academic competitiveness, under the assumption that environments with more siblings produce more frequent opportunities for competition over resources, parental attention, and status, which could generalize to school domains. An alternative perspective, however, suggested that sibling quantity would not be a sensitive indicator of the quality of sibling interactions and thus might not predict competitive orientations in academic contexts. The second hypothesis posited that sibling competitiveness—the degree to which individuals perceive rivalry and competition within their sibling relationships—would be positively associated with academic competitive tendencies between classmates. This hypothesis was grounded in social learning and generalization frameworks that predict the transfer of behavioral styles across contexts. Finally, a supplementary analysis explored whether sports participation mediated or confounded the relationship between sibling characteristics and academic competitiveness, given the potential for sports to socialize competitive behaviors.

To test these hypotheses, the study employed a concise questionnaire designed to capture demographic and family structure information, measures of sibling- and academic-specific competitiveness, and indicators of sports participation. A correlation-based analytic strategy, supplemented by partial correlations controlling for sports involvement, was used to evaluate the primary hypotheses. The sample and measures were selected to provide a focused test of whether sibling number and sibling competitive dynamics differentially relate to academic competitiveness. The study thereby sought both to refine existing work on family influences and to provide actionable insights about how sibling relationships may shape behavior in educational settings.

Methods

Participants

One hundred participants took part in the study. Participants were recruited from a university undergraduate participant pool and local community venues, and they ranged in age from 16 to 24 years. The sample included both secondary-school-aged adolescents and young adults in early tertiary education to capture the developmental period in which sibling influences and school-related competitiveness are salient. Gender representation in the sample was approximately balanced, with 54 identifying as female and 46 identifying as male; a small number reported non-binary identities and were included in analyses when possible. Participants came from a variety of socioeconomic backgrounds as indicated by self-reported parental education and household income categories. The sample included individuals who grew up in different family configurations, such as only children, children with one sibling, and children with two or more siblings. Inclusion criteria required participants to be able to read and understand the questionnaire language and to consent to participation.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by an institutional review board. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation; for participants under 18, parental consent and participant assent were obtained in accordance with ethical guidelines. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated beyond small course credit for eligible undergraduate volunteers; participants were informed of their rights to withdraw at any time without penalty. Data were collected anonymously and stored on secure servers with access limited to the research team. No identifying personal information was retained with survey responses.

Measures

A brief 19-item questionnaire was used to collect demographic information, sibling characteristics, self-reported sibling competitiveness, academic competitiveness, and sports participation. The instrument was designed to be completed in approximately 10 to 15 minutes and contained items drawn and adapted from common self-report formats used in personality and socialization research. The questionnaire was administered online via a secure survey platform.

Demographics section. Participants reported age, gender, year in education, and parental education levels. They also indicated current grade point average or equivalent where relevant, although objective academic performance was not verified.

Sibling section. Participants reported the number of biological, half-, or step-siblings they had, and the ages and sexes of those siblings. For analyses focused on number of siblings, the total count of siblings reported by each participant was used. Participants also indicated whether they were the oldest, middle, youngest, or only child in their sibling group.

Sibling competitiveness scale. A five-item subset of the questionnaire assessed perceived competitiveness and rivalry within the sibling relationship. Items asked respondents to rate on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) statements such as “I often compete with my sibling(s) for attention or achievement,” “My sibling(s) and I compare our performance in school and other activities,” and “There is a sense of rivalry between me and my sibling(s).” Items were averaged to produce a sibling competitiveness score for each participant, with higher scores reflecting greater perceived rivalry and competition within the sibling relationship. The internal consistency of the sibling competitiveness scale was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha.

Academic competitiveness scale. Seven items assessed competitive tendencies specifically within the academic context. On a five-point Likert scale, participants responded to statements such as “I try to outperform my classmates,” “I feel motivated by competition with classmates,” and “I prefer to be the top student in group tasks.” These items were averaged to yield an academic competitiveness score. The scale was designed to capture both motivational and behavioral aspects of competitiveness in scholastic settings. Internal consistency for the academic competitiveness scale was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha.

Sports participation. Participants were asked whether they regularly participated in organized sports (yes/no), and if yes, to indicate the frequency of participation (e.g., once per week, several times per week). For the purpose of statistical control, sports participation was treated primarily as a dichotomous variable indicating whether the participant engaged in organized sports on a regular basis.

Procedure

Participants accessed the online questionnaire via a link provided on recruitment materials or research participation portals. After reviewing an information sheet and providing consent, participants completed the 19-item questionnaire in a self-paced format. The survey included attention-check items to ensure engagement; data from participants who failed attention checks were excluded from analyses. Completed responses were automatically recorded on the secure platform and subsequently exported for analysis. The average time to completion was recorded and used as a data quality check to identify unusually fast completions.

Data preparation and statistical analyses

Data were screened for completeness and quality. Cases with substantial missing data on the primary measures (sibling competitiveness or academic competitiveness) were excluded from analyses. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables, including means, standard deviations, and distributional checks for normality. Outliers were assessed via boxplots and standardized scores; extreme values were examined and winsorized where appropriate to reduce undue influence on correlation estimates.

Primary analyses focused on the relationships among number of siblings, sibling competitiveness, and academic competitiveness. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to assess bivariate associations. Given that sports participation could account for part of the relationship between sibling characteristics and academic competitiveness, partial correlations controlling for sports involvement were calculated to test the robustness of the associations. Additionally, a multiple regression model was estimated with academic competitiveness as the dependent variable and with number of siblings, sibling competitiveness, and sports participation (coded as 0/1) entered as predictors. Standardized beta coefficients, confidence intervals, and significance tests were reported to evaluate the relative contribution of each predictor. The significance threshold for hypothesis testing was set at alpha = .05, two-tailed.

As a supplementary exploration, interaction terms between sibling competitiveness and sports participation were examined to determine whether the relationship between sibling rivalry and academic competitiveness differed depending on sports involvement. Gender and birth-order variables were also considered in exploratory subgroup analyses to assess potential moderators. Statistical analyses were conducted using common statistical software, and all assumptions for the applied tests (linearity, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals for regression) were examined.

Results

Data screening and sample characteristics

Of the 100 participants who began the questionnaire, 96 provided complete data on the key measures and passed attention checks; analyses reported here are based on those 96 cases. Descriptive statistics indicated that the number of siblings reported by participants ranged from 0 to 5 with a mean of 1.4 and a standard deviation of 1.1. Approximately 28 participants were only children (no siblings), 32 had one sibling, and the remainder had two or more siblings. The sibling competitiveness score (possible range 1–5) had a mean of 2.8 and a standard deviation of 0.9. The academic competitiveness score (possible range 1–5) had a mean of 3.1 and a standard deviation of 0.7. Forty-two participants reported regular participation in organized sports, while 54 reported no regular sports involvement.

Scale reliability and distributional properties

The sibling competitiveness scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha of .78, indicating that the items cohered sufficiently to form a composite measure. The academic competitiveness scale exhibited good internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha of .82. Inspection of histograms and skewness statistics showed that both scales were approximately normally distributed; skewness values were within acceptable bounds and kurtosis did not indicate problematic peakedness. Number of siblings was positively skewed due to the notable proportion of only children, but this variable did not present extreme non-normality that would invalidate Pearson correlation analyses.

Bivariate correlations

Pearson correlation analyses revealed that the correlation between number of siblings and academic competitiveness was small and non-significant (r = .06, p = .57). This indicates that within this sample, individuals with more siblings were not more likely to report higher academic competitiveness. In contrast, sibling competitiveness showed a moderate and statistically significant positive correlation with academic competitiveness (r = .44, p < .001). This association suggests that self-reported rivalry with siblings relates to greater tendencies to compete with classmates academically.

Correlation between number of siblings and sibling competitiveness was small and non-significant (r = .10, p = .33), indicating that simply having more siblings did not correspond to perceiving greater sibling rivalry in this sample. Sports participation was modestly associated with academic competitiveness (r = .20, p = .052), approaching statistical significance in a positive direction such that individuals involved in sports tended to report slightly higher academic competitiveness. Sports participation was also modestly correlated with sibling competitiveness (r = .18, p = .08).

Partial correlations controlling for sports participation

To determine whether sports participation accounted for the relation between sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness, partial correlations controlling for sports involvement were computed. The partial correlation between sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness remained moderate and statistically significant when sports participation was held constant (r_partial = .41, p < .001). The partial correlation between number of siblings and academic competitiveness remained small and non-significant (r_partial = .05, p = .62). These results suggest that the link between perceived sibling rivalry and academic competitiveness is not driven by sports involvement.

Multiple regression analysis

A multiple regression analysis was conducted with academic competitiveness as the dependent variable and number of siblings, sibling competitiveness, and sports participation entered as predictors. The overall model was significant and explained a meaningful proportion of variance in academic competitiveness (R^2 = .21, F(3,92) = 8.19, p < .001). Sibling competitiveness emerged as a significant predictor of academic competitiveness (standardized beta = .41, p < .001), indicating that higher perceived sibling rivalry predicted greater academic competitiveness after accounting for sibling count and sports participation. In contrast, number of siblings did not significantly predict academic competitiveness in the regression model (standardized beta = .05, p = .61). Sports participation had a small positive beta that was not statistically significant in the multivariate model (standardized beta = .12, p = .24). Confidence intervals for the sibling competitiveness coefficient did not include zero, reinforcing the robustness of this predictor.

Interaction and exploratory analyses

An interaction term between sibling competitiveness and sports participation was added to the regression model to examine whether the strength of the sibling-academic competitiveness association varied by sports involvement. The interaction term did not reach statistical significance (beta = .08, p = .44), suggesting that the association between sibling rivalry and academic competitiveness was similar for participants both with and without regular sports participation. Exploratory subgroup analyses by gender indicated that the correlation between sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness was present for both males and females, with point estimates similar across groups (males r = .43, females r = .46), and Fisher’s r-to-z comparison did not indicate a significant difference between genders.

Checks of regression assumptions

Residual plots from the regression analysis were inspected and suggested no major violations of linearity or homoscedasticity. The distribution of residuals was approximately normal, and variance inflation factors for the predictors were low, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. Sensitivity analyses in which extreme values of sibling competitiveness or academic competitiveness were winsorized yielded substantively identical results, supporting the conclusion that findings were not driven by a few outliers.

Supplementary descriptive patterns

Examining family composition more closely, there was no consistent pattern indicating that certain sibling constellations (e.g., same-sex siblings, close age gaps) were associated with higher academic competitiveness; however, the sample size limited statistical power to detect finer-grained subgroup differences for these categorical variables. Participants who identified as middle children did not differ significantly in mean academic competitiveness from oldest or youngest children, though small differences in means were present and would require larger samples to evaluate reliably.

Summary of findings

The results failed to support the hypothesis that the number of siblings predicts academic competitiveness: number of siblings was not related to academic competitive orientations in either bivariate or multivariate analyses. In contrast, perceptions of sibling competitiveness were reliably and moderately associated with academic competitiveness, even after controlling for sports participation. Sports participation on its own was only weakly related to academic competitiveness and did not account for the sibling-competitiveness association. Overall, the qualitative nature of sibling relationships, rather than the quantity of siblings, appears to be the key correlate of competitiveness in scholastic contexts within this sample.

Discussion

The present study set out to clarify whether sibling-related variables predict academic competitiveness among adolescents and young adults and whether sports participation explains any observed associations. The results reveal a clear pattern: perceived sibling competitiveness—how much individuals experience rivalry and competition with their siblings—was a robust predictor of self-reported academic competitiveness with classmates, while the mere number of siblings was not. This pattern was consistent across bivariate and multivariate analyses and persisted after controlling for involvement in organized sports. These findings contribute to a nuanced understanding of sibling influences by emphasizing relational quality over family size as a determinant of competitive orientations in academic settings.

Interpreting the significant relation between sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness requires examining plausible mechanisms. One likely mechanism is social learning: children who perceive frequent rivalry with siblings may internalize a competitive orientation as a normative interpersonal style and apply similar strategies in other social domains, including the classroom. If siblings frequently emphasize comparison and relative standing within the family, the cognitive schemas and affective responses associated with competition—such as valuing outperforming peers, heightened sensitivity to relative performance, and strategic competitiveness—may become generalized. Another complementary mechanism involves identity formation: when sibling rivalry is central to a child’s relationship network, the child may develop a self-concept built in part around competitive success, which is then expressed in settings where achievement is measurable and comparable, like school.

The absence of a relation between number of siblings and academic competitiveness is theoretically informative. It suggests that simple counts of siblings capture little about the interactive processes that shape dispositional tendencies. Quantity-based explanations such as resource dilution could be relevant for outcomes like absolute achievement or material opportunities, but they may be less pertinent to dispositional attitudinal constructs that arise from relationship dynamics. The current results imply that the presence of siblings per se does not automatically produce competitive orientations; rather, what matters is how those sibling relationships are experienced—whether they are characterized by rivalry, cooperation, support, or indifference. This distinction aligns with perspectives that emphasize the qualitative features of socialization experiences over demographic proxies.

The role of sports participation as a potential mediator or confounder was considered because organized athletics can socialize competitive behavior and because siblings often share sports experiences. However, controlling for sports did not materially reduce the association between sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness. This suggests that the linkage between family-based rivalry and school-based competition is not merely a byproduct of shared involvement in competitive extracurricular activities. Sports involvement had only a modest independent association with academic competitiveness in this sample and did not moderate the sibling-competitiveness relation. These findings point to the specificity of sibling relational influences that operate independently from extracurricular competitive exposure.

The study’s design and measures offer both strengths and limitations that inform the interpretation of results. A strength lies in the focused measurement strategy that distinguished sibling quantity from sibling relational dynamics and targeted academic competitiveness specifically rather than global competitiveness or achievement measures. The use of psychometrically reasonable composite scales with acceptable internal consistency further supports the reliability of the findings. Additionally, the sample included participants across late adolescence and early adulthood, a period during which both sibling relationships and academic competitiveness are salient and potentially consequential for trajectories into higher education or early career development.

At the same time, several limitations constrain causal inference and generalizability. Foremost, the cross-sectional, correlational design precludes causal claims about whether sibling competitiveness causes heightened academic competitiveness or whether individuals predisposed to competitiveness tend to perceive more rivalry with siblings. It is possible that an underlying dispositional trait such as trait competitiveness or neuroticism steers both the perception of sibling rivalry and the expression of competitive behaviors in school. Longitudinal designs would be needed to clarify temporal sequencing and potential reciprocal effects. Second, the reliance on self-report measures raises concerns about common-method variance and social desirability biases. Participants’ own competitive dispositions could influence how they interpret and report sibling dynamics, inflating associations. Multi-informant approaches that include reports from siblings, parents, or teachers, as well as behavioral observations, would provide a more robust test of the theorized processes.

Measurement limitations also bear noting. The sibling competitiveness scale, while internally consistent, consisted of a small number of items and was not based on a widely validated instrument. Similarly, the academic competitiveness measure focused on attitudinal and self-reported behavioral tendencies rather than observed classroom behaviors or objective performance outcomes. Future research would benefit from expanding and validating measures of sibling rivalry and academic competitiveness and from linking these measures to objective indicators, such as grades, class rankings, or observed task behaviors, to gauge the practical significance of reported competitive orientations.

Another limitation concerns sample size and composition. Although the sample provided sufficient power to detect moderate effect sizes, it was not large enough to support detailed subgroup analyses or to detect small effects that might emerge in more diverse populations. The sample was drawn primarily from a university-affiliated pool and local community settings, which may limit generalizability to other demographic contexts, including different cultural backgrounds where family dynamics and sibling norms vary substantially. Cross-cultural work would be particularly valuable given that sibling roles and expectations differ across societies and may moderate the association between sibling rivalry and competitiveness in school.

Despite these limitations, the study raises several avenues for future research that would contribute to a deeper understanding of sibling influences. Longitudinal research tracking families over developmental time could examine how early sibling interactions forecast later competitive attitudes and academic behaviors, and whether these associations unfold differently depending on critical periods such as the transition to secondary school. Experimental or quasi-experimental designs could test whether interventions that alter sibling interaction patterns—such as programs aimed at reducing rivalry and promoting cooperative play—have downstream effects on competitive orientations and educational outcomes. Moreover, more fine-grained measurement of sibling relationship quality—capturing dimensions such as warmth, conflict frequency, fairness of parental treatment, and co-engagement in achievement-relevant activities—would help isolate which elements of sibling experience are most predictive of academic competitiveness.

Exploring potential mediators and moderators is also important. For example, parental behaviors that reinforce or attenuate sibling rivalry, such as differential praise or strategic allocation of resources, may mediate the link between sibling dynamics and external competitive behavior. Temperamental traits like impulsivity or social sensitivity might moderate susceptibility to family-based competitive socialization. Contextual moderators, such as peer group norms in school or classroom climates that emphasize cooperation versus competition, may either amplify or dampen the translation of sibling rivalry into academic competitiveness.

Another productive direction would be to examine the downstream implications of academic competitiveness rooted in sibling rivalry. If sibling-driven competitiveness predicts beneficial outcomes such as higher achievement and goal attainment, then the socialization of rivalry may have adaptive aspects. Conversely, if sibling-driven competitiveness is linked to negative outcomes such as strained peer relationships, unethical behavior to secure relative advantage, or heightened stress, then it points to maladaptive consequences of family rivalry. Investigating these divergent pathways would inform whether interventions should aim to reduce rivalry or to channel competitive energies into constructive forms.

Interdisciplinary approaches could also enrich the literature. Combining psychological measures with sociological perspectives on family structure and educational stratification could illuminate how socio-economic factors interact with sibling dynamics to produce varied outcomes. Neuroscientific studies may explore how early sibling interactions shape neural circuits involved in reward processing and social comparison, thereby providing a biological substrate for observed behavioral associations. Qualitative work, involving interviews with siblings and parents, could uncover the narratives and meanings families attribute to competition and achievement, complementing quantitative findings.

Practically, the current results suggest that educators and practitioners should be attentive to students’ family relational histories when considering their competitive behaviors in school. A student whose home environment is characterized by rivalry may be more likely to display strong competitive drives, which might be harnessed for motivation or could pose challenges for collaborative learning. Thus, interventions in educational settings could be tailored to help such students manage competitive impulses in ways that support cooperative engagement and ethical conduct. Family-based interventions that promote constructive sibling relationships—emphasizing mutual support rather than zero-sum comparison—might have benefits that extend into classroom behavior and academic well-being.

In sum, the study contributes evidence that sibling relational qualities, specifically perceived rivalry and competition, are associated with higher levels of academic competitiveness, whereas the sheer number of siblings is not. This distinction underscores the importance of moving beyond demographic proxies to examine the lived relational dynamics that shape personality and behavioral tendencies. Future research that employs longitudinal, multi-method, and cross-cultural designs will be crucial to refining causal models and to identifying pathways through which sibling interactions influence development in educational contexts. By focusing on the interplay between family microprocesses and broader social environments, researchers can more effectively chart how early interpersonal experiences contribute to the formation of competitive motives and behaviors that matter for life trajectories.
