Introduction

Decisions under risk are ubiquitous in daily life and have been the subject of extensive theoretical and empirical investigation across psychology, economics, and neuroscience. A central question in this literature concerns how people process probabilistic information and how the mode of information acquisition influences choice. Research over the past decades has revealed systematic differences between choices made when probabilities are explicitly described and choices made when probabilities must be learned through sampling experience. These differences are sometimes large and robust enough to be called the description–experience gap. The description–experience gap has been reported across many tasks and contexts and, when characterized, usually points toward a pattern in which rare events are overweighted when described explicitly and underweighted when learned about through experience. The theoretical implications of such a gap are consequential because they suggest that the same nominal gamble may be valued very differently depending only on how information is presented or acquired, which challenges unitary models of choice under risk.

Early empirical demonstrations of differences between description and experience suggested that people treat probabilities differently depending on whether they are given numerically or must be inferred through sampling. In description tasks, participants are typically presented with a choice between options described by explicit probabilities and outcomes, such as "A: 1% chance to win $100, otherwise nothing; B: sure $1." Under such descriptions, many participants choose the option that prioritizes the rare large payoff, a pattern that has been interpreted as overweighting of small probabilities. In experience-based tasks, participants are typically allowed to learn about the gamble by drawing samples from the outcome distribution; when asked to choose after such sampling, participants often underweight the rare large payoff, instead favoring safer alternatives. The contrast between these patterns has been reported in multiple experimental paradigm variants, including repeated sampling procedures, partial-feedback learning, and single-shot choices after sampling phases.

Several theoretical frameworks have been developed to account for the description–experience gap. One strand of explanation emphasizes memory and sampling limitations in experience-based tasks: because rare events occur infrequently in limited samples, participants may simply fail to observe them often enough for their likelihood to be represented accurately, leading to underweighting. This sampling explanation predicts that increasing the number of samples or otherwise improving access to past samples should reduce or eliminate the underweighting seen in experience tasks. Another strand emphasizes differences in the cognitive and affective processes engaged by the two types of tasks. For instance, descriptions provide explicit numerical information that invites deliberate analytical processing, whereas experience-based learning may encourage reliance on retrieval of exemplars or affective reactions to vivid samples. This latter set of accounts is often framed in terms of dual-process theories of cognition, which posit the existence of two complementary systems: a fast, associative, and intuitive system (often labeled System 1) and a slower, analytic, and deliberative system (System 2). Under a dual-process framing, description tasks may engage System 2 to a greater extent, leading to more normative integration of probabilities or, paradoxically, to overweighting of small probabilities because of the salience of the specified rare outcome. Experience tasks may rely more on System 1 and on sampled exemplars or recency-weighted memories, yielding underweighting of rare events when they are underrepresented in experience.

Despite an appealing logic, empirical attempts to attribute the description–experience gap to dual-process differences have produced mixed results. Some studies have attempted to manipulate deliberative capacity directly, for instance by imposing a concurrent cognitive load, or by time pressure, in an attempt to shift processing toward or away from deliberative modes. If the dual-process hypothesis is correct and the gap is driven by differential engagement of System 2, then imposing cognitive load should attenuate the difference in choices between description and experience by impairing System 2 processing where it would normally operate. For example, when participants are under cognitive load, choices in description conditions might come to resemble choices in experience conditions because the analytic machinery that amplifies the salience of rare described outcomes is compromised. Conversely, reducing analytic engagement in experience tasks might exacerbate underweighting of rare events. However, empirical tests have not produced a conclusive pattern: some experiments report that load increases reliance on the risky rare-outcome option in description tasks, others find null effects, and still others find that load affects experience and description conditions similarly or in unexpected ways.

Another important factor that complicates the interpretation of previous findings is heterogeneity across tasks and participants. The description–experience gap is not uniform across all types of gambles: it tends to be most pronounced when the rare event has a large payoff and a very small probability, whereas for moderate probabilities or symmetric gambles the gap may vanish. Individual differences in numeracy, working memory capacity, or risk attitudes may moderate whether and how cognitive load affects decision processes. Methodological differences across studies—such as the number of samples allowed in experience conditions, the exact nature of the load manipulation, whether incentives are real or hypothetical, and whether participants receive feedback about realized outcomes—also vary and may contribute to inconsistent findings.

Given the mixed empirical evidence and the unresolved theoretical debate, replication and systematic testing are necessary. In particular, replicating classic demonstrations of the description–experience gap while simultaneously manipulating deliberative capacity would provide a direct test of whether dual-process mechanisms account for the gap. One influential study reported that participants overweighted rare events in description conditions and underweighted them in experience conditions across a set of choice problems; however, that study did not strongly manipulate cognitive load to test the dual-process account directly. A careful replication that follows previous task structures and extends the design with a robust cognitive load manipulation could therefore clarify whether differences in information processing systems explain the observed gap.

The current study had two primary aims. First, it aimed to replicate the classic finding that decisions from description lead to overweighting of rare events whereas decisions from experience lead to underweighting of those same events. To address this aim, participants completed a series of risky choice tasks that have been used in prior description–experience comparisons. The tasks were selected to include situations where a rare large payoff is pitted against a safer alternative. Participants either received numerical descriptions of the gambles or learned about them through a sampling phase. Second, and crucially, the study aimed to test whether differences in cognitive processing underlie the description–experience gap by manipulating cognitive load. The hypothesis motivated by dual-process theory was straightforward: if the gap arises because description-based choices draw more on deliberative System 2 processes than experience-based choices, then reducing deliberative capacity with a concurrent cognitive load should reduce the gap. Specifically, imposing load should make choices in the description condition more like those in the experience condition, evidenced by a reduced tendency to choose rare-payoff options under description. By contrast, if the gap is driven primarily by sampling artifacts or other factors independent of deliberative resources, then the cognitive load manipulation should have little effect on the gap.

To make these hypotheses concrete, we formulated the following predictions. Prediction 1: across the six choice problems, we expected to observe overweighting of rare outcomes in description conditions such that participants would choose rare-high-payoff options more often in description relative to experience. Prediction 2: we expected to observe underweighting of rare outcomes in experience conditions, such that choices would favor safer alternatives relative to description. Prediction 3: if dual-process differences explain the gap, then imposing cognitive load should reduce the difference between description and experience by reducing the propensity to choose rare-high-payoff options in the description condition, whereas load should have smaller effects in the experience condition. We preregistered a set of analyses to test these predictions that included both choice-based analyses and parametric fits of probability weighting functions to capture underlying changes in probability distortion.

The current study builds on prior work by combining a direct replication approach with a targeted cognitive manipulation, and it does so in a sufficiently powered sample to detect medium-sized interactions between information format and cognitive load. By examining multiple choice problems, fitting computational models to capture probability weighting, and performing robustness checks, we aimed to clarify whether dual-process explanations account for the description–experience gap or whether alternative mechanisms are more plausible. This investigation is an important step toward resolving a theoretically central question in decision research: whether the way information is acquired fundamentally changes the cognitive system engaged in choice and thus the probabilistic representations that guide behavior.

Methods

Participants and power considerations
Participants were recruited online through a subject pool for paid experimental tasks. Our target sample size was 240 participants, which was selected based on a conservative power analysis designed to detect medium-sized effects (Cohen's d ≈ 0.5) in between-subject interactions with 80% power at alpha = .05. The design included a between-subject manipulation of information format (Description vs Experience) and a between-subject manipulation of cognitive load (Load vs No-load), producing four groups with approximately 60 participants each. We anticipated small attrition from attention check failures and exclusion criteria and therefore oversampled slightly. Participants provided informed consent electronically prior to participation. The study protocol adhered to ethical guidelines for human subjects research.

Design overview
The experiment used a 2 × 2 between-subjects design with information format (Description vs Experience) and cognitive load (Load vs No-load) as factors. Each participant completed six distinct choice problems presented in randomized order. Each choice problem involved a binary choice between an option with a rare large payoff and an alternative safer option with a smaller but more probable payoff. The tasks were chosen to reflect a range of probabilities and payoff magnitudes to probe whether the description–experience gap replicates across different gamble structures.

Materials: choice problems
Six gambles were constructed to be similar in spirit to those used in influential previous studies comparing description and experience. Each gamble had one option (Option R for rare) that offered a large payoff with a small probability p and a small payoff otherwise. The other option (Option S for safe) offered a certain or highly probable modest payoff. For example, one problem presented Option R as a 1% chance to win $50 and 99% chance to win $0, whereas Option S offered a sure $0.50. Payoffs and probabilities were presented in the description condition exactly as described numerically. The six gambles spanned small p values (1% and 5%), moderate p values (10%), and varied payoff magnitudes to ensure heterogeneity.

Experience sampling procedure
Participants assigned to the experience condition learned about each gamble by drawing samples from the underlying payoff distribution. For each gamble, participants were allowed to draw up to 100 samples, where each sample revealed an outcome from the gamble’s true distribution. Samples were drawn sequentially using a random number generator controlled by the experimental software, and outcomes were displayed for 750 ms each. Participants could stop sampling at any time and then make their choice between the two options. The rationale for allowing up to 100 samples was to provide ample opportunity to encounter rare events without making the sampling phase tedious; pilot testing indicated that most participants sampled between 20 and 60 draws on average. The realized number of observed rare outcomes was recorded for each participant and each gamble to allow post hoc analyses of sampling exposure.

Description condition procedure
Participants in the description condition were presented with the same gambles as those in the experience condition, but all probabilities and payoffs were provided explicitly in textual and numerical form. For example, the description might state: "Option R: 1% chance to win $50, 99% chance to win $0. Option S: 100% chance to win $0.50." Participants were given as much time as they wanted to read the descriptions before making their choice. To make the choice context comparable to the experience condition, the description condition included an information-check phase in which participants answered comprehension questions about the presented probabilities; failure on these checks resulted in exclusion.

Cognitive load manipulation
The cognitive load manipulation was implemented as a concurrent working memory task administered throughout the block of six choice problems. In the Load condition, participants were presented with an eight-digit number at the beginning of each trial and were instructed to retain it in memory while they completed the sampling (if in experience condition) or the choice (if in description condition). At the end of the trial, participants were asked to type the number from memory; accuracy was incentivized with a small bonus. In the No-load condition, participants were not given any number to remember and did not perform a secondary memory task. The eight-digit retention task was chosen because it is commonly used to occupy working memory resources and is effective in reducing available capacity for deliberative processing. The memory task was active on every trial in the Load condition to maintain consistent load throughout the experiment.

Incentives and payoff procedure
To promote incentive-compatible choices, participants were informed that one of their choices would be randomly selected at the end of the experiment and realized for actual payment. For participants in the experience condition, at the end of the experiment the selected trial’s underlying probability distribution was used to generate a single lottery draw that determined the bonus payment. For participants in the description condition, the realized payoff was determined by a lottery draw consistent with the described probabilities. The base payment for participation was provided regardless of choices, and bonus payments ranged from $0 to $50 depending on the realized outcome in the selected trial. The expected bonus values were explained to participants in advance.

Procedure and task flow
After consent and demographic questions, participants were randomly assigned to one of the four groups. They then completed practice trials to familiarize themselves with either the sampling interface (experience) or the description interface. Practice trials included examples of both safe and risky options and, for those in the Load condition, included the number-retention task. After practice, participants completed the six main choice problems in randomized order. For each problem, participants in the experience condition could sample outcomes up to 100 times, while those in the description condition reviewed the numerical probabilities and payoffs. After each decision, participants in the Load condition were prompted to enter the retained eight-digit number before proceeding. At the end of the experiment, one trial was randomly selected for bonus payment, and participants completed a brief post-task questionnaire that included measures of numeracy, self-reported strategies, and perceived difficulty of the tasks. Attention checks and the comprehension questions were embedded to ensure data quality.

Exclusions and data preprocessing
Participants who failed comprehension questions in the description condition or who failed to recall the eight-digit number above chance in the Load condition were not automatically excluded, but failure rates were used as manipulation checks. Participants who failed a combination of attention checks or who completed the experiment in an implausibly short time (less than one-third of the median completion time) were excluded from analyses. In addition, participants who never sampled in the experience condition (i.e., chose zero samples across all gambles) were excluded because no information would have been available to inform their choices in those trials. The final analytic sample after exclusions was reported in the results section.

Analytic strategy
Our primary dependent variable was choice: for each trial, whether the participant chose the rare-payoff option (Option R) or the safer alternative (Option S). We analyzed choices using mixed-effects logistic regression models that included fixed effects for information format, cognitive load, gamble, and their interactions, and random intercepts for participants. To assess probability weighting more directly, we fit a simple parametric model of choice grounded in cumulative prospect theory with a one-parameter Prelec weighting function for each participant, estimating a probability weighting parameter separately for the description and experience conditions. Group-level comparisons of estimated parameters were performed using t-tests and nonparametric robustness checks. Where appropriate, we supplemented inferential tests with equivalence tests to assess evidence for absence of effects for the cognitive load manipulation.

Results

Sample and exclusions
A total of 242 participants completed the experiment. After applying exclusion criteria for attention check failure, implausibly fast completion times, and zero sampling in the experience condition, 224 participants remained in the analytic sample. These participants were distributed across the four experimental groups with 56 in each cell of the 2 × 2 design. Demographic characteristics were similar across groups, with an average age of 32.1 years (SD = 10.4) and 54% identifying as female. Self-reported numeracy scores and baseline risk-taking measures did not differ significantly across groups, suggesting successful random assignment.

Manipulation checks
The cognitive load manipulation was effective in occupying working memory as intended. Participants in the Load condition recalled the eight-digit number correctly on 67% of trials on average, which was substantially above chance but below perfect performance, indicating an effortful memory load. Participants in the No-load condition had no numbers to recall and thus had no analogous metric. Self-reported difficulty ratings confirmed that participants found the Load condition more demanding than the No-load condition (mean difficulty rating in Load = 5.1 on a 7-point scale vs 2.3 in No-load; t(222) = 18.3, p < .001). In the description condition, comprehension checks indicated high levels of understanding: 94% of comprehension questions were answered correctly on average. In the experience condition, the mean number of samples drawn per gamble was 38.7 (SD = 19.4), and the range was 2 to 100, indicating variability in sampling strategies across participants.

Descriptive choice patterns
We first examined raw choice proportions for the rare-payoff option (Option R) across formats and load conditions for each of the six gambles. Averaging across all six gambles, the overall probability of choosing the rare option was 0.43 in the description condition and 0.36 in the experience condition, a difference of 0.07. However, this aggregate difference masked heterogeneity across gambles. For three of the six gambles—those with the smallest rare-event probability and largest payoff (Gambles 1, 2, and 3)—the proportion choosing Option R was meaningfully higher in the description condition relative to the experience condition. For the other three gambles (Gambles 4, 5, and 6), choice proportions were similar across formats. The descriptive pattern was consistent with partial replication of the canonical description–experience gap, concentrated in a subset of choice problems.

Mixed-effects logistic regression
We next fit a mixed-effects logistic regression predicting choice of Option R with fixed effects for format (Description = 1, Experience = 0), cognitive load (Load = 1, No-load = 0), gamble indicators, and the critical Format × Load interaction, with random intercepts for participants. The main effect of format across all gambles was positive but modest (β = 0.28, SE = 0.12, z = 2.33, p = .020), indicating higher odds of choosing Option R in the description condition overall. The main effect of load was nonsignificant (β = -0.04, SE = 0.10, z = -0.40, p = .689), suggesting that imposing cognitive load did not, in general, change the probability of choosing the rare option. Crucially, the Format × Load interaction was not significant (β = -0.09, SE = 0.15, z = -0.60, p = .549), providing no support for the hypothesis that load reduces the description–experience gap at the aggregate level.

To probe heterogeneity across gambles, we included Format × Gamble interaction terms. The interaction was significant for Gambles 1–3 (joint test χ2(3) = 11.4, p = .010), reflecting that the format effect was concentrated in those specific tasks. For these gambles, the format main effects were substantial: Gamble 1 (β = 0.61, SE = 0.18, z = 3.39, p < .001), Gamble 2 (β = 0.47, SE = 0.17, z = 2.76, p = .006), Gamble 3 (β = 0.39, SE = 0.16, z = 2.44, p = .015). For Gambles 4–6, format coefficients were small and nonsignificant. Importantly, the Format × Load × Gamble three-way interaction did not reach significance (χ2(5) = 3.2, p = .669), indicating that the absence of load effects held across individual gambles as well.

Model-based probability weighting
To examine the psychological mechanism of probability distortion more directly, we fit a cumulative prospect theory-inspired choice model to each participant’s choices across the six gambles. The model incorporated a one-parameter Prelec probability weighting function, w(p) = exp{-[(-ln p)α]}, where α parameterizes the curvature of the weighting function and values less than 1 indicate overweighting of small probabilities and underweighting of large probabilities with an inverse-S shape. We fit the model separately to data from the description and experience formats at the participant level and then compared estimated α parameters across groups.

Across the sample, the mean α in the description condition for the three gambles showing format effects was 0.78 (SD = 0.29), whereas in the experience condition it was 0.95 (SD = 0.34). The difference was statistically significant in a paired comparison for participants who experienced both formats (in a subset analysis where within-subject comparisons were possible due to design variants; t(110) = -3.12, p = .002). For the other three gambles, α values were 0.91 (SD = 0.31) in description and 0.93 (SD = 0.33) in experience, a nonsignificant difference (t(222) = -0.74, p = .460). These model-based results support the interpretation that probability weighting differed by format for a subset of gambles, with description leading to more pronounced overweighting of small probabilities.

Effect of cognitive load on model parameters
We next examined whether cognitive load altered estimated probability weighting parameters. Comparing α parameters between Load and No-load groups yielded no reliable differences for either format. In the description condition, the mean α in Load was 0.80 (SD = 0.30) and in No-load it was 0.76 (SD = 0.28); this difference was not significant (t(110) = 0.88, p = .381). In the experience condition, α values were 0.96 (SD = 0.35) in Load and 0.94 (SD = 0.33) in No-load (t(110) = 0.40, p = .689). Equivalence tests comparing Load versus No-load parameter estimates failed to declare equivalence for extremely small effects, but the 95% confidence intervals were narrow enough to rule out medium-sized effects of load on probability weighting.

Exploratory analyses: sampling exposure
Because sampling variability could underlie underweighting in experience conditions, we examined whether the number of rare events observed during sampling predicted choice of Option R. For participants in the experience condition, we regressed choice on the number of observed rare outcomes in the sampling phase, controlling for gamble and cognitive load. The number of observed rare events strongly predicted selection of the rare option (β = 0.12 per rare event observed, SE = 0.03, z = 4.00, p < .001), consistent with a sampling account in which observed frequency informs choices. However, even after controlling for observed rare events, a residual format effect remained for the three replicated gambles (β = 0.34, SE = 0.14, z = 2.43, p = .015), indicating that sampling exposure did not fully account for the description–experience difference.

Secondary outcomes and robustness checks
We conducted several robustness checks to ensure the stability of our findings. Excluding participants with extreme sampling behavior (more than 90 samples on average) did not alter the main pattern of results. Similarly, excluding trials where participants in the experience condition sampled fewer than five times yielded similar effects, although sample sizes decreased. We also examined the possibility that the load manipulation impaired sampling behavior: participants in the Load condition sampled slightly fewer times on average (mean = 36.1) than those in No-load (mean = 41.2), a difference that reached marginal statistical significance (t(110) = -1.98, p = .050). However, including sample count as a covariate in models did not reveal any load-induced mediation of the format effect.

Evidence synthesis
Taken together, the results provide partial replication of the canonical description–experience gap. For three of the six gambles, participants in the description condition showed overweighting of rare events relative to participants in the experience condition, both in raw choice proportions and in model-based probability weighting parameters. For the remaining three gambles, the format did not significantly affect choice. The cognitive load manipulation was effective in imposing working memory demands and slightly altered sampling behavior, but it did not meaningfully change the description–experience gap or the estimated probability weighting parameters. Thus, the data do not support the dual-process hypothesis that the gap is primarily driven by differential engagement of deliberative processing as manipulated via concurrent working memory load.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether differences in information acquisition—specifically learning about probabilities from description versus from experience—lead to systematic differences in risk-taking behavior, and whether such differences are attributable to differential engagement of cognitive systems as articulated in dual-process theory. By replicating a set of canonical choice problems and by manipulating cognitive load, we tested two central propositions. First, we tested whether described probabilities lead to overweighting of rare events and whether experience-based learning leads to underweighting. Second, we tested whether impeding deliberative cognitive capacity through a concurrent memory load would attenuate the difference between description and experience, as predicted if System 2 processes are primarily responsible for the description–experience gap. Our results provide partial support for the first proposition but no support for the second.

Across six gambles, we observed that the format of information acquisition influenced choices in a subset of cases. For three gambles characterized by very small rare-event probabilities and large payoffs, participants in the description condition more frequently chose the rare-high-payoff option than participants in the experience condition. Model-based analyses of probability weighting further corroborated this observation: the estimated weighting parameter indicated greater overweighting of small probabilities in description than in experience for those three gambles. These findings align with a body of prior work showing that description and experience often yield divergent probability representations, with small probabilities overweighted when communicated explicitly and underweighted when inferred through sampling. The replication of this pattern in specific task types strengthens the claim that the gap is real and replicable for certain classes of risky choices.

However, the replication was only partial: for half of our gambles we observed no reliable difference between formats. This heterogeneity is notable and illuminates an important boundary condition on the description–experience gap. The fact that the gap appeared primarily for gambles with very low probabilities and large payoffs may indicate that the psychological processes that drive the gap are sensitive to the extremity of probability and payoff parameters. When probabilities are moderate or outcomes are less extreme, both description and experience may lead to similar effective representations and choices. This observation suggests that any complete theory of the description–experience gap must incorporate dependence on gamble characteristics rather than treating the contrast as universal.

Our manipulation of cognitive load produced clear evidence that working memory resources were taxed: participants reported higher difficulty, and retention accuracy for eight-digit numbers was far from ceiling. The load manipulation had some marginal behavioral impact: participants under load sampled slightly less in experience conditions. Nevertheless, the cognitive load manipulation did not significantly alter choice patterns in description or experience conditions in the way predicted by a straightforward dual-process account. The Format × Load interaction was nonsignificant both in choice-based mixed models and in model-based probability weighting parameters. This null finding held across aggregate analyses and across individual gambles, and it was robust to several robustness checks and covariates.

Several interpretations of the null cognitive load effect are possible. One interpretation is that the dual-process explanation, at least as operationalized by working memory load, is not the primary driver of the description–experience gap. In other words, the distinction between description and experience might not be adequately characterized as a simple shift in the balance between an analytic System 2 and an intuitive System 1 that can be modulated by imposing a concurrent memory task. Instead, the gap may reflect factors more closely tied to the nature of the information itself—such as the explicitness of probability information, the ease of encoding precise numerical probabilities, or the salience of described outcomes—rather than to the availability of deliberative cognitive capacity per se.

A second interpretation is that our cognitive load manipulation, while effective at occupying working memory, did not target the specific cognitive operations responsible for the putative System 2 processes that might underlie the description–experience gap. Dual-process constructs are broad, and various facets of analytic processing (e.g., numerical computation, probabilistic reasoning, counterfactual simulation) may be differentially affected by different types of load. An eight-digit retention task primarily occupies working memory in a domain-general way, but it may not impair the aspects of deliberation that lead to overweighting of rare described events. For instance, overweighting in description may arise from explicit attention to payoff magnitudes and a tendency to overemphasize salient outcomes when probabilities are presented, a process that might be robust to the particular memory load we induced. Alternatively, time pressure or manipulations targeting controlled numeric computation specifically might produce different effects.

A third possible account is that individual differences and strategic responses complicate the expected pattern. People differ in numeracy, risk preferences, and sampling strategies. Some participants may naturally engage in more deliberative conversion of probabilities into subjective utilities even under load, whereas others may rely on heuristics regardless of the extent to which working memory resources are free. Our sample-level analyses may therefore average over heterogeneous individual responses, potentially masking effects that occur only for subsets of participants. We did explore moderation by numeracy and found some suggestive evidence that participants with higher numeracy exhibited stronger format effects, but these interactions were not stable across analyses and require further investigation.

The exploratory finding that the number of observed rare events during sampling strongly predicted choice in the experience condition is consistent with the classic sampling account of underweighting: limited exposure to rare events in finite samples leads to underrepresentation of their true probability, which in turn reduces the attractiveness of rare-high-payoff options. Importantly, however, even after accounting for sampling frequency, a residual format effect remained for the gambles that showed replication. This observation suggests that sampling variability alone is insufficient to explain the entire gap and that additional mechanisms—such as differential encoding or valuation of explicitly described rare events—contribute to the phenomenon.

The partial replication and null load effects have implications for theoretical models of risky choice. Unitary models that posit a single mapping from objective probabilities to subjective weights would struggle to account for why the same objective gamble produces different choices depending on format. On the other hand, dual-process accounts that attribute the gap solely to differential engagement of deliberative processes mediated by working memory may be overly simplistic. A more nuanced theoretical approach might integrate several mechanisms: sampling and memory limitations in experience-based learning, salience and attentional amplification in description, and possible interactions with individual differences in numerical cognition and affective responsiveness. In such an integrated model, the description–experience gap would emerge from multiple interacting components rather than from a single binary processing distinction.

Several limitations of the current study warrant acknowledgement. First, the experiment was conducted online with monetary incentives, which, while practical and broadly representative, lacks the tightly controlled laboratory setting of some prior work. Second, although our load manipulation was effective, it targeted a domain-general working memory component and may not have fully captured the aspects of deliberation most relevant to probabilistic weighting. Third, the number of samples allowed in the experience condition could have influenced the degree of underweighting; although we allowed up to 100 samples and participants averaged about 39, some participants sampled very little and others sampled extensively, creating heterogeneity in exposure. Fourth, we did not include direct neurocognitive measures that might more finely dissociate processing modes, such as physiological indices of cognitive effort or neuroimaging markers of analytic versus associative processing.

Future research could build on our findings in several productive ways. First, experimental manipulations that specifically target numerical computation or probabilistic reasoning (for instance, by providing computational aids, instructing participants to calculate expected values, or using domain-specific dual tasks) might more directly test whether deliberative probabilistic computation underlies the overweighting observed in description. Second, manipulating the sampling regime systematically—by constraining sample counts tightly or by providing externally generated sampling sequences that equate exposure across participants—could distinguish between sampling-frequency explanations and other factors. Third, within-subject designs in which the same participants experience both description and experience formats could help isolate individual-level consistency and heterogeneity in format sensitivity, although care would be needed to avoid carryover and learning effects. Fourth, combining behavioral methods with process-tracing approaches such as mouse-tracking, think-aloud protocols, or eye-tracking could reveal the microstructure of processing differences between description and experience. Finally, computational modeling that integrates sampling models with attention- and salience-based weighting rules could be developed and tested against the type of behavioral data collected here.

In sum, this study replicated the description–experience gap in a subset of risky choice problems and provided evidence that the gap is not uniformly present across all gamble types. The lack of effect of a concurrent working memory load suggests that the gap cannot be straightforwardly attributed to a single dual-process distinction that is modulated by general working memory capacity. The findings instead point to a multifactorial account in which sampling limitations, attention to and salience of described outcomes, and individual differences jointly determine whether and how descriptions and experience lead to different choices. Clarifying the relative contributions of these factors remains an important goal for future research, because understanding how people form and use probabilistic beliefs is central to theories of judgment and decision making and to applications in domains as diverse as health communication, financial decision support, and public policy design.
