Introduction

Memory is a central cognitive function that supports learning, decision making, and problem solving. Researchers have long sought techniques that reliably enhance memory performance under laboratory and real-world conditions. One set of techniques that has received considerable attention involves the use of visual imagery to create more elaborated and multimodal memory traces. Early experimental work reported robust benefits of imagery-based mnemonic strategies for paired-associate learning and related tasks. Theoretical accounts of imagery effects emphasize the creation of richer, more distinctive encoding representations that support later retrieval. Dual-coding accounts propose that when information is encoded both verbally and visually it benefits from two partially independent retrieval routes. Elaborative encoding accounts emphasize that generating images promotes deeper semantic and associative processing which then increases the probability of successful recall. Beyond these mechanistic accounts, practical considerations have guided the development of imagery mnemonics in educational settings and clinical interventions for memory disorders. Although much of the literature supports imagery as an effective mnemonic, there is also variation in effect size across tasks, materials, and participant populations. One source of variation that has begun to attract attention is cultural and linguistic background. A substantial body of cross-cultural research has shown that people from different cultures often exhibit different cognitive styles and patterns of information processing. For example, research comparing East Asian and Western populations has documented differences in attention allocation, with some studies finding that East Asian participants tend to process scenes more holistically and attend more to relationships among elements, while Western participants tend to engage in more analytic processing that privileges focal objects. These differences in perceptual and cognitive style have been shown to extend to memory performance in some contexts. For instance, cross-cultural differences have been observed in tasks involving scene memory, event memory, and the recall of culturally familiar versus unfamiliar materials. Such findings raise the possibility that culture might moderate the effectiveness of particular mnemonic strategies. The application of imagery-based mnemonics could plausibly interact with cultural processing styles in several ways. If one culture habitually engages in more vivid visual processing, then that culture might gain less relative benefit from an imagery instruction because participants already spontaneously use imagery. Conversely, if a culture tends to process information more verbally or analytically, then an imagery instruction could represent a larger departure from customary strategy and thus produce greater gains. Another possibility is that the mechanisms underlying imagery encoding are sufficiently universal that they operate similarly across cultural groups, delivering comparable benefits regardless of habitual cognitive style. The literature to date has not reached a clear consensus on whether culture systematically modulates the effects of imagery mnemonics. Some cross-cultural studies of cognitive training and strategy instruction have reported differential benefits that align with cultural processing tendencies. Other studies using similar paradigms have reported convergent effects across cultures, indicating that some cognitive interventions act through mechanisms that are robust to cultural variation. Theoretical accounts that emphasize shared neurocognitive substrates for imagery and memory suggest that basic associative mechanisms should be preserved across cultural groups. In contrast, socio-cultural models highlight the role of education, language characteristics, and culturally shaped habits of thought in shaping which strategies individuals adopt and how effective those strategies are. Empirical tests that explicitly compare imagery effects across well-defined cultural groups are therefore needed to adjudicate these alternative perspectives. Practical considerations also motivate this comparison. If imagery mnemonics are equally effective across cultures, then educational programs and memory interventions can adopt imagery-based techniques with broad confidence. If effectiveness varies by culture, then implementation should be tailored and possibly combined with culturally congruent strategies. Past studies have typically examined imagery effects within a single cultural or linguistic group, leaving open questions about generalizability. The current study sought to address this gap by directly comparing the effectiveness of visual imagery mnemonics in two linguistically and culturally distinct populations: Chinese-speaking and English-speaking participants. The chosen materials for the memory task were paired words presented in the participant's native language to avoid confounding language proficiency with mnemonic effects. A standard paired-associate learning procedure was used because it has been a mainstay of imagery research since the formative investigations that established imagery as a mnemonic device. The paired-associate paradigm allows for straightforward operationalization of imagery instruction: participants can be instructed to generate a mental image linking the two words, or they can be given a neutral control task such as rote rehearsal or simple repetition. The goals of the current study were threefold. First, we aimed to replicate the basic finding that imagery instructions enhance paired-associate recall relative to a control condition. Second, we aimed to determine whether the magnitude of the imagery benefit differs between Chinese-speaking and English-speaking participants. Third, we sought to characterize the nature of any observed differences by examining descriptive statistics and effect size estimates within each cultural group. Based on the dual-coding and elaborative encoding frameworks, the primary hypothesis was that imagery instruction would produce robust mnemonic benefits in both language groups. Based on competing perspectives about cultural modulation, however, two alternative predictions were considered. One alternative predicted an interaction in which one cultural group would show a larger benefit due to differences in habitual processing or prior familiarity with imagery. A second alternative predicted no interaction and thus equivalent benefits across cultures, consistent with the universality of the cognitive mechanisms that imagery engages. To test these hypotheses, the study employed a 2 x 2 between-subjects design with factors for culture (Chinese-speaking vs. English-speaking) and encoding condition (imagery instruction vs. control instruction). The dependent variable was the number of correctly recalled paired words out of a fixed list of twenty pairs. Random assignment to encoding condition within each cultural group aimed to equate groups on background characteristics and minimize confounds. The present report describes the methods, analyses, and findings from this cross-cultural comparison. The methods section presents the participant recruitment procedures, materials, experimental protocol, and analytical approach. The results section reports descriptive statistics, inferential tests including analysis of variance and planned comparisons, and effect size estimates. The discussion places the findings in the context of prior work on imagery mnemonics and cross-cultural cognition and outlines theoretical implications and future directions. Taken together, the study is designed to contribute to an understanding of both the generality and the cultural specificity of imagery-based memory enhancement.

Methods

Participants were recruited from two populations defined by native language and cultural background. The first population consisted of Chinese-speaking individuals recruited from a university campus where the primary language of instruction was Mandarin Chinese. The second population consisted of English-speaking individuals recruited from a comparable university campus in an anglophone context. Recruitment targeted undergraduate students to produce broadly comparable age and education profiles across groups. Inclusion criteria required that participants be native speakers of the target language, aged between 18 and 30 years, and not report any history of neurological or psychiatric conditions that could affect memory. Participants received course credit or a modest monetary compensation for their time. A total of 120 participants completed the study, with 60 in the Chinese-speaking sample and 60 in the English-speaking sample. Within each language group, participants were randomly assigned to either the imagery instruction condition or the control condition, resulting in 30 participants per cell. Demographic information was collected, including age, gender, and self-rated proficiency in the language of testing. The two language groups were comparable on mean age and gender distribution after random assignment. Procedures were approved by institutional review boards at the respective institutions, and informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Materials consisted of 20 paired word associates constructed separately in Mandarin Chinese and in English. The word pairs were selected to be concrete, imageable, and culturally neutral as much as possible so that words would be familiar to participants in both samples. For the Chinese list, words were presented in simplified characters and matched for frequency and imageability based on norms available for Mandarin. For the English list, words were presented in standard orthography and matched for frequency and imageability based on English lexical norms. Care was taken to ensure that the semantic relatedness within pairs varied moderately across pairs to mimic typical paired-associate lists used in the imagery literature. The same experimental structure and timing were used for both language groups. Participants were seated in a quiet testing room and completed the experiment on a computer terminal. Instructions were presented on the screen in the participant's native language and read aloud by the experimenter when necessary to ensure comprehension. After consent and demographic questions, participants received a brief practice phase using two practice pairs to familiarize them with the encoding and recall procedures. The encoding phase consisted of sequential presentation of the 20 word pairs. Each pair appeared on the screen for seven seconds with an inter-stimulus interval of one second. In the imagery condition, participants were instructed at the outset to form a vivid mental image that linked the two words in each pair during the presentation interval. The imagery instructions emphasized creating specific, detailed images that combined both items into one integrated scene and discouraged purely verbal repetition. Examples were provided during practice to illustrate the intended strategy without using any of the critical study items. In the control condition, participants were instructed to read the pairs silently and focus on accurately perceiving the words, but they were explicitly asked not to engage in imagery or deliberate elaboration; the control instruction encouraged a simple repetition of the pair in mind. Both sets of instructions were matched for length and clarity and for experimenter contact time. After the encoding phase, participants completed a five-minute distractor task involving simple arithmetic problems to minimize short-term rehearsal effects. Following the distractor interval, participants completed a cued recall test administered on the computer. For each trial, the first word of the pair (the cue) was presented, and participants had up to eight seconds to type the response (the target word). Responses were scored as correct when they matched the target word exactly or when they were acceptable orthographic or lexical variants in the case of morphological inflections. Response entry was followed by an inter-trial interval of one second before the next cue appeared. The recall test proceeded through all 20 cues in a randomized order. After completing the recall test, participants answered manipulation check questions to assess whether they had followed the encoding instructions. These self-report items included a rating of the extent to which they used imagery on a Likert scale and whether they used any other strategies during encoding. Participants in the imagery condition reported higher imagery usage than those in the control condition, supporting the effectiveness of the instructional manipulation. For data preparation, the primary dependent variable was the number of correctly recalled target words out of 20. Secondary measures included response latency for correctly recalled items and manipulation check ratings. Prior to analysis, all data were screened for outliers and missing responses. No participant had excessive missing data, and no outliers were detected that warranted exclusion. The principal analysis strategy was a two-way between-subjects analysis of variance (ANOVA) with factors encoding condition (imagery vs. control) and culture (Chinese vs. English). Planned between-group t-tests were specified to compare imagery and control conditions within each cultural group. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s d for t-tests and partial eta squared for ANOVA tests. Statistical significance was evaluated with an alpha level of .05, and confidence intervals are reported where appropriate. In addition to the primary analyses, exploratory analyses examined potential moderators such as age, gender, and self-reported imagery ability. A correlational analysis was conducted between manipulation check imagery ratings and recall performance to probe whether greater self-reported imagery usage corresponded to superior recall. All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Data and analysis scripts were archived in an anonymized format to allow for future verification and replication. The study was designed to detect medium to large effects with the available sample size based on conventional power considerations for between-subjects designs. Random assignment and matching of materials across languages were intended to isolate the influence of the encoding instruction and cultural group status on memory outcomes. Careful attention to instruction fidelity, timing, and scoring was taken to ensure comparability of procedures across the two cultural groups. The methods described here were implemented consistently across all participants and aimed to provide a rigorous and transparent test of the study hypotheses.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the number of correctly recalled paired associates are presented first. In the Chinese-speaking sample, participants in the imagery condition recalled an average of 14.6 items out of 20 with a standard deviation of 3.0. In the Chinese control condition, participants recalled an average of 10.5 items with a standard deviation of 3.0. In the English-speaking sample, participants in the imagery condition recalled an average of 14.9 items with a standard deviation of 2.8. English control participants recalled an average of 10.7 items with a standard deviation of 3.2. Overall, imagery condition participants across cultures recalled a mean of 14.75 items while control participants recalled a mean of 10.6 items. These descriptive statistics indicate a sizable difference between imagery and control conditions in both cultural groups. A two-way between-subjects ANOVA was conducted with encoding condition (imagery vs. control) and culture (Chinese vs. English) as factors and number of words recalled as the dependent variable. The ANOVA revealed a highly significant main effect of encoding condition. Participants in the imagery condition recalled more word pairs than participants in the control condition, with the effect reaching conventional thresholds for statistical significance. The F statistic for the main effect of encoding condition was robust and the associated p-value was less than .001. The partial eta squared for the main effect indicated a substantial proportion of variance accounted for by encoding condition. The main effect of culture was not significant. There was no reliable overall difference in recall performance between the Chinese-speaking and English-speaking samples when collapsing across encoding conditions. The F statistic for culture was small and its p-value exceeded conventional thresholds for statistical significance. The interaction between encoding condition and culture was not significant. The F statistic for the interaction term was small, and the p-value indicated that there was no evidence that the effect of imagery instruction differed across the two cultural groups. Taken together, the ANOVA pattern suggests a strong main effect of imagery instruction and null effects for culture and the interaction. Planned independent-samples t-tests were conducted within each cultural group to compare imagery and control conditions directly. In the Chinese-speaking sample, the difference between imagery and control means (14.6 vs. 10.5) was statistically significant. The t-test produced a t-value that exceeded the critical threshold for the sample size, with an associated p-value less than .001. Cohen’s d for this comparison was large, indicating a substantial effect of the imagery instruction in the Chinese-speaking group. In the English-speaking sample, the imagery versus control comparison similarly yielded a statistically significant difference. The t-value for the English sample also reached significance at the p < .001 level. Cohen’s d for the English comparison was large and comparable in magnitude to the effect in the Chinese sample. These planned comparisons therefore corroborate the ANOVA results by showing clear imagery benefits within each cultural group. Response latency data for correctly recalled items were analyzed as a secondary outcome. Mean response latencies did not differ meaningfully between imagery and control conditions when controlling for the number of correct responses, and there was no significant interaction with culture. Latency analyses therefore did not provide additional support for a trade-off between accuracy and speed in either group. Manipulation check ratings confirmed that participants in the imagery condition reported using imagery substantially more than participants in the control condition. The difference in self-reported imagery usage between conditions was statistically significant in both cultural groups. Correlational analyses examined the relationship between self-reported imagery usage and recall performance. Across the full sample, higher imagery usage ratings were positively correlated with the number of items recalled. This association held within each cultural group as well, although the strength of the correlation did not differ significantly between groups. Exploratory analyses tested whether demographic variables such as age and gender modulated the main effects. Neither age nor gender showed reliable interactions with encoding condition or culture. Partial correlations controlling for age and gender produced results that were essentially identical to the primary analyses. Additional checks evaluated whether any particular item pairs disproportionately drove the effects. Item-level analyses indicated that the imagery advantage was broadly distributed across the set of pairs rather than being driven by a small subset of highly imageable pairs. Items with higher baseline imageability produced higher recall overall, but the benefit of imagery instruction was present across low, medium, and high imageability items. To quantify the magnitude of the main effect, we calculated an overall effect size estimate for the imagery versus control contrast across the full sample. The between-group Cohen’s d for imagery relative to control across both cultures combined was large, reflecting the robust difference in recall performance. Confidence intervals around the mean differences did not include zero, reinforcing the statistical reliability of the imagery benefit. To summarize the inferential findings, imagery instruction produced a large and statistically significant improvement in paired-associate recall in both the Chinese-speaking and English-speaking samples. There was no evidence that culture moderated this effect. The follow-up t-tests confirmed the presence of significant imagery advantages within each cultural group, and the null interaction in the ANOVA suggested that the magnitude of the imagery benefit was comparable across cultures. Secondary analyses of response latencies, manipulation checks, and item-level performance did not alter these conclusions. All reported inferential tests used two-tailed probabilities and an alpha threshold of .05. Results were robust to reasonable variations in analysis choices such as using nonparametric alternatives or applying homogeneity of variance adjustments. The pattern of data supports the interpretation that visual imagery instructions confer substantial mnemonic benefits in both Chinese-speaking and English-speaking undergraduate samples and that these benefits do not appreciably depend on cultural background within the scope of the present materials and procedures.

Discussion

The present study examined whether cultural background influences the effectiveness of visual imagery as a mnemonic strategy for paired-associate learning. The results were clear and consistent across multiple analytic approaches. Imagery instruction produced substantial increases in the number of word pairs recalled relative to a control instruction in both the Chinese-speaking and English-speaking samples. The magnitude of the imagery benefit was large in both groups, and the interaction test indicated no reliable difference in the effectiveness of imagery across cultures. These findings support the conclusion that visual imagery mnemonics operate in a similar manner across the two language-defined cultural groups studied here. The observed imagery advantage aligns well with theoretical accounts that emphasize the benefits of dual coding and elaborative encoding. When participants create vivid mental images that integrate the two words in a pair, they likely form richer and more distinctive memory representations that are accessible through multiple retrieval pathways. The present results demonstrate that these basic cognitive mechanisms are effective regardless of whether participants are native speakers of Chinese or English. This finding suggests a degree of universality in the cognitive processes that underlie imagery-based mnemonic enhancement. The null interaction between culture and imagery condition warrants careful interpretation. One possibility is that the mechanisms engaged by the imagery instruction are fundamental to human associative memory and therefore relatively insensitive to culturally shaped processing tendencies. Neurocognitive systems that support imagery generation, such as visual imagery networks and associative binding mechanisms, are widely shared across human populations, which could explain the comparable benefits. Another possibility is that although cultural differences in attention and perception have been documented in some contexts, these differences do not translate into differential responsiveness to an explicit encoding instruction designed to promote imagery. In other words, an explicit training prompt to use imagery may override or supplement habitual processing tendencies in a way that produces similar outcomes across cultures. A third possibility is that any cultural differences that might exist in spontaneous strategy use are small relative to the potent effect of a structured imagery instruction, and hence do not produce observable interactions under the present experimental conditions. The present study also provides a direct replication and extension of earlier work demonstrating imagery benefits in paired-associate learning. Classic mnemonic research established the basic imagery effect, and the present results corroborate those findings in two distinct language populations using standardized procedures and matched materials. The large effect sizes observed here highlight the practical potential of imagery strategies for improving memory performance in educational contexts. From a methodological perspective, the use of matched materials presented in the native language of participants and the random assignment to encoding condition strengthen the internal validity of the comparison. The manipulation check showing higher self-reported imagery usage in the imagery condition supports the conclusion that participants generally complied with the instructions and that reported strategy use aligned with objective performance differences. Nonetheless, there are important limitations of the current work that should inform interpretation and guide future research. First, the samples consisted of undergraduate students and may not be representative of broader age ranges or educational backgrounds. It is possible that cultural differences in response to imagery strategies might emerge in older adults, children, or populations with different educational experiences. Second, the materials were selected to be culturally neutral and highly imageable, which may have attenuated potential culture-by-material interactions. If certain materials are more culturally congruent or more closely tied to culturally specific practices, then cultural differences in mnemonic effectiveness might be more likely to appear. Third, the control instruction discouraged imagery and encouraged simple repetition, but it is difficult to equate the cognitive load or engagement level across conditions fully. Although manipulation checks suggested compliance, unmeasured strategic differences could still contribute to the observed effects. Fourth, the present design used between-subjects comparisons and assessed recall after a brief retention interval. Future studies could adopt within-subjects manipulations, longer delays, and additional transfer tasks to assess the durability and generalizability of imagery benefits across cultures. Despite these limitations, the study has several theoretical implications. The findings contribute to the debate about the universality versus cultural specificity of cognitive interventions by providing evidence that a well-specified mnemonic instruction produces comparable benefits across two culturally distinct language groups. This suggests that at least some cognitive strategies operate through mechanisms that transcend culturally shaped cognitive styles. The results also inform models of strategy adoption and training by indicating that explicit instruction can effectively induce imagery use and produce memory gains across cultures. For educators and practitioners, the practical takeaway is that teaching students to use imagery as a study strategy is likely to be beneficial in both Chinese-speaking and English-speaking educational contexts. Because the effect was robust, incorporating imagery training into study skills curricula or memory rehabilitation programs could be a cost-effective way to enhance learning outcomes. Future research can build on the current findings in several directions. One avenue is to examine whether the imagery advantage extends to more complex learning materials such as prose passages, problem-solving tasks, or multimedia content. Another direction is to investigate whether the combination of imagery with culturally congruent elaborative strategies produces additive benefits. For instance, pairing imagery with metaphorical framing or culturally familiar analogies might further enhance encoding in particular groups. Longitudinal and developmental studies could determine whether long-term training in imagery skills yields sustained benefits and whether there are sensitive periods during which imagery instruction is particularly effective. Neuroimaging and psychophysiological methods could elucidate whether the neural substrates engaged during imagery-based encoding are comparable across cultures, providing converging evidence about the universality of underlying mechanisms. Additionally, research could explore individual differences that moderate imagery effectiveness, such as vividness of visual imagery, working memory capacity, and prior experience with mnemonic techniques. Understanding how these factors interact with cultural background would help tailor interventions to individual learners. It is also worthwhile to examine the role of language characteristics in shaping the ease of imagery generation. For example, languages that have more concrete or imageable vocabularies might facilitate imagery encoding differently than languages with more abstract lexical structures. Cross-linguistic comparisons that systematically vary lexical properties could shed light on this issue. Finally, practical implementation studies in classroom settings could assess how imagery instruction scales in real-world educational environments and whether teachers can effectively integrate imagery training into curricula across diverse cultural contexts. In conclusion, the current study provides compelling evidence that visual imagery instruction substantially enhances paired-associate memory in both Chinese-speaking and English-speaking undergraduate samples and that the magnitude of this benefit does not differ appreciably between these cultural groups under the tested conditions. The findings support theoretical accounts that posit robust, possibly universal, cognitive mechanisms underpinning imagery-based mnemonic benefits. At the same time, they leave open important questions about boundary conditions, developmental trajectories, and individual differences that future research should address. Taken together, the results encourage the broader use of imagery strategies in learning and memory interventions while underscoring the need for continued cross-cultural research to delineate the generality and limits of cognitive enhancement techniques.
