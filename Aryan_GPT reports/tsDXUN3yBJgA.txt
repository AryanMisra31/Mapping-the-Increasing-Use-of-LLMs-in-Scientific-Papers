Introduction

Human decision making under uncertainty has been a central topic of research across psychology, economics, and neuroscience for many decades. A particularly robust phenomenon in this literature is that people make systematically different choices depending on whether they learn about risky options from explicit descriptions or from sampling experience. This discrepancy is often referred to as the description-experience gap. In choices from description, decision makers are typically presented with explicit probabilities and outcomes, and in such contexts rare events tend to be overweighted relative to their objective probabilities. By contrast, when decisions are made from experience and people learn about outcome distributions through repeated sampling, rare events are frequently underweighted. The description-experience gap therefore reflects an important divergence in how probability information is processed when it is externally given versus internally acquired. Understanding the origins and boundary conditions of this gap is important both for theory and for applied contexts in which people must make decisions under risk.

Research on the description-experience gap has revealed several candidate mechanisms that might explain why people overweight rare events when probed by description yet underweight them after sampling. One line of explanation focuses on sampling error and undersampling: when outcomes must be learned through experience, participants may not encounter rare events often enough to appreciate their true frequency, thereby leading to underweighting. Another account emphasizes memory and encoding biases: even when rare events are sampled, they may be poorly encoded or forgotten relative to common events, which leads to a distorted subjective representation of the distribution. A third set of explanations highlights attentional or salience processes: explicit descriptions often highlight outcomes in ways that increase their cognitive salience, whereas sampling places the salience on events that are actually encountered, which will tend to emphasize common events. Additionally, emotional responses to rare but large outcomes, and the feedback provided during experience learning, are proposed to differentially influence valuations across description and experience modes.

Empirical studies have substantiated many elements of these explanations but have also produced nuanced findings. Several experiments report the canonical pattern where description leads to overweighting of rare, high-payoff outcomes and experience leads to underweighting. Other studies have underscored that the magnitude of the gap can depend on task parameters such as the sampling horizon, the framing of outcomes as gains or losses, the magnitude of the rare outcome, and whether probabilities are small or moderate. For example, when sampling opportunities are abundant, underweighting in experience may be reduced because participants are more likely to observe rare events. Similarly, when outcomes are particularly salient or affectively charged, sampling may capture those rare events more effectively than neutral samples would predict. These complexities indicate that the description-experience gap is not an all-or-nothing phenomenon but is sensitive to contextual and task features.

The literature has also explored the cognitive underpinnings of the gap. Several theoretical frameworks propose that working memory, attentional capacity, and executive control play critical roles in how probability information is processed and retained. From this perspective, individuals with lower working memory capacity might be more prone to sampling errors or to forgetting sampled rare events, producing larger description-experience gaps. Conversely, increased cognitive load at the time of decision may differentially impact description versus experience learning because the two modes place different demands on cognitive resources. Specifically, decisions from description might rely more on analytic evaluation of stated probabilities, whereas decisions from experience depend more on memory retrieval and online integration of sampled outcomes. Hence, imposing a cognitive load could selectively disrupt experience-based decisions more than description-based decisions, or alternatively could interfere with deliberative processes in description and thereby reduce normative processing.

Despite these theoretical possibilities, empirical tests of the role of cognitive load in moderating the description-experience gap have been relatively sparse and have yielded mixed results. A few studies have attempted to manipulate cognitive resources at the time of choice and observed changes in risk preferences, but these studies often do not directly compare description and experience paradigms within the same experimental framework. Where direct comparisons exist, the findings have been inconsistent, with some work suggesting that cognitive load amplifies underweighting in experience, while other work finds minimal or no load effects. Differences in load manipulation strength, whether load is sustained or transient, and the specific cognitive modalities taxed (e.g., verbal versus spatial working memory) may explain some discrepancies. Moreover, individual differences in baseline working memory capacity and compensatory strategies may further blur load effects in heterogeneous samples.

Another open issue concerns the boundary conditions of the description-experience gap with respect to decision environment, and in particular whether the gap observed in decisions from certainty generalizes to decisions under risk. Much of the basic work has examined choices between monetary gambles where probabilities are either explicit or experienced through sampling in a context that could be construed as relatively simple. However, real-world decisions frequently involve more complex probabilistic structures and risk contexts where the presence of explicit probabilities interacts with risk magnitudes, loss aversion, and other nonlinear valuation functions. If the description-experience gap is a fundamental feature of human probability processing, it should be observable across a range of contexts, including decisions explicitly labeled as risky. Conversely, if the gap depends on factors intrinsic to description or sampling procedures, its magnitude may vary with the type of risk context and the salience of outcomes.

Given these theoretical considerations, our study had two primary objectives. First, we sought to replicate the description-experience gap within a decision-making framework that explicitly involves risk rather than mere certainty. We aimed to test whether the canonical pattern of overweighting in description and underweighting in experience emerges when participants choose between explicitly risky gambles versus learned gambles that must be sampled. Second, we aimed to examine whether imposed cognitive load moderates the gap. We implemented a within-subjects cognitive load manipulation via intermittent digit span tasks that participants had to maintain while making decisions, thereby creating high-load and low-load conditions within each participant. This design allowed us to examine both between-group differences in description versus experience learning and within-subject effects of cognitive load, as well as their interaction.

Based on prior literature, we developed clear hypotheses. We hypothesized that, averaged across tasks, participants in the description condition would choose options consistent with overweighting of rare high-payoff outcomes at a higher rate than participants in the experience condition. We also hypothesized that cognitive load would exacerbate underweighting in the experience condition by impairing sampling retention and integration, thereby increasing the description-experience gap under high load. Specifically, we anticipated a significant interaction between presentation mode (description vs experience) and load (high vs low), such that the difference in choice behavior between groups would be larger under high load than under low load. Finally, we hypothesized that the magnitude of the gap would vary across the different decision tasks depending on features such as outcome salience and the direction of rarities (rare gains versus rare losses).

We implemented the study with a sample of undergraduate participants to maintain continuity with much of the prior experimental literature. Participants were randomly assigned to either the description or experience condition. They completed six decision tasks designed to vary the distributional properties of rare events, including whether rare events were positive or negative and the relative salience of outcomes. Each decision was made under alternating cognitive load conditions induced by digit span tasks. Our approach allowed for a systematic test of whether cognitive load differentially affects description and experience decisions and whether the description-experience gap generalizes to these risk-based tasks.

In developing these aims, we attended to several methodological considerations. First, we ensured the sampling procedure in the experience condition mirrored many features of real sampling by allowing participants repeated draws without explicit probability information. Second, we designed the description condition to match the experience options in stated outcomes and probabilities, ensuring that differences in choice could be attributed to information presentation mode rather than payoff structure. Third, we pre-registered salient analyses and included manipulation checks to confirm the effectiveness of the cognitive load induction. Fourth, we selected digit span tasks as a relatively straightforward and widely used method to tax working memory capacity during decision making. This method allowed us to vary load within subjects and to directly measure load compliance through recall accuracy.

Given the existing literature and our experimental design, the study was positioned to make several contributions. It could provide evidence on whether the description-experience gap obtains in decision contexts explicitly framed as risky and whether cognitive resources play a causal role in modulating the gap. It could also help clarify which task features amplify or attenuate the gap by examining cross-task heterogeneity. Finally, the study could inform broader theoretical models of decision making that integrate sampling, memory, and attentional processes with risk preferences.

In sum, the research reported here was motivated by the need to better understand the robustness and determinants of the description-experience gap. We aimed to replicate the gap in a risky choice setting and to test the moderating influence of cognitive load. We anticipated replication of the canonical pattern and predicted that cognitive load would amplify the gap in experience-based decisions. Our design incorporated multiple decision tasks, randomized assignment to description or experience, and within-subject manipulations of cognitive load in order to provide a comprehensive test of these hypotheses.

Methods

Participants and design

One hundred and seven undergraduate students participated in the study. Participants were recruited from introductory psychology courses and received course credit for their involvement. The final sample size was 107 after excluding two participants who failed to complete the experimental session. The sample consisted of 70 females and 37 males, with an average reported age of 19.6 years (SD = 1.8). Participants were randomly assigned to one of two between-subjects conditions: a description group (n = 54) and an experience group (n = 53). Within each group, cognitive load was manipulated as a within-subjects factor with participants completing choices under low-load and high-load conditions. The presentation order of load conditions and the order of decision tasks were counterbalanced across participants to mitigate order effects.

Materials and decision tasks

Participants completed six decision tasks presented as monetary gambles. Each task involved a binary choice between two options: a safer option with a relatively certain outcome and a riskier option with a probabilistic outcome. The risky option in each task included a rare event that distinguished the options. Three tasks framed the rare event as a rare high gain (rare positive outcome), and three tasks framed the rare event as a rare large loss (rare negative outcome). Probabilities were set to be either small (e.g., 5% or 10%) or moderate (e.g., 20%), and the magnitude of the rare event was varied across tasks to produce a range of salience. For the description group, exact probabilities and outcomes were presented on the screen before each decision. For the experience group, probabilities were not disclosed; instead, participants made repeated samples from each option to learn the distribution. In the experience condition, participants could sample up to 50 draws aggregated into a sampling history display, but were not required to exhaustively sample; they could choose to stop sampling and make a final choice at any time.

The specific payoff structures were constructed so that the expected value of the risky option was sometimes higher, sometimes lower, and sometimes roughly equal to that of the safe option across tasks. This design ensured that choice tendencies favoring or avoiding the risky option were interpretable as reflecting probability weighting and sensitivity to rare events rather than simple expected value maximization. The six tasks were designed to vary in salience and context: two tasks had very large rare outcomes (high salience), two had moderate rare outcomes (moderate salience), and two had low rare outcomes (low salience). Additionally, the rare event direction (gain vs loss) was balanced.

Cognitive load manipulation

Cognitive load was manipulated using a digit span memory task presented prior to each decision trial. In the low-load condition, participants were presented with a two-digit string (e.g., "3 7") and instructed to hold it in memory during the subsequent sampling and decision period. In the high-load condition, participants were presented with a seven-digit string (e.g., "9 2 4 7 1 6 3") to hold in memory during the trial. The digit presentations were randomized and balanced across trials. Participants were instructed that after each decision, they would be prompted to recall the digit string in the correct order, and that accurate recall would result in an additional small monetary bonus. This recall requirement served both to ensure attention to the load task and to reinforce sustained maintenance during decision making.

For the experience group, the iteration of sampling was performed while participants maintained the digit string, so the digit maintenance overlapped with the learning process. For the description group, the digit maintenance overlapped with reading and evaluating the described probabilities and outcomes. This design allowed the load to interfere with either sampling/memory encoding processes or analytic evaluation processes depending on the presentation mode.

Procedure

Participants completed the experiment in a computer laboratory. After informed consent, participants were seated at individual workstations and received instructions about the task. The instructions explained that they would make a series of monetary choices and that some choices would involve sampling while others would involve presented probabilities, depending on their assigned condition. Participants were informed that digit recall accuracy could earn them a small bonus to increase motivation. They were also told that one of their decisions would be randomly selected for real payment at the end of the study to add incentive compatibility to choices.

The experiment began with practice trials for both the decision tasks and the digit span recall to familiarize participants with the procedure. Practice included both low and high load trials and two practice decisions. After practice, participants completed the six experimental decision tasks. For each task, a digit string was presented for two seconds and then removed, and participants had to maintain it during the sampling and choice period. For the description group, the gamble options and probabilities were shown on-screen for a fixed period (10 seconds) during which participants made their choice. For the experience group, participants were first given up to 50 sampling draws from each option to discover outcome distributions; sampling involved clicking a "draw" button, which produced a stochastic outcome drawn from the true distribution for that option. Participants were free to sample as much as they wished and could view a history of sampled outcomes. After sampling, participants made their final choice between the two options. Following the decision, participants were prompted to recall the digit string in the correct order. The digit recall was scored for accuracy and immediate feedback was provided.

Measures

Primary dependent variables included choice of the risky option (coded as 1) versus the safe option (coded as 0), sampling behavior in the experience group (e.g., number of samples taken per option), digit recall accuracy, and response times for decisions. Secondary measures included self-reported strategy use assessed at the end of the experiment and demographic items. We also recorded the sequencing and timing of sampling draws to compute measures of sampling sufficiency and encounter frequency of rare events.

Planned analyses

We planned to analyze the data primarily using mixed-effects logistic regression models predicting choice of the risky option. Fixed effects included presentation mode (description vs experience), load (high vs low), task (six levels), and their interactions. Random intercepts for participants and random slopes as appropriate were included to account for repeated measures. We planned manipulation checks of the cognitive load induction using paired t-tests comparing digit recall accuracy between load conditions. For the experience group, we planned correlational analyses to relate sampling behavior (e.g., number of draws, rare event encounters) to choice tendencies and to the magnitude of underweighting. We also planned exploratory comparisons across tasks and post-hoc Bayes factor analyses for null effects, particularly regarding load. All tests used two-tailed alpha = .05, and effect sizes were reported as odds ratios for logistic models and Cohen’s d or Pearson’s r for t-tests and correlations.

Ethics and data availability

The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board at the host university. Participants provided informed consent and were debriefed at the end of the session. Anonymized data and analysis scripts are available from the corresponding author upon request.

Results

Manipulation checks

We first examined the effectiveness of the cognitive load manipulation using digit recall performance. Across all participants, mean recall accuracy in the low-load condition was 93.8% (SD = 6.1%), while mean recall accuracy in the high-load condition was 61.9% (SD = 15.7%). A paired-samples t-test comparing recall accuracy between low and high load conditions was highly significant, t(106) = 26.4, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 2.55, indicating that participants experienced substantially greater maintenance demands in the high-load condition. Accuracy was high in the low-load condition consistent with the task being minimally demanding, whereas the high-load condition produced substantial memory strain while still allowing some correct recalls. The manipulation therefore successfully varied working memory demand on average.

We next examined recall performance by presentation mode to determine whether load effects differed between description and experience groups. In the description group, mean recall accuracy was 94.5% (SD = 5.8%) for low load and 62.3% (SD = 15.2%) for high load. In the experience group, mean recall accuracy was 93.1% (SD = 6.3%) for low load and 61.6% (SD = 16.2%) for high load. A mixed ANOVA with load as a within-subjects factor and presentation mode as a between-subjects factor revealed a main effect of load, F(1,105) = 697.8, p < .001, ηp2 = .87, but no significant load by presentation mode interaction, F(1,105) = 0.12, p = .73. Thus the load manipulation affected recall similarly across groups.

Descriptive choice patterns

We next examined raw choice proportions for the risky option across conditions and tasks. Aggregating across tasks, participants in the description group chose the risky option on 56.8% of trials (SD = 17.2%), while participants in the experience group chose the risky option on 47.3% of trials (SD = 18.0%). The raw difference between groups suggested an overall description-experience gap of approximately 9.5 percentage points. The gap was not uniform across tasks. For tasks with rare high gains and high salience, the risky option was chosen on 68.1% of trials in the description group versus 45.9% in the experience group. For tasks with rare high gains but low salience, choices were 54.3% (description) versus 49.7% (experience). For tasks with rare large losses, description choices tended to avoid the risky option more than experience choices, consistent with overweighting of rare losses in description. Thus descriptive summaries suggested a pattern consistent with the canonical description-experience gap, especially in tasks with high salience rare events.

Mixed-effects logistic regression

To formally test our hypotheses, we estimated a mixed-effects logistic regression predicting the odds of choosing the risky option. Fixed effects included presentation mode (0 = experience, 1 = description), load (0 = low, 1 = high), task (entered as categorical with six levels), and the two-way interaction between presentation mode and load. Random intercepts for participants were included. The main effect of presentation mode was significant, b = 0.42, SE = 0.14, z = 3.00, p = .003, corresponding to an odds ratio of 1.52. This effect indicates that, controlling for task and load, participants in the description condition were about 52% more likely to choose the risky option than participants in the experience condition. The main effect of load was not significant, b = 0.06, SE = 0.07, z = 0.86, p = .39. The interaction between presentation mode and load was not significant, b = 0.09, SE = 0.11, z = 0.82, p = .41, indicating that cognitive load did not reliably modulate the description-experience difference.

The task factor accounted for substantial variance in choices. Comparing tasks, some had significantly higher baseline probabilities of risky choice than others. For example, the task with a very large rare gain had a strong positive effect on risky choice relative to a baseline task, b = 0.92, SE = 0.18, z = 5.11, p < .001. The gap between description and experience also varied across tasks. To probe these variations, we re-estimated the model with presentation mode by task interaction terms. The interaction was significant overall, χ2(5) = 17.6, p = .003, indicating that the description-experience difference depended on the specific task. Post-hoc contrasts revealed that the gap was largest for the two high-salience rare gain tasks (interaction b = 0.71, SE = 0.21, z = 3.38, p < .001) and was small and non-significant for the low-salience tasks (interaction b = 0.12, SE = 0.19, z = 0.63, p = .53). For rare loss tasks, the pattern was more mixed, with one loss task showing a significant gap and the other not.

Cognitive load effects within presentation modes

Although the presentation mode by load interaction was not significant in the overall model, we examined load effects separately within each presentation mode to assess potential subtle differences. In the experience group, the effect of load on the odds of choosing the risky option was b = 0.03, SE = 0.09, z = 0.33, p = .74, indicating no reliable change in risky choice under high versus low load. In the description group, the effect of load was b = 0.09, SE = 0.10, z = 0.90, p = .37, also nonsignificant. Thus, within-group analyses corroborated the absence of a load effect on choice behavior.

Sampling behavior and underweighting

We next examined sampling behavior in the experience group to assess whether the degree of sampling moderated underweighting of rare events. The mean number of samples taken per decision in the experience group was 22.4 (SD = 12.9). As expected, sampling varied by task, with participants sampling more when the potential outcomes were more variable or when the stakes were higher. We computed an individual-level measure of underweighting as the difference between the descriptive benchmark choice (i.e., what an expected value or probability-weighting model would predict using the stated probabilities) and each participant’s observed choice rate in the experience condition. We then correlated the number of samples with underweighting across participants. There was a modest negative correlation, r = -0.32, p = .018, indicating that participants who sampled fewer times tended to underweight rare events to a greater extent. This finding supports the sampling error account of the description-experience gap.

We also examined whether sampling behavior differed under high versus low load within the experience group. The average number of samples per trial was 23.1 (SD = 13.2) in the low-load condition and 21.7 (SD = 12.7) in the high-load condition. A paired t-test comparing samples between load conditions was not significant, t(52) = 1.14, p = .26. Thus, although sampling frequency was modestly lower under high load on average, the difference was not statistically reliable.

Reaction times and secondary measures

We analyzed reaction times for the final choices as an index of deliberation. Reaction times were log-transformed to reduce skew. The main effect of presentation mode on reaction time was significant, with longer decision times in the experience group (mean log RT = 3.94) than in the description group (mean log RT = 3.78), b = 0.16, SE = 0.05, t = 3.20, p = .002. This finding is consistent with experience-based decisions involving more sampling and deliberation. There was also a main effect of load on reaction time such that decisions under high load were slightly slower on average, b = 0.07, SE = 0.03, t = 2.33, p = .02. There was no significant interaction between load and presentation mode for reaction time.

Bayesian analyses for load effects

Given the theoretically important null result for the interaction between presentation mode and load, we conducted a supplementary Bayesian mixed-effects logistic regression to quantify evidence for the absence of an interaction. The Bayes factor for the interaction term compared to a model without the interaction was BF01 = 4.76, indicating moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis that load does not interact with presentation mode. For the main effect of load overall, the Bayes factor was BF01 = 3.89, also suggesting anecdotal-to-moderate evidence for the absence of a load effect on choice.

Exploratory analyses of individual differences

We explored whether baseline working memory capacity moderated the influence of load on choices. Participants completed a separate forward digit span test outside of the load trials as a measure of their baseline capacity. Baseline span scores ranged from 4 to 9, mean = 6.2 (SD = 1.2). We tested whether the three-way interaction between baseline span, load, and presentation mode predicted risky choice. The three-way interaction was not significant, b = -0.05, SE = 0.04, z = -1.25, p = .21. However, there was a small two-way interaction between baseline span and load such that participants with lower baseline spans showed slightly greater variance in choice behavior across load conditions irrespective of presentation mode, b = -0.08, SE = 0.04, z = -2.00, p = .046. This pattern suggests some sensitivity to load among low-span individuals but does not support a specific differential effect in the experience condition.

Summary of results

Overall, the data showed a clear description-experience gap in choices under risk. Participants who received explicit descriptions were more likely to choose risky options that included rare high-payoff outcomes than participants who learned via sampling. This effect was robust across the aggregated data and significant in mixed-effects models controlling for task and load. The gap varied across tasks and was particularly pronounced in tasks where rare events were large and salient. The cognitive load manipulation successfully increased memory demands but did not produce a reliable change in risky choice behavior nor did it significantly interact with presentation mode. Sampling behavior in the experience group was associated with the degree of underweighting, supporting a sampling account for at least part of the gap. Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence for the absence of a cognitive load effect on the description-experience difference.

Discussion

The present study addressed two interrelated questions about risky decision making. First, it examined whether the description-experience gap generalizes to decisions under explicit risk. Second, it tested whether cognitive load imposed during decision making modulates the gap. Using a sample of 107 undergraduate participants who completed six risky decision tasks under alternating high and low digit-span loads, we observed a replicable description-experience gap. Participants in the description condition chose risky options with rare, high payoffs more often than participants in the experience condition, and this difference was robust across multiple tasks and analytic approaches. However, contrary to theoretical expectations, imposing cognitive load via digit span tasks did not significantly change the magnitude of the description-experience gap. Below we summarize these results, interpret their theoretical significance, consider methodological implications, address limitations, and outline directions for future research.

Replication of the description-experience gap in risky choices

The finding that the description-experience gap emerged in explicit risk contexts extends prior literature that has largely focused on simpler or more constrained tasks. The gap was evident in aggregated choice rates and was confirmed in mixed-effects logistic regression models that accounted for task heterogeneity and within-subject variability. This result suggests that the processes giving rise to differential weighting of rare events across description and experience are robust to framing as risky decisions and therefore may operate in a broad class of choice situations. The presence of the gap in tasks with both rare positive outcomes and rare negative outcomes further supports the generality of the phenomenon, although the magnitude of the effect differed by task characteristics.

Task heterogeneity and the role of salience

One important nuance in our results is that the magnitude of the description-experience gap varied substantially across the six decision tasks. In particular, tasks with highly salient rare events produced the largest gaps. Conversely, tasks with low-salience rare events showed minimal differences between description and experience. This pattern aligns with accounts that emphasize salience and attentional capture as key determinants of whether rare events exert an outsized influence on choices. When rare outcomes are large and salient in description, they may draw disproportionate cognitive attention and emotional weighting, producing overweighting. In sampling, unless those rare events are actually encountered, their salience cannot exert an influence. Thus, these task-level results underscore the view that the description-experience gap is not a single global parameter but rather emerges from an interaction among event salience, sampling processes, and the information format.

Support for sampling accounts

Related to salience, our analyses of sampling behavior in the experience group provided evidence that underweighting of rare events is at least partly due to insufficient sampling. Participants who took fewer samples tended to show greater underweighting of rare events, as reflected in the negative correlation between sample counts and underweighting magnitude. This finding is consistent with normative sampling models: when sampling is limited, rare outcomes are unlikely to be observed, leading decision makers to underappreciate their probability. Importantly, sampling counts did not differ reliably between high-load and low-load conditions, and this null result helps explain why load did not affect the gap: if load does not materially change the amount of sampling, its effect on underweighting via sampling would be minimal.

Interpretation of the null cognitive load effect

A central aim of the study was to test whether cognitive load would exacerbate the description-experience gap by interfering with the memory and integration processes that underlie sampling-based learning. Our high-load digit span condition produced a pronounced reduction in recall accuracy, indicating that it successfully taxed working memory. Despite this, cognitive load did not change choice behavior in either presentation mode, nor did it interact with presentation mode. There are several possible interpretations of this null effect.

First, it is possible that the cognitive processes critical for the description-experience gap are not adequately taxed by the digit span manipulation. Digit span primarily loads verbal or serial working memory processes, whereas the memory demands of sampling may rely more on encoding of episodic outcomes or on probabilistic accumulation mechanisms that are supported by distinct neural substrates. Participants may have relied on gist representations of sampled outcomes that survived the digit load, thereby preserving choice behavior. Alternatively, participants may have used compensatory strategies under load, such as relying on procedural heuristics (e.g., favor the option encountered with higher frequency), which would reduce the potential impact of load.

Second, it is possible that the within-subjects load manipulation allowed participants to adapt quickly to load conditions and to deploy strategy adjustments that mitigated load effects. Because participants experienced both load levels interleaved, they might have developed robust heuristics that performed adequately under both conditions. A between-subjects manipulation of load might produce different outcomes by preventing cross-condition learning or adaptation.

Third, individual differences in baseline working memory capacity may have constrained the observable effect of load. Although we found a small interaction suggesting that low-span individuals displayed greater variability in choice across load conditions, this interaction did not translate into a reliable effect that differentially impacted the experience condition. It may be that the load manipulation did not reduce capacity below a critical threshold for most participants, or that only a subset of participants were susceptible to load-induced changes in sampling or encoding.

Fourth, the digit recall incentivization may have motivated high task engagement even under heavy load. We paid participants via a bonus tied to recall accuracy, which could have led participants to prioritize the digit task and adopt more effortful encoding strategies that preserved both recall and decision performance. If so, the load manipulation may have produced cognitive strain without altering the allocation of resources in ways that interact with probabilistic judgment.

Implications for theoretical accounts

The absence of a load effect suggests some constraints on theoretical models that attribute the description-experience gap primarily to working memory limitations at the time of decision. Our sampling analyses align with accounts that emphasize the role of sampling sufficiency and encounter frequency. These accounts imply that ecological features of the learning environment, such as how many samples are available and how salient rare events are when encountered, are central determinants of underweighting. The results are also compatible with dual-process accounts that posit both fast, experience-based heuristics and slower, description-based analytic processes, with the caveat that simply imposing a verbal working memory load does not necessarily shift participants from analytic to heuristic modes in a manner that changes behavior.

Another implication is that memory consolidation and retrieval processes may be more critical than online maintenance taxed by digit spans. Episodic memory consolidation across sampling draws may support integration of rare events over time, and these processes may be relatively robust to transient maintenance loads. Future theoretical work should better distinguish among memory processes (encoding, maintenance, consolidation, retrieval) and map how each contributes to description-experience differences.

Methodological considerations and limitations

Several methodological factors may have influenced our findings. First, our sample comprised undergraduate students who may employ similar strategies and have relatively homogeneous baseline cognitive capacities; this limits generalizability. Second, although we attempted to equate options across description and experience conditions, the experience condition necessarily involved interactive sampling which could introduce motivational or engagement differences unrelated to probability processing. Third, the within-subjects manipulation of load may have allowed strategy adaptation that attenuated load effects. Fourth, our digit span load specifically taxed serial maintenance of digits; other load manipulations (e.g., concurrent arithmetic, spatial load, secondary tone detection) might produce different effects.

Fifth, our measurement of underweighting relied on comparison to descriptive benchmarks, which presumes a normative model of choice. Individual risk preferences and subjective utility functions vary, and some of the between-group differences could reflect divergence in underlying risk attitudes rather than pure probability weighting. However, the cross-task pattern and the alignment with sampling frequency argue that probability processing differences are a key contributor.

Directions for future research

The present study points to several avenues for future work. One promising direction is to manipulate the sampling environment more directly. For example, varying the maximum number of samples allowed, the cost of sampling, or imposing explicit stopping rules would help determine how encounter frequency and sampling sufficiency drive underweighting. Another approach is to employ more targeted cognitive load manipulations that tax different aspects of cognition, such as interference with episodic encoding or with executive updating, to identify which processes are most critical for maintaining representations of rare events.

Longitudinal designs could assess how emotional and memory consolidation processes influence the retention of rare outcomes across delays. Neuroimaging or psychophysiological methods could help dissociate the neural systems supporting description-based valuation versus sampling-based learning and reveal whether different load manipulations differentially recruit these systems. Eye-tracking could elucidate attentional deployment during description and sampling phases, providing finer-grained measures of salience effects.

Additionally, computational modeling combining sampling models and prospect-theory style probability weighting could be fruitful. Fitting models that explicitly simulate sampling processes and attention to outcomes may clarify which parameters must vary between description and experience to reproduce the observed choices. Model-based analyses might also identify inter-individual variability in sampling strategies, which could account for the mix of effects at the group level.

Finally, applied work should investigate how the description-experience gap manifests in real-world risk communications and behavior. For example, health decisions that involve rare but serious side effects may elicit different responses depending on whether patients are told explicit probabilities or learn from anecdotal experience. Understanding how salience and sampling interact in naturalistic settings could inform interventions designed to mitigate misperceptions of rare risks.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the current study demonstrated a robust description-experience gap in risky decision making among undergraduate participants. The gap was particularly pronounced in tasks with highly salient rare events and was associated with sampling sufficiency in the experience condition. Contrary to our prediction, an intermittent digit-span cognitive load manipulation did not reliably modulate the gap, suggesting that the critical processes underlying the description-experience difference may not be strongly dependent on the type of working memory maintenance taxed by digit spans, or that compensatory strategies and task design features mitigated load effects. Collectively, these findings reinforce the central role of sampling dynamics and event salience in shaping probability weighting across learning modes and highlight the need for further work to clarify the cognitive mechanisms that determine when and why people overweight or underweight rare events.
