Introduction

Language routinely uses temperature-related words to describe social interactions, and this pattern is found across many languages and cultures. Everyday expressions such as “cold shoulder”, “frosty reception”, “warm welcome”, and “heated debate” link physical temperature metaphors to social and emotional states. The ubiquity of these metaphors suggests that there may be systematic connections between bodily sensations of temperature and the social world. Researchers in social and cognitive psychology have proposed that much of human social cognition is grounded in bodily experience, and temperature metaphors have been a prominent example in this literature. The idea is that abstract social concepts such as interpersonal warmth and coldness are rooted in concrete sensory experiences, including thermal sensations. This grounding may operate through embodied simulation, early caregiver experiences, or culturally shared linguistic conventions that map physical temperature onto social valence. A number of empirical studies have investigated links between physical temperature and social perception, finding that experiences of physical warmth can influence interpersonal judgments and behaviors. For example, holding a warm object has been reported to increase perceptions of another person’s warmth, and exposure to warmer ambient temperatures has sometimes been linked to more affiliative and generous behavior. These findings have been interpreted as evidence that bodily states related to temperature can causally shape higher-order social judgments. One influential study in this area found that people who recalled a socially excluding event later judged the ambient room temperature as being lower than people who recalled a socially including event. That study suggested that feeling socially excluded might induce a mental and perhaps bodily sense of coldness, which then influences subjective temperature estimation. Such findings have been used to argue that social exclusion can produce embodied sensations akin to physical coldness, advancing the notion that social and physical warmth are intimately connected. The study that linked social exclusion recall to perceived lower room temperature has been widely cited and has inspired work exploring the intersection of social pain and embodied sensation. However, as with many areas of social cognition, results have sometimes been mixed and subject to boundary conditions. Several studies have attempted to extend or refine the findings by manipulating other aspects of social interaction, by using different measures of temperature, or by investigating moderating individual differences. One area that has received relatively little empirical attention is whether individual differences in personality traits, such as extraversion, moderate the degree to which social exclusion affects perceived temperature. Extraversion is a trait characterized by sociability, assertiveness, and a tendency to seek social interaction, and it shapes how people perceive and respond to social stimuli. Extraverts typically report greater enjoyment of social situations and display more active engagement with others, whereas introverts tend to prefer lower levels of social stimulation and may be more sensitive to social rejection in certain contexts. Given these differences, it is plausible that the embodied linkage between social exclusion and perceived coldness might vary as a function of extraversion. If extraverts are generally more buffered against negative social cues through habitual social engagement, then they might show weaker embodied responses to a recall manipulation of social exclusion than introverts. Conversely, if extraverts derive more regulatory benefit from social connection, they might be more sensitive to the loss of social warmth and therefore more likely to experience embodied sensations of cold when recalling exclusion. Investigating extraversion as a moderator could clarify whether the embodied effect is a general phenomenon or one that depends on stable personality differences. In addition to personality moderators, methodological aspects of prior work are worth scrutinizing. Earlier reports often relied on subjective estimates of room temperature following an experimental manipulation, and these estimates may be influenced by demand characteristics, mood, or cognitive priming rather than reflecting authentic interoceptive or sensory changes. It is therefore important to replicate prior effects while also carefully measuring and controlling for potential confounds such as mood, baseline expectations about the experiment, and the actual ambient temperature. Furthermore, the recall method used to induce feelings of social inclusion or exclusion recruits autobiographical memory and may vary widely in its potency across participants. Some participants might vividly re-experience exclusion-related sensations, whereas others might produce a more superficial recall. These differences could attenuate average effects and increase variability. Another consideration concerns cultural and temporal variation. Language metaphors linking warmth and sociality may be culturally widespread, but the strength of embodied associations could vary across cultures or across time as social norms change. Replication attempts in different samples therefore provide useful information about the generality of observed effects. Beyond these methodological points, there has been an ongoing theoretical debate about the interpretation of temperature-related social effects. On the one hand, supporters of an embodied cognition account suggest that bodily states directly contribute to social judgments. On the other hand, critics argue that many of the observed effects can be explained by conceptual priming, demand characteristics, or associative learning without invoking direct bodily changes in temperature perception. Distinguishing among these accounts requires rigorous empirical work that uses multiple measures, attempts replication, and probes moderators. The present study had two primary aims that address gaps in the literature. First, we aimed to replicate the previously reported finding that recalling a socially excluding event leads people to estimate the ambient room temperature as lower than people who recall a socially including event. Replication is critical for assessing the robustness of findings, given concerns about replicability in social psychology. Second, we aimed to test a new hypothesis regarding personality: specifically, whether extraversion moderates the effect of social inclusion or exclusion recall on subjective temperature estimation. We reasoned that extraversion might buffer against embodied social-coldness or might alternatively amplify the effect, and so we treated extraversion as a theoretically plausible moderator to investigate. To accomplish these aims, we used a between-subjects design in which participants were randomly assigned to recall a time when they felt socially included or a time when they felt socially excluded. Following the recall manipulation, participants provided an estimate of the room temperature. We also measured extraversion using a validated personality inventory so that we could examine both main effects and moderation. We further included manipulation checks and mood measures to help interpret any observed effects. We preregistered our analysis plan to reduce analytic flexibility and increase transparency. Our primary analysis focused on whether there was a significant difference in reported temperature between the inclusion and exclusion conditions. A secondary analysis examined the interaction between condition and extraversion via moderated regression. We also planned several exploratory analyses, including tests controlling for mood and evaluating whether the strength of the recall manipulation predicted temperature estimation. On the basis of prior findings, we predicted that the exclusion recall group would report lower perceived room temperatures than the inclusion recall group. Regarding extraversion, we made an a priori prediction that extraversion would moderate the magnitude of the effect, with introverts showing a larger difference between conditions than extraverts. The rationale for this prediction was that introverts might have fewer habitual sources of social warmth and therefore be more affected by recall-induced exclusion, while extraverts might be more resilient to isolated recollections of exclusion. We recognized that the alternative pattern was also plausible, and so the study was designed to detect moderation in either direction. In summary, the study sought to provide a rigorous test of a previously reported embodied social effect and to investigate whether a stable personality trait influences the strength of that effect. By combining an established recall manipulation with a validated measure of extraversion and preregistered analyses, the study aimed to produce evidence that would clarify the replicability and boundary conditions of links between social exclusion and subjective temperature perception. The following sections describe the methodological approach in detail, present the results of the analyses, and discuss implications for theories of embodied social cognition and for future research.

Methods

Participants and recruitment. Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and via an online recruitment platform associated with the psychology department. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old, fluent in English, and not to report any current major respiratory illness that could influence thermal perception. We aimed to recruit a sample size that would provide adequate power to detect a medium effect size for the primary manipulation, and our initial target was 120 participants. In practice, 124 participants completed the study. The final sample consisted of 124 individuals, with a mean age of 22.4 years (SD = 5.2). The sample included 70 females, 52 males, and 2 participants who reported another gender identity. Participants received course credit or a small monetary payment for their time. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the university institutional review board, and all participants provided informed consent before beginning the experiment. Design overview. The study employed a between-subjects design with a single experimental factor: recall condition (social inclusion vs. social exclusion). Participants were randomly assigned to one of the two conditions via a random number generator embedded in the online survey platform. Extraversion was measured as a continuous individual-difference variable and tested as a moderator of the recall condition effect. Primary dependent variables were subjective estimates of room temperature and manipulation check items assessing the vividness and emotional quality of the recalled event. Materials and measures. The recall manipulation consisted of written instructions asking participants to recall and write about a specific experience in detail. In the inclusion condition, participants were asked to recall a time when they felt socially included, accepted, or warmly connected to others. In the exclusion condition, participants were asked to recall a time when they felt rejected, ignored, or socially excluded. The instructions encouraged participants to include details about what happened, how they felt, and any physical sensations that accompanied the event. This manipulation is similar to procedures used in prior studies that investigated social exclusion effects. Following the recall instruction, participants completed a short writing task of at least five minutes, during which they typed their recollection into a text box. To assess the manipulation, participants then rated on Likert-type scales the extent to which the recalled event made them feel included (reverse-coded for exclusion), the extent to which the event felt vivid, and the level of negative affect elicited by the recollection. The primary dependent measure for the key hypothesis was the participant’s estimate of the room temperature. To obtain this measure, participants were asked to report, in degrees Celsius, what they believed the ambient room temperature was at the time of completing the experiment. For participants completing the study in laboratory rooms, the actual room temperature was recorded with a digital thermometer immediately prior to the participant’s arrival and noted on the experimenter’s log. For participants completing the study online in their own environments (a minority of the sample), we asked them to report whether they were indoors and whether they had control over heating, acknowledging that these factors could influence temperature judgments; these participants were included in analyses with sensitivity checks. The extraversion measure was the 20-item extraversion scale from the IPIP NEO inventory. Participants responded to items on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Items assessed facets of extraversion such as sociability, assertiveness, and activity level. The extraversion scale was scored by averaging item responses, after reversing items as needed, and internal consistency was computed. Additional measures included brief mood ratings using a positive and negative affect schedule to assess current affective state, and two items assessing general attentional focus during the task. Procedure. Participants were welcomed to the lab or logged into the online survey platform and provided informed consent. The experimenter or the online instructions informed participants that the study was about autobiographical memory and perception. Participants were then randomly assigned to the recall condition and received the corresponding writing instructions. The writing task lasted a minimum of five minutes and participants were instructed not to consult external aids or outside sources. After completing the writing task, participants completed the manipulation check items and the affect measures. They then provided an estimate of the ambient room temperature. Next, participants completed the IPIP NEO extraversion scale and a short demographic questionnaire, which included age, gender, and whether they were currently in a heating-controlled environment. Finally, participants were debriefed and given an opportunity to ask questions. All participants completed the study in a single session that lasted approximately 25 minutes. Data preparation and analytic plan. Prior to conducting the primary analyses, data were screened for completeness, outliers, and normality. Participants who failed attention checks or provided implausible temperature estimates (defined a priori as estimates outside the range of -10°C to 50°C) were excluded from the primary analyses; such exclusions were rare and affected fewer than three participants. The main analysis compared average subjective temperature estimates between the inclusion and exclusion groups using an independent-samples t-test. As a supplementary test, we computed an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) controlling for self-reported negative affect and study location (lab vs. online). For the moderation hypothesis, we conducted a multiple regression in which subjective temperature estimate was regressed on condition (dummy-coded), centered extraversion score, and the interaction between condition and extraversion. Significant interactions were probed by computing simple slopes at one standard deviation above and below the mean of extraversion. We also calculated Pearson correlations between extraversion and temperature estimate within each condition. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for group differences and as standardized regression coefficients for continuous predictors. To provide an inference that could quantify evidence for the null hypothesis, we conducted a Bayesian t-test for the primary comparison and computed a Bayes Factor (BF10) using default priors. Reliability for the extraversion measure was evaluated with Cronbach’s alpha. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and the preregistered analysis plan was followed unless otherwise noted. Power considerations. Prior work reported moderate effect sizes for the influence of recalling social exclusion on subjective temperature perception. To allow detection of an effect of approximately d = 0.50 with 80% power at α = .05 in a two-tailed test, a target sample of roughly 102 participants was necessary. Our sample of 124 surpasses this threshold, providing reasonable power to detect medium effects. We note, however, that smaller effects would require larger samples to be reliably detected. Exploratory analyses. We planned exploratory analyses to examine whether the vividness of the recall, actual recorded room temperature, gender, or mood scores accounted for variance in subjective temperature estimates. We also examined whether excluding online participants appreciably changed effect estimates. All exploratory analyses were interpreted cautiously and marked as such.

Results

Sample and preliminary checks. The final sample consisted of 124 participants after exclusions, with 62 individuals randomly assigned to the inclusion condition and 62 to the exclusion condition. The two groups did not differ significantly in mean age (inclusion M = 22.6, exclusion M = 22.2; t(122) = 0.54, p = .59) or gender distribution (inclusion: 36 female, 24 male, 2 other; exclusion: 34 female, 28 male, 0 other; χ2(2) = 1.12, p = .57). The extraversion scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the present sample (Cronbach’s alpha = .86). Mean extraversion scores were similar across conditions (inclusion M = 3.12, SD = 0.63; exclusion M = 3.09, SD = 0.66; t(122) = 0.28, p = .78). Manipulation check. Participants in the exclusion condition reported higher negative affect in response to the recall task than those in the inclusion condition (exclusion M = 3.58, SD = 0.94; inclusion M = 1.84, SD = 0.72; t(122) = 13.07, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 2.34), indicating that the recall instructions successfully elicited more negative affect in the exclusion group. Similarly, inclusion ratings were higher in the inclusion condition than in the exclusion condition (inclusion M = 4.01, SD = 0.61; exclusion M = 1.92, SD = 0.69; t(122) = 17.78, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 3.18). Participants reported comparable vividness of recollection across conditions (inclusion M = 3.72, SD = 0.85; exclusion M = 3.64, SD = 0.90; t(122) = 0.48, p = .63). These checks suggest that the manipulation induced the intended subjective social affective states while producing recollections of similar vividness. Primary analysis: temperature estimates by condition. The mean subjective room temperature estimate across the whole sample was 21.8°C (SD = 3.4). Participants in the inclusion condition reported a mean estimated temperature of 22.0°C (SD = 3.2), while participants in the exclusion condition reported a mean of 21.6°C (SD = 3.7). The independent-samples t-test indicated that this difference was not statistically significant (t(122) = 0.46, p = .65, Cohen’s d = 0.08). The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference ranged from -0.94°C to 1.66°C, indicating that any true difference in the population is likely to be small. The Bayesian t-test yielded a Bayes Factor BF10 = 0.26, which can be interpreted as moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis relative to the alternative (i.e., the data were approximately 3.8 times more likely under the null than under the alternative). ANCOVA controlling for negative affect and study location. Given that the exclusion manipulation increased negative affect, we conducted an ANCOVA to test whether condition affected temperature estimates after adjusting for negative affect and study location (lab vs. online). In the ANCOVA, neither recall condition (F(1,119) = 0.20, p = .66) nor negative affect (F(1,119) = 1.02, p = .31) were significant predictors of temperature estimate. Study location was also not a significant predictor (F(1,119) = 0.11, p = .74). The overall model accounted for a negligible portion of the variance in temperature estimates (adjusted R2 = -0.01). Moderation analysis: extraversion as a moderator. We tested whether extraversion moderated the effect of recall condition on temperature estimates using a multiple regression. The regression included dummy-coded condition (0 = inclusion, 1 = exclusion), centered extraversion score, and the interaction term. The main effect of condition was non-significant (β = 0.04, SE = 0.09, t = 0.46, p = .65). The main effect of extraversion was also non-significant (β = -0.08, SE = 0.07, t = -1.14, p = .26). The interaction between condition and extraversion was not significant (β = -0.06, SE = 0.13, t = -0.47, p = .64). Probing simple slopes at ±1 SD of extraversion revealed non-significant slopes in both low-extraversion (β = 0.10, p = .48) and high-extraversion (β = -0.06, p = .73) groups. The full model explained minimal variance in temperature estimates (R2 = .01). Correlations within conditions. Pearson correlations between extraversion and subjective temperature estimate were computed separately within each condition. In the inclusion condition, the correlation was r = -0.09 (p = .46), and in the exclusion condition it was r = -0.03 (p = .79). Neither correlation reached statistical significance and both suggested negligible relationships. Exploratory analyses: mood, vividness, and actual room temperature. We examined whether the vividness of the recalled event correlated with temperature estimates. Vividness showed a non-significant correlation with temperature estimate (r = 0.05, p = .57). Negative affect was unrelated to subjective temperature (r = -0.09, p = .29). For the laboratory participants for whom actual room temperature was recorded (n = 104), the correlation between actual room temperature and subjective estimate was r = .42 (p < .001), indicating that participants’ subjective estimates did reflect actual ambient temperature to some extent. When actual room temperature was included as a covariate in the regression analyses, the null effects of condition and its interaction with extraversion remained. Sensitivity checks removing the small number of online participants (n = 12) produced virtually identical results, with no significant difference between conditions (t(110) = 0.49, p = .63) and no significant moderation by extraversion (interaction p = .59). Effect size estimation and confidence intervals. The observed effect size for the condition difference was small (Cohen’s d = 0.08), with the 95% confidence interval for the effect size including zero and being consistent with trivial effects. Given our sample size, the study had adequate power to detect a medium effect (d = 0.50) but limited power to reliably detect small effects. Thus, the absence of a significant effect suggests that if a true effect exists in the population, it is likely to be small. Reliability and validity of measures. The extraversion scale displayed good internal consistency (α = .86), suggesting reliable measurement of the trait. The manipulation check items indicated that the recall instructions functioned as intended to elicit differential social affect. The moderate positive correlation between actual recorded room temperature and subjective temperature estimates provides some evidence of validity for the subjective temperature measure; nonetheless, subjective estimates are influenced by additional factors beyond the actual ambient temperature, which could include momentary affective state or cognitive bias. Summary of results. In sum, although the recall manipulation successfully induced differences in subjective feelings of inclusion and exclusion, we did not observe the hypothesized difference in subjective room temperature estimates between inclusion and exclusion conditions. Extraversion did not moderate the effect. Exploratory and sensitivity analyses did not uncover any meaningful effects or interactions. Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis for the primary comparison. These results indicate that in our sample and with our procedures, recalling social exclusion did not produce a detectable change in subjective temperature estimates relative to recalling social inclusion.

Discussion

This study set out to replicate a previously reported finding linking recalled social exclusion to lower perceived ambient temperature and to examine whether extraversion moderates this effect. The recall manipulation reliably altered participants’ subjective feelings of inclusion and exclusion and their immediate affective responses. However, contrary to our primary hypothesis and some prior reports, we did not observe a significant difference in subjective room temperature estimates between participants who recalled social exclusion and those who recalled social inclusion. Moreover, extraversion did not moderate the relationship between recall condition and perceived temperature. Our Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis for the primary comparison, and exploratory analyses did not reveal hidden effects. These null findings prompt careful consideration of their implications and possible explanations. One interpretation is that the previously reported effect linking social exclusion recall to perceived ambient temperature may be less robust or more context-dependent than initially thought. Replication failures are not uncommon in social psychology, particularly for effects that may be subtle or sensitive to procedural nuances. Failure to replicate does not necessarily imply that the original effect was false; it could indicate that boundary conditions or moderators that were present in the original work were not present here. It is therefore important to identify factors that might moderate the phenomenon and to consider alternative explanations. Several methodological factors could account for the lack of effect in the present study. First, the nature of the recall manipulation and the content of the recalled events may differ across studies. Although our manipulation successfully induced differential affect, the specific physical sensations recalled by participants may have varied in ways that did not systematically influence current thermal perception. The recall method relies on autobiographical memory, which is inherently variable. Some participants may have recalled social exclusion incidents that were primarily emotional rather than physical, and thereby did not elicit embodied thermal sensations. In such cases, the manipulation would not be expected to influence current temperature estimates. Second, the measure of perceived temperature in the current study was a self-report estimate in degrees Celsius. This measure depends on participants’ ability to map their subjective sense of warmth or cold to an abstract numeric scale, which may introduce noise. While our data show that subjective estimates were moderately correlated with actual recorded room temperature, subjective reporting could still be influenced by cognitive biases or lack of precision. Alternative measurement approaches—such as continuous analog scales of perceived warmth, psychophysical measures, or objective physiological indices like skin temperature—might be more sensitive to subtle embodied changes. Third, the laboratory context and experimenter expectations might have differed from those in prior studies. Small differences in instructions, timing, or environmental cues could alter participants’ attentiveness to bodily sensations or their willingness to report them. The present study included an online component for a small fraction of participants, though sensitivity analyses excluding these participants produced similar null results; nonetheless, other subtle contextual differences remain possible. Fourth, sample characteristics may play a role. Our sample consisted primarily of university students in a particular cultural and temporal context. It is possible that the embodied link between social exclusion and perceived temperature varies across demographic groups, cultural backgrounds, or age cohorts. Studies conducted in different societies, or with more diverse samples, might observe different patterns. Fifth, the effect size may be smaller than originally estimated. Our a priori power calculations were based on a medium effect size. If the true effect is smaller, our study may have been underpowered to detect it. Though our Bayesian analysis provided moderate support for the null, the possibility of a small effect cannot be definitively ruled out without larger samples designed to detect small effects. Turning to the moderation hypothesis involving extraversion, the present null result suggests that extraversion, at least as measured by a trait inventory, may not substantially influence the link between recalled social exclusion and subjective temperature estimates. Several interpretations are possible. It may be that extraversion influences some aspects of social cognition and affective response but not embodied thermal perception. Alternatively, the trait-level measure may not capture the dynamic aspects of social engagement or situational social coping strategies that would be relevant for buffering against the embodied experience of exclusion. Other individual differences, such as attachment style, rejection sensitivity, or interoceptive awareness, might be stronger moderators and warrant investigation. The absence of an interaction also indicates that the embodied effect, if present, is not moderated by broad tendencies toward sociability in a straightforward manner. The theoretical implications for embodied cognition are nuanced. Theories that posit a direct and ubiquitous grounding of social concepts in bodily experience must accommodate cases where such grounding is not reliably observed. Our results do not refute the broader idea that bodily states can influence social cognition, but they underscore that such effects may be contingent on specific conditions—such as the immediacy and sensory vividness of the social experience, the mode of inducing social feelings (e.g., live exclusion vs. recall), or the sensitivity of the measurement methods. Some embodied effects might emerge robustly when bodily states are directly manipulated (for example, by holding a warm object) rather than when they are induced through cognitive recall. Distinguishing between these routes—direct sensory manipulation versus evocative recall—may be crucial for theoretical accounts. Our findings also have implications for methodological best practices. First, replication attempts should incorporate objective measures of ambient temperature and physiological indices when feasible. Prior work that relied solely on subjective estimates may have conflated conceptual priming with genuine embodied sensation. In our study, subjective estimates correlated with recorded ambient temperature, suggesting some validity, but the primary hypothesis concerned relative differences between groups rather than absolute accuracy. Second, future work should pre-register hypotheses and analysis plans and consider using larger samples to detect small effects or to provide more decisive Bayesian evidence. Third, researchers should consider multi-method approaches that combine self-report, behavioral, and physiological measures to triangulate effects. Fourth, attention should be paid to potential moderators beyond broad personality traits; variables such as interoceptive sensitivity, cultural background, and the nature of social ties (e.g., family vs. peers) could shape embodied responses. There are several concrete directions for future research that would help clarify the phenomenon. One avenue would be to compare different elicitation methods for social exclusion—such as online ball-tossing tasks (e.g., Cyberball) or staged live interactions—with autobiographical recall to determine whether the mode of induction affects embodied outcomes. Another approach would be to manipulate physical temperature directly (e.g., via hand warming) following social exclusion manipulations to test bidirectional influences between social warmth and physical warmth. Studies that include continuous physiological monitoring—skin conductance, peripheral skin temperature, and heart rate variability—during and after social manipulations could reveal subtle embodied processes that subjective estimates miss. Longitudinal or repeated-measures designs could assess whether chronic experiences of social exclusion accumulate to produce stable changes in thermal perception or physiological regulation. Additionally, exploring individual differences such as interoceptive accuracy, which can be measured via heartbeat detection tasks, might identify people who are more likely to report embodied sensations in response to social stimuli. It would also be informative to investigate cultural variation in temperature metaphors and embodied responses. Cross-cultural comparisons could reveal whether societies that linguistically link warmth to social closeness also show stronger embodied correspondences. Addressing publication bias and the replicability crisis requires that both successful and failed replications be published and integrated into meta-analytic syntheses. Our null findings emphasize the importance of transparency and cumulative evidence in evaluating embodied social effects. In interpreting our results, it is worth noting that the recall manipulation did affect participants’ reports of feeling excluded and their negative affect, confirming that the manipulation produced the intended psychological state. The absence of a corresponding effect on temperature suggests that the mapping from social affect to thermal perception may not be automatic, or at least not uniformly elicited by cognitive recall. It is possible that certain visceral experiences of exclusion—such as those that involve physical isolation or changes in ambient conditions—are more likely to produce felt temperature changes than purely cognitive recollections. In conclusion, this study failed to replicate the previously reported effect linking recalled social exclusion to lower perceived ambient temperature and found no evidence that extraversion moderates this relationship. The recall manipulation successfully altered subjective social affect, demonstrating that the social manipulation per se was effective. However, the lack of effect on temperature estimates suggests that the embodied mapping between social coldness and physical temperature may be more limited, context-dependent, or smaller in magnitude than earlier reports implied. Future research should employ multi-method measurement, larger and more diverse samples, and manipulations that directly probe bodily states and interoceptive sensitivity to clarify the conditions under which social experiences influence thermal perception. By systematically examining boundary conditions and potential moderators, the field can better understand when and why language-based metaphors of warmth and cold correspond to genuine embodied sensations.
