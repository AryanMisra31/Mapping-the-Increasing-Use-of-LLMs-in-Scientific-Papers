Introduction

Work productivity has long been a central focus of research in organizational psychology because of its importance for individual careers, team functioning, and organizational success. Researchers have sought to understand the dispositional and situational determinants of productivity because insights about these factors can inform both selection and management practices. Personality traits constitute one central class of dispositional factors that have been repeatedly linked to performance across contexts. In parallel, situational factors such as incentives, feedback, and recognition have been shown to influence motivation and performance, and there is a rich literature on how these factors can interact with personality dispositions. Despite a substantial body of work on both personality and situational drivers of productivity, there remain important gaps in our understanding of how specific trait configurations shape responses to recognition and, ultimately, influence measurable output on standardized tasks. The present study was designed to address these gaps by examining relations between three traits emphasized in Eysenckian theory—extraversion, psychoticism, and neuroticism—and performance on a brief computational/attentional work analog, and by testing whether a simple manipulation of recognition altered productivity or moderated trait–performance relations.

Extroversion has been associated with a range of work-related outcomes in prior research. Extroverts tend to be energetic, socially assertive, and approach oriented, and these tendencies often translate into greater engagement with social and task demands. In many organizational contexts, extroversion has been linked to higher levels of performance, particularly in roles that require social interaction, persuasion, or rapid responsiveness. Laboratory studies have also reported that extroverted individuals sustain effort on tasks that afford stimulation or social visibility more readily than their introverted counterparts. At the same time, however, extroversion does not uniformly predict better performance across all task types, and some studies suggest that extroverts may be less meticulous on monotonous tasks unless other incentives or social cues are present.

Psychoticism, a trait associated in Eysenck’s theory with toughness, nonconformity, and impulsivity, has a more complex relation with work outcomes. High psychoticism has been linked to risk-taking and lower cooperativeness, and in some contexts this trait has been associated with poorer adherence to norms or procedures that underpin consistent productivity. On the other hand, certain facets of psychoticism, such as independence and a tolerance for ambiguity, might correlate positively with creative problem solving or with willingness to take initiative. The existing literature therefore suggests the possibility that psychoticism might relate negatively to productivity on routine or rule-bound tasks while potentially relating positively to tasks that reward unorthodox strategies. However, empirical tests that examine psychoticism in relation to simple measures of output remain limited.

Neuroticism is characterized by emotional instability, anxiety-proneness, and susceptibility to stress. High neuroticism is often associated with poorer performance under pressure, reduced persistence, and greater variability in output. On the other hand, neurotic individuals sometimes show heightened attention to threat or error and may therefore perform adequately on tasks that require careful scrutiny, at least when anxiety does not incapacitate performance. Prior research suggests that neuroticism often predicts absenteeism and lower job satisfaction and, in some cases, reduced productivity. The net effect of neuroticism on short, standardized measures of productivity remains an empirical question sensitive to task demands and motivational context.

Recognition and feedback are widely used situational levers for boosting worker motivation. Studies across behavioral economics and organizational science have shown that acknowledgment of effort, feedback that signals social appreciation, and public recognition can produce measurable increases in effort and output. For example, some laboratory experiments have demonstrated that participants who expect to receive public acknowledgment for their contributions increase their output relative to conditions in which their efforts remain anonymous. Such findings support models in which social image concerns and reciprocity motives amplify intrinsic or extrinsic drivers of effort. However, the effects of recognition appear to be moderated by individual differences and by the perceived sincerity and immediacy of the recognition.

There is also evidence that recognition interacts with personality in shaping motivation. Extroverts, who are more socially oriented, may be particularly sensitive to acknowledgment and may show larger increases in effort when recognition is expected. Conversely, introverts or individuals high in psychoticism may be indifferent to or even averse to public recognition. Neurotic individuals might respond to recognition in variable ways depending on whether acknowledgment diminishes or heightens evaluative anxiety. Despite these plausible interactions, the extant empirical literature provides relatively few direct tests combining Eysenckian traits and manipulations of social recognition in relation to objective measures of productivity. Most studies either focus on trait effects in the absence of manipulation or test situational effects in samples that do not include fine-grained personality measurement.

Studies using simple, countable output tasks have advantages for isolating motivational influences on productivity because they yield direct, easily interpretable measures of quantity of output. Behavioral economists have made extensive use of such tasks to study effort, honesty, and strategic behavior. One influential paradigm involves a letter-search or worksheet completion task in which participants complete standardized sheets and the number of completed sheets serves as the dependent variable. This form of measurement minimizes ambiguity about what constitutes “productivity” in the experimental context and allows researchers to measure fine-grained differences in output as a function of trait or situational variables.

The present study makes use of such a worksheet completion paradigm adapted from prior behavioral research. Participants were administered a standardized set of sheets and were permitted to complete as many as they wished under timed conditions. Two conditions were created to manipulate recognition. In a recognized (acknowledged) condition, participants were told that their individual performance would be acknowledged publicly and that the experimenters would convey appreciation for completed work. In an ignored condition, participants were told that performance would remain anonymous and that no acknowledgment would be forthcoming. These manipulations were designed to simulate differences in anticipated social recognition that are common in workplace settings.

The study’s primary questions were threefold. First, do dispositional scores on extraversion, psychoticism, and neuroticism predict productivity as measured by the number of completed worksheets? Second, does manipulating anticipated recognition affect productivity? Third, do the Eysenckian traits moderate the effect of recognition on productivity, such that extroverts respond more to recognition while psychoticism or neuroticism levels attenuate or reverse recognition effects? Together, these questions aim to illuminate how stable dispositional differences intersect with a simple social-cognitive manipulation to shape effortful output.

I hypothesized that extroversion would be positively associated with productivity because extroverts are more socially oriented and may be more willing to expend visible effort, particularly when social evaluation is salient. I expected psychoticism to be negatively associated with productivity in this routine task context because impulsivity and low conformity should reduce adherence to a repetitive task. Neuroticism was predicted to be negatively related to productivity given the trait’s association with anxiety and reduced persistence under stress. Regarding the manipulation, I hypothesized that acknowledged participants would complete more sheets than ignored participants, and that there would be a significant interaction between extroversion and condition such that acknowledged extroverts would show the largest increases in productivity. These hypotheses were grounded in theory and prior empirical observations linking social recognition to motivation and extroversion to social responsiveness and approach behaviors.

There are several reasons why this line of inquiry is important. First, organizations routinely use recognition systems to motivate workers; a better understanding of who benefits most from such practices would enable more targeted interventions. Second, extant personality–productivity work often focuses on Big Five traits in field settings and gives less attention to Eysenckian dimensions that capture important aspects of sociality and nonconformity. Third, testing these relations in a controlled laboratory environment allows us to estimate the magnitude of trait effects on standardized output free from many confounds present in the field. Finally, exploring the potential moderating role of personality can reveal whether some individuals require different forms of social or institutional reinforcement to perform optimally.

There are also methodological considerations that warrant mention. The chosen measures and manipulations are brief and economical, making replication and further study feasible. The EPQ-R short form was selected for its specific coverage of extraversion, psychoticism, and neuroticism and for its historical relevance to theory. The worksheet completion task provides an objective, count-based dependent variable that reflects sustained effort in a narrow domain.

At the same time, the laboratory context imposes limits on the generalizability of findings. College student samples and brief tasks may not capture the full complexity of workplace roles where tasks are more varied and recognition systems more nuanced. Nevertheless, a laboratory demonstration that extroversion predicts output and that recognition has an effect would be a valuable initial step. Conversely, a null finding for recognition would suggest that simple acknowledgment is insufficient to alter effort, at least in the context studied, and would point to boundary conditions for the effectiveness of recognition systems.

In sum, the study was designed to test theoretically motivated hypotheses about trait and situational determinants of productivity. By combining a well-established personality inventory with a straightforward behavioral task and a clear recognition manipulation, the study intended to provide evidence bearing on both basic theory and practical management questions. The expectation was that extroversion would emerge as a positive predictor of productivity, psychoticism and neuroticism would predict lower productivity, and that recognition would increase output, with extroversion moderating the recognition effect.

Methods

Participants were 180 undergraduate students recruited from a large urban university participating for course credit and a small performance-based bonus. The sample consisted of 180 participants after excluding two individuals who failed an attention check embedded in the questionnaire battery. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 34 years with a mean age of 20.6 years (SD = 2.4). The sample included 102 women and 78 men. Participants provided informed consent and were told that the study concerned personality and task performance. The study complied with institutional ethical standards and all data were collected in a single session lasting approximately 30 minutes.

Design was between-subjects with a binary manipulation of recognition (acknowledged vs ignored) and continuous predictors of interest corresponding to extroversion, psychoticism, and neuroticism scores derived from the EPQ-R Short form (EPQR-S). Recognition condition was randomly assigned using a computerized randomization sequence in equal proportion, producing 90 participants in the acknowledged condition and 90 participants in the ignored condition. The primary dependent variable was the number of completed worksheets in a fixed work period. Secondary measures included self-reported perceived value of one’s labor, which was assessed after the task to probe subjective appraisal of task worth.

Personality measurement was conducted using the EPQR-S, which yields separate scores for extraversion, psychoticism, and neuroticism. The instrument was administered in paper form and completed prior to the task. Scale scores were computed according to standard scoring procedures. The EPQR-S was selected because of its concise assessment of the Eysenckian dimensions relevant to the study aims. In addition to the trait scales, a brief set of demographic questions and an attention-check item were included to ensure data quality.

The task used to measure productivity was a worksheet completion paradigm adapted from established letter-search and worksheet tasks used in behavioral research. Participants were provided with identical sheets containing a matrix of characters and a set of instructions to mark target items and then move on to the next sheet. Each completed sheet could be turned in for a small monetary payment (a token amount) to maintain motivation. The sheet design required sustained attention and speed; accuracy was monitored but not strictly enforced beyond basic compliance. Participants were told that they had 10 minutes to complete as many sheets as possible, and that they could stop at any time and claim the payment for the last sheet turned in.

The recognition manipulation was delivered at the outset of the task phase following random assignment. Participants in the acknowledged condition were told that their completed work would be publicly acknowledged at the end of the session and that their name and the number of sheets they completed would be posted on a results board in the hallway and that the experimenter would verbally thank all high performers. This information was provided in a friendly manner and accompanied by a short scripted expression of appreciation to ensure consistency. Participants in the ignored condition were told that their work would remain anonymous and that no public acknowledgment or posting would occur; they were told only that they would receive payment for completed work and that data would be aggregated anonymously. Care was taken to match the amount of procedural information given to both groups while varying only the presence versus absence of anticipated recognition.

After receiving instructions, participants completed a brief practice sheet to familiarize themselves with the task. The 10-minute work period then commenced, and participants completed as many sheets as they wished. Experimenters monitored compliance unobtrusively and collected completed sheets. At the end of the session participants completed a short questionnaire that included the perceived value of their labor (rated on a Likert scale), affective state measures, and manipulation-check items probing whether participants expected their work to be acknowledged.

All experimenters were trained to deliver instructions and the recognition script consistently across sessions. Scripts were used to minimize variation in tone or content. The order of the EPQR-S and other questionnaire items prior to the task was fixed to avoid pre-task mood alterations. The session concluded with debriefing, explanation of the manipulation, and distribution of payments.

Data preparation involved scoring the EPQR-S scales and calculating descriptive statistics for all key variables. Internal consistency of the EPQR-S scales was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The primary dependent variable, number of completed sheets, was inspected for normality and outliers. Cases with extreme values beyond three standard deviations on the number of sheets were checked for accuracy and retained if attributable to legitimate performance differences. Correlations among trait scores and the dependent variable were computed first to provide a bivariate view of relations.

Primary hypothesis tests were conducted using independent-samples t tests comparing acknowledged and ignored conditions on number of completed sheets. Hierarchical multiple regression analyses were then employed to test the unique and combined effects of the three personality traits and the recognition condition on productivity. The base regression model included only control variables (age and gender). The second step added the three trait scores. The third step added recognition condition as a dummy-coded predictor. The fourth step included interaction terms between recognition and each trait to test moderation hypotheses. Predictor variables were standardized prior to creating interaction terms to aid interpretability and minimize multicollinearity. Variance inflation factors were inspected to ensure that multicollinearity did not bias parameter estimates.

Secondary analyses examined whether perceived value of labor mediated any observed relations and whether manipulation checks confirmed participant sensitivity to the recognition manipulation. Mediation analyses used bootstrapped confidence intervals to test indirect effects when appropriate. Effect sizes were reported using Cohen’s d for t tests and standardized betas and R-squared change for regression steps. All statistical tests were conducted using standard software with alpha set at .05 for hypothesis tests and two-tailed testing. Assumptions of normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity were evaluated through inspection of residual plots and formal tests where relevant.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The mean number of completed sheets across the full sample was 6.87 with a standard deviation of 3.21. In the acknowledged condition the mean number of completed sheets was 7.10 (SD = 3.18) and in the ignored condition the mean was 6.64 (SD = 3.24). The EPQR-S extraversion scores had a mean of 12.4 (SD = 3.5), psychoticism had a mean of 8.1 (SD = 3.2), and neuroticism had a mean of 11.6 (SD = 3.6). Cronbach’s alpha for the extraversion scale was .82, indicating good internal consistency. Cronbach’s alpha for the psychoticism scale was .68, which is acceptable for exploratory work but lower than desirable. Cronbach’s alpha for the neuroticism scale was .79. The manipulation check indicated that 85% of participants in the acknowledged condition correctly reported expecting public acknowledgment, whereas 90% of participants in the ignored condition correctly reported expecting anonymity. The majority of participants rated the manipulation as believable on a post-task item.

An independent-samples t test compared the number of completed sheets between the acknowledged and ignored conditions. The difference was not statistically significant, t(178) = 1.08, p = .28, Cohen’s d = 0.16. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference ranged from -0.85 to 1.45 sheets. Given the non-significant between-group difference, the simple main effect of recognition on productivity was not supported in the sample.

Correlations among the personality scales and the number of completed sheets are instructive. Extraversion correlated positively with number of completed sheets (r = .31, p < .001). Psychoticism correlated negatively with number of completed sheets, although the relation was smaller and non-significant at conventional levels (r = -.11, p = .14). Neuroticism showed a small negative correlation with number of completed sheets (r = -.09, p = .22). The three EPQR-S scales exhibited modest intercorrelations: extraversion and psychoticism correlated at r = -.04 (p = .56), extraversion and neuroticism correlated at r = -.18 (p = .01), and psychoticism and neuroticism correlated at r = .07 (p = .34).

A hierarchical multiple regression was then conducted to examine the unique contributions of the traits and recognition to predicting the number of completed sheets. In the first step, control variables age and gender were entered. This base model accounted for 2.3% of the variance in productivity (R^2 = .023), which was not significant, F(2,177) = 2.10, p = .12. In the second step, the three standardized trait scores were added. This addition produced a significant increase in explained variance, ΔR^2 = .081, p < .001, with the step accounting for an incremental 8.1% of variance. In this model extraversion emerged as a significant positive predictor (standardized β = .29, t = 4.12, p < .001). Psychoticism had a small negative beta (β = -.09, t = -1.28, p = .20) and neuroticism had a small negative beta (β = -.06, t = -0.89, p = .37). The overall model after Step 2 accounted for R^2 = .104 of variance in completed sheets and was statistically significant, F(5,174) = 4.03, p = .002.

In the third step, recognition condition (dummy coded: 1 = acknowledged, 0 = ignored) was added to the model. The addition of recognition did not produce a significant increase in explained variance, ΔR^2 = .004, p = .27. Recognition itself was a non-significant predictor (β = .06, t = 1.07, p = .29) when entered alongside the trait predictors. The full model through Step 3 accounted for R^2 = .108 of the variance in the number of completed sheets, F(6,173) = 3.49, p = .003.

To test moderation hypotheses, interaction terms between recognition condition and each trait were entered in the fourth step. The three interaction terms together did not significantly improve model fit, ΔR^2 = .008, p = .19. Examination of individual interaction coefficients indicated that the interaction of extraversion and recognition was non-significant (β = .07, t = 1.12, p = .26). The psychoticism × recognition and neuroticism × recognition interactions were likewise non-significant (ps > .30). Thus, there was no evidence that the recognition manipulation differentially affected participants as a function of their trait levels.

Residual diagnostics were examined for the final model. Residuals were approximately normally distributed and there was no evidence of severe heteroscedasticity. Variance inflation factors for all predictors were below 2.1, indicating that multicollinearity was not a serious concern. Sensitivity analyses excluding a small number of extreme scores (three cases with completed sheets above three standard deviations) yielded virtually identical patterning of results; extraversion remained a significant predictor while recognition remained non-significant.

Given the hypothesized role of perceived value of labor as a potential mediator, secondary analyses tested whether perceived value was associated with extraversion, recognition, and productivity. Perceived value correlated modestly with extraversion (r = .16, p = .03) and with number of completed sheets (r = .21, p = .005). However, adding perceived value to the regression model did not substantively change the extroversion coefficient, and mediation analysis using bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) produced a non-significant indirect effect of extraversion on productivity via perceived value (95% CI included zero). Thus, perceived value did not statistically mediate the extroversion–productivity link in the present dataset.

Additional exploratory analyses examined potential gender differences in trait–productivity relations. Interaction terms between gender and extraversion were entered and found to be non-significant (p = .43), suggesting that the extroversion effect was not substantially different across men and women in this sample. Age did not interact with any of the traits to predict productivity.

Effect size estimates indicate that extroversion accounted for approximately 7.2% of the variance in productivity when entered alone in a bivariate regression and contributed uniquely to the multivariate model accounting for just under 9% of incremental variance in Step 2. These effect sizes are small to moderate in magnitude but are meaningful for short, controlled laboratory tasks in which many sources of variance are attenuated. The lack of a recognition effect suggests that the manipulation as operationalized did not have the hypothesized impact on output under these conditions.

Overall, the results indicate that extraversion was a robust positive predictor of the number of sheets completed, while psychoticism, neuroticism, and the recognition manipulation were not significant predictors in multivariate models. The hypothesized interaction between extraversion and recognition was not supported. Discussion

The present study examined the relations between three Eysenckian personality traits—extraversion, psychoticism, and neuroticism—and productivity on a standardized worksheet completion task, and tested whether a manipulation of anticipated recognition influenced output. The principal findings are clear and theoretically informative. Extraversion emerged as a reliable positive predictor of the number of completed sheets. Psychoticism and neuroticism did not significantly predict productivity in multivariate models, and the manipulation of recognition produced no main effect and did not interact with the personality dimensions to influence performance. Taken together, these results provide empirical support for the role of extraversion in motivating greater effort on a simple labor-like task, while casting doubt on the potency of a mild recognition manipulation under the conditions tested.

The finding that extraversion was positively associated with productivity converges with a body of literature suggesting that extraverted individuals are more willing to expend effort in social or stimulating contexts. Extroverts’ energy, approach orientation, and proclivity for engagement may have translated into greater task persistence and speed during the timed worksheet period. The effect size observed—a modest but consistent relation accounting for a meaningful portion of variance—suggests that personality, even as a broad factor, can have measurable consequences for short-term output in controlled settings. The extroversion result is theoretically consistent with models that describe extraverts as seeking stimulation and social feedback and as being relatively resilient to monotony when a motivational frame is present.

It is notable that extraversion predicted productivity even though the recognition manipulation was not effective. This suggests that extroverts’ greater output was not merely a reaction to anticipated social acknowledgement as manipulated in the study. One interpretation is that extraverts may be more generally oriented toward action and reward-seeking, which leads them to produce more regardless of the specific presence of public recognition. Another possibility is that extraverts were internally motivated by self-presentation concerns or by an intrinsic preference to be productive in an experimental setting, and these motivations were not captured by the specific recognition manipulation employed.

The absence of significant effects for psychoticism and neuroticism requires careful interpretation. Psychoticism’s hypothesized negative relation with productivity was not robust in our sample. While psychoticism correlated modestly and negatively with output in bivariate analyses, the association did not reach statistical significance in multivariate models. One reason may be that psychoticism’s behavioral consequences are more pronounced in contexts that strictly sanction conformity or require fine-grained rule following; the simple worksheet task may not have provided a sufficiently salient normative frame to draw out psychotic tendencies. In addition, the psychoticism scale’s internal consistency was lower than that for the other scales, which may attenuate observed relations. Future research using more comprehensive measures of psychoticism or alternative operationalizations of impulsivity and nonconformity could clarify this trait’s relation with productivity.

Neuroticism likewise did not predict fewer completed sheets at a statistically significant level. This outcome may reflect the particular demands of the task and sample. The short duration and low-stakes nature of the worksheet activity might not have elicited the degree of stress or performance pressure under which neuroticism’s deleterious effects typically emerge. In real-world work settings, where errors have higher costs and deadlines may be more severe, neuroticism may exert a more pronounced negative impact. The lack of a neuroticism effect in the present study therefore does not negate its possible relevance in other contexts.

The recognition manipulation’s null effect is a central finding that merits exploration. Despite being designed to mirror common workplace recognition practices, the manipulation did not significantly alter objective output. Several explanations are possible. First, the manipulation may have been too weak to change behavior. Telling participants that their names and performance would be posted in a hallway and that experimenters would thank high performers may not generate sufficient social evaluative pressure to change effort, particularly among a sample of university students who may have limited concern for brief public posting in an experimental corridor. Second, participants may have distrusted the believability or value of the promised acknowledgment; although manipulation checks suggested the majority believed the instructions, the practical salience of the acknowledgment may have been perceived as low. Third, the worksheet task may not be responsive to recognition because its effort–reward structure is dominated by immediate intrinsic engagement or by small monetary rewards, leaving little room for added variance from social recognition.

It is also possible that recognition effects are more nuanced and depend on the nature of acknowledgment. Public posting and verbal thanks may have different motivational implications than private praise, symbolic rewards, or tangible incentives. Previous literature suggests that the effectiveness of recognition is moderated by factors such as perceived sincerity, timing, visibility to valued referents, and cultural norms regarding modesty or self-promotion. The single-dimensional manipulation used here does not speak to these subtleties, and future research should examine different forms and intensities of recognition to determine boundary conditions.

Another important issue concerns the hypothesized interaction between extraversion and recognition. Although theory suggested that extraverts would be more responsive to expected acknowledgment, this interaction was not supported. This null finding could be due to the absence of a substantive main effect of recognition; interactions are difficult to detect without a reliable main effect or sufficient statistical power. It is also conceivable that extraverts’ responsiveness to social cues operates more via real-time feedback or direct interpersonal exchange rather than via a general announcement of posting after the session. More potent or immediate social recognition, such as a live, public commendation during the task, might elicit differential effects by trait.

Several methodological limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. First, the sample consisted of undergraduate students, and thus generalizability to occupational populations is limited. Student samples may vary in motivational priorities compared with employees, and the salience of public posting in a lab corridor is likely different from recognition on a workplace floor. Second, the laboratory task was intentionally simple and brief; while this design affords control and straightforward measurement of output, it constrains inferences to tasks of similar cognitive and motivational demands. Third, the psychoticism scale exhibited lower internal consistency in this sample, which could reduce the ability to detect relations involving that trait. Fourth, the recognition manipulation may not have captured the multifaceted nature of how organizations recognize contributions. Finally, the incentive structure included a small monetary component that may have interacted with recognition in complex ways; disentangling the relative influence of monetary and social incentives warrants additional study.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. It combined a theoretically grounded personality assessment with an objectively measured productivity outcome and a randomized experimental manipulation. The use of hierarchical regression allowed examination of unique trait effects controlling for demographic covariates and of potential moderation by recognition. The internal consistency of the extraversion and neuroticism scales was adequate, and manipulation checks indicated that participants generally understood the recognition conditions as intended.

Theoretical implications of the findings center on the robustness of extraversion’s relation to short-term productivity. The results align with conceptions of extraversion as an approach-oriented disposition that fosters engagement and output in contexts where quick, energetic responding is advantageous. The lack of recognition effects suggests caution in assuming that simple acknowledgment will boost productivity in all settings. Managers and organizational designers should consider that personality predispositions may be stronger determinants of spontaneous effort than low-intensity recognition practices, at least in some contexts.

Future research should pursue several directions. First, field studies in organizational environments could examine whether the extraversion–productivity link observed in the lab generalizes to real tasks and extended timeframes. Longitudinal designs could reveal whether extraversion predicts sustained performance and whether recognition systems have cumulative effects over time. Second, experimental work should test different forms and intensities of recognition, including immediate versus delayed acknowledgment, private versus public praise, and symbolic versus material recognition. Such manipulations could clarify which forms of recognition interact with personality and under what conditions.

Third, measurement considerations invite the use of alternative or supplementary personality instruments. Incorporating Big Five measures alongside Eysenckian scales would allow comparisons across trait frameworks and might reveal complementary predictive patterns. Fourth, it would be valuable to investigate mediators and processes, such as perceived esteem, task engagement, affective arousal, and social image concerns, to better understand why extraversion predicts output and why recognition may or may not influence effort.

Fifth, research could examine subgroup differences and boundary conditions, such as cultural norms that shape responses to public acknowledgment or job types that differentially reward visible effort. Sixth, interventions could be designed that tailor recognition practices to individuals’ dispositional profiles, testing whether matching the form of recognition to personality enhances productivity more than a one-size-fits-all approach.

Practical implications for organizations are cautiously optimistic about the utility of considering personality in selection and management. While it would be simplistic to advocate hiring only extraverts, understanding that certain roles and tasks may naturally favor extraverted dispositions can inform placement and task allocation decisions. At the same time, organizations should recognize that informal or low-intensity recognition practices may not reliably increase productivity for all workers and that more substantive or individualized recognition systems may be necessary to produce meaningful changes in output.

In conclusion, the study found that extraversion predicted greater productivity on a brief, standardized worksheet task, while psychoticism, neuroticism, and a simple recognition manipulation did not significantly influence output. These findings highlight the importance of dispositional factors in shaping short-term effort and suggest that the motivational power of recognition may be more circumscribed and context-dependent than sometimes assumed. Building on these results, future research should explore stronger and more varied forms of recognition, broader and more diverse samples, and the psychological mechanisms that link personality to productivity.
