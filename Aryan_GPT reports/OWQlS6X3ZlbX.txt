Introduction

Cognitive performance varies across tasks, individuals, and contexts, and understanding the sources of that variation has long been a central aim of cognitive psychology. One long-standing theme in this field concerns the role of individual differences in cognitive style, which refers to relatively stable preferences and habitual modes of processing information. Cognitive style constructs have been used to characterize whether people tend to rely more on analytical, deliberative reasoning processes or on more intuitive, experiential processes. A popular instantiation of this distinction is the dual-process framework that contrasts reflective, rule-governed, slow processing with automatic, affect-laden, fast processing. Although dual-process models vary in their theoretical commitments, they commonly posit that these two modes of processing operate with different characteristic speeds, degrees of awareness, and susceptibilities to interference. The idea that some individuals habitually favor one mode over the other—often called cognitive style—has motivated questions about how style relates to performance on cognitive tasks that place different demands on speed, attention, and conflict resolution.

One methodological tool that has been widely used to probe basic aspects of attention, automaticity, and response conflict is the colour-naming Stroop task. In the classic Stroop paradigm, participants are required to name the ink colour of words that can either be congruent with the semantic content of the word (e.g., the word "red" printed in red ink) or incongruent (e.g., the word "red" printed in blue ink). The robust observation that naming responses are slower and more error-prone on incongruent trials than on congruent trials has been taken as evidence for automatic semantic activation interfering with goal-directed response selection. The Stroop effect is often characterized as a relatively pure measure of the cost of conflict between an automatic tendency (reading the word) and a deliberately cued response (naming the ink colour), and it therefore provides a useful assay for the interplay between automatic and controlled processing.

Because the Stroop task juxtaposes fast, automatic semantic processing with slower, controlled response selection, it seems particularly apt for examining whether individual differences in cognitive style relate to basic speeded perceptual and response processes. If some individuals habitually rely more on experiential, intuitive processing, while others habitually rely more on rational, analytical processing, then their performance patterns on tasks that differentially tax automatic versus controlled processes might differ in predictable ways. For instance, experiential thinkers might show smaller Stroop interference because they are faster at integrating perceptual and affective cues, or experiential thinkers might be more susceptible to interference because they more readily activate meaning and associated automatic responses. Conversely, rational thinkers might slow down and apply deliberate strategies to overcome interference, resulting in smaller or similar interference depending on task dynamics. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive and require empirical testing.

A common self-report instrument used to quantify individual differences in preference for intuitive versus analytic processing is the Rational-Experiential Inventory (REI). The REI separates self-reported tendencies into two scales: experiential processing, which taps preference for intuition, affective responsiveness, and reliance on impressions; and rational processing, which taps preference for deliberation, logic, and reflective thinking. Individuals differ on both scales, and the combination of scores can yield profiles such as high experiential/low rational, high rational/low experiential, and so on. Importantly, the REI is a trait measure of preferential processing, not a state measure of momentary cognitive operations. Thus, it is particularly useful for investigating whether habitual thinking styles are associated with stable differences in performance across tasks like the Stroop.

Another individual difference that has attracted interest in the context of speeded perceptual and cognitive tasks is biological sex. Researchers have sometimes reported sex differences in speeded tasks, with small to moderate female advantages on certain verbal and processing-speed measures and mixed outcomes on tasks involving spatial transformations and executive control. In the context of the Stroop task, some studies have observed that females can be slightly faster at colour naming, although this is not a universal finding and effect sizes tend to be modest. These observed sex differences raise the question of their underlying causes. One plausible route is that habitual differences in cognitive style between sexes might drive performance differences. For example, if females tend to score higher on measures of experiential processing, and if experiential processing confers an advantage on fast, perceptual tasks such as color naming, then experiential cognitive style could mediate the observed sex difference in Stroop performance.

The idea that cognitive style could mediate sex differences in cognitive performance is attractive because it links dispositional tendencies with behavioural outcomes. However, this mediation hypothesis depends on at least two empirical claims: first, that sex differences exist in REI scales (for instance, that females report greater experiential thinking), and second, that REI scores are predictive of Stroop performance in a manner that could account for the sex difference. Both claims have been examined to varying degrees in prior literature, with inconsistent patterns. Reports of sex differences on the REI experiential scale are mixed, and the extent to which self-reported experiential tendency translates into behavioural speed or interference on tasks like the Stroop is uncertain. There are plausible reasons for a null relation: self-report preferences may not reflect moment-to-moment processing choices in a speeded perceptual task, or the Stroop task might depend more on basic perceptual and motor processes than on the style of higher-order thinking.

Beyond mediation, the relationship between cognitive style and Stroop performance could also take other forms. Cognitive style might moderate sex differences in Stroop performance, such that sex differences only emerge among individuals with particular cognitive profiles. Alternatively, both sex and cognitive style could independently contribute to variance in Stroop performance without a mediational relation. For example, sex might influence motor response speed due to physiological differences, and cognitive style might independently influence how quickly an individual tends to commit to a response strategy. Disentangling these possibilities requires a careful empirical approach that assesses both sex and cognitive style within the same sample and employs analyses that can test for mediation, moderation, and independent contributions.

Given these considerations, the present study sought to investigate whether different cognitive styles, measured by the REI, affect colour naming performance and whether cognitive style is a viable explanation for previously reported female advantages in colour naming in the Stroop task. The study was designed to provide a clear test of three interrelated questions. The first question concerned the basic Stroop effect: would participants, overall, be quicker at colour naming in congruent conditions than in incongruent conditions? This is an essential replication of a robust phenomenon and serves as a baseline check for task validity. The second question concerned sex differences and their relation to cognitive style: would females rely more on experiential thinking as indexed by the REI, and would this reliance relate to faster overall colour naming performance? The third question directly addressed the mediation hypothesis: are experiential thinkers different in their Stroop performance relative to rational thinkers, and do REI scores statistically account for sex differences in colour naming speed?

To address these questions, we used a standard color-naming Stroop task with separate congruent and incongruent blocks, and we administered the REI to quantify experiential and rational processing preferences. Our analytic strategy included both group comparisons and regression-based mediation analyses to assess whether REI scales could account for sex differences in reaction time. We also examined interactions between sex and cognitive style to determine whether the relation between REI scores and Stroop performance differed by sex. The sample size was chosen to provide reasonable power to detect medium-size effects on group comparisons and to estimate reliably the size of correlations between REI scores and reaction time measures.

The predicted outcomes were straightforward. We anticipated a robust Stroop effect characterized by longer reaction times on incongruent trials relative to congruent trials. We also expected to observe that females would report higher experiential processing scores than males and that females would perform somewhat faster on the Stroop task overall. Regarding the mediation hypothesis, several scenarios were possible: if experiential thinking substantially facilitated rapid color naming, then controlling for experiential scores should reduce the magnitude of the sex difference in reaction time; conversely, if experiential scores did not predict Stroop performance, then the sex difference would persist when experiential scores are included in the model. Alternatively, experiential thinking could operate as a moderator rather than a mediator, selectively affecting the size of the Stroop effect in one sex. Testing these alternatives in a single coherent study contributes to understanding both the sources of Stroop performance variation and the behavioural relevance of cognitive style measures.

In addition to testing the central hypotheses, the current study probed the possibility that the connection between REI and Stroop performance might be contingent on the congruency of trials. That is, experiential processing might be more relevant for congruent trials where automatic reading aligns with the response, whereas rational processing might be more engaged during incongruent trials that require overriding an automatic tendency. If so, then interactions between REI scales and condition (congruent vs. incongruent) could emerge. Evaluating such interactions required a within-subjects design for condition and between-subjects variation in REI scores. Accordingly, the present design embedded congruent and incongruent blocks within a speeded colour-naming task while capturing individual differences in experiential and rational processing via the REI instrument.

Methodological rigor was further enhanced by specifying clear preprocessing rules for reaction time data and by verifying that observed effects were not driven by outliers or by a small subset of participants. We applied standard exclusions for anticipatory responses and unusually long responses, and we examined both raw and trimmed means to ensure robustness. Analyses included repeated-measures ANOVA to test the within-subjects Stroop effect and its interactions with between-subjects factors, t-tests to examine sex differences on REI scales and mean reaction times, and regression-based mediation models to probe whether REI scores could statistically mediate sex differences in reaction time. Where appropriate, effect sizes were reported to aid interpretation.

In summary, the present research sought to integrate approaches from individual differences and experimental cognitive psychology to clarify whether cognitive style, as measured by the REI, contributes to variability in Stroop colour-naming performance and whether cognitive style accounts for reported sex differences in speeded colour naming. The study's multi-pronged analytic approach was intended to adjudicate whether the putative female advantage in color naming is attributable to dispositional differences in experiential versus rational processing, or whether alternative explanations are more consistent with the data. The following sections detail the methods employed, present the results, and discuss the implications of the findings.

Methods

Participants

One hundred and twenty adult participants were recruited from a university community and the surrounding area. The sample comprised 60 females and 60 males, all aged between 18 and 35 years. Participants were screened to ensure normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal colour vision using a brief self-report screener to exclude those with colour blindness. Inclusion criteria required fluent English comprehension because the Stroop stimuli involved colour words in English. Participants received course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to testing. Demographic information including age, sex, handedness, and level of education was collected via a short questionnaire. The final sample included predominantly right-handed individuals, but handedness was not used as an exclusion criterion; it was recorded to allow post hoc examination if necessary.

Materials

Rational-Experiential Inventory. Participants completed the Rational-Experiential Inventory (REI), a self-report measure designed to assess individual differences in preference for rational and experiential thinking. The REI in this study contained two subscales: Experiential Engagement (reflecting preference for and trust in intuition) and Rational Engagement (reflecting preference for analytic, deliberative reasoning). Each subscale comprised 20 items, and items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Scores for each subscale were obtained by summing the relevant items and then converting to a standardized scale for ease of interpretation. Higher scores indicated greater self-reported preference for that mode of processing. Reliability of each subscale was assessed by computing Cronbach's alpha for the current sample prior to hypothesis testing.

Colour-naming Stroop task. The Stroop task was presented on a desktop computer using experimental presentation software. Stimuli were colour words ("RED," "BLUE," "GREEN," "YELLOW") displayed in capital letters and in one of the four ink colours corresponding to the words. There were two condition types: congruent trials, where the word meaning matched the ink colour (e.g., "RED" in red ink), and incongruent trials, where the word meaning did not match the ink colour (e.g., "RED" in blue ink). Each participant completed two blocks, one consisting entirely of congruent trials and the other consisting entirely of incongruent trials. Block order was counterbalanced across participants to control for order effects. Each block contained 80 trials, yielding a total of 160 experimental trials per participant. On each trial, a fixation cross appeared at the centre of the screen for 500 ms, followed by the stimulus word which remained on screen until the participant responded or until a 2000 ms timeout. Participants were instructed to vocalize the ink colour as quickly and accurately as possible; voice key recordings were used to register response onset times. A short practice block of 16 trials (mixed congruent and incongruent) preceded the experimental blocks to familiarize participants with the task and the voice key.

Procedure

Participants were tested individually in a quiet laboratory room. After providing informed consent, participants completed the demographic questionnaire followed by the REI on a computer. The order of REI administration and the Stroop task was fixed so that the REI was always completed before the Stroop task to avoid any potential short-term task-induced changes in self-report responding. After completing the REI, participants received standardized instructions for the Stroop task. The instructions emphasized both speed and accuracy and explicitly asked participants to respond by naming the ink colour, not the word itself. Participants were told that some trials might be difficult and that occasional errors were acceptable, but they were encouraged to balance speed and accuracy.

A brief microphone test was conducted to ensure proper voice key functioning. The practice block then commenced and experimenters monitored voice key accuracy during practice and adjusted microphone sensitivity if needed. After practice, participants completed the two experimental blocks with a short rest break between blocks. Block order (congruent first or incongruent first) was assigned randomly for each participant. The experimenter remained in the testing room but out of view of the participant to monitor the session and to note any technical issues. After task completion, participants were debriefed and given the opportunity to ask questions before receiving credit or payment.

Data preprocessing and exclusion criteria

Reaction time (RT) data were preprocessed to exclude trials with incorrect responses and trials with voice key failures. Trials with RTs less than 200 ms were considered anticipatory and excluded, and trials with RTs greater than 2000 ms were considered timeouts and were also excluded. Following these basic exclusions, individual participant means were computed separately for congruent and incongruent blocks using remaining correct trial RTs. Participants with fewer than 75% valid trials in either block were excluded from further analyses; in the current sample, this led to the exclusion of two participants whose voice key malfunctioned during the session, resulting in a final sample size of 118 participants (59 females, 59 males). Outliers at the participant level were examined by calculating z-scores for congruent and incongruent mean RTs; no participant had an RT z-score exceeding ±3 SD on both conditions, so no further exclusions were applied. Both raw mean RTs and trimmed mean RTs (with the upper and lower 5% of trial RTs removed per participant) were inspected to ensure consistency of results; reported analyses use raw means after the exclusions described above, but key analyses were re-run with trimmed means to verify robustness.

Statistical analysis plan

The primary dependent measure was mean RT for correctly named trials in each condition (congruent and incongruent). The primary within-subjects predictor was condition (congruent vs. incongruent), and the primary between-subjects predictors were sex (female vs. male) and REI scores (Experiential and Rational). REI scores were analyzed both as continuous variables and as categorical groupings based on a median split to facilitate comparison between experiential-dominant and rational-dominant individuals. Preliminary analyses assessed the internal consistency of the REI subscales. Initial tests included a repeated-measures ANOVA with condition as a within-subjects factor and sex as a between-subjects factor to test the Stroop effect and sex differences. Subsequent analyses included correlations between REI scores and RT measures, t-tests comparing REI scores between sexes, and regression analyses to examine whether REI scores predicted RT while controlling for sex and whether REI scores mediated the relation between sex and RT using bootstrapped confidence intervals for the indirect effect. Interaction terms between REI scores and condition were tested within mixed-effects models to evaluate whether cognitive style differentially predicted performance across congruent and incongruent trials. Effect sizes such as partial eta-squared for ANOVAs and Cohen's d for t-tests were computed to aid interpretation. All statistical tests used an alpha level of .05 for significance, and where assumptions of parametric tests were in question, nonparametric alternatives were used as checks.

Results

Descriptive statistics and reliability

The final sample comprised 118 participants, with 59 females and 59 males. Mean age was 22.6 years (SD = 3.8) and did not differ significantly by sex. The REI experiential subscale had high internal consistency in this sample, Cronbach's alpha = .88, and the rational subscale also exhibited good reliability, Cronbach's alpha = .85. Mean scores (standardized) on the Experiential subscale were higher for females (M = 3.47, SD = 0.52) than for males (M = 3.21, SD = 0.49). An independent-samples t-test confirmed that this sex difference on the Experiential scale was statistically significant, t(116) = 2.75, p = .007, Cohen's d = 0.51. In contrast, mean scores on the Rational subscale did not differ substantially between females (M = 3.44, SD = 0.48) and males (M = 3.41, SD = 0.50), t(116) = 0.42, p = .67, Cohen's d = 0.08. These descriptive results indicate that, in this sample, females reported greater experiential thinking tendencies than males, whereas rational tendencies were comparable across sexes.

Stroop performance overview

Mean reaction times for correct responses were computed separately for congruent and incongruent blocks for each participant. Across all participants, mean RT for congruent trials was 528 ms (SD = 64 ms) and mean RT for incongruent trials was 641 ms (SD = 82 ms). The within-subjects Stroop effect, calculated as the difference between incongruent and congruent means, averaged 113 ms (SD = 48 ms). A repeated-measures ANOVA with condition (congruent vs. incongruent) as the within-subjects factor yielded a highly significant main effect of condition, F(1,117) = 546.45, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .82. This robust Stroop effect confirmed that the task elicited the expected interference effect and that reaction time measures were sensitive to the manipulation.

Sex differences in Stroop performance

To examine whether there were overall sex differences in Stroop reaction times, we conducted a repeated-measures ANOVA with condition as the within-subjects factor and sex as a between-subjects factor. The main effect of sex on mean RTs was significant, F(1,116) = 6.48, p = .013, partial eta-squared = .053, indicating that females were generally faster than males across conditions. The mean RT for females collapsed across conditions was 578 ms (SD = 70 ms), whereas the mean for males was 591 ms (SD = 72 ms). However, the interaction between condition and sex was not significant, F(1,116) = 1.22, p = .27, partial eta-squared = .010, suggesting that the magnitude of the Stroop effect (the difference in RT between incongruent and congruent blocks) did not differ reliably between males and females. Post hoc comparisons revealed that females were significantly faster than males on congruent trials, t(116) = 2.17, p = .032, Cohen's d = 0.40, with mean congruent RTs of 517 ms (SD = 60 ms) for females and 539 ms (SD = 66 ms) for males. On incongruent trials, the sex difference was smaller and not statistically significant, t(116) = 1.52, p = .13, Cohen's d = 0.29, with mean incongruent RTs of 639 ms (SD = 79 ms) for females and 643 ms (SD = 84 ms) for males.

Relation between REI scores and Stroop performance

We next examined whether REI scales predicted Stroop reaction times. Pearson correlations indicated a modest negative correlation between Experiential scores and overall mean RT, r = -.21, p = .023, suggesting that individuals who reported greater experiential preference tended to be somewhat faster in the colour-naming task. The Rational subscale was not significantly correlated with overall mean RT, r = .06, p = .49. When correlations were computed separately for congruent and incongruent conditions, the Experiential score correlated more strongly with congruent RTs (r = -.26, p = .005) than with incongruent RTs (r = -.12, p = .19). These patterns indicate that self-reported experiential preference was associated with faster colour naming especially when the stimulus meaning aligned with the response.

We also conducted mixed-effects regression analyses with trial condition (coded congruent = 0, incongruent = 1) as a within-participant predictor and Experiential and Rational scores as between-participant predictors, controlling for sex. In this model, the Experiential score had a significant main effect on RT, b = -21.8 ms per standardized unit, SE = 8.3, t = -2.63, p = .009, indicating that higher experiential preference predicted faster responses overall. The interaction between Experiential score and condition was significant at a trend level, b = 12.4 ms, SE = 6.9, t = 1.80, p = .074, indicating some tendency for experiential preference to be more strongly associated with faster congruent responding than with incongruent responding. Rational scores had no significant main effect or interactions in these models.

Median-split group comparisons

To examine whether experiential-dominant versus rational-dominant individuals differed in Stroop performance, participants were divided into groups using a median split on the Experiential minus Rational difference score, creating an experiential-dominant group (n = 59) and a rational-dominant group (n = 59). An ANOVA with condition (congruent vs. incongruent) as within-subjects factor and cognitive style group as between-subjects factor revealed a significant main effect of condition, F(1,116) = 542.30, p < .001, and no significant main effect of cognitive style group, F(1,116) = 2.01, p = .16. The interaction between condition and cognitive style group was not significant, F(1,116) = 1.61, p = .21. These results suggest that, when considered categorically, experiential versus rational groups did not differ reliably in the magnitude of Stroop interference, although continuous analyses had suggested a modest relationship with mean RTs primarily on congruent trials.

Mediation analysis: Does Experiential thinking account for sex differences?

Given that females scored higher on Experiential preference and that Experiential scores correlated with faster RTs, we tested whether Experiential scores mediated the observed sex difference in RT. Following standard mediation procedures, we first confirmed the paths: sex predicted Experiential score, b = 0.26 standardized units, SE = 0.10, t = 2.60, p = .01; Experiential score predicted RT after controlling for sex, b = -19.6 ms per standardized unit, SE = 8.1, t = -2.42, p = .017; and sex predicted RT in the absence of Experiential, b = -13.2 ms (females faster), SE = 4.86, t = -2.72, p = .007. A bootstrapped mediation analysis with 5,000 resamples estimated the indirect effect of sex on RT via Experiential score at b = -5.1 ms with a 95% bias-corrected confidence interval of [-12.9, 1.6] ms. Because this confidence interval included zero, the indirect effect was not statistically significant at the .05 level. The direct effect of sex on RT after controlling for Experiential score remained significant, b = -8.1 ms, SE = 3.9, t = -2.08, p = .039. Thus, although Experiential preference was a modest predictor of faster RT and females reported higher Experiential scores, Experiential did not statistically account for the sex difference in Stroop speed.

Additional checks and robustness analyses

To ensure that the main effects were not driven by outliers or by differences in error rates, we examined accuracy across conditions and groups. Overall accuracy was high, with mean correct response rates of 96% for congruent trials and 92% for incongruent trials. Error rates did not differ significantly by sex or by Experiential group, and when errors were included as a covariate in the RT analyses, the pattern of results remained substantively unchanged. Repeating the primary analyses with trimmed mean RTs (5% trimming) and with log-transformed RTs to address skewness produced comparable results, including a robust Stroop effect, a small but significant female advantage in overall speed, a modest correlation between Experiential score and congruent RTs, and a non-significant mediation effect. Overall, the results were robust to reasonable analytic variations.

Summary of key findings

Across analyses, three main patterns emerged. First, the classic Stroop effect was replicated strongly: participants were substantially faster on congruent trials than on incongruent trials. Second, females showed a small but reliable speed advantage in colour naming overall and were faster particularly on congruent trials. Third, females reported higher experiential processing tendencies on the REI, and Experiential scores modestly predicted faster colour naming (especially on congruent trials), but Experiential preference did not statistically mediate the sex difference in speed. In categorical analyses using median splits, experiential and rational groups did not differ in Stroop interference magnitude. Taken together, these findings indicate that while experiential thinking relates to faster naming in some contexts, it does not appear to be the primary cause of females' faster colour-naming performance in this sample.

Discussion

The present study set out to examine the relations among cognitive style, sex, and performance on a colour-naming Stroop task. Broadly speaking, the results provided a nuanced picture. First, the Stroop effect—a fundamental phenomenon of interference between automatic word reading and instructed colour naming—was strongly replicated, providing a solid foundation for interpreting individual differences. Second, there was a modest but reliable sex difference in reaction time, with females responding faster on average than males, particularly in the congruent condition. Third, female participants reported higher experiential processing tendencies on the Rational-Experiential Inventory, and higher experiential preference was associated with somewhat faster colour naming, especially when the word meaning aligned with the ink colour. Crucially, however, experiential preference did not mediate the sex difference in reaction time: although females were more experiential and experiential preference related to faster responses, the indirect effect was small and statistically non-significant, and the direct effect of sex remained after controlling for experiential scores. Additionally, categorical comparisons between experiential-dominant and rational-dominant groups did not reveal differences in Stroop interference magnitude. These results suggest that while cognitive style is related to behaviour in speeded perceptual tasks, it does not fully explain the observed female advantage in colour-naming speed in the present sample.

Interpreting the relation between experiential thinking and Stroop performance requires careful consideration of what the REI captures and how that trait might map onto speeded task behaviour. The REI experiential scale indexes a dispositional preference for intuitive, affect-laden, and impression-based processing. Individuals high on this scale report greater trust in gut feelings and a greater tendency to rely on affective cues. In the current study, those higher in experiential preference were somewhat faster at naming colours, particularly on congruent trials. This pattern is interpretable in at least two complementary ways. One possibility is that experiential thinkers possess a generally faster mode of responding for tasks that interface smoothly with automatic semantic activation, such as congruent Stroop trials where reading and naming are aligned. Another possibility is that those high in experiential style are less likely to impose deliberative checks on their responses and therefore produce faster output when the stimulus-response mapping does not require conflict resolution. Both interpretations suggest that experiential preference may be associated with quicker deployment of response tendencies in contexts that favor automatic or fluent responding.

Despite this relation between experiential preference and speed, the mediation analysis indicated that experiential style does not account for the female speed advantage. This outcome indicates that while females reported higher experiential preference, the magnitude of the association between experiential preference and response speed was insufficient to statistically explain the sex difference. In practical terms, the sex difference in mean RT decreased only slightly when experiential score was included as a covariate and remained statistically significant. Thus, the female advantage in colour-naming speed cannot be reduced to simple dispositional differences in self-reported cognitive style. This result underscores an important caveat when linking self-report measures of cognitive style to task performance: modest correlations do not necessarily imply causal mediating relations.

The finding that the sex difference was most evident in congruent trials provides a clue to possible alternative mechanisms. Because congruent trials align automatic reading with the instructed response, the speed advantage observed for females in this condition could reflect faster perceptual encoding, faster vocal response initiation, or subtler motoric differences rather than differences in conflict resolution per se. The congruent-specific advantage suggests that processes upstream of conflict monitoring and inhibition might contribute. For example, if females in this sample had, on average, faster vocal response initiation times or more efficient translation of perceptual colour information into speech motor commands, these mechanisms could produce faster congruent responses and a corresponding small or negligible effect on interference magnitude. These interpretations are speculative and would require targeted measurement of motor response latencies or the incorporation of non-verbal response modalities to adjudicate the contribution of motoric versus cognitive factors.

Another consideration is that the REI assesses a trait-level preference that may not map directly onto the momentary strategies deployed in a lab task. An individual might generally prefer intuitive processing in everyday life while nevertheless adopting a deliberative approach in a specific experimental context if instructed to prioritize accuracy or to attend closely to task demands. In the current study, participants were explicitly instructed to prioritize both speed and accuracy, and this instruction may have attenuated the expression of dispositional tendencies in actual task performance. In other words, the REI may predict behaviour in more naturalistic or ambiguous situations where strategy choice is left to the individual, but laboratory tasks with clear instructions and constrained responses may reduce variance attributable to cognitive style.

It is also notable that categorical grouping of participants into experiential-dominant and rational-dominant categories via median split did not yield reliable differences in interference magnitude. This null finding is consistent with the regression results showing only a modest continuous association between experiential preference and congruent RT. The lack of categorical differences cautions against using median splits or other dichotomization methods to detect effects that are, in reality, dimensional and modest in magnitude. Continuous variation in REI scores appears to capture small predictive information, but arbitrary binning may obscure rather than illuminate these relations.

The current findings have implications for dual-process theories linking automatic and controlled processing modes with habitual thinking styles. Dual-process frameworks often imply that individuals who favor experiential processing may be more susceptible to automatic influences but also quicker when automatic processes are aligned with goals. The present results correspond to this complexity: experiential preference was associated with faster responding in congruent situations where automatic processes are beneficial, but it did not exacerbate interference in incongruent situations, nor did it moderate the magnitude of the Stroop effect in a strong way. This pattern suggests that experiential propensity may influence the baseline speed of automatic or fluent responding more than the capacity to resolve conflict when automaticity conflicts with task goals. Consequently, the role of cognitive style as conceived by dual-process models may be more about biasing the likelihood of relying on one processing mode over another in ambiguous contexts, rather than determining the strength of automatic interference in tightly controlled laboratory tasks.

The robust Stroop effect in this study confirms the utility of the paradigm for studying automaticity and interference, but it also highlights that individual differences in REI and marginal sex differences in speed are relatively small in comparison to the within-person effect of congruency. In practical terms, the bulk of variance in colour-naming RT was accounted for by task condition rather than by between-subjects differences. This observation underscores that task manipulations designed to elicit large within-person effects are often more powerful than individual difference measures for explaining variance in lab-based performance.

There are several methodological limitations to note. The sample was relatively homogeneous in age and educational background, being drawn primarily from a university participant pool, which may limit generalizability. The voice key method for measuring vocal response onsets can be sensitive to microphone placement and participant articulation variability; although we took care to calibrate the equipment and to exclude sessions with malfunctioning hardware, residual measurement noise could have attenuated individual difference correlations. Additionally, the REI is a self-report instrument and may be subject to social desirability or introspective limits; using behavioural or physiological measures that index propensity for intuitive versus analytic processing could provide convergent evidence. Finally, the cross-sectional design does not permit causal inference; mediation analyses based on observed covariation are informative but do not establish that one variable causes changes in another.

Future research could fruitfully pursue several avenues. One approach would be to incorporate additional measures of motor response speed and sensorimotor translation to disentangle perceptual and motoric contributions to sex differences in speeded tasks. For example, using non-verbal response measures such as button presses keyed to colour patches would reduce the vocal motor component and might reveal whether sex differences persist when vocal initiation is not a factor. Another promising direction would be to use process-level methods such as mouse-tracking, electrophysiology, or eye tracking to capture the temporal unfolding of processing and to determine whether experiential preference shapes early perceptual encoding or later decisional processes. Longitudinal or training studies that experimentally manipulate reliance on experiential versus rational processing could test causal hypotheses about whether habitually favoring one processing mode alters speeded response tendencies on tasks like the Stroop.

It would also be informative to examine whether the association between experiential preference and faster congruent responding generalizes to other tasks in which automaticity supports performance, such as lexical decision tasks, simple semantic categorization, or affective priming paradigms. If experiential preference consistently predicts faster performance in contexts where automatic processes align with task demands, this would support the notion that experiential thinkers have a behavioural signature of fluency rather than heightened susceptibility to interference. Conversely, if experiential preference predicts greater interference or error rates in tasks requiring inhibitory control, that would indicate a dual-edged role of experiential processing.

Cultural and developmental factors may also moderate the relations observed here. Cognitive style and its expression in behaviour could vary by cultural norms around intuition and deliberation, educational training, or age-related shifts in processing speed and strategy use. Including more diverse samples and comparing across age cohorts could shed light on the stability and boundary conditions of the relations among sex, cognitive style, and speeded performance. Additionally, hormonal influences such as menstrual cycle phase or circulating sex hormones have been implicated in cognitive performance variability and could be explored as moderators of sex differences in speeded tasks.

The current study focused on simple congruent versus incongruent blocks; future research could explore mixed-trial designs or manipulations of proportion congruency to assess strategic adjustments and flexible control. In mixed designs, where congruent and incongruent trials appear unpredictably, individuals might adopt different control strategies that reveal stronger individual differences linked to cognitive style. If experiential thinkers favor rapid, heuristic responses, they might perform differently under mixed conditions where strategic modulation is required.

In conclusion, this study contributes to understanding how cognitive style relates to basic speeded cognitive performance and to the question of whether cognitive style accounts for observed sex differences in colour-naming speed. We replicated the robust Stroop effect, found that females were somewhat faster in colour naming and reported higher experiential preferences, and observed a modest relation between experiential preference and faster congruent responding. Importantly, experiential preference did not mediate the sex difference in reaction time, indicating that other factors are likely at play in producing the observed female advantage. Future work that incorporates motoric measurements, process-tracing methods, experimental manipulations of processing mode, and broader samples will be valuable for further unpacking these relations and for clarifying the behavioural implications of dispositional cognitive styles.
