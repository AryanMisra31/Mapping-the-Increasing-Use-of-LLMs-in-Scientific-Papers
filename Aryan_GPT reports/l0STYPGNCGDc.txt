Introduction

Body image concerns and disturbances are widely recognized as significant psychological issues with implications for mental health, interpersonal functioning, and quality of life. Across diverse cultures and age groups, individuals report dissatisfaction with specific body parts, apprehension about weight and shape, and engagement in behaviors aimed at altering appearance. Researchers have repeatedly linked these disturbances to a range of social and environmental influences, among which media exposure has been foregrounded as a potent contributor. Media environments present highly curated and idealized images of bodies, lifestyles, and relationships, and such portrayals can establish salient comparison standards that shape viewers' self-perceptions. The mechanisms by which media exposure exerts influence on body image have been described through a variety of theoretical perspectives, including social comparison theory, objectification theory, and cultivation theory. Social comparison theory posits that individuals evaluate themselves relative to accessible comparison targets, which in modern environments often include market-driven, digitally altered images of thinness, muscularity, and beauty ideals. Objectification theory suggests that constant exposure to objectified portrayals of people, particularly women, facilitates self-objectification where individuals habitually view their bodies as objects evaluated by others, which in turn heightens body surveillance and dissatisfaction. Cultivation theory emphasizes the cumulative effect of repeated media portrayals in shaping norms and expectations about appearance and social roles, implying that prolonged exposure could consolidate maladaptive appearance standards over time. Empirical work has built upon these frameworks to document robust associations between media consumption patterns and body image disturbance, especially among women. Numerous experimental and correlational studies have demonstrated that exposure to thin-ideal or sexualized media content can temporarily increase body dissatisfaction, reduce self-esteem, and elicit negative mood. Meta-analytic reviews have indicated that the effect sizes for media exposure on body dissatisfaction are meaningful and often more pronounced for female samples than for male samples. Beyond direct effects of media on body image, researchers have also investigated how relational and attitudinal variables interact with media influences. One line of inquiry has examined the role of romantic beliefs and relationship ideals as predictors or moderators of body image concerns. Romantic beliefs, which encompass expectations about the nature of romantic relationships, the importance of romantic fulfillment, and idealization of mates and partnership dynamics, may shape how individuals appraise their desirability and attractiveness within relationship contexts. For example, individuals who endorse idealized romantic beliefs that place high emphasis on physical attractiveness as a cornerstone of relationship success may be more sensitive to appearance-based pressures and thus more vulnerable to body dissatisfaction. Conversely, those with more pragmatic or less appearance-focused romantic beliefs might exhibit resilience against appearance-related messaging. Some prior empirical work suggests that lower ideals about relationships, or more pessimistic beliefs about romantic outcomes, can predict higher body dissatisfaction, although the direction and robustness of these associations have varied across studies. For instance, one study reported that women who rated romantic ideals more strongly exhibited worse body image over time, whereas another suggested that romantic hopelessness could lead to disengagement from self-care and thus increase body concerns. Given this mixed picture, further research is needed to delineate the relationships among romantic beliefs, media exposure, and body image. Importantly, gender differences in the strength and nature of these relationships have been reported but not fully characterized. Historically, most work has concentrated on women due to higher average levels of reported body dissatisfaction and a greater prevalence of some eating disorders. However, more recent investigations have highlighted that men also experience body image concerns, often focused on muscularity and leanness, and that men’s media consumption patterns may exert different effects than those observed for women. Some studies have found that the relationship between media exposure and body image is weaker or different in form for men compared to women, potentially because male-targeted media emphasize different body ideals or because cultural norms afford men different appraisal frameworks. Taken together, the literature points to a need to examine simultaneously media exposure, romantic beliefs, and body image within the same sample, and to investigate whether gender moderates these associations. Correlational designs can reveal the degree to which these constructs covary in everyday life, and statistical tests of moderation can indicate whether associations differ for men and women. Such work is valuable not only for theoretical refinement but also for informing intervention strategies that might target media literacy, relationship expectations, or gender-specific vulnerabilities. The present research responds to these needs by examining correlational relationships among romantic beliefs, body image, and media exposure in an adult sample spanning young adulthood into middle age. The study focuses on self-reported exposure to media, endorsement of romantic beliefs, and reported body image disturbance, each measured with validated multi-item questionnaires anchored on seven-point Likert scales to capture variance in attitudes and experiences. The age range of participants was selected to include a broad range of adult developmental stages, thereby allowing for examination of age-related variation in these constructs while prioritizing generalizability beyond college student samples. The primary hypothesis, drawn from prior research indicating stronger media–body image links among women, was that media exposure would be positively associated with body dissatisfaction overall and that this association would be stronger in women than in men. A secondary hypothesis, informed by work suggesting that romantic beliefs can shape body-related outcomes, was that romantic beliefs would be associated with body image, with more idealized or appearance-focused romantic beliefs predicting higher body dissatisfaction. A tertiary consideration was whether romantic beliefs would moderate the media–body image association, with the expectation that the influence of media exposure on body dissatisfaction might be amplified among those high in conventional or idealized romantic beliefs. To examine these hypotheses, we conducted correlational analyses, gender-stratified correlations, and hierarchical multiple regressions including interaction terms to test moderation by gender and by romantic beliefs. We also examined simple demographic controls, particularly age, to determine whether associations held independent of age-related variation in media use and body concerns. Given the cross-sectional and correlational nature of the design, we emphasized interpretation in terms of association rather than causation, while acknowledging that consistent patterns across subgroups can provide meaningful evidence about differential vulnerability. The study contributes to a growing literature seeking to clarify the social and relational determinants of body image disturbance and to identify subgroups for whom media influences are particularly salient. Specifically, it provides a test of whether previously reported relationships between media exposure and body dissatisfaction replicate in a broader adult sample, and whether romantic beliefs independently account for variation in body image or interact with media exposure to shape outcomes. Establishing that media exposure is more strongly associated with negative body image among women would reinforce calls for gender-sensitive media literacy interventions and for policies addressing media representations of bodies. Conversely, evidence that romantic beliefs play a central role would suggest the potential utility of relationship-focused cognitive interventions that reframe idealized expectations. By integrating these lines of inquiry, the present study seeks to advance theory and applied practice related to body image, media effects, and interpersonal cognition.

Methods

Participants were recruited from community and university settings through online advertisements and flyers over a three-month period. A total of 246 adult participants completed all study measures and were included in the analyses. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 56 years, with a mean age of 28.4 years and a standard deviation of 8.1 years. The sample comprised 158 women and 88 men. Recruitment materials invited adults to participate in a study examining social perceptions and media behaviors, and participants were compensated with either course credit when eligible or a small monetary voucher for their time. All participants provided informed consent prior to beginning the survey, and procedures were approved by the supervising institutional review board. Data were collected via an online survey platform that required complete responses to each questionnaire section before proceeding, ensuring that the dataset contained no missing responses for the primary variables. Questionnaire completion took approximately 20 to 30 minutes on average. The study administered three primary measures in the following order: a Media Exposure Scale, a Body Image Scale, and a Romantic Beliefs Scale, followed by demographic questions. Each of the three primary measures used seven-point Likert response formats where higher scores indicated greater endorsement of the construct. The Media Exposure Scale consisted of 10 items assessing the frequency of exposure to appearance-focused media content, the amount of attention paid to body-related media portrayals, and the perceived influence of media on personal ideals. Items included prompts such as “I frequently view media that emphasizes physical attractiveness” and “I pay close attention to how people’s bodies look in magazines, television, and social media.” Participants rated items from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The scale was designed to capture both quantity and qualitative engagement with media. The internal consistency of the Media Exposure Scale in the present sample was acceptable, with a Cronbach’s alpha of .86. The Body Image Scale comprised 12 items tapping core aspects of body dissatisfaction, including negative evaluations of appearance, preoccupation with weight and shape, and avoidance behaviors related to body image. Representative items were “I am unhappy with my body shape” and “I avoid social situations because of concerns about how my body looks.” Responses were provided on a seven-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The Body Image Scale was scored so that higher values indicated greater body dissatisfaction. Reliability in the current sample was high, with a Cronbach’s alpha of .90. The Romantic Beliefs Scale involved 8 items measuring the extent to which participants endorsed idealized views of romantic relationships, expectations about partner appearance and chemistry, and beliefs about the centrality of romantic fulfillment to life satisfaction. Sample items included “A romantic relationship should be passionate and intense” and “Physical attraction is essential for the success of a romantic relationship.” Again, responses were on a seven-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating stronger romantic idealization. The Romantic Beliefs Scale showed acceptable internal consistency in this sample (Cronbach’s alpha = .77). Demographic variables gathered included age, gender, education level, and self-reported ethnicity. For gender, participants selected male or female; given the study’s focus on gender differences and the available sample sizes, analyses reported here compare those identifying as female and male. For descriptive purposes, education was collapsed into three categories (high school or less, some college or vocational training, and bachelor’s degree or higher). Statistical analyses were pre-registered prior to data inspection. Primary analyses consisted of Pearson product-moment correlations among the three main variables in the full sample and stratified by gender. Fisher r-to-z transformations were used to compare correlation magnitudes between the female and male subgroups. A series of hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted with body image as the outcome variable. In the first step of the regression models, age and gender were entered as control variables. In the second step, Media Exposure and Romantic Beliefs scores were introduced to test their unique contributions. In the third step, two interaction terms were added: Gender x Media Exposure and Gender x Romantic Beliefs, to test if the associations differed by gender. Regression predictors were mean-centered prior to creating interaction terms to reduce multicollinearity. We also ran a complementary model replacing Gender with Romantic Beliefs as a moderator to examine whether beliefs moderated the impact of media exposure irrespective of gender (i.e., Media Exposure x Romantic Beliefs interaction). Statistical significance was evaluated at the .05 alpha level. Effect sizes were reported as standardized regression coefficients (beta) for regression analyses and as Pearson r for correlations, with 95% confidence intervals provided where appropriate. Assumptions of parametric testing were checked prior to hypothesis testing. Inspection of variable distributions revealed acceptable skewness and kurtosis for Media Exposure, Body Image, and Romantic Beliefs, and visual inspection of Q-Q plots and histograms indicated adequate normality for the purposes of Pearson correlations and linear regression. Residual diagnostics for the regression models indicated no substantial heteroscedasticity or nonlinearity. Multicollinearity diagnostics showed variance inflation factor (VIF) values below 2.0 for all predictors, indicating multicollinearity was not a concern. In addition to the primary planned analyses, we conducted sensitivity checks including partial correlations controlling for age, and subgroup analyses within narrower age bands to assess whether patterns were robust across developmental stages. We also conducted exploratory t-tests comparing mean levels of the three constructs across genders to provide descriptive context for the correlational findings. All analyses were performed using standard statistical software. Data, syntax, and code used to generate the reported results are archived and available upon request, subject to ethical restrictions related to participant confidentiality.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the full sample indicated that mean Media Exposure score was 4.12 (SD = 1.15) on the seven-point scale, mean Body Image score was 3.89 (SD = 1.24), and mean Romantic Beliefs score was 4.01 (SD = 0.98). When examined by gender, women reported a mean Media Exposure of 4.28 (SD = 1.12) and a mean Body Image score of 4.10 (SD = 1.22). Men reported a mean Media Exposure of 3.79 (SD = 1.13) and a mean Body Image score of 3.39 (SD = 1.18). An independent-samples t-test comparing Media Exposure means between women and men yielded t(244) = 3.12, p = .002, indicating that women reported significantly greater media engagement of the type assessed. A similar t-test for body image indicated that women reported greater body dissatisfaction than men, t(244) = 5.06, p < .001. The Romantic Beliefs means did not differ significantly between women (M = 4.05, SD = 0.98) and men (M = 3.94, SD = 0.97), t(244) = 1.02, p = .31. In the full sample of 246 participants, Pearson correlations showed a modest positive association between Media Exposure and Body Image (r = .24, p < .001), indicating that greater media exposure was associated with higher body dissatisfaction. Romantic Beliefs showed a small, non-significant negative association with Body Image (r = -.06, p = .34) in the full sample. Romantic Beliefs and Media Exposure were essentially uncorrelated in the overall sample (r = -.03, p = .66). Given the theoretical interest in gender differences, correlations were computed separately for women and men. Among women (n = 158), Media Exposure and Body Image were positively correlated at r = .31 (p < .001), 95% CI [.16, .44], indicating a medium-sized association according to conventional benchmarks. The correlation between Romantic Beliefs and Body Image in women was r = -.04 (p = .63), and the correlation between Romantic Beliefs and Media Exposure was r = .02 (p = .78), both non-significant. Among men (n = 88), the correlation between Media Exposure and Body Image was r = .05 (p = .62), 95% CI [-.13, .22], which was not statistically significant and was substantially smaller than the corresponding correlation for women. The correlations between Romantic Beliefs and Body Image in men (r = -.11, p = .31) and between Romantic Beliefs and Media Exposure (r = -.08, p = .44) were also non-significant. A Fisher r-to-z test comparing the Media Exposure–Body Image correlation for women (r = .31) and men (r = .05) yielded z = 2.09, p = .036, indicating that the correlation was significantly stronger among women than among men. To test the unique predictive value of Media Exposure and Romantic Beliefs for body image, hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted using the full sample and including interaction terms for gender. In Step 1, age and gender (female coded 1, male coded 0) were entered as controls. Age was not a significant predictor of Body Image in this model (beta = -.06, p = .31), whereas gender was a significant predictor (beta = .28, p < .001), reflecting higher body dissatisfaction among women. In Step 2, Media Exposure and Romantic Beliefs were added. Media Exposure was a significant positive predictor of Body Image (beta = .29, p < .001), whereas Romantic Beliefs did not significantly predict Body Image (beta = -.07, p = .23). The overall model at Step 2 accounted for R2 = .18 of the variance in Body Image (adjusted R2 = .15), F(4,241) = 13.30, p < .001. In Step 3, interaction terms for Gender x Media Exposure and Gender x Romantic Beliefs were entered to test for moderation by gender. The Gender x Media Exposure interaction was significant (beta = -.18, p = .02), indicating that the strength of the association between media exposure and body image varied by gender. Specifically, the negative coefficient for the interaction term, combined with the positive main effect of Media Exposure, reflected that the association was stronger for those coded as female than for those coded as male. The Gender x Romantic Beliefs interaction was not significant (beta = -.09, p = .18). The full model at Step 3 accounted for R2 = .20 of the variance (adjusted R2 = .17), F(6,239) = 10.03, p < .001, and the increment in R2 due to the interaction terms was statistically significant (ΔR2 = .02, p = .02). To probe the significant Gender x Media Exposure interaction further, simple slopes were computed. For women, the simple slope for Media Exposure predicting Body Image was b = 0.42 (SE = 0.10), t = 4.20, p < .001. For men, the simple slope was b = 0.09 (SE = 0.12), t = 0.75, p = .45. These results corroborated the subgroup correlations and indicated that higher media exposure was associated with greater body dissatisfaction primarily among women in this sample. We conducted sensitivity analyses to determine the robustness of these findings. Partial correlations between Media Exposure and Body Image controlling for age yielded a similar pattern (full sample partial r = .23, p < .001; women partial r = .30, p < .001; men partial r = .04, p = .67), suggesting that age did not account for the observed associations. A further regression model tested whether Romantic Beliefs moderated the association between Media Exposure and Body Image by entering the Media Exposure x Romantic Beliefs interaction term. This interaction was not significant (beta = .05, p = .42), indicating that the relationship between media exposure and body dissatisfaction did not vary as a function of romantic belief endorsement in this sample. Exploratory analyses segmented the sample into three age bands (18–24, 25–34, 35–56) to inspect age-specific patterns. In the youngest band, the Media Exposure–Body Image correlation was r = .26 (p = .01); in the middle band r = .21 (p = .04); in the oldest band r = .18 (p = .17). Although the correlation appeared somewhat stronger in younger participants, age band comparisons did not yield significant differences after adjusting for multiple comparisons, and sample sizes in the oldest band were smaller, limiting power. Effect sizes for key findings were computed to aid interpretation. The overall Media Exposure–Body Image correlation (r = .24) corresponds to a small-to-moderate effect, while the female-only correlation (r = .31) is in the moderate range. The regression standardized coefficient for Media Exposure (beta = .29) likewise represents a small-to-moderate effect. Variance inflation factors were all below 2.0, confirming that multicollinearity did not bias parameter estimates. Residual diagnostics for the regression models did not reveal influential outliers or violations of normality, and Cook’s distance values were low for all observations. In sum, the results indicate a robust positive association between media exposure and body dissatisfaction overall that is concentrated among female participants, with romantic beliefs showing no significant associations with body image or media exposure in this sample.

Discussion

This study examined the relationships among media exposure, romantic beliefs, and body image in an adult sample and investigated whether these associations differed by gender. The principal finding was that media exposure was positively associated with body dissatisfaction overall, and that this association was significantly stronger for women than for men. Romantic beliefs did not show significant associations with body image or media exposure and did not moderate the media–body image relationship. Age did not account for the observed associations. These findings contribute to extant research by replicating and extending prior work indicating that media engagement is linked to body image disturbance, with a gender-specific pattern favoring stronger effects among women. The observed positive relationship between media exposure and body dissatisfaction is consistent with social comparison and objectification frameworks. Media content often portrays narrowly defined body ideals that are difficult or impossible for most individuals to attain. Repeated attention to such portrayals may facilitate upward social comparison processes, wherein individuals compare their own bodies unfavorably to idealized images, thereby fostering dissatisfaction. Objectification theory also provides a coherent account: frequent exposure to objectified images can encourage self-objectification, leading to increased body monitoring and negative evaluations. The present results align with experimental work demonstrating that brief exposures to thin-ideal or sexualized images can induce temporary increases in body dissatisfaction. The current study extends this literature by showing that habitual or self-reported patterns of exposure in everyday life are similarly associated with greater body concerns. The gender difference observed, whereby the media–body image association was robust among women but negligible among men, echoes several prior studies reporting stronger media effects for women. Cultural context may help explain this discrepancy. Historically and contemporaneously, media targeted at or featuring women have amplified thinness and sexualized appearance as central to female desirability, whereas male-oriented media may emphasize muscularity, success, or competence with less uniformity in body ideals. Women therefore encounter a convergence of social messages that valorize a narrow body shape, and they may be socialized to place greater value on physical attractiveness in self-evaluations. This cultural scaffolding can increase sensitivity to media messages about appearance. Additionally, women may be more likely to engage in appearance-focused media consumption (as reflected by higher mean Media Exposure scores among women in the present sample), which could compound effects. It is also possible that measurement issues contribute to the gender differences. The Body Image Scale used in this study targeted general body dissatisfaction without separate scales for concerns such as muscularity, which may be more salient among men. If male participants' body concerns were directed towards dimensions not captured by the scale, the observed weak association in men might underrepresent meaningful media effects on male-specific forms of body dissatisfaction. Future studies could employ measures that distinguish thinness and muscularity concerns to more precisely capture gender-differentiated patterns. Another notable finding is the absence of significant relationships involving romantic beliefs. Romantic Beliefs did not predict body image in the full sample or within gender subgroups, nor did they moderate the media–body image association. This lack of association contrasts with some prior reports suggesting that relationship ideals can influence appearance concerns. Several explanations are possible. First, the construct of romantic beliefs, as operationalized in the present study, may capture broad idealization tendencies that are not tightly linked to appearance-specific concerns. That is, endorsing passionate or idealized views of romance does not necessarily imply that a person prioritizes physical attractiveness within those ideals. Second, it is plausible that romantic beliefs shape body image indirectly via other constructs not assessed here, such as relationship anxiety, self-esteem, or mate-value perceptions. If romantic beliefs exert only secondary or mediated effects, direct correlations with body image may be weak. Third, the sample and measures might not have afforded sufficient variability to detect modest associations: the mean Romantic Beliefs score indicated moderate endorsement across participants, and floor or ceiling effects could obscure relationships. Fourth, cultural and cohort variation might attenuate previously observed links; for example, contemporary normative shifts in relationship expectations could decouple romantic idealization from appearance-based self-evaluations. Given these possibilities, the null results regarding romantic beliefs should be interpreted cautiously. They do suggest, however, that media exposure exerts a more directly observable relationship with body dissatisfaction in adults than do general romantic beliefs, at least when the latter are measured broadly. The moderation analysis helped clarify the differential impact of media exposure across gender. The significant Gender x Media Exposure interaction and simple slopes analysis showed that media exposure predicted body dissatisfaction among women but not among men. This nuance is important for interventions and theory. It indicates that efforts to reduce the negative impact of media exposures on body image may need to be especially prioritized for women, or at least tailored to address the ways in which women interact with and interpret media messages. Media literacy programs that teach critical analysis of media imagery, the use of digital editing, and the commercial motives behind beauty standards could reduce the salience of harmful comparisons. In addition to educational approaches, policies and industry practices that encourage diverse and realistic body representations might help attenuate the adverse impact of media on female body dissatisfaction. The absence of a Media Exposure x Romantic Beliefs interaction suggests that romantic idealization does not amplify or buffer the influence of media exposure on body dissatisfaction in a straightforward way. Practical applications might therefore focus on media-related cognitions and behaviors rather than on modifying generalized romantic beliefs when the goal is to reduce body dissatisfaction stemming from media. Several methodological and substantive limitations of the current study warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes claims about causality or temporal ordering. Although the pattern of results aligns with theoretical models positing media exposure as a driver of body dissatisfaction, it is also plausible that individuals with greater body dissatisfaction seek out appearance-focused media, or that a bidirectional relationship exists. Longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to test causal pathways and reciprocal influences. Second, the study relied exclusively on self-report measures, which are subject to social desirability bias, response styles, and inaccuracies in reporting media habits. Objective measures of media consumption, such as logged time spent on specific platforms or ecological momentary assessments tracking exposure in real time, would provide complementary evidence less vulnerable to recall bias. Third, while the sample included adults across a range of ages, it was not a nationally representative sample, and the gender categories were binary due to sample size constraints; findings may not generalize to non-binary individuals or to populations with different cultural contexts. Fourth, the Body Image Scale used here measured general dissatisfaction but did not separate shape, weight, or muscularity concerns, which may have obscured nuanced gendered patterns. Future research should employ multi-dimensional body image measures to capture the diversity of appearance-related concerns. Fifth, the Romantic Beliefs Scale assessed general relationship idealization but did not specify the role of physical attractiveness within romance; more focused measures that assess the weight individuals place on partner appearance might detect stronger links to body image. Sixth, the study did not assess potential mediators such as internalization of ideals, appearance comparison tendencies, or self-objectification, which could illuminate mechanisms linking media exposure to body dissatisfaction. Addressing these limitations could strengthen causal inferences and provide more granular insights into the processes at work. Notwithstanding these limitations, the findings have important implications for future research and practice. A key avenue for future work is longitudinally assessing whether changes in media exposure predict subsequent changes in body dissatisfaction and vice versa. Experimental designs that manipulate media content (e.g., comparing exposure to diverse versus idealized body images) and interventions that reduce media exposure or modify interpretative frameworks can test causal hypotheses and inform preventive programs. Additionally, examining potential moderators beyond gender—such as baseline self-esteem, internalization of appearance ideals, social comparison orientation, and peer group norms—could reveal subgroups particularly susceptible to harmful media effects. Investigating the role of social media specifically is increasingly important, given the interactive and curated nature of platforms where users often encounter images of peers, influencers, and celebrities in mixed contexts. Interactivity and personalized algorithms may intensify exposure to appearance-focused content and alter the pathways linking media to body image. From a clinical perspective, the results highlight the relevance of addressing media consumption in interventions for individuals presenting with body dissatisfaction. Cognitive-behavioral strategies that target maladaptive comparisons, challenge internalized appearance ideals, and reduce engagement with triggering content could be incorporated into therapeutic work. For prevention, school- and community-based media literacy curricula that emphasize critical evaluation of media messages and promote body diversity may be beneficial, especially for younger women who appear to be particularly affected. Policy initiatives that incentivize media outlets and advertisers to present diverse body types and to disclose image editing practices could complement educational efforts. Finally, the measurement and conceptualization of romantic beliefs warrant refinement. Future research might focus on specific beliefs that logically bear on appearance concerns, such as beliefs about the importance of physical attractiveness for partner selection or beliefs about the permanence of romantic love irrespective of changing appearance. Such targeted measures could better capture potential pathways linking relational cognitions and body image. In summary, this study provides evidence that amount and quality of media exposure are meaningfully associated with body dissatisfaction in adults, with the association concentrated among women. Romantic beliefs, when measured broadly, did not predict body image or interact with media exposure. These outcomes underscore the salience of media influences in shaping body-related self-evaluations and suggest the need for gender-attuned interventions and future research that clarifies mechanisms, tests causal directions, and explores moderating factors. Addressing media-related pathways represents a tangible route for reducing body image disturbances and fostering healthier self-perceptions in contemporary media-saturated environments.
