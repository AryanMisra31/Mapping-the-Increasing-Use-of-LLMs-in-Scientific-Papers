Introduction

Memory is a fundamental cognitive function that allows organisms to store information about their experiences and to use that information to guide future behavior. Human memory is not a passive recording device but an active, reconstructive process that integrates incoming information with prior knowledge, expectations, and contextual cues. The reconstructive nature of memory makes it susceptible to distortions, such that individuals can recall events inaccurately or even report entirely false details. Memory distortion has been demonstrated across many domains and can be induced by factors that occur both before and after an event, including suggestions, stress, substance use, and environmental context. Because accurate memory is important for everyday functioning and for applied contexts such as eyewitness testimony, researchers have long sought to identify both factors that degrade memory and interventions that can enhance memory retention. The quest for memory enhancers encompasses a broad range of approaches, including pharmacological agents, cognitive training, mnemonic strategies, and manipulation of stimulus properties. Color is one stimulus property that has been proposed as a potential modulator of memory encoding and retrieval. Color is a salient perceptual attribute that can influence attention, emotional responses, and semantic associations, all of which are processes known to interact with memory performance. As a consequence, a body of research has considered whether presenting material in different colors can lead to differences in memory outcomes. Some studies have reported that color can enhance memory for words and images relative to achromatic presentations, whereas other studies have found minimal or context-dependent effects. The theoretical mechanisms proposed to account for color effects on memory include increased perceptual distinctiveness, modulation of arousal and attention, and associative links between colors and semantic categories. Perceptual distinctiveness posits that colored stimuli stand out more from a homogenous background and from other items, thereby capturing attention and facilitating encoding. The arousal hypothesis suggests that certain colors may elicit physiological or affective responses that enhance consolidation. For example, warm colors have been associated with increased arousal, which in some contexts leads to enhanced memory retention. Associative accounts emphasize that colors can carry semantic or cultural meanings that interact with the content of stimuli and thus affect memory encoding and retrieval. The literature on color and cognition more broadly indicates that color influences not only memory but also perception, categorization, and decision making. Experimental work has examined color effects on a range of tasks including speeded attention tasks, visual search, mood induction, and educational outcomes. Within the memory literature specifically, several studies have compared color versus grayscale images, or specific hues such as red, blue, and green, to evaluate differential effects on recognition, free recall, and associative memory. Some experiments have observed better recognition for colored images relative to grayscale, with the proposed interpretation that color provides additional cues that facilitate later discrimination. Other work, however, has found that color effects are attenuated when memory tasks demand deeper, semantic processing, suggesting that color may have a larger effect when processing is more perceptual than conceptual. The diversity of findings across studies implies that color effects on memory may be moderated by task type, stimulus materials, participant characteristics, and contextual variables such as lighting and background. One variable that has received less attention in the literature is shade variability within a single hue. Most existing studies compare different basic hues or compare color to achromatic stimuli, but relatively few have systematically manipulated subtle differences within a color family such as light green versus dark green. A focus on shades is theoretically interesting because shades within a hue can differ in perceived brightness and saturation, properties that are known to influence visual salience. For example, a light, desaturated green may be perceived as less salient than a deep, saturated green, which could lead to differential attention at encoding and thus differential memory outcomes. Moreover, shades may have distinct emotional or semantic connotations; a vivid forest green may evoke associations to nature and calmness, whereas a pale mint green might be associated with cleanliness or sterility. These associative differences could, in turn, influence encoding processes or retrieval cues. Despite these plausible mechanisms, empirical evidence comparing shades within a hue remains sparse, creating an empirical gap that this study aims to address. Another practical reason to investigate shades within a hue is that many real-world contexts in which memory is important—educational materials, instruction cards, product packaging, and signage—use subtle color variations rather than stark color contrasts. Understanding whether such variations can yield meaningful differences in memory performance has both theoretical and applied implications. If certain shades within a color family reliably enhance memory, designers could leverage this information in instructional design and information presentation. Conversely, if shade differences do not influence memory, then concerns about minor color selections may be less warranted. The present study was designed to evaluate whether two different shades of green, compared to a white achromatic control, influence free recall of pictured items. Green was chosen as the hue of interest for several reasons. First, prior work has sometimes suggested beneficial effects of green on cognitive tasks, possibly related to associations with nature, restoration, and reduced cognitive fatigue. Second, green is commonly used in educational and informational materials, making the question of shade-related effects practically relevant. Third, focusing on a single hue reduces potential confounds that arise when comparing colors with different fundamental semantic or affective meanings. The experimental procedure involved presenting participants with a set of 25 images, each printed on a card of a single color that was consistent for each participant. Participants were randomly assigned to receive the images on either light green cards, dark green cards, or white cards. After presentation, participants were given an unfilled interval and then tested on their ability to recall the images in a free recall task. The central hypothesis was that at least one of the green shades would produce superior recall performance relative to the white control, reflecting either increased perceptual distinctiveness or beneficial associative effects. A secondary hypothesis was that the darker, more saturated shade of green would lead to better recall than the lighter, less saturated shade, given that increased saturation often corresponds to greater perceptual salience. The study also considered the possibility that color effects might be small and thus required sufficient sample size to detect. Accordingly, the study employed a between-subjects design with random assignment and a planned sample large enough to detect at least moderate effects. The experiment further included manipulation checks and control measures to document participants' color vision and familiarity with the stimuli. We also considered potential confounds such as differential item memorability by counterbalancing the order of items across participants. Given the mixed findings in the extant literature and the specific focus on shades within a hue, several possible outcomes were anticipated. If green shade had a robust effect, we expected to observe statistically significant differences in mean recall across conditions and possibly an effect size in the small-to-moderate range. If no differences were observed, it might suggest that shade-level manipulations within a single hue are insufficient to alter encoding in a free recall task or that other factors such as semantic depth outweigh subtle perceptual differences. Additionally, we planned to examine whether any observed differences were specific to recall format or reflected broad changes in encoding processes by analyzing intrusions and memory errors. This approach aimed to provide a nuanced understanding of whether color shading affects not only quantity of recall but also qualitative aspects of memory performance. The primary contribution of this study is to address the relative paucity of data on shade-level color manipulations and their impact on memory. By evaluating two shades of green against an achromatic control in a controlled laboratory setting, the study seeks to clarify whether small variations within a color family can measurably influence free recall of pictorial stimuli. The findings have implications for theories of perceptual distinctiveness and associative color effects, and they can inform applied decisions about color usage in instructional and informational materials. In what follows, we describe the methods used to test these hypotheses, present the results of the statistical analyses, and discuss the implications of the findings for memory theory and practical applications.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from the local community. A total of  ninety participants completed the study. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of color vision deficiencies. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 45 years, with a mean age of 22.7 years. The sample included both undergraduate students and community volunteers. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were compensated with course credit or a small monetary payment. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the university. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three experimental conditions using a computerized randomization routine. The three conditions were light green, dark green, and white control. Each participant received the set of 25 pictorial stimuli printed on card stock of the assigned color. Randomization was implemented in blocks to ensure approximately equal cell sizes. The resulting group sizes were balanced, with 30 participants in the light green condition, 30 participants in the dark green condition, and 30 participants in the white condition. The stimuli comprised 25 color photographs of common objects drawn from everyday life. Images included items such as a bicycle, a coffee mug, a tree, a shoe, a lamp, a book, a clock, and other easily nameable objects. Images were selected to be concrete and readily identifiable, and were standardized for size and resolution. To reduce item-specific memorability differences, the order of presentation of the images was randomized for each participant. The colored cards were produced to be identical in all dimensions except for color. The white cards served as an achromatic control and were matched in paper type and sheen to the green cards. The light green and dark green colors were chosen based on pre-testing to represent clearly distinguishable shades within the green hue family. The light green was a pale mint shade with relatively high luminance and lower saturation, whereas the dark green was a deep forest shade with lower luminance and higher saturation. Pre-testing with a small convenience sample confirmed that participants consistently described the two greens as distinct shades and could reliably classify them as lighter or darker. The experiment was conducted in a quiet laboratory room with consistent overhead lighting. Ambient lighting was kept constant across sessions to minimize interactions between room lighting and card color. Participants were seated at a table and were presented with the cards sequentially by the experimenter. Each card was displayed face-up for five seconds, during which participants were instructed to study the image and to try to remember it for a later memory test. The five-second exposure time was chosen to balance the need for adequate encoding time with the goal of encouraging naturalistic, spontaneous encoding rather than elaborate study strategies. Participants were not informed about the nature of the later memory test beyond the instruction to remember the items. After the presentation of all 25 images, participants completed a two-minute distractor task consisting of simple arithmetic problems to create a short retention interval and to reduce rehearsal effects. Following the distractor period, participants were given a free recall test. They were asked to write down the names of as many of the images as they could remember in any order within a five-minute time limit. The free recall format was selected because it is sensitive to differences in encoding quality and is less supported by external cues than recognition tasks. Experimenters recorded the number of correctly recalled items for each participant as the primary dependent variable. Correct recall was defined as producing the canonical name of the object or a close synonym that clearly indicated successful retrieval of the target item. Minor spelling errors were accepted if the intended item was unambiguous. Intrusion errors, defined as recalling items not included in the study list, were also recorded as a secondary dependent measure. The procedure included a brief post-test questionnaire to assess participant demographics, self-reported study strategies, familiarity with the stimuli, and color vision. The color vision item asked participants whether they had any known color blindness; participants who reported a known color vision deficiency would have been excluded, but none did. Participants also rated how noticeable they found the card color while studying the images on a Likert scale from 1 to 7. This manipulation check assessed whether the color manipulation was perceived by the participants and whether perceived noticeability correlated with memory performance. Data analysis followed a pre-registered plan. The primary analysis tested for differences in mean free recall across the three color conditions using a one-way analysis of variance. Prior to conducting the ANOVA, the data were screened for normality and homogeneity of variance. If assumptions were violated, we planned to use a nonparametric alternative. In addition to the ANOVA, planned pairwise comparisons between each green shade and the white control were specified. Effect sizes were computed using Cohen’s d for pairwise contrasts and partial eta squared for the ANOVA. To supplement frequentist analyses, Bayesian statistics were used to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis relative to the alternative hypothesis. Bayesian analyses can provide information about the strength of evidence supporting the absence of a color effect, which is particularly relevant when null results are observed. We also planned exploratory analyses to examine correlations between self-reported noticing of the card color and recall scores, as well as potential demographic moderators such as age. All analyses were performed using standard statistical software. Data cleaning involved removal of any participants who failed attention checks or who had incomplete data. No participants were excluded due to attention failures. All decisions regarding data handling and statistical testing were made in accordance with the pre-registered protocol.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the recall data were computed first and are reported here. Across the entire sample, the mean number of items recalled out of 25 was 11.9 with a standard deviation of 4.4. Group means were as follows: the light green condition had a mean recall of 12.3 items with a standard deviation of 4.2. The dark green condition had a mean recall of 11.8 items with a standard deviation of 4.6. The white control condition had a mean recall of 11.6 items with a standard deviation of 4.5. These descriptive statistics indicate similar central tendency and spread across the three conditions. Visual inspection of histograms suggested that the recall distributions approximated normality, with some skewness toward lower recall but no extreme departures from a bell-shaped distribution. Formal tests for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test indicated no significant departures from normality in any group at an alpha level of .05. Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances was non-significant, indicating that the assumption of equal variances was tenable for the planned ANOVA. Given that assumptions were adequately met, a one-way ANOVA was conducted to test for differences in recall across the three color conditions. The ANOVA produced an F statistic of F(2,87) = 0.37. This F value corresponds to a p-value of .693, which is well above conventional thresholds for statistical significance. The partial eta squared for the effect of color condition was .008, indicating a very small proportion of variance accounted for by condition. Planned pairwise comparisons between each green shade and the white control were conducted using independent samples t-tests. The contrast between light green and white yielded t(58) = 0.62 with a p-value of .538. The contrast between dark green and white yielded t(58) = 0.27 with a p-value of .789. The contrast between light green and dark green yielded t(58) = 0.34 with a p-value of .738. None of these pairwise comparisons approached statistical significance. Cohen’s d effect sizes for the pairwise contrasts were small, with d = 0.16 for light green versus white, d = 0.05 for dark green versus white, and d = 0.11 for light green versus dark green. These effect sizes are small in magnitude and suggest negligible practical differences between conditions. Considering the null results from the frequentist analyses, Bayesian analyses were conducted to quantify the relative evidence for the null hypothesis. A Bayesian one-way ANOVA yielded a Bayes factor in favor of the null over the alternative, BF01 = 3.4. This Bayes factor indicates moderate evidence for the null hypothesis that there is no effect of card color on free recall in this sample. Bayesian t-tests for the pairwise comparisons produced BF01 values of 2.6 for light green versus white and 3.9 for dark green versus white, further indicating that the data are more likely under the null model than under models positing a color effect. The secondary outcome of intrusion errors was examined next. The total number of intrusions produced by participants was low across conditions, with group means of 0.4 intrusions in the light green condition, 0.5 in the dark green condition, and 0.6 in the white condition. An ANOVA on intrusions produced F(2,87) = 0.21, p = .810, indicating no significant differences in intrusion frequency across conditions. Next, we examined the manipulation check regarding perceived noticeability of card color. Participants in the light green and dark green conditions reported higher noticeability ratings (means of 5.1 and 5.4 respectively on a 1-7 scale) compared with the white control (mean of 2.8), indicating that participants in colored conditions did perceive the card color. A one-way ANOVA on noticeability yielded a significant effect, F(2,87) = 68.2, p < .001, confirming that the color manipulation was experienced by participants as intended. Despite this perceived noticeability, noticeability ratings did not correlate strongly with recall performance (r = .07, p = .483), indicating that higher subjective awareness of the color did not translate into better memory. Exploratory analyses examined whether demographic variables moderated the relationship between color condition and recall. Age did not significantly correlate with recall within this young adult sample (r = .08, p = .417). Self-reported study strategies, such as creating mental associations or verbal rehearsal, were marginally related to recall performance across the sample (r = .21, p = .047), but there were no interactions between strategy use and color condition in predicting recall. A subsequent analysis considered potential item-level effects by computing recall probabilities for each item across participants. Some items were recalled more frequently than others, consistent with item-specific differences in intrinsic memorability, but these item-level recall rates did not systematically vary by color condition. That is, items that were highly memorable in one condition tended to be highly memorable in the other conditions as well. To assess whether the null findings might reflect insufficient statistical power, an a priori power calculation assumed a medium effect size (f = .25), which would have required approximately 159 participants to achieve 80% power at alpha = .05 in a three-group ANOVA. Our actual sample of 90 participants thus had lower power to detect small-to-medium effects, but was adequately powered to detect moderate-to-large effects. Given the observed effect sizes, which were small, the likelihood that a true moderate or large effect was missed is low. Finally, we conducted sensitivity analyses using nonparametric tests. A Kruskal-Wallis test on recall across conditions yielded H(2) = 0.61, p = .737, in line with the parametric results. Taken together, the statistical evidence consistently indicates no meaningful differences in free recall performance between the two shades of green and the white control under the present experimental conditions.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether two distinct shades of green would differentially affect free recall of pictorial stimuli compared to a white control. The primary finding was a consistent absence of a color effect on recall performance. Mean recall was quite similar across the light green, dark green, and white conditions, and statistical analyses produced non-significant results across ANOVA, pairwise t-tests, and nonparametric alternatives. Bayesian analyses further suggested moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis, indicating that the data were more consistent with no effect of shade than with an effect. The manipulation check confirmed that participants noticed the color differences, which suggests that the null result is not explained by a failure to perceive the experimental manipulation. Instead, the findings imply that subtle shade differences within the green hue do not have a measurable impact on free recall under the conditions tested. Interpreting a null result requires careful consideration of both methodological factors and theoretical implications. One important methodological point concerns statistical power. The study included 90 participants, which provided adequate power to detect moderate to large effects but limited power for detecting small effects. Effect sizes observed in the study were small, suggesting that if there is any true effect of green shade on free recall, it is likely to be small in magnitude. The Bayesian analyses, which favored the null, bolster confidence that there was not a moderate or large effect that was missed due to insufficient power. Nevertheless, the possibility remains that a very small effect exists that would require larger samples to detect reliably. The free recall format used in this experiment is another relevant factor. Free recall is a demanding memory task that places a premium on deeper encoding and retrieval strategies. Prior research suggests that color manipulations may exert larger effects on recognition tasks, which benefit from perceptual cues, than on free recall tasks that rely more heavily on semantic or associative processing. If color primarily enhances perceptual discrimination rather than semantic encoding, we might expect color effects to be more evident in recognition or cued recall tasks than in free recall. Thus, the null finding in the present free recall study does not necessarily imply that color has no influence on memory in other formats. The nature of the stimuli may also moderate color effects. We used concrete, easily nameable objects presented as color photographs. Because these images contain inherent color information and semantic content, the added color from the background card might have had limited additional utility for encoding. In situations where stimuli are less inherently colorful, or where color contrasts are directly applied to the stimuli themselves, color might exert a stronger influence. In other words, the card color used here functioned as a contextual cue rather than as an intrinsic attribute of the items, and contextual cues can be less effective unless they are diagnostic at retrieval. The manipulation in this study involved presenting images on colored cards, which created a consistent color context across items. It is possible that color effects emerge more strongly when color varies item-by-item, thereby creating item-specific distinctiveness. By using a single card color per participant, we emphasized between-subjects color differences rather than within-subject color distinctiveness. Future research could examine whether item-level color variation enhances memorability by making individual items more distinctive against the background of other items. The specific shades of green chosen for this experiment merit consideration. Pre-testing confirmed that participants perceived the shades as distinct and could classify them as light or dark. However, perceptual distinctions do not necessarily map onto cognitive salience. The differences in luminance and saturation between the shades may have been insufficient to drive attentional capture or arousal changes that would meaningfully influence encoding. Studies that have reported color effects often compare more extreme color contrasts or hues that differ substantially in arousal associations, such as red versus blue. Green may be a relatively neutral hue with modest arousal-inducing properties, and shade differences within green may therefore have limited psychological impact. The fact that participants reported noticing the card color yet this awareness did not correlate with recall further supports the interpretation that noticing color alone is insufficient to enhance memory. Another possible explanation for the null results relates to encoding strategies used by participants. Some participants may have relied on verbal labeling or semantic elaboration to remember items, strategies that could overshadow subtle perceptual cues like card color. Our exploratory analyses showed that self-reported strategy use was marginally related to recall but did not interact with condition, suggesting that strategy variance influenced overall performance but not the effect of color. In contexts where participants are encouraged or required to engage in perceptual encoding, color effects might be more pronounced. The present findings also speak to theoretical accounts that propose color influences memory via attentional and arousal mechanisms. If color enhances memory primarily by increasing attention at encoding, then color manipulations must reliably affect attentional allocation to have downstream memory effects. Our design did not include direct measures of attention such as eye tracking or event-related potentials, so we cannot definitively rule out subtle attentional shifts that did not translate into measurable recall differences. Incorporating physiological or behavioral attention measures in future studies would help clarify whether color manipulations modulate attention in ways that could plausibly affect memory. The broader literature on color and cognition is mixed, and our results fit within a pattern of findings that point to context-dependent effects. Some studies have found beneficial effects of color on recognition and learning, whereas others have found minimal or inconsistent effects. The current study adds nuance to this literature by showing that shade-level manipulations within a single hue do not necessarily yield memory benefits in a free recall paradigm. From an applied perspective, the findings suggest that small shade choices within the green family may not substantially influence the memorability of pictorial content when presented in a consistent color context. Designers of instructional materials and information displays may therefore prioritize other design features such as content organization, contrast, and clarity over subtle shade selection if the goal is to optimize free recall. However, caution is warranted in generalizing this implication beyond the specific conditions tested here. There are several clear directions for future research that arise from these findings. First, researchers should examine whether color effects are task-specific by comparing free recall, cued recall, and recognition within the same experimental framework. Such a within-study comparison would illuminate whether color serves more as a perceptual cue that aids recognition than as a semantic aid for recall. Second, future studies could manipulate color at the item level rather than at the participant level to assess whether item-specific distinctiveness enhances memory. Third, incorporating measures of attention such as eye-tracking, pupillometry, or EEG would help determine whether color manipulations modulate attentional engagement or arousal during encoding. Fourth, testing a broader array of hues and more extreme contrasts could clarify whether green in general is less potent than other colors for influencing memory. Fifth, researchers could manipulate delay intervals to examine whether color effects emerge over longer retention intervals, perhaps by influencing consolidation processes. Sixth, investigating individual differences in color associations, mood, and prior experiences could shed light on moderators of color effects. Seventh, cross-cultural studies might explore whether cultural associations with particular colors alter their mnemonic impact. The present study has limitations that should be acknowledged. The sample consisted primarily of young adults, which may limit generalizability to older adults or to populations with differing color associations. The laboratory setting, while allowing control over extraneous variables, may not capture ecological complexities present in real-world contexts where color interacts with text, layout, and task demands. Additionally, the between-subjects design precluded assessment of within-subject sensitivity to color shade, which could be informative. Finally, although the study included a manipulation check for perceived noticeability, it did not directly measure subjective affective responses to the colors, which could mediate memory effects. In conclusion, this experiment found no evidence that two different shades of green enhance free recall of pictorial stimuli relative to a white control under the tested conditions. The data suggest that shade-level differences within green do not meaningfully influence free recall performance in young adults when color is presented as a uniform background context. The findings contribute to a nuanced understanding of color-memory relationships by highlighting boundary conditions under which color manipulations may fail to produce measurable benefits. Future work that varies task demands, stimulus properties, attentional measures, and participant populations will be valuable for delineating when and how color can be harnessed as an aid to memory.
