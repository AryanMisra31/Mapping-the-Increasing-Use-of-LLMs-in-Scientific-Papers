Introduction

Bilingualism has been a subject of intensive empirical and theoretical interest across cognitive psychology and applied domains. Researchers have long debated whether the experience of managing two languages leads to measurable changes in cognitive systems beyond language itself. One domain in which bilingualism has been proposed to confer advantages is working memory, a core component of cognition that supports temporarily storing and manipulating information needed for ongoing tasks. Working memory is central to many everyday functions, including language comprehension, problem solving, and decision making, and so any systematic influence of bilingual experience on working memory would have broad theoretical and practical implications. A large literature has explored different aspects of how bilingual experience might interact with working memory, but the findings have been heterogeneous and often contradictory. Some studies report advantages for bilinguals in tasks that tax executive control and working memory, whereas others find null effects or report that any observed advantages diminish after accounting for confounding variables. These mixed findings have produced heated debate and highlight the need for theoretically guided work that specifies mechanisms through which bilingual experience could affect working memory.

One influential framework for understanding the dynamics of working memory is the time-based resource-sharing (TBRS) model. The TBRS model posits that maintenance of items in working memory is a dynamic process in which memory traces decay over time and must be refreshed by attention to prevent loss. According to the model, cognitive load is a function of the proportion of time during which attention is captured by processing demands and is therefore unavailable for refreshing memory traces. When processing demands occupy attention for a greater proportion of the time available, there is less opportunity for refreshing and memory performance declines. Conversely, when processing demands are lighter or allow for more frequent moments of attentional disengagement, there is more opportunity to refresh memory traces and maintain performance. The TBRS framework therefore makes precise predictions about how manipulations of processing time and attentional demands should affect memory performance and provides a mechanistic link between temporal characteristics of processing and outcomes in complex span tasks.

A key implication of the TBRS model for individual differences is that the ability to manage attention under concurrent processing demands should influence the efficacy of refreshing and thus working memory performance. If certain experiences lead to more efficient allocation of attention or faster switching between processing and refreshing, then those individuals might maintain a larger set of memory items under identical processing demands. Bilingual experience has been proposed to train aspects of attentional control because bilingual speakers frequently need to manage attention across competing linguistic systems, inhibit non-target language representations, and resolve cross-language interference. On this account, bilinguals may develop enhanced attentional control or faster attentional switching, and such enhancements could translate to more efficient refreshing during concurrent processing, thereby reducing the effective cognitive load and improving working memory performance.

Despite the theoretical plausibility of a link between bilingual experience and more efficient refreshing in line with TBRS, empirical investigation has produced inconsistent results. Some investigations using complex span tasks and n-back paradigms have reported that bilinguals outperform monolinguals, particularly on tasks that require simultaneous processing and storage. Other carefully controlled studies have failed to replicate such advantages, and some meta-analytic work suggests that bilingual advantages are small and sensitive to methodological variation. The variation in findings has been attributed to differences in how bilingualism is operationalized, heterogeneity in participant samples, the particular memory or executive tasks employed, and potential confounds such as socioeconomic status, education level, and cultural background.

A notable methodological concern in prior work is the heterogeneity in bilingual groups. The bilingual category can encompass a wide range of experiences, including differences in age of acquisition, proficiency in each language, frequency of use, and contexts of language switching. These dimensions can have different cognitive consequences, and aggregating across heterogeneous bilingual samples may hide meaningful effects or produce spurious group differences. Another methodological issue is the sensitivity of the tasks used to index working memory. Some tasks may be dominated by verbal rehearsal or linguistic processing strategies that are differentially affected by proficiency or language dominance. Tasks designed explicitly to manipulate the time available for refreshing, in the spirit of the TBRS approach, offer one way to disentangle attentional factors from purely verbal rehearsal mechanisms.

Given these considerations, theorists have suggested that the TBRS model can serve as a useful framework for testing hypotheses about bilingualism and working memory. The TBRS model directs attention to the temporal allocation of attention and suggests specific experimental manipulations that vary the proportion of time available for refreshing. If bilinguals have superior attentional allocation or faster switching between processing and refreshing, then under TBRS-informed manipulations that reduce free time for refreshing, bilinguals should show relatively preserved memory performance compared with monolinguals. Conversely, if bilingual experience does not impact the speed or quality of refreshing, then bilinguals and monolinguals should show comparable declines in memory performance as cognitive load increases.

Despite the promise of the TBRS perspective for guiding empirical tests, few studies have explicitly combined TBRS task structures with carefully characterized bilingual and monolingual groups. Many prior bilingual advantage studies have relied on conventional span tasks or executive paradigms without manipulating the time budget for refreshing in a principled way. This leaves a gap in the evidence regarding whether bilinguals have an advantage specifically in tasks that require frequent switching of attention between processing and maintenance. Addressing this gap requires a task that approximates the TBRS logic and allows direct measurement of memory performance under concurrent processing requirements that systematically restrict the time available for refreshing.

The present study was designed to fill this gap by applying a TBRS-inspired dual-task procedure to a sample of young adult bilingual and monolingual participants. The procedure includes a simple reading task that imposes processing demands and a letter recall task that indexes the outcome of maintenance under those demands. The processing task requires participants to read aloud simple sentences or items at a prescribed pace, and the embedded constraints allow control over the proportion of attentional time captured by processing, thereby manipulating cognitive load in a time-based way. Memory materials consist of short lists of letters presented between processing items; participants must maintain and subsequently recall those letters. The design therefore pits short-term storage demands against timed processing demands in a way that directly tests the TBRS proposition about the relationship between time and cognitive load.

The primary hypothesis derives from combining the TBRS model with proposals about bilingual-enhanced attentional control. Specifically, if bilingual experience confers advantages in attentional allocation and switching, bilingual participants should be able to refresh memory traces more effectively during intermittent gaps in processing, and this should yield superior letter recall relative to monolinguals. This advantage should be most evident under conditions that restrict available refreshing time because those conditions place a premium on fast and efficient switching to refreshing. A corollary prediction is that any bilingual advantage should be attenuated or absent under conditions that provide ample free time for refreshing, because then both groups can maintain memory traces adequately without differential efficiency.

In operational terms, the present study tested bilingual and monolingual young adults on a TBRS-style dual-task in which letters to be remembered were interleaved with a paced reading task. The reading task created alternating streams of attentional capture and brief moments of free time for refreshing, and the pace was calibrated to yield a moderate overall cognitive load that would tax but not overwhelm working memory. The outcome measure was the number of letters correctly recalled in order at the end of each trial. Secondary measures included reaction time and accuracy in the processing task to ensure comparable engagement across groups, and self-reported language history measures to characterize bilingual experience.

In formulating the study, attention was given to potential confounds that have plagued prior research. Participants were drawn from the same university community and were matched on basic demographic variables such as age and educational level. The bilingual group was defined with criteria that required frequent use of two languages and functional proficiency in both, and language history was assessed to permit exploratory analyses linking degrees of bilingual experience to memory performance. The testing session included practice trials and checks to ensure participants understood the task, and instructions emphasized both processing and memory goals to encourage genuine dual-task responding.

By grounding the design in the TBRS model and by using a dual-task procedure that manipulates the temporal allocation of attention, the present study aims to test whether bilingual experience confers measurable advantages in working memory under time-constrained refreshing conditions. A demonstration of bilingual advantage in this context would support the idea that bilingual experience improves attentional allocation in ways that impact core maintenance processes. A null result, on the other hand, would suggest either that bilingual experience does not meaningfully alter refreshing efficiency or that any such effects are too small to detect under the present conditions. Given the ongoing debate, both outcomes would advance the literature by clarifying the specific conditions under which bilingualism might or might not influence working memory.

Because the TBRS framework specifies the role of time in determining cognitive load, the present study focused on a manipulation of processing pace and used measures of memory performance that are sensitive to changes in available refreshing time. The primary prediction was directional: bilinguals should recall more letters than monolinguals under the paced dual-task conditions used here. A secondary prediction was that the magnitude of any advantage would correlate with self-reported indices of bilingual experience, such as age of acquisition and frequency of use, under the assumption that more extensive bilingual experience might be linked to greater attentional control skills. The study also included checks of processing accuracy and time to rule out the alternative explanation that bilinguals might simply adopt different trade-offs between processing and storage.

In sum, this study tests a theoretically motivated hypothesis about the relationship between bilingual experience and working memory, with a specific focus on temporal dynamics of attention as articulated in the TBRS model. It does so by applying a dual-task that taxes processing and storage concurrently and by comparing performance in carefully characterized bilingual and monolingual samples drawn from the same community. The outcome of this test bears on debates about bilingual cognitive advantages and on theoretical accounts of how experience shapes attentional control and memory refreshing processes.

Methods

Participants

A total of 48 adult participants were recruited from the University of Warwick community. The sample consisted of 24 self-identified bilinguals and 24 self-identified monolinguals. All participants were students or staff members at the university and were between 18 and 35 years of age. The bilingual and monolingual groups were matched to the extent possible on age, years of education, and sex distribution during recruitment. Mean age across the whole sample was 21.8 years, with a standard deviation of 3.1 years. Participants provided written informed consent before participation and were compensated with course credit or a small monetary payment for their time. The study procedures were approved by the university’s human research ethics committee.

Bilingual classification

Bilingual participants were included if they reported regular use of two languages in daily life and functional proficiency in both languages. Regular use was operationalized as using a second language at least 20% of the time in conversational contexts over the past year. Participants completed a brief language history questionnaire that asked about age of acquisition for each language, current frequency of use, self-rated proficiency in speaking and comprehension on a 7-point scale, and contexts in which each language was used (home, work, study, social). Participants who reported learning a second language after adolescence were still eligible if they met the usage and proficiency criteria. Monolingual participants were included if they reported speaking English only and having minimal exposure to other languages, defined as less than 5% use of another language in daily life and no functional proficiency.

Sample characteristics and matching

Group means for age, years of education, and sex ratio were compared during recruitment screening to avoid large systematic differences. Mean age for the bilingual group was 22.0 years (SD = 3.3) and for the monolingual group was 21.7 years (SD = 2.9). Mean years of education did not differ significantly between groups, with an average of 14.6 years for bilinguals and 14.8 years for monolinguals. The bilingual group included speakers of a variety of language pairs, including English with European and non-European languages, and all testing was conducted in English. Language dominance varied within the bilingual group, but all reported sufficient proficiency to perform the tasks in English without difficulty.

Materials

The experimental task was an adaptation of a time-based resource-sharing dual-task that combined a paced reading or processing component with a letter memory component. Memory items consisted of consonant letters drawn from a pool excluding easily confusable letters (for example, excluding letters like B and V if confusability was a concern), and letter lists were five items in length for each trial. A set of 32 letter lists was created and randomized across trials for each participant. Letters were presented sequentially visually, each appearing for 500 ms at the center of the screen, with an inter-stimulus interval of 300 ms to approximate a rapid but manageable presentation pace.

The processing component consisted of short, syntactically simple sentences presented visually at a fixed pace. Participants read each sentence aloud and judged whether it made sense (yes/no) while an experimenter recorded accuracy or the computer recorded response via key press. The sentence stimuli were constructed to be short enough that reading aloud each sentence took approximately 1,200 ms on average, based on pilot testing. The trial structure interleaved processing items and memory letters in the following way: a letter would be presented, then a processing sentence would appear which the participant read aloud and judged, then another letter, and so on until the list of five letters had been presented and five sentence-processing items had been completed.

Pacing and cognitive load

To manipulate the time available for refreshing, the pace of the processing component and the duration of the intervals between processing were calibrated to yield a moderate cognitive load consistent with TBRS paradigms. Specifically, the sentence presentation and response window were fixed so that processing occupied approximately 70% of the time within each letter-processing cycle and left approximately 30% of the cycle as a brief interval during which participants could attempt to refresh memory traces. This proportion was chosen to create a condition that taxed but did not overwhelm working memory in pilot testing. All participants experienced the same pacing parameters to permit direct group comparisons.

Procedure

Testing took place in a quiet laboratory room in a single session of approximately 45 minutes per participant. After informed consent and completion of the language history questionnaire, participants received standardized task instructions on the computer and then completed four practice trials with feedback to ensure understanding. The instructions emphasized that both the sentence processing and the letter recall were important and that participants should perform both as well as possible. Following practice, participants completed the experimental trials in a single block of 32 trials. Trials were presented in randomized order, and brief rest breaks were offered after every eight trials.

On each experimental trial, participants viewed a cue indicating the start of the trial. The sequence of letter and sentence items then began with an initial fixation cross for 500 ms followed by the first letter for 500 ms. A sentence appeared immediately after and remained on screen during the fixed response window of 1,200 ms; participants read the sentence aloud and pressed one of two keys to indicate whether the sentence made sense. The next letter then appeared after an inter-item gap of 300 ms, and the sequence continued until five letters and five sentences had been presented. After the final processing item, there was a recall prompt asking participants to type the letters in the order they remembered them. Responses were recorded and time-stamped. Participants were encouraged to respond as quickly and accurately as possible during the processing judgments.

Measures and scoring

The primary outcome measure was the number of letters correctly recalled in the correct serial position for each trial, averaged across trials to yield a mean letters-recalled score for each participant. Secondary measures included accuracy on the sentence processing judgments, mean processing response times, and self-reported language history variables (age of acquisition and frequency of use). Trials on which a participant failed to respond to the processing judgment were flagged and excluded from analysis; participants with more than 20% missed processing responses would have been excluded from the final sample, but no participant met this exclusion criterion.

Data preparation and analysis plan

Data were preprocessed by computing average letters recalled per participant and evaluating distributional properties for normality. Descriptive statistics were computed for each group, and independent-samples t-tests were planned to compare bilingual and monolingual groups on the primary outcome. To test the robustness of any group differences, additional analyses included nonparametric tests and Bayesian t-tests to quantify evidence for the null or alternative hypotheses. Because the study also collected language history measures, exploratory Pearson correlations were planned between bilingual experience indices (for bilinguals only) and memory performance. Processing accuracy and response times were compared across groups to ensure that any group differences in memory were not due to trade-offs in processing. Potential covariates, including age and years of education, were screened and included in linear regression models only if they correlated with the dependent variable.

Statistical procedures

Planned statistical procedures included independent-samples t-tests with two-tailed alpha of 0.05 for primary comparisons, Levene’s test for equality of variances, Shapiro-Wilk tests for normality, and computation of Cohen’s d as a standardized effect size. Bayesian t-tests were computed to provide Bayes factors representing the relative evidence for the null and alternative hypotheses. All analyses were performed using standard statistical software, and critical values, effect sizes, and Bayes factors are reported to permit assessment of the strength of evidence.

Ethical considerations

All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were debriefed after the testing session. The research conformed to ethical standards for the protection of human participants, and data were anonymized for analysis. Participants were free to withdraw at any point without penalty.

Results

Data screening and descriptive statistics

All 48 participants completed the experimental session without protocol deviations that required exclusion. No participant had more than the allowable number of missed processing responses, and so the full sample proceeded to analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed for mean letters recalled per participant, processing accuracy, and processing response times. The distribution of the primary dependent variable, mean letters recalled, was inspected for normality using histograms and the Shapiro-Wilk test. The Shapiro-Wilk test indicated no significant departure from normality for the letters-recalled measure across the full sample, suggesting that parametric tests would be appropriate. Visual inspection of the distribution also revealed a roughly symmetric distribution without extreme skew. Levene’s test for equality of variances was conducted to check the homogeneity of variance assumption for the primary group comparison and indicated no significant variance difference between the bilingual and monolingual groups for letters recalled.

Group comparisons on the primary outcome

The bilingual group had a mean letters recalled score of 3.12 out of 5 across trials with a standard deviation of 0.61. The monolingual group had a mean letters recalled score of 3.06 with a standard deviation of 0.68. An independent-samples t-test comparing these means yielded t(46) = 0.31, p = 0.76, indicating no statistically significant difference between bilinguals and monolinguals on the primary memory measure. Cohen’s d for the between-group difference was 0.09, a very small effect size. These descriptive numbers indicate that both groups performed similarly on the letter recall task under the dual-task timing parameters used here.

Bayesian analysis of the primary comparison

To quantify evidence for the absence of an effect, a Bayesian independent-samples t-test was calculated. The resulting Bayes factor in favor of the null hypothesis relative to the alternative hypothesis was BF01 = 3.8. This Bayes factor indicates moderate evidence supporting the null hypothesis that there is no difference between bilinguals and monolinguals in mean letters recalled under the present task parameters. The Bayesian result thus reinforces the frequentist null finding and suggests that the data provide appreciable support for the absence of a bilingual advantage in this specific time-based dual-task.

Processing task performance and trade-offs

To rule out the possibility that bilinguals adopted different trade-offs between processing and storage, processing task accuracy and mean response times were compared across groups. Mean sentence judgment accuracy was high and similar across groups, with bilinguals averaging 96.5% correct and monolinguals averaging 95.9% correct. An independent-samples t-test on processing accuracy yielded t(46) = 0.48, p = 0.63, indicating no group difference. Mean response times for the sentence judgments were also comparable, with bilinguals producing a mean response time of 1,078 ms (SD = 104 ms) and monolinguals producing a mean of 1,092 ms (SD = 121 ms). The t-test for response times yielded t(46) = -0.43, p = 0.67, again indicating no significant difference. These results suggest that participants across groups engaged similarly with the processing component and that memory performance differences cannot be attributed to systematic differences in processing effort.

Exploratory correlations with language history

Within the bilingual group, exploratory Pearson correlations examined associations between self-reported bilingual experience measures and mean letters recalled. Age of acquisition of the second language correlated weakly and non-significantly with letters recalled (r = -0.11, p = 0.61), indicating that earlier age of acquisition was not reliably associated with better memory performance in this sample. Frequency of use of the second language correlated weakly and non-significantly with letters recalled (r = 0.17, p = 0.43). Self-rated proficiency in the second language likewise showed a weak and non-significant association with letters recalled (r = 0.08, p = 0.72). These exploratory results do not provide evidence that within-group variation in bilingual experience predicted memory performance under the timed dual-task.

Regression analyses controlling for covariates

Given the possibility that subtle demographic differences could mask effects, linear regression analyses were conducted with mean letters recalled as the dependent variable and group, age, and years of education as predictors. Group (bilingual vs monolingual) was dummy coded and entered in the first step, with age and years of education entered in a second step. The overall model did not explain a significant proportion of variance in recall performance (R^2 = 0.03, F(3,44) = 0.47, p = 0.71). None of the predictors, including group, made a significant independent contribution to the model. These regression results corroborate the simple group comparison and suggest that demographic covariates did not obscure an effect of bilingualism on the primary memory measure.

Outlier and sensitivity checks

A set of sensitivity analyses was performed to ensure that the null result was not driven by a small number of outlier observations. Standardized residuals and Cook’s distance were inspected for each subject in the primary t-test model. No observation exceeded conventional thresholds for influential cases, and removing the two most extreme observations did not meaningfully change the result from non-significant to significant. Repeating the group comparison using a nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test yielded a p-value of 0.68, further supporting the robustness of the null effect irrespective of the statistical method.

Additional analyses of serial position

Because TBRS-based effects could vary with serial position in the letter lists, means for letters recalled by serial position (positions 1 through 5) were computed separately for each group. Both groups showed the expected primacy and recency trends, with slightly higher accuracy for the first and last positions and somewhat lower accuracy for middle positions. A mixed-design ANOVA with serial position as a within-subjects factor and group as a between-subjects factor indicated a strong main effect of serial position (F(4,184) = 51.2, p < 0.001), reflecting the typical serial position curve, but no main effect of group (F(1,46) = 0.10, p = 0.75) and no significant interaction between group and serial position (F(4,184) = 0.45, p = 0.77). These results indicate that bilinguals and monolinguals exhibited similar patterns of serial position effects under the present timing constraints.

Power considerations

A post hoc power analysis based on the observed pooled standard deviation indicated that with the present sample size of 48, the study had approximately 80% power to detect a medium effect size of d = 0.65 at alpha = 0.05. The observed effect size was much smaller (d = 0.09), indicating that the present study was not powered to detect very small effects. However, the Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence in favor of the null model, suggesting that the absence of an observed effect is not merely a consequence of insufficient sample size but rather that the data are more consistent with the null hypothesis than with a meaningful bilingual advantage under the present conditions.

Summary of results

In summary, group comparisons revealed no significant difference between bilingual and monolingual young adults in the number of letters recalled under a TBRS-style timed dual-task. Processing accuracy and response times were similar across groups, indicating no obvious trade-off between processing and storage that could account for the null result. Exploratory correlations within bilinguals did not reveal relationships between indices of bilingual experience and memory performance. Regression analyses that controlled for demographic covariates did not alter the conclusion. Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence in support of the null hypothesis. Together, these analyses indicate that bilingual experience, as operationalized in this study, was not associated with improved maintenance of letter information under the time-constrained dual-task conditions employed here.

Discussion

The present study examined whether bilingual experience confers an advantage in working memory under an experimentally controlled time-based dual-task that draws directly on the logic of the time-based resource-sharing model. The central finding was that bilingual and monolingual young adults performed similarly on a letter recall task embedded in a paced processing stream designed to create intermittent opportunities for attentional refreshing. Both groups recalled an average of approximately three letters out of five under these conditions, and statistical comparisons revealed no significant group differences. Complementary analyses showed comparable accuracy and response times on the processing judgments across groups, indicating that there was no simple trade-off in which one group prioritized processing at the expense of storage or vice versa. Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence favoring the null hypothesis of no group difference. Exploratory analyses within the bilingual group did not reveal relationships between self-reported language history variables and recall performance. Overall, the pattern of results supports the conclusion that bilingual experience, at least as characterized in the present sample and measured by the present task, is not associated with superior working memory performance when memory maintenance must be achieved through brief, time-limited refreshing episodes.

These results bear directly on theoretical claims linking bilingual experience to enhanced attentional control and, by extension, to more efficient refreshing in working memory. The TBRS model posits that memory maintenance depends critically on the proportion of time attention is available for refreshing. If bilingual experience led to reliably faster switching to refreshing or more effective use of brief attentional gaps, then bilinguals should have shown a recall advantage under the present task parameters that constrained refreshing time. The absence of such an advantage suggests that bilingual experience does not translate into superior refreshing efficiency in a way that is detectable by the present paradigm. This conclusion is consistent with a body of recent work that calls into question broad claims of bilingual advantages in domain-general cognitive control and working memory.

It is important to situate these findings within the broader literature and to consider potential explanations for why some previous studies have reported bilingual advantages while others, like the present study, have not. One critical factor is the heterogeneity of bilingual experience. Not all forms of bilingualism are the same, and different dimensions of bilingual practice—such as early simultaneous bilingualism, late second-language acquisition, frequency of language switching, and degree of balanced proficiency—may have different cognitive consequences. The bilingual participants in this study represented a range of language backgrounds and did not comprise a homogenous group of balanced early bilinguals. It is possible that specific subtypes of bilinguals, for example, those who engage extensively in frequent language switching in everyday life, might exhibit measurable advantages in attentional allocation that support refreshing. Our exploratory analyses of within-group variation did not detect robust associations between self-reported language experience and memory performance, but such analyses may lack sensitivity given the sample size and the coarse nature of self-report measures.

Another consideration is task sensitivity. The present study used a TBRS-inspired timed dual-task that deliberately constrained the time available for refreshing using a single pace calibrated to create a moderate cognitive load. While this approach has strong theoretical grounding, it is possible that the chosen pace did not tap the temporal dynamics in which bilingual advantages, if present, would be most evident. For example, a task that more severely restricts refreshing time or that varies the time budget parametrically across a wider range might reveal group differences that are not apparent at a single intermediate pace. Conversely, tasks that allow for strategic rehearsal or use different modalities (e.g., auditory vs visual presentation) might produce different outcomes. Future studies could use multiple pacing conditions to test whether group differences emerge under more extreme constraints or under freer pacing.

The sensitivity of the measure is also relevant. Letter recall in a short list format relies on both attentional refreshing and perhaps covert verbal rehearsal processes that might differ across individuals. If bilingual experience affects executive aspects of attention more than verbal rehearsal per se, tasks that isolate attentional switching from rehearsal may be more diagnostic. Researchers may consider combining TBRS-style manipulations with articulatory suppression to reduce the contribution of rehearsal and better isolate refreshing processes. Similarly, employing neurophysiological measures such as EEG markers of attentional allocation or fMRI measures of brain networks engaged during refreshing may detect subtler group differences that are not evident in overt behavioral accuracy.

Sample size and statistical power are practical factors that warrant discussion. The study included 48 participants, evenly divided across groups, and was sufficiently powered to detect medium-sized effects. The observed effect was small and not statistically reliable. The Bayesian analysis suggested that the data provided moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis, which reduces the concern that the null result is merely due to insufficient power. Nonetheless, very small effects could exist that the study was not sensitive enough to detect. If bilingual advantages in refreshing are genuine but small, then much larger sample sizes or highly sensitive within-subject manipulations may be required to identify them.

An additional point concerns potential confounding variables. Although efforts were made to match groups on demographic variables and to recruit from a single university community to reduce variability in socioeconomic status and education, unmeasured differences could still have influenced performance. For example, differences in sleep, stress, or domain-specific cognitive training outside of language could influence working memory capacity and attentional allocation. The lack of group differences in processing accuracy and response times suggests comparable engagement with the task, which reduces the likelihood of certain confounds, but it does not rule out all potential extraneous influences.

Theoretical implications of the present findings relate to how bilingualism is conceptualized as a cognitive experience. The absence of a detectable advantage in a TBRS-style dual-task suggests that bilingual experience may not systematically enhance the temporal dynamics of attention in the way required for more effective refreshing. This outcome challenges accounts that posit broad, domain-general cognitive enhancements as a direct result of bilingualism. Instead, it supports a more nuanced view in which bilingualism may influence certain cognitive processes in specific contexts or might produce differences in strategy use rather than in core attentional mechanics. For instance, bilinguals may develop language-specific control processes or improved monitoring abilities that do not generalize to the kind of rapid switching required for refreshing in a letter recall task.

The findings also inform the TBRS framework by indicating the boundary conditions under which individual differences in experience affect refreshing. The TBRS model emphasizes the role of time and attentional allocation but does not make strong claims about how life experiences might tune these processes. The present null result implies that, at least for the bilingual experiences represented in this sample, TBRS-predicted benefits are not observed. This suggests that the temporal efficiency of refreshing may be relatively stable across these participants, or that improvements in attentional control arising from bilingualism do not manifest as faster or more effective refreshing. Future theoretical work could explicate the cognitive processes that would need to change for bilingualism to benefit TBRS-defined refreshing and design tasks that specifically target those processes.

Future research directions emerge naturally from the present work. One priority is to characterize bilingual samples more finely, using objective measures of proficiency, language switching frequency collected via experience sampling, and task-based measures of linguistic control. This would allow tests of whether particular bilingual experiences predict working memory performance. Another direction is to manipulate the time budget parametrically across multiple levels to determine whether group differences manifest at specific temporal scales. Adding articulatory suppression or cross-modal presentation could help isolate the refreshing component from rehearsal. Longitudinal studies of second-language learning and experimental training paradigms that induce language switching practice could provide causal evidence about whether bilingual-like experiences alter refreshing dynamics.

Neurophysiological methods could also add value by revealing whether bilinguals and monolinguals recruit different neural circuits during timed refreshing intervals even when behavioral outcomes are similar. For example, EEG could detect differences in attentional allocation or the timing of neural signatures associated with switching that are not reflected in recall accuracy. Functional neuroimaging could reveal group differences in network efficiency or connectivity patterns during concurrent processing and maintenance. Such converging evidence would enrich our understanding of whether and how bilingualism intersects with the neurocognitive substrates of working memory.

Another important extension is to examine different age groups. Some evidence suggests that bilingual advantages may be more likely to appear in children during developmental windows or in older adults as a form of cognitive reserve. Applying TBRS-style paradigms to these populations could reveal developmental or aging-related interactions between language experience and the temporal dynamics of refreshing. Similarly, examining clinical populations or individuals with attentional control deficits could test whether bilingual experience interacts with baseline levels of cognitive functioning in shaping memory outcomes.

Practical implications of the findings caution against blanket assertions that bilingualism confers robust benefits to domain-general working memory in typical young adults. Educators and policymakers should therefore be wary of using presumed cognitive benefits of bilingualism as a primary justification for language education policies. That said, the present results do not negate the many cultural, social, and communicative advantages of bilingualism, nor do they rule out cognitive benefits in other domains or in different populations.

Limitations of the current study must be acknowledged. The sample size, while adequate for detecting medium effects, limits inferences about very small effects. The bilingual group was heterogeneous, and self-report measures may not capture the nuances of language experience that matter for cognitive outcomes. The task employed a single pacing condition and relatively short letter lists, which may have constrained the range of observable behavior. Finally, the study focused on a specific manifestation of working memory—the capacity to maintain serial order of letters under time-constrained processing—and results may not generalize to other working memory tasks that tap different processes.

In conclusion, the present study applied a theoretically grounded, time-based dual-task paradigm to test whether bilingual experience confers advantages in working memory maintenance through more efficient refreshing. The data indicated no significant difference between bilingual and monolingual young adults in letter recall under the timed processing conditions used, and Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence supporting the null hypothesis. These results contribute to a growing literature suggesting that bilingual advantages in domain-general cognitive functions are neither universal nor large and may be contingent on specific experiential, task, or sample characteristics. Future research that disaggregates bilingual experience more precisely, uses a range of temporal manipulations, and integrates behavioral and neurophysiological measures will be valuable for delineating the conditions under which bilingualism might impact the temporal dynamics of attention and working memory.
