Introduction

Social media has become a pervasive part of everyday life for people across a wide range of ages and backgrounds. Patterns of use vary across platforms and individuals, but for many people social networks now mediate a large proportion of social interaction and self-presentation. Understanding the psychological correlates and consequences of social media use has therefore become an increasingly important focus for research and for public health. Among the psychological constructs that have been linked to social media behavior, self-esteem and body image are two that have received substantial attention. Self-esteem is a broad evaluative attitude toward the self, and it has long been recognized as central to mental health and well-being. Body image refers to an individual’s perceptions, thoughts, and feelings about their physical appearance, and it is intimately tied to self-esteem for many people. Social media environments are visually oriented, socially comparative, and often quantified through visible metrics such as “likes,” friends, and followers. These features have the potential to influence both body image and global self-evaluations such as self-esteem.

Research has suggested multiple, sometimes competing pathways linking social media use and self-esteem. One common line of thought emphasizes social comparison processes. On visually driven platforms, users are frequently exposed to curated images of peers and influencers that may depict idealized body shapes, lifestyles, and achievements. When users compare themselves to these idealized images, they may experience diminished body satisfaction and lower self-esteem. Several studies have found that upward social comparisons made while using social media are associated with negative affect, lower body satisfaction, and decreases in self-esteem. The image-centric nature of some platforms can therefore make social comparison particularly salient. Another line of research emphasizes social reinforcement processes. Social media platforms provide quantifiable feedback mechanisms such as “likes,” comments, and follower counts which can serve as external gauges of social approval. Positive feedback received through these channels can bolster feelings of social connectedness and self-worth. Consequently, receiving more likes or having a larger network of friends could in some contexts be associated with higher self-esteem. These two broad mechanisms — social comparison and social reinforcement — can operate simultaneously and may exert opposing effects on self-esteem.

Different social media platforms emphasize different kinds of content and social processes, and this heterogeneity matters for understanding psychological outcomes. Facebook has traditionally emphasized networks of acquaintances and friends, status updates, and a mixed media feed. Instagram, by contrast, is a visually centered platform that highlights images and short videos, with an emphasis on curated visual self-presentation. The affordances of Instagram — including filters, image editing, and the central role of photographs — make it particularly likely to encourage appearance-based comparisons. These platform differences have led researchers to argue that the psychological correlates of social media use may vary by platform. For instance, Facebook may promote social connectedness through maintenance of broader social networks, while Instagram may be more likely to induce appearance-based comparison and concerns about body image. It is therefore theoretically plausible that the same objective metrics — such as number of friends or likes — could bear different relations to self-esteem depending on the platform on which they are observed.

Prior research has yielded mixed findings about the direction and strength of associations between social media metrics and self-esteem. Some studies indicate that larger networks and more frequent positive feedback are associated with higher self-reported self-esteem and reduced loneliness. These studies often emphasize the social support and belonging that can be derived from online interaction. Other studies, however, have documented associations between greater social media use and lower self-esteem, particularly when use is passive, involves extensive comparison, or centers on image-based platforms. The nuance of these findings suggests that the nature of the engagement — active versus passive, social versus appearance-focused — is likely a crucial determinant of psychological outcomes. Researchers have also pointed to individual differences that moderate the relationship between social media use and self-esteem. For example, people with pre-existing low self-esteem or body image concerns may be more vulnerable to the negative effects of social comparison. Gender may also moderate relations because body image pressures differ in magnitude and content across genders.

Measured social media metrics themselves are diverse. Count variables like number of friends on Facebook or followers on Instagram are coarse proxies for social capital and visibility, but they may capture different phenomena across platforms. The number of likes received on posts is a more direct indicator of momentary social reinforcement. Moreover, time spent on a platform and usage style — whether a user actively posts and interacts or passively consumes content — are additional dimensions that may be critical to psychological outcomes. Research has started to unpack these dimensions, showing that active use can strengthen social ties and be associated with positive outcomes, while passive use is more likely to promote social comparison and negative affect.

Given this background, there is a clear need for studies that examine multiple aspects of social media engagement in relation to self-esteem and body image, and that distinguish between platforms with different affordances. In particular, it is valuable to examine how quantifiable metrics of online social feedback — likes, friends, and followers — relate to self-esteem on Facebook and Instagram. It is also important to consider how overall platform usage interacts with these metrics and with indicators of body image. Previous literature points to the possibility that likes on Facebook might be positively associated with self-esteem through social reinforcement and perceived social support. In contrast, likes on Instagram might be less uniformly positive and could be linked to appearance-focused comparisons, which may attenuate any positive association with self-esteem. Similarly, having many Facebook friends could reflect a broad social network that bolsters belonging and self-worth, whereas a large number of Instagram followers might reflect public visibility and comparative pressures that are not necessarily protective of self-esteem.

Another reason to investigate both platforms concurrently is to explore whether relationships observed on one platform generalize to the other. Investigating cross-platform patterns in the same sample allows for within-person comparisons and for testing whether platform-specific features moderate associations between social metrics and self-esteem. Prior studies that focused on single platforms cannot disentangle whether observed associations are platform-general or platform-specific. The current study aims to address this gap by examining Facebook and Instagram concurrently in the same participants.

Beyond bivariate correlations, it is also informative to examine whether associations between social media metrics and self-esteem persist after controlling for body image. Because body image is tightly linked with self-esteem and may be especially relevant to Instagram use, controlling for it allows us to test whether social media metrics have unique associations with global self-evaluations. If likes on Facebook predict self-esteem even after accounting for body image, this would support the notion that social reinforcement per se, rather than appearance satisfaction, contributes to self-worth. Conversely, if Instagram metrics lose association with self-esteem after controlling for body image, this would be consistent with the idea that the link between Instagram use and self-worth is mediated by body image concerns.

The present study used a cross-sectional survey design to investigate correlations between self-esteem, body image, and multiple social media variables, focusing specifically on Facebook and Instagram. The sample was drawn from a mix of university students and community participants to capture a reasonable age range. Participants reported on their number of Facebook friends and Instagram followers, their typical number of likes received on each platform, the frequency and duration of use of each platform, and their style of engagement (active posting vs passive browsing). Standardized self-report scales were used to assess global self-esteem and body image. Our primary aims were to examine: (1) whether likes, friends, and followers on Facebook and Instagram were associated with self-esteem; (2) whether usage levels on each platform were associated with self-esteem; and (3) whether any observed associations remained after controlling for body image, age, and gender.

Based on prior theory and empirical work, we formulated several hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that the number of likes on Facebook would be positively correlated with self-esteem, reflecting social reinforcement and perceived social approval. Second, we hypothesized that likes on Instagram would show a weaker or non-significant positive relation with self-esteem and might even be unrelated or negatively related due to appearance-based comparisons. Third, we hypothesized that the number of Facebook friends would be positively associated with self-esteem, whereas the number of Instagram followers might show a weaker or negative association. Fourth, we hypothesized that overall levels of Facebook use would be positively associated with self-esteem, reflecting maintenance of social ties, while levels of Instagram use would be negatively associated with self-esteem, reflecting passive use and appearance comparison effects for some users. Fifth, we hypothesized that any positive associations between Facebook social metrics and self-esteem would remain after controlling for body image, but that associations for Instagram would be attenuated when controlling for body image.

The study also included exploratory analyses. We explored whether gender moderated any of the key associations between social media metrics and self-esteem. We also examined whether active versus passive usage styles moderated the relationships between platform metrics and self-esteem. Finally, we conducted multiple regression analyses to identify unique predictors of self-esteem among the social media variables and body image when controlling for demographic covariates. The current design is correlational and cross-sectional, and we therefore do not make causal claims. Nevertheless, the study aims to clarify platform-specific associations and to inform future longitudinal or experimental work.

In sum, the study sought to advance understanding of how quantifiable social feedback and usage behaviors on Facebook and Instagram relate to global self-evaluations and body image. The comparison across platforms and the inclusion of multiple social metrics and controls provide a more nuanced test of competing theoretical accounts involving social reinforcement and social comparison. The results have implications for understanding the mechanisms through which social media can affect psychological well-being and for designing interventions or educational guidance for healthier social media use.

Methods

Participants

A total of 125 individuals participated in the study. Participants were recruited through a university subject pool and community-based advertisements on local online forums. The age range of participants was from 18 to 56 years. The mean age of the sample was 24.8 years with a standard deviation of 7.1 years. The sample included both students and non-student adults to obtain a mixed demographic profile. Regarding gender, 72 participants identified as female, 50 as male, and 3 as non-binary or another gender identity. The recruitment materials indicated that participants should be regular users of both Facebook and Instagram in order to provide meaningful responses about both platforms. Regular use was defined as logging into each platform at least once per month.

Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old and to have active accounts on both Facebook and Instagram. Exclusion criteria included reporting cognitive impairments that could compromise the ability to complete the survey and not completing key sections of the survey. Participants who failed an embedded attention check item were excluded from analysis. A total of 7 respondents were excluded for incomplete data or failing the attention check, resulting in the final analytic sample of 125.

Ethics

The study was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics board at the university conducting the research. Participants provided informed consent electronically prior to beginning the online survey. Consent materials explained that participation was voluntary, that responses would be anonymized, and that participants could withdraw at any time. No personally identifiable information was collected. Participants were provided with debriefing information and resources for mental health support at the end of the survey.

Measures

Demographics and social media accounts. Participants reported their age, gender, and student status. They also reported whether they maintained active accounts on Facebook and Instagram and provided estimates of their number of Facebook friends and Instagram followers. For both platforms, participants estimated their average number of likes per post over the past three months. Participants also reported how many times per day, on average, they logged into each platform, and how much time per day they spent on each platform on average (in minutes).

Self-esteem. Global self-esteem was measured using a commonly used 10-item self-report scale. Items were rated on a four-point Likert-type scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Items were scored such that higher total scores indicated higher self-esteem. Internal consistency for the self-esteem scale in the current sample was high, with Cronbach’s alpha calculated to assess reliability.

Body image. Body image was assessed with a 7-item appearance satisfaction measure that asked participants to rate their agreement with statements regarding satisfaction with appearance and body-related self-evaluations. Items were rated on a five-point Likert scale and were aggregated to form a single body image score, with higher scores indicating more positive body image. Internal consistency for the body image scale was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha.

Usage style. Participants completed a brief set of items designed to capture active versus passive social media usage. Active usage items assessed behaviors such as posting photos, commenting on others’ content, and direct messaging friends. Passive usage items assessed behaviors such as browsing others’ profiles, viewing images without interacting, and scrolling through feeds. Responses were given on a five-point frequency scale and aggregated to form active usage and passive usage indices for each platform.

Procedures

The study was conducted online using a secure survey platform. After providing informed consent, participants completed demographic items followed by sections on Facebook and Instagram use. The order of platform-related sections was counterbalanced across participants to reduce order effects. For each platform, participants provided numeric estimates for friends or followers and average likes per post. They then completed the usage frequency and style items. Finally, participants completed the self-esteem and body image scales, interspersed with attention check items. The entire survey took approximately 15 to 20 minutes to complete.

Data screening and analytic plan

Data were screened for missing values and response validity. Participants with incomplete key measures or who failed attention checks were excluded. Continuous variables were examined for normality, skewness, and kurtosis. Transformations were applied to highly skewed count variables if necessary; for example, follower counts that exhibited long right tails were log-transformed for parametric analyses. Reliability analyses were conducted for multi-item scales. Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables including means, standard deviations, and ranges.

The primary analytic approach involved Pearson correlation coefficients to examine bivariate associations among self-esteem, body image, and social media metrics for Facebook and Instagram. Partial correlations controlling for age and gender were also computed to assess whether associations were robust to demographic covariates. Multiple regression analyses were conducted to identify unique predictors of self-esteem. In a hierarchical regression, demographic covariates (age and gender) were entered in the first block, body image in the second block, and social media predictors (likes, friends/followers, usage levels for Facebook and Instagram, and active/passive usage indices) were entered in the third block. This approach permitted evaluation of whether social media variables explained variance in self-esteem beyond demographics and body image.

Exploratory moderation analyses were conducted to examine whether gender or active/passive usage style moderated the association between platform-specific likes and self-esteem. Interaction terms were computed after centering predictor variables. Simple slope analyses were conducted for significant interactions to clarify the nature of moderation effects.

Statistical significance was evaluated at the conventional alpha level of .05. Effect sizes for correlations were interpreted according to standard conventions. For regression analyses, standardized beta weights, t-tests, and R-squared values were reported. All analyses were conducted using a standard statistical software package.

Data handling and transparency

All data were de-identified prior to analysis. Descriptive and inferential statistical results are presented in the Results section. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, causal inferences were avoided. The study followed established practices in social psychology for survey research, including the use of established scales and indices for key constructs. The sample size of 125 provided adequate power to detect medium-sized correlations and medium effects in multiple regression analyses, though smaller effects would be more challenging to detect reliably.

Results

Descriptive statistics and scale reliability

Descriptive statistics for the main variables are presented here. The final sample consisted of 125 participants after exclusions. The mean age was 24.8 years (SD = 7.1). Females constituted 57.6% of the sample, males 40.0%, and non-binary/other 2.4%. The mean self-esteem score on the 10-item scale was 27.6 (SD = 4.9) on a scale with a theoretical range from 10 to 40, indicating generally moderate to high self-esteem in this sample. The mean body image score was 3.4 (SD = 0.8) on a five-point scale where higher scores indicated more positive body image.

Participants reported a mean of 420 Facebook friends (SD = 312), with a range from 50 to 2000 friends. Instagram follower counts were more variable, with a mean of 310 followers (SD = 560) and a range from 10 to 4000 followers. Because follower and friend counts were right-skewed, log-transformed values were computed and used in correlational and regression analyses where appropriate. Average likes per post differed by platform. For Facebook, participants reported a mean of 35 likes per post (SD = 28), while for Instagram the mean reported likes per post was 89 (SD = 210). Likes per post on Instagram showed greater skew and kurtosis and were log-transformed for inferential tests.

Time spent per day on Facebook averaged 28 minutes (SD = 24), while time spent per day on Instagram averaged 44 minutes (SD = 38). Frequency of logins per day averaged 2.6 for Facebook and 3.8 for Instagram.

Internal consistency for the self-esteem scale in this sample was high, with Cronbach’s alpha = .88. Internal consistency for the body image scale was acceptable, with Cronbach’s alpha = .83. The active usage and passive usage indices demonstrated acceptable reliability (active usage alpha = .76, passive usage alpha = .79).

Bivariate correlations

Pearson correlations among the key variables were computed. Self-esteem and body image were positively and strongly correlated in the expected direction (r = .52, p < .001). This affirmed the relevance of body image as a correlate of global self-evaluations in this sample.

Focusing on platform-specific metrics, the number of likes received on Facebook was positively correlated with self-esteem (r = .30, p = .001). This association remained significant when controlling for age and gender in a partial correlation (r = .28, p = .002). In contrast, the number of likes received on Instagram was not significantly correlated with self-esteem (r = .05, p = .59). The difference in the magnitude of the correlation between Facebook likes and self-esteem versus Instagram likes and self-esteem was notable and consistent with the hypothesis that platform context matters.

Number of Facebook friends was positively but weakly correlated with self-esteem (r = .10, p = .26), and this association did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance. In contrast, the number of Instagram followers was negatively but weakly correlated with self-esteem (r = -.12, p = .18), which also did not reach significance. These correlations were in the directions anticipated by the hypotheses (positive for Facebook friends, negative for Instagram followers) but were small in magnitude.

Regarding usage levels, time spent on Facebook was modestly and positively correlated with self-esteem (r = .16, p = .07), a correlation that approached but did not reach conventional significance thresholds. Time spent on Instagram was modestly and negatively correlated with self-esteem (r = -.15, p = .09), which also approached significance but did not meet the .05 threshold. Frequency of login showed a similar but slightly weaker pattern. These trends are consistent with the theoretical expectation that Facebook use may, on average, be associated with maintaining social ties while Instagram use may, on average, be associated with passive browsing and social comparison.

Active versus passive usage style correlated differentially with self-esteem. Active Facebook usage (posting, commenting, messaging) correlated positively with self-esteem (r = .22, p = .01), whereas passive Facebook usage correlated negatively with self-esteem (r = -.11, p = .21). For Instagram, active usage was weakly and non-significantly correlated with self-esteem (r = .08, p = .35), while passive usage on Instagram was negatively correlated with self-esteem (r = -.23, p = .007). These patterns suggest that the manner of engagement — active versus passive — is meaningfully related to self-evaluations and that passive browsing on Instagram may be particularly linked to lower self-esteem.

Correlations between likes and follower counts and body image were also examined. Facebook likes showed a modest positive correlation with body image (r = .18, p = .04). Instagram likes were not significantly correlated with body image (r = .06, p = .51). Instagram follower counts were negatively correlated with body image (r = -.14, p = .11), but this correlation was not significant.

Regression analyses

To examine unique predictors of self-esteem, a hierarchical multiple regression was conducted. In the first block, demographic covariates age and gender were entered. Age was positively associated with self-esteem in the regression (standardized beta = .15, p = .04), while gender was not a significant predictor in the first block (beta = -.07, p = .36). The first block accounted for 3.4% of the variance in self-esteem (adjusted R^2 = .03).

In the second block, body image was entered. Body image was a strong positive predictor of self-esteem (beta = .47, p < .001) and accounted for an additional 23% of variance beyond demographics. The model after two blocks had an adjusted R^2 = .26, indicating that body image was a substantive correlate of self-esteem in this sample.

In the third block, social media variables were entered simultaneously. These variables included log-transformed Facebook friends, log-transformed Instagram followers, log-transformed Facebook likes per post, log-transformed Instagram likes per post, time spent on Facebook, time spent on Instagram, and active and passive usage indices for both platforms. In this full model, Facebook likes emerged as a significant positive predictor of self-esteem (beta = .24, t = 3.15, p = .002) even after controlling for body image and demographics. Body image remained a robust predictor in the full model (beta = .42, t = 6.34, p < .001). Other social media variables, including Instagram likes, Facebook friends, Instagram followers, and time spent on Instagram, did not reach conventional levels of significance in the presence of body image and Facebook likes. Time spent on Facebook showed a small positive but non-significant beta (beta = .09, p = .18). Passive usage on Instagram had a negative beta that approached significance (beta = -.12, p = .06), suggesting a trend whereby passive Instagram consumption might be linked to lower self-esteem when other variables are controlled.

The full model including demographics, body image, and social media variables accounted for 34% of the variance in self-esteem (adjusted R^2 = .34), indicating that together these variables provided a substantial explanation for individual differences in self-esteem in this sample. The addition of social media variables in block three explained an incremental 5% of the variance beyond demographics and body image (change in R^2 = .05, p = .01).

Partial correlations controlling for body image

Given the strong relation between body image and self-esteem, partial correlations were computed to examine associations between social media metrics and self-esteem after controlling for body image. The partial correlation between Facebook likes and self-esteem remained significant when controlling for body image (partial r = .21, p = .01). The partial correlation between Instagram likes and self-esteem remained non-significant (partial r = .02, p = .79). The partial correlations for Facebook friends and Instagram followers with self-esteem remained non-significant (partial r = .08, p = .33; partial r = -.09, p = .28, respectively).

Moderation analyses

Exploratory moderation analyses tested whether gender or active/passive usage style moderated the relation between Facebook likes and self-esteem. Interaction terms were computed between Facebook likes and gender, and between Facebook likes and active usage for Facebook. The interaction between Facebook likes and gender was not significant (interaction beta = -.03, p = .74), indicating that the positive association between Facebook likes and self-esteem was similar across genders in this sample. The interaction between Facebook likes and active usage was also not significant (beta = .06, p = .49), suggesting that the association between Facebook likes and self-esteem did not significantly differ between those who reported higher active engagement and those with lower active engagement.

For Instagram, the interaction between passive usage and Instagram likes in predicting self-esteem was tested. This interaction was not significant (beta = -.09, p = .21), though passive usage on its own correlated negatively with self-esteem as noted earlier.

Supplementary analyses

Given the skew in follower and like counts, alternative analyses using nonparametric Spearman correlations were conducted as a robustness check. The Spearman correlations largely mirrored the Pearson results: Facebook likes had a positive Spearman rho with self-esteem (rho = .27, p = .003), Instagram likes did not correlate substantially with self-esteem (rho = .07, p = .42), and passive Instagram usage had a negative association with self-esteem (rho = -.21, p = .02).

Taken together, the results indicate a consistent pattern: likes on Facebook were positively associated with self-esteem and remained so after controlling for body image and demographics. Instagram metrics did not show a similar positive association; passive usage on Instagram was associated with lower self-esteem. Number of friends or followers had small and non-significant correlations with self-esteem, suggesting that simple counts of connections are not strong predictors in this sample when measured cross-sectionally.

Discussion

The present study examined associations among self-esteem, body image, and multiple social media variables across Facebook and Instagram in a sample of 125 adults. The design allowed us to compare platform-specific metrics and usage patterns while controlling for demographic variables and body image. The central findings were that the number of likes received on Facebook was positively associated with self-esteem, and this association held even after controlling for body image, age, and gender. Instagram metrics, including likes and follower counts, did not show comparable positive associations with self-esteem; in some cases, trends suggested negative associations between passive Instagram use and self-esteem. Counts of friends and followers were weakly related to self-esteem and did not reach significance. Overall, the pattern of results supports the idea that platform context and usage style matter for psychological outcomes, and that social reinforcement on Facebook may be linked to greater self-esteem whereas Instagram’s image-focused affordances and passive browsing may be associated with less favorable self-evaluations for some users.

These findings contribute to a growing literature that emphasizes heterogeneity in social media effects. Not all social media use is the same, and different platforms can have distinct psychological correlates. Our observation that Facebook likes were positively associated with self-esteem aligns with theoretical accounts that posit social reinforcement and perceived social approval as important contributors to self-worth. Likes on Facebook may be experienced as signals of closeness or validation from one’s broader social network, and such social reinforcement could bolster feelings of belonging and self-esteem. Facebook’s design and norms may encourage more interpersonal exchanges that are grounded in existing offline relationships, and likes in that context may serve to reinforce a sense of social integration.

In contrast, Instagram’s focus on curated images and public-facing follower metrics may engender social comparison processes that attenuate or even reverse any positive effect of likes on self-esteem. The fact that Instagram likes did not significantly predict self-esteem in our sample suggests that the meaning of likes may be more ambiguous on that platform. For some Instagram users, likes could confer status and boost self-esteem, but for others, the same indicators might intensify social comparison and pressure to conform to idealized standards. The lack of a clear positive association for Instagram is consistent with theoretical frameworks that emphasize appearance-focused comparisons on image-centric platforms.

Passive usage emerged as an important correlate in the anticipated direction. Passive consumption on Instagram was negatively associated with self-esteem and body image, a pattern consistent with prior work suggesting that passive browsing fosters upward social comparison and negative affect. Conversely, active usage, particularly on Facebook, tended to correlate positively with self-esteem. Active behaviors that involve interpersonal exchange, self-disclosure, and direct messaging may strengthen social ties and enhance perceived social support, thereby supporting self-esteem. These differential associations between active and passive use underscore the significance of usage style as a moderator of psychological outcomes.

The small and non-significant correlations between raw counts of friends and followers and self-esteem are informative. While it is tempting to assume that having many friends or followers confers social capital that enhances self-esteem, our data suggest that simple counts are not necessarily strong predictors of self-evaluations. The quality of interactions and the meaning attached to those relationships likely matter more than numerical size. For Facebook, a large number of friends could include many weak ties that do not meaningfully contribute to belonging. For Instagram, a large follower count could reflect a public audience that increases visibility but does not necessarily provide substantive social support. The lack of robust associations for these count variables suggests that future research should attend more to the qualitative nature of online ties and to the subjective interpretation of social metrics.

Body image was a robust predictor of self-esteem in this study, which is consistent with extant literature linking appearance satisfaction to global self-evaluations. Body image explained a substantial portion of variance in self-esteem, and it remained significant even after accounting for social media variables. This underscores the central role of body-related self-appraisals in self-esteem. Importantly, the positive association between Facebook likes and self-esteem persisted after controlling for body image, indicating that Facebook likes may influence self-esteem through mechanisms independent of appearance satisfaction. Conversely, some of the associations involving Instagram and passive usage were reduced when body image was accounted for, suggesting that body image may mediate or partially account for the relation between Instagram use and self-esteem.

The exploratory moderation analyses did not find that gender significantly moderated the association between Facebook likes and self-esteem. This suggests that the positive relation between likes on Facebook and self-esteem may be similar across genders in this sample. However, the sample size and distribution across gender categories may have limited statistical power to detect moderation effects. Additionally, gender differences in the psychological impact of appearance-focused platforms could be complex and contingent on other factors such as internalization of cultural beauty norms, which were not measured here.

The hierarchical regression model indicated that social media metrics provided incremental explanatory power for self-esteem beyond demographics and body image, albeit a modest increment. This suggests that while body image is a central correlate of self-esteem, social media experiences contribute additional variance and therefore deserve attention in models of self-evaluation. The fact that only Facebook likes emerged as a robust social media predictor in the full model reinforces the importance of platform-specific analyses.

Several limitations of the study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional and correlational design precludes causal inference. While Facebook likes were associated with higher self-esteem, it is equally plausible that individuals with higher self-esteem elicit more likes on their posts, or that a third variable influences both. Longitudinal or experimental designs would be necessary to disentangle directionality. Second, self-report measures of likes, friends, followers, and usage may be subject to recall bias and social desirability. Objective logging of social media metrics would provide more precise measurement. Third, the sample, while mixed in terms of student and community participants, was relatively young on average and drawn from a single regional context, which limits generalizability. Fourth, Instagram and Facebook continue to evolve in terms of features and norms; the specific dynamics observed here may shift over time. Fifth, we measured body image as a general construct; future research could benefit from more detailed measures that capture appearance comparison tendencies, internalization of beauty ideals, and appearance-based contingent self-worth.

Despite these limitations, the study has strengths that enhance the credibility of the findings. The concurrent examination of Facebook and Instagram in the same sample allowed direct comparison of platform-specific associations. The inclusion of multiple social metrics (likes, friends, followers), usage indices (time spent and frequency), and usage styles (active vs passive) provided a nuanced picture of how different aspects of platform engagement relate to self-esteem. The use of validated scales for self-esteem and careful data screening further supported the analytic rigor.

Theoretical implications of the findings are noteworthy. The results support a differentiated conceptualization of social media effects that integrates social reinforcement and social comparison frameworks and emphasizes platform affordances and usage styles. The positive association between Facebook likes and self-esteem aligns with social reinforcement accounts, while the negative relation between passive Instagram use and self-esteem is consistent with social comparison accounts. Thus, both mechanisms appear to be operative in social media contexts, and their relative influence may depend on platform design and user behavior.

The findings point to several avenues for future research. Longitudinal studies that track changes in self-esteem and body image in relation to fluctuations in social media feedback and usage patterns would help to clarify causal pathways. Experimental manipulations that alter feedback (e.g., variations in visible like counts) or encourage active versus passive engagement could isolate causal mechanisms and inform interventions. Research that examines the subjective meanings users attach to likes, followers, and friends would enhance understanding of why similar metrics have different psychological correlates across platforms. Qualitative methods, daily diary studies, and ecological momentary assessment could provide rich within-person data on the dynamics of social media experiences and their immediate emotional impacts.

Intervention-oriented research could explore whether promoting active engagement and strengthening offline social ties mitigates the potentially negative effects of passive image-based browsing. Educational programs that build media literacy and resilience to appearance-focused comparison may also be relevant, especially for younger users. Designers and policymakers might consider how platform features such as hiding like counts or promoting interactions that foster supportive social connections could influence psychological outcomes at scale; however, rigorous empirical testing is needed to assess the consequences of such design changes.

Additional work could examine individual difference moderators beyond gender, such as trait self-esteem, neuroticism, social anxiety, and body dysmorphic tendencies, which may render some users more susceptible to negative outcomes. Cross-cultural studies would also be valuable because norms around appearance, privacy, and social feedback differ across cultural contexts and could shape the meaning and impact of social media interactions.

In conclusion, the present study contributes to the nuanced understanding of how social media metrics and usage relate to self-esteem and body image. The evidence suggests that social reinforcement, as indexed by likes on Facebook, is positively associated with self-esteem, whereas Instagram’s image-focused environment and passive browsing are more likely to relate to lower self-evaluations. These findings underscore the importance of examining platform-specific features and usage styles when assessing the psychological correlates of social media, and they highlight the need for longitudinal and experimental research to unpack causal pathways and inform interventions that promote healthier social media engagement.
