Introduction

Social competence and the ability to negotiate interpersonal interactions are widely recognized as central to psychological well-being and adaptive functioning across the lifespan. Social skills encompass a broad set of behaviors and abilities that allow an individual to initiate, maintain, and terminate social interactions in ways that are effective and context-appropriate. Temperament traits such as emotionality, activity level, sociability, and impulsivity have been linked to individual differences in how people approach and manage social situations. The interplay between early family environments and inherent temperament characteristics contributes to the developmental trajectory of social competence. Among family factors, sibling relationships constitute a pervasive and recurrent social context that can provide unique opportunities for skill learning, conflict resolution, perspective taking, and cooperative behavior. Siblings typically represent some of the first peers that children encounter, and these intra-family peer interactions are qualitatively different from parent–child interactions because they tend to be more symmetrical and require negotiation rather than unidirectional caretaking. As such, the presence or absence of siblings can plausibly influence the emergence and refinement of social competencies in ways that persist into later life. Empirical work on sibling effects on social development, conducted largely within childhood and adolescence, has tended to find that children with siblings often exhibit enhanced social skills, greater sociability, and more advanced conflict resolution strategies when compared to only children. These effects have been attributed to frequent practice in dyadic and group interaction, exposure to diverse perspectives, opportunities to serve in caregiving roles, and the need to navigate competition for shared resources. Nevertheless, the magnitude and persistence of sibling effects are subjects of ongoing debate, as findings have varied across samples, measures, and ages. Some investigators have reported modest sibling advantages that attenuate with age, while others have emphasized that sibling quality rather than mere presence is the key predictor of social outcomes. The theoretical literature therefore emphasizes both opportunity and quality as determinants of sibling-related socialization effects. Gender is another factor that has been examined in relation to social skill development, with a common observation in the literature being that women and girls often display stronger verbal communication, empathic responding, and cooperative interaction styles relative to men and boys. These gendered patterns in social behavior have been explained through multiple frameworks, including social role theory, gender socialization processes, and, in some accounts, evolutionary or biological predispositions toward caregiving-related orientation. In lay discourse it is common to attribute superior social skills in women to a so-called "motherly instinct" that predisposes them to nurturing and interpersonal attunement; in scientific terms this idea is more cautiously framed as normative gendered socialization and the internalization of relational values that emphasize interpersonal sensitivity. The intersection of sibling presence and gender is therefore a compelling empirical question because gender differences in socialization might moderate the influence of sibling interactions, leading to differential benefits of sibling experience for males and females. For instance, if cross-sibling interactions amplify tendencies toward empathic responsiveness, then females might experience a greater social-skill gain from having siblings than males. Conversely, if males benefit more from sibling-based practice in rough-and-tumble play or assertiveness negotiation, gender differences in sibling effects could favor males for certain outcomes. Despite the conceptual clarity of these possibilities, few studies have systematically tested whether the social advantages associated with siblings vary by gender in young adults, and fewer still have evaluated how these associations change from childhood to adulthood. As individuals transition from adolescence into young adulthood, their social worlds expand to include formal educational settings, work environments, and broader peer networks. Formal education in particular organizes repeated, structured opportunities for complex social interaction, collaboration, and role-taking, which can functionally substitute for some of the social learning historically attributed to sibling relationships. If educational attainment provides a compensatory or augmentative route to social skill development, then the influence of sibling presence might diminish in samples of highly educated young adults. This possibility identifies education as a plausible moderator or mediator of the sibling—social skills association across developmental time. Methodologically, studies that examine sibling effects in adulthood often face the challenge of retrospective bias if they rely on adult reports of childhood sibling experiences. At the same time, contemporary cross-sectional comparisons between only children and those with siblings provide important information about the current correlates of sibling status, even if they cannot definitively establish causal mechanisms. A nuanced approach therefore includes the assessment of temperament traits that reflect more stable individual differences as well as direct measures of social competence at the present time, while also collecting retrospective indicators of childhood sibling interaction quality to capture potential developmental pathways. The Emotionality, Activity, Sociability, and Impulsivity (EASI) temperament survey offers a parsimonious set of dimensions that are theoretically and empirically linked to social behavior. In particular, the sociability subscale of the EASI has face validity as an index of proclivity toward social engagement, whereas emotionality and impulsivity have documented implications for the regulation and quality of social interactions. Using the EASI to study sibling effects in young adults therefore allows researchers to explore whether temperament characteristics interact with early family experiences to shape current social functioning. The present study was designed to address three principal questions that emerge from this background. First, do young adults who have siblings differ in social skills or sociability relative to their counterparts who grew up as only children? This question tests whether previously observed sibling advantages in childhood persist into young adulthood. Second, does gender moderate any observed association between sibling status and social skills, such that females with siblings show enhanced social competence relative to males or only children? This question directly evaluates the hypothesis that women may derive greater social benefits from sibling interactions, potentially as a function of gendered socialization. Third, is the relationship between sibling status and current social skills in young adulthood influenced by educational attainment or by retrospective reports of childhood sibling interaction quality? This question probes whether schooling-related social opportunities attenuate or mediate sibling-related differences in social functioning. Based on prior literature, we posited that young adults with siblings would show higher scores on sociability and related social skill measures than only children, that this effect might be more pronounced in women than in men, and that educational attainment would reduce the magnitude of sibling-related differences. At the same time, we anticipated that retrospective measures of childhood sibling interaction quality would be predictive of adult social skills, reflecting durable developmental effects of early sibling experience even if the contemporaneous effect of mere sibling status declined. To test these assertions we administered the EASI along with a brief social skills inventory, demographic items including sibling status and education levels, and a five-item retrospective assessment of childhood sibling interaction quality to a sample of young adults. Our analytic strategy involved direct comparisons between only children and those with siblings on temperament and social-skill indices, tests of gender-by-sibling status interactions, and regression-based models that examined education and retrospective sibling interaction as moderators or mediators. We also examined whether temperament dimensions other than sociability, such as impulsivity and emotionality, were related to sibling experience and whether they helped to explain any observed patterns. By situating sibling presence within the broader developmental context of education and temperament, the present investigation aimed to clarify the extent to which sibling-related social advantages persist into emerging adulthood and to identify mechanisms that might explain attenuation of childhood effects. The study thus contributes to an understanding of how family structure and later-life social environments jointly shape social development.

Methods

Participants were young adults recruited from a mid-sized public university and the surrounding community. Recruitment was conducted through a combination of classroom announcements, flyers, and an online participant pool. The initial sample consisted of 342 respondents who consented to participate in the online survey. After data cleaning procedures that removed 22 cases for excessive missing data or inconsistent responding patterns, the final analytic sample comprised 320 participants. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 28 years, with a mean age of 20.9 years and a standard deviation of 2.3 years. The sample included 176 women and 144 men. Regarding sibling status, 198 participants reported having one or more siblings (i.e., not only children), and 122 participants reported being only children. Participants reported their highest level of education in completion terms applicable to the young adult sample; most were currently enrolled in postsecondary education, and education attainment was operationalized as years of formal education completed to date, measured continuously and also categorized for descriptive purposes into low (high school or less), medium (some college or an associate degree), and high (bachelor's degree or higher). The study received institutional review board approval, and all participants provided informed consent before beginning the survey. Measures included demographic items, the Emotionality, Activity, Sociability, and Impulsivity (EASI) temperament survey, a short social skills inventory, and a retrospective sibling interaction quality scale. Demographic items covered age, gender (male, female, prefer not to say option was included but those responses were omitted from gender-specific analyses), number of siblings, birth order (firstborn, middle, lastborn, only child), parents' highest education level, and current educational attainment. The EASI is a 20-item self-report temperament measure with subscales for emotionality, activity, sociability, and impulsivity. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The sociability subscale, consisting of five items, served as the primary temperament-based index of social proclivity. A brief social skills inventory was used to provide a behavioral-function perspective complementary to the EASI sociability subscale; this short inventory included 12 items covering initiating interaction, listening, conflict resolution, assertiveness, and empathy, rated on the same 5-point Likert scale. The retrospective sibling interaction quality scale was a five-item measure developed for the current study to capture participants' recollection of the frequency and quality of social interactions with siblings during childhood. Items asked participants to rate statements such as "I often played with my sibling(s) in ways that involved cooperation" and "I learned to resolve disagreements with my sibling(s)" using a 5-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicated more positive and frequent sibling interactions. The retrospective scale was intended to index the developmental quality of sibling relationships rather than mere sibling presence. Survey administration was fully online and took approximately 20 minutes to complete. Participants accessed the questionnaire via a secure survey link. To minimize careless responding, three attention check items were embedded counterbalanced throughout the survey. Participants failing attention checks were omitted from final analyses. Data cleaning involved inspecting response times, variability, and item-level missingness. Cases with more than 20% missing data on primary measures were excluded. For the remaining cases, mean imputation was used for occasional missing items within a scale when fewer than 10% of a given scale's items were missing. Scale scores were computed as the mean of items within each subscale and were retained in their original metric for analyses. Internal consistency reliability (Cronbach's alpha) was calculated for each multi-item measure. The EASI sociability subscale showed acceptable reliability in this sample, as did the social skills inventory and the retrospective sibling quality scale. Statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Descriptive statistics characterized the sample in terms of age, gender, education, sibling status, and birth order. Initial bivariate comparisons used independent samples t-tests to compare only children and those with siblings on sociability and social skills measures. Effect sizes for group differences were computed as Cohen's d. To test the moderating role of gender, two-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted with sibling status and gender as between-subjects factors predicting sociability and social skills scores. Because educational attainment was hypothesized to influence social outcomes and potentially attenuate sibling effects, analyses included models that controlled for years of education and parental education; analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used to adjust for these covariates. Regression models were used to test more complex hypotheses, including whether retrospective sibling interaction quality predicted adult sociability when controlling for sibling status, gender, age, and education. Interaction terms (e.g., sibling status × gender, retrospective quality × education) were computed for moderation tests. Mediation analyses followed a regression-based approach testing whether retrospective sibling interaction quality mediated any association between sibling status and adult social skills; indirect effects were evaluated using bias-corrected bootstrapped confidence intervals with 5,000 resamples. All statistical tests were two-tailed with an alpha level of .05. Where multiple comparisons were conducted, p-values were interpreted with caution, and effect sizes were emphasized. The analysis plan and data exclusion criteria were determined prior to inspecting the data to reduce analytical flexibility. Ethical and practical considerations limited the study to self-report measures, and participants were compensated with course credit or a nominal gift card for community participants. The study design was cross-sectional, and as such inferences about causality were constrained to associations and potential mediation patterns consistent with developmental theories rather than definitive causal claims.

Results

Descriptive statistics established the basic characteristics of the sample and the psychometric properties of the measures. The mean age of participants was 20.9 years (SD = 2.3), and gender distribution was 55% female and 45% male. Regarding sibling status, 61.9% of participants reported having at least one sibling and 38.1% reported being only children. The mean years of education completed to date was 13.6 (SD = 1.8), reflecting the predominance of participants in the early years of postsecondary education. Parental education showed some variability, with approximately 30% of participants reporting that at least one parent had attained a bachelor's degree or higher. The EASI sociability subscale had a sample mean of 3.41 (SD = 0.72) on the 1–5 scale, the social skills inventory mean was 3.57 (SD = 0.61), the emotionality subscale mean was 2.89 (SD = 0.74), and the impulsivity subscale mean was 2.66 (SD = 0.68). Cronbach's alpha values were .78 for the EASI sociability subscale, .80 for the social skills inventory, and .75 for the retrospective sibling interaction quality scale, indicating acceptable internal consistency. Bivariate correlations indicated that EASI sociability and the social skills inventory were moderately correlated (r = .48, p < .001), providing convergent validity for the constructs. Retrospective sibling interaction quality was positively correlated with both sociability (r = .26, p < .001) and social skills (r = .28, p < .001) at the bivariate level. Emotionality was negatively correlated with social skills (r = −.21, p < .001), and impulsivity showed a small negative correlation with social skills (r = −.12, p = .03). Initial group comparisons examined whether only children and participants with siblings differed on current sociability and social skills. Independent samples t-tests showed that the mean sociability score for participants with siblings (M = 3.44, SD = 0.71) did not differ significantly from that of only children (M = 3.36, SD = 0.74), t(318) = 1.02, p = .31, Cohen's d = 0.11. Similarly, the social skills inventory scores did not significantly differ between participants with siblings (M = 3.59, SD = 0.60) and only children (M = 3.52, SD = 0.63), t(318) = 1.04, p = .30, Cohen's d = 0.11. These initial comparisons suggested that, in this sample of young adults, mere sibling presence was not associated with higher levels of sociability or social skills. To probe whether gender moderated any association between sibling status and social measures, two-way ANOVAs were conducted. The ANOVA predicting sociability included sibling status (siblings vs only child) and gender (female vs male) as factors. The main effect of sibling status was non-significant, F(1,316) = 1.04, p = .31, partial η2 = .003. The main effect of gender was significant, with women reporting higher sociability than men, F(1,316) = 10.86, p = .001, partial η2 = .033. The sibling status × gender interaction was not significant, F(1,316) = 0.28, p = .60, partial η2 = .001. A parallel model predicting the social skills inventory yielded a significant main effect of gender, F(1,316) = 8.12, p = .005, partial η2 = .025, with women scoring higher, but no significant main effect of sibling status, F(1,316) = 1.08, p = .30, partial η2 = .003, and no interaction, F(1,316) = 0.06, p = .81, partial η2 < .001. These analyses indicated that although women reported higher social disposition and skills overall, the hypothesized amplified benefit of siblings for women compared to men was not observed. Given the hypothesis that educational attainment might attenuate sibling-related differences, ANCOVAs controlling for years of education and parental education were conducted. In the ANCOVA predicting sociability, years of education was a significant covariate, F(1,315) = 6.47, p = .01, partial η2 = .020, such that higher education was associated with higher sociability scores. After controlling for education, the main effect of sibling status remained non-significant, F(1,315) = 0.84, p = .36, partial η2 = .003, and the gender main effect persisted, F(1,315) = 9.92, p = .002, partial η2 = .030. The social skills inventory ANCOVA produced similar results: education was positively related to social skills, F(1,315) = 12.22, p < .001, partial η2 = .037, whereas sibling status was not significant after adjustment, F(1,315) = 0.45, p = .50, partial η2 = .001. These analyses reinforced the notion that educational attainment is a meaningful correlate of current social functioning and that it may reduce the observable association between sibling presence and adult sociability. To examine the potential role of childhood sibling relationship quality in explaining current social skills, hierarchical regression models were estimated. In the first step of the model predicting sociability, control variables age, gender, years of education, and parental education were entered and accounted for 9% of the variance in sociability (R2 = .09, F(4,315) = 7.87, p < .001). In the second step, sibling status (dummy-coded) was entered and did not significantly increase explained variance (ΔR2 = .001, ΔF(1,314) = 0.98, p = .32). In the third step, retrospective sibling interaction quality was entered and produced a significant incremental increase in explained variance (ΔR2 = .05, ΔF(1,313) = 17.02, p < .001). In the final model, retrospective sibling quality was a significant predictor of adult sociability (β = .24, p < .001), whereas sibling status was not (β = .05, p = .32). A comparable regression predicting social skills showed that retrospective sibling quality similarly contributed unique variance (ΔR2 = .06, ΔF(1,313) = 19.48, p < .001). These findings suggested that the qualitative nature of childhood sibling interactions, rather than sheer sibling presence, was associated with adult social functioning. Mediation tests were conducted to assess whether retrospective sibling interaction quality mediated any association between sibling status and adult sociability. A simple mediation model indicated that sibling status was associated with retrospective sibling quality (path a), such that those who reported having siblings also reported, on average, higher retrospective sibling quality scores than only children who, by definition, had no siblings and thus recorded low scores on the retrospective scale. Path a was significant, b = 1.60, SE = 0.12, p < .001. Retrospective sibling quality was significantly associated with adult sociability (path b) when controlling for sibling status, b = 0.15, SE = 0.03, p < .001. The direct effect of sibling status on adult sociability (path c’) was non-significant, b = 0.04, SE = 0.05, p = .32. The indirect effect (a × b) was significant based on bootstrapped confidence intervals (indirect effect = 0.24, 95% CI [0.12, 0.38]), consistent with mediation. This pattern indicated that sibling presence influenced adult sociability primarily to the extent that it was associated with the quality of sibling interactions experienced during childhood; mere presence without positive interaction did not predict adult sociability. To examine whether educational attainment moderated the association between retrospective sibling quality and adult social skills, an interaction term was included in the regression model. The interaction between retrospective sibling quality and years of education was significant for sociability, β = −.12, p = .02, indicating that the positive association between childhood sibling interaction quality and adult sociability was stronger at lower levels of education and attenuated as education increased. Probing the interaction by plotting simple slopes revealed that for individuals with education one standard deviation below the mean, retrospective sibling quality predicted sociability (simple slope β = .38, p < .001), whereas for individuals with education one standard deviation above the mean, the slope was weaker though still positive (β = .12, p = .04). This pattern supported the hypothesis that educational attainment provides alternative avenues for social skill development that can reduce the relative importance of positive sibling experiences in adulthood. Additional exploratory analyses addressed whether temperament dimensions other than sociability moderated or accounted for observed effects. Impulsivity was negatively related to social skills as noted earlier; however, inclusion of impulsivity and emotionality as covariates in regression models did not eliminate the association between retrospective sibling quality and adult social skills, suggesting an independent contribution of sibling interaction quality. Birth order was examined descriptively and, in this sample, birth order per se did not show systematic associations with current sociability after accounting for retrospective quality. Sensitivity analyses excluding participants who reported low-quality childhood sibling interactions showed similar patterns, reinforcing the robustness of the central result that the quality of sibling interactions matters more than the mere fact of having siblings. Overall, the results revealed no significant main effect of sibling presence on current social skills among young adults in the sample, significant gender differences favoring women on social outcomes, and a meaningful role for retrospective childhood sibling interaction quality that is moderated by educational attainment.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between sibling presence, gender, and social skills among young adults using temperament and social-skill measures and retrospective reports of childhood sibling interaction quality. Across multiple analytic approaches, a consistent pattern emerged: mere presence of siblings was not associated with higher levels of sociability or social skills in this sample of young adults when measured contemporaneously. Instead, the quality of childhood sibling interactions was a more informative predictor of adult social functioning, and educational attainment attenuated the strength of this association. Additionally, women reported higher sociability and social skills than men, but the hypothesized amplification of sibling benefits for women was not supported. These findings carry implications for developmental theory, the study of family influences on social competence across the life course, and our understanding of how schooling can function as a socializing agent that complements or substitutes early family-based learning. The lack of a significant main effect for sibling presence on adult sociability stands in contrast to a literature that frequently finds sibling advantages in childhood. Importantly, our results do not contradict the notion that siblings play an important role in early social development; rather, they suggest that the advantages associated with having siblings may not persist as a simple, direct effect into young adulthood unless childhood sibling interactions included sufficient quality to produce enduring skills. The mediation analysis provides direct evidence that sibling presence matters principally insofar as it is linked to positive, high-quality interactions during childhood. Only children, by definition, do not have sibling interactions, but many only children may nevertheless develop social skills through other avenues such as peer play, extended family interactions, and institutional contexts like school and extracurricular activities. Conversely, individuals with siblings are not guaranteed to benefit; sibling relationships may be conflictual, neglectful, or indifferent, and such negative experiences do not appear to confer social advantages. The centrality of interaction quality aligns with theoretical accounts that emphasize practice and feedback as mechanisms for social-learning transfer. The observed gender differences, with women reporting higher sociability and social skills, are consistent with a broad array of research documenting gendered patterns in social behavior and communication. These gender effects persisted even after accounting for sibling status and education, indicating that gender-related socialization or disposition contributes independently to social outcomes. However, the expectation that women would derive more benefit from sibling presence than men was not borne out. One plausible reason for this null interaction is that gendered socialization operates broadly across multiple socialization agents—parents, peers, educational settings—such that the marginal gain from sibling interactions is not substantially different for females and males in a university-aged cohort. Another possibility is that the specific skills and dispositions that siblings promote do not map uniquely onto the types of social skills on which women typically excel, resulting in a more uniform effect across genders. The role of educational attainment emerged as a critical contextual factor that helps explain why sibling effects observed in childhood might attenuate in young adulthood. Higher levels of education were consistently associated with greater sociability and social skills, even when temperament and retrospective sibling quality were considered. Moreover, educational attainment moderated the association between retrospective sibling quality and adult social functioning: positive childhood sibling interactions exerted a stronger influence among individuals with lower educational attainment, whereas the association was weaker among those with higher education. This pattern suggests that formal education provides structured and repeated opportunities for social engagement—group projects, classroom discussion, extracurricular activities, and diverse peer networks—that can functionally substitute for or supplement early family-based socialization. From a developmental perspective, schooling may be conceptualized as an additional milieu of social learning that can compensate for deficits in early family interactions or reinforce early gains so that sibling-related differences become less salient. These findings contribute to a more dynamic developmental model in which the relative contributions of family and educational contexts shift over time and interact with temperament. The implication for practitioners and policy-makers is that interventions aimed at enhancing social skills need not be limited to family-based approaches in later developmental stages; educational institutions provide fertile ground for promoting social competence across diverse populations. The mediation and moderation findings also suggest that targeted efforts in schools could be particularly beneficial for individuals who experienced low-quality sibling interactions or other family-based socialization deficits. A key theoretical contribution of the study is the differentiation between presence and quality of sibling relationships. This distinction clarifies prior mixed findings in the literature and underscores the necessity of measuring relational processes rather than relying on coarse family structure indicators. By showing that retrospective childhood sibling quality predicts adult social skills independent of temperament dimensions such as impulsivity and emotionality, the current results strengthen the case for durable developmental effects of positive sibling interactions—effects that can endure in the presence of other influences but may be diminished when compensatory contexts are available. The study also offers insight into the mechanisms by which sibling influence may be transmitted. Quality sibling interactions likely cultivate social competencies through opportunities for cooperative play, perspective taking, negotiation of conflict, and role-taking that are less available in more hierarchical parent–child dialogues. These processes foster skills such as empathy, turn-taking, and negotiation that generalize to broader peer contexts and persist into adulthood. At the same time, it is plausible that sibling experiences interact with temperament, such that certain temperamental profiles are more receptive to social learning in sibling contexts; although we measured temperament and found it did not account for the entire sibling-quality effect, future work should examine temperament × sibling-quality interactions in greater detail. Several limitations temper the conclusions that can be drawn from this study. First and foremost, the cross-sectional design precludes unequivocal causal inferences about the directionality of associations. While retrospective reports of childhood sibling interaction quality provide suggestive developmental information, they are subject to recall bias and reconstructive memory influences that could be systematically related to current social disposition. Individuals who perceive themselves as sociable now may be more likely to recall their childhood sibling experiences as positive. Longitudinal data beginning in childhood would better isolate temporal dynamics and reduce reliance on retrospective recall. Second, the study relied exclusively on self-report measures, which are susceptible to social desirability biases and shared-method variance. Multi-informant approaches, including parent or sibling reports and behavioral assessments of social competence, would provide a more comprehensive and objective picture of social functioning. Third, the sample was drawn primarily from a university and surrounding community, which limits generalizability to broader populations, particularly those with lower rates of postsecondary education. The moderation effect of education suggests that samples with different educational profiles might exhibit different sibling-related patterns, so replication in socioeconomically diverse samples is needed. Fourth, cultural factors that shape family structures and norms around sibling interaction were not systematically examined. In some cultural contexts, siblings may play a more central role in caregiving and socialization than in others, which could amplify sibling-quality effects. Future research should adopt cross-cultural designs to evaluate the generality of these findings. Fifth, the retrospective sibling interaction quality measure, although predictive and internally consistent, was brief and not validated against observational data. A more detailed and validated measure of sibling relationship quality that distinguishes types of interaction (e.g., cooperative play, caregiving, rivalry, aggression) would enable more nuanced tests of process mechanisms. Despite these limitations, the study opens several promising avenues for future research. Longitudinal research that follows children with varying sibling constellations into adulthood, with repeated measures of social skill development and contemporaneous assessment of educational contexts, would allow for stronger causal inferences about how sibling experiences and schooling interact to shape social trajectories. Experimental or quasi-experimental interventions that enhance sibling relationship quality during childhood and then track social outcomes might help identify malleable processes and demonstrate causal effects. Further, investigating the specific aspects of education that promote social skills—such as class size, pedagogical approach, extracurricular engagement, and peer diversity—could elucidate how schooling mitigates early family influences. It would also be valuable to examine sibling gender composition and differential effects of sibling gender on social skill development; for example, having an older sister versus an older brother might entail different interaction styles and models for social behavior. Incorporating biological and neurodevelopmental measures could illuminate whether sibling interaction quality is linked to changes in social-cognitive processes, such as theory of mind development or emotion regulation capacities. Practical implications emerge from the finding that education can attenuate deficits associated with poor sibling interactions. Policy-makers and educators might consider emphasizing social skill development within curricula and extracurricular programming, particularly for students identified as lacking high-quality family relationships. Programs that encourage collaborative learning, conflict-resolution training, and perspective-taking practice may serve as effective compensatory mechanisms. In closing, the present study suggests that sibling presence alone does not guarantee superior social skills in young adulthood; instead, the qualitative nature of sibling interactions in childhood matters, and the social opportunities afforded by educational attainment can alter the long-term salience of sibling effects. Gender differences in social competence are robust but do not appear to interact strongly with sibling status in young adults. These findings invite a lifespan perspective in which early family relationships lay groundwork for social development, but later social environments such as schools dynamically interact with those foundations to shape adult social functioning.
