Introduction

Prejudice is a widely studied phenomenon in psychology and social science. Prejudice can be defined as an unjustified or incorrect attitude toward an individual based solely on the individual’s membership of a social group. Prejudice manifests in many domains, including race, ethnicity, gender, sexual orientation, and social class. Researchers have examined the correlates and contributors to prejudice across these domains to understand its origins and persistence. Socioeconomic status (SES) has emerged as an important predictor of a variety of social attitudes. SES is a multidimensional construct that commonly includes income, education, and occupational status. SES has been linked to cognitive styles, exposure to diverse social networks, and access to information. These links may shape the development and expression of prejudicial attitudes.

Previous research has suggested that lower SES is often associated with higher levels of certain forms of prejudice. For example, studies examining racial prejudice have documented correlations between markers of socioeconomic disadvantage and more prejudiced attitudes toward racial outgroups. Several theoretical accounts have been proposed to explain such findings. One account emphasizes perceived threat and competition for resources, which may be more salient among individuals with fewer economic resources. A second account highlights the role of education and cognitive sophistication, which tend to be associated with higher SES and more tolerant attitudes. A third account frames prejudice as related to social identity and the need for group-based status maintenance, with lower-SES individuals possibly endorsing dominant group norms that maintain hierarchies.

Sexism, like racism, is a form of prejudice that involves negative attitudes, beliefs, and behaviors toward individuals on the basis of their gender. Sexism can be expressed overtly as hostile attitudes, or it can be expressed in more subtle or ambivalent ways, such as through benevolent but patronizing beliefs. The ambivalent model of sexism distinguishes between hostile sexism and benevolent sexism. Hostile sexism involves overtly negative evaluations and stereotypes about women. Benevolent sexism involves subjectively positive but patronizing beliefs that justify traditional gender roles. Understanding how SES relates to different forms of sexism could clarify whether the SES-prejudice relationship observed in racial domains generalizes to gender-based prejudice.

Empirical work on the relationship between SES and sexism has produced mixed findings. Some studies have reported that lower socioeconomic indicators are associated with greater endorsement of traditional gender roles and sexist attitudes. Other studies have found that gendered prejudices cut across SES lines and may be driven by ideological, cultural, or structural factors that do not map neatly onto SES. These divergent findings suggest that the relationship between SES and sexism may be complex and may depend on the specific measures of both SES and sexism. For instance, SES measured by education may show a different pattern than SES measured by subjective social class. Similarly, hostile sexism and benevolent sexism may exhibit distinct associations with SES.

Several mechanisms may link SES to sexist attitudes. Education provides exposure to diverse perspectives and critical thinking skills that can reduce stereotypical thinking. Occupational environments associated with higher SES may involve greater gender equality norms, mixed-gender collaboration, and formalized policies that discourage sexist behaviors. Economic security associated with higher income may reduce the perceived need to defend traditional norms that promise stability through clear gender roles. Conversely, lower-SES contexts may reinforce traditional gender roles due to economic constraints and gendered division of labor, thus promoting beliefs aligned with benevolent sexism.

At the same time, cultural factors and socialization processes transmit gender norms in ways that can transcend SES. Family upbringing, religious beliefs, and conservative ideology can foster sexist attitudes across economic strata. For example, individuals from higher SES backgrounds may adopt liberal views about race and class while still endorsing traditional gender norms in private domains. Therefore, anticipating simple one-to-one relationships between SES and sexism risks overlooking the complexity of the social and psychological forces at work.

Research that aims to test the relationship between SES and sexism must carefully operationalize both constructs. Objective measures such as household income, parental education, and occupational prestige can complement subjective measures like perceived social standing. Sexism measures should capture both hostile and benevolent dimensions to provide a nuanced picture. In addition, demographic factors such as participant gender, age, and cultural background must be considered as potential confounds or moderators.

Studies in other areas of prejudice suggest that gender differences in prejudice may exist but can be small or context-dependent. Men have sometimes been found to endorse higher levels of sexism, particularly hostile sexism, but gender itself may interact with other variables such as ideology and social norms. If SES is correlated with sexism, it is possible that this relationship will be observable above and beyond any gender differences. Alternatively, gender might moderate the SES-sexism association so that the association is stronger for one gender than another.

The present study sought to extend previous work on the SES-prejudice relationship by focusing on sexism. The research question was whether socioeconomic background is associated with levels of sexist attitudes in a sample of adult participants. Specifically, we tested whether higher SES is associated with lower levels of sexism, and whether this pattern holds for both hostile and benevolent forms of sexism. We also examined whether participant gender is associated with sexism and whether gender moderates the relationship between SES and sexism.

To test these questions, we conducted a cross-sectional correlational study using standardized self-report measures. We collected data on multiple indicators of SES, including parental education and household income, in order to capture both objective and background socioeconomic conditions. We included a measure of subjective social class to capture participants’ perceptions of their standing. For sexism, we used a validated ambivalent sexism measure that yields separate scores for hostile and benevolent sexism. We also included a composite overall sexism score for analyses that required a single dependent variable.

We expected to find a negative correlation between SES and overall sexism, meaning that higher SES would be associated with lower sexism. We further hypothesized that the negative association would be stronger for hostile sexism than for benevolent sexism, based on the idea that hostile sexism reflects overt antagonism that may be more sensitive to educational and cognitive factors. Regarding gender differences, we anticipated that men would report higher levels of hostile sexism on average, but we did not make a strong prediction about whether gender would substantially alter the SES-sexism relationship. We also acknowledged alternative possibilities and planned exploratory analyses to examine interactions and potential nonlinearities.

The study builds on a broader literature linking socioeconomic indicators to social attitudes. It aims to contribute specificity to that literature by focusing on gender-based prejudice and distinguishing between its different forms. The findings could have implications for theories that emphasize the role of economic resources and education in shaping prejudiced attitudes. They could also inform interventions designed to reduce sexism by indicating whether economic and educational pathways are relevant targets.

We designed the study to provide rigorous tests of the hypothesized relationships while acknowledging the limitations inherent to correlational research. We implemented data quality checks and psychometric analyses to ensure the reliability of the measures. We also preregistered the primary hypotheses and analytic plan prior to data collection to reduce analytic flexibility. The approach balanced confirmatory and exploratory analyses to allow for hypothesis testing and hypothesis generation.

In sum, the Introduction has outlined the rationale for examining SES and sexism together. We reviewed theoretical accounts and empirical evidence linking SES to other forms of prejudice. We highlighted relevant distinctions within sexism and within SES measurement. We articulated specific predictions and noted alternative possibilities. We emphasized the study’s contribution to ongoing debates about the social determinants of prejudice. With this foundation, we proceed to describe the methods used to address these questions in detail.

Methods

Participants were recruited using an online research panel. Participants were required to be adults aged 18 years or older and to reside in the country where the study was conducted. The sampling strategy aimed to achieve diversity in socioeconomic backgrounds. We set quotas to ensure a spread of educational attainment and household income levels. A total of 320 individuals completed the survey. Data screening removed 12 participants for failing attention checks or providing implausible responses, resulting in a final analytic sample of 308 participants. The final sample included 158 women, 146 men, and 4 participants who identified as nonbinary or used another term; for analyses that required a binary gender variable, we retained those participants but conducted sensitivity analyses excluding them.

Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 72 years with a mean age of 36.8 years and a standard deviation of 12.4 years. The sample included a range of educational backgrounds. Approximately 22% of participants had completed high school or less, 38% had completed some college or a college degree, and 40% had completed graduate-level education. Household income categories were used to capture objective economic status. Participants reported household incomes spanning low to high categories, with approximately 25% reporting incomes below the median national level, 50% near the median, and 25% above the median.

Several measures were used to operationalize socioeconomic status. First, parental education was assessed by asking participants to indicate the highest level of formal education achieved by the primary caregiver during their childhood. Responses were coded on an ordinal scale from 1 (no formal education) to 7 (postgraduate degree). Second, current household income was assessed using banded response options that were recoded to approximate midpoints for continuous analyses. Third, subjective social class was measured by a single-item ladder measure in which participants placed themselves on a 1-to-10 ladder relative to others in society. These three indicators were combined into a composite SES index using z-score standardization and averaging, after confirming sufficient intercorrelations among the indicators.

Sexism was measured using an established ambivalent sexism instrument. The instrument yields a hostile sexism subscale and a benevolent sexism subscale. Each subscale includes items rated on a seven-point Likert scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Scale scores were computed by summing item responses and transforming to a consistent metric for interpretability. Internal consistency reliability was assessed for each subscale using Cronbach’s alpha. An overall sexism composite was computed as the mean of the standardized hostile and benevolent scores for analyses that required a single dependent measure.

In addition to the main variables, we collected demographic and control variables. These included age, gender identity, race/ethnicity, occupational status, religiosity, and political ideology. Religiosity was assessed using a single-item frequency of religious practice measure and an item assessing the importance of religion in daily life. Political ideology was measured on a self-placement left-right continuum. Collecting these covariates allowed us to examine whether the SES-sexism relationship persisted when accounting for plausible confounds.

Participants completed the survey online in one session. The survey began with informed consent and brief eligibility screening. Attention checks were embedded throughout the questionnaire to flag inattentive responses. The order of scales was counterbalanced across participants to reduce order effects. The entire study took approximately 20 minutes to complete. Upon completion, participants received compensation consistent with the panel’s standard practice.

Ethical approval was obtained from an institutional review board prior to data collection. All participants provided informed consent and were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. Data were collected anonymously and stored on secure servers to protect participant confidentiality. No identifying information was collected beyond the demographic data necessary for analyses.

Data analysis involved both descriptive and inferential procedures. Initial analyses examined the distributional properties of the variables and tested assumptions for parametric tests. We inspected histograms, skewness, and kurtosis for the sexism scales and SES indicators. Where variables showed departures from normality, transformations were considered, but in most cases parametric analyses were robust to minor deviations given the sample size.

Primary analyses focused on correlations and regression analyses. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to test bivariate associations among SES indicators and sexism measures. We interpreted effect sizes according to conventional benchmarks while giving attention to practical significance. Multiple linear regression models were used to test whether SES predicted sexism controlling for demographics and other covariates. Hostile sexism and benevolent sexism served as dependent variables in separate models. Interaction terms between SES and gender were included in follow-up models to test moderation.

We conducted reliability analyses for the sexism measures. Cronbach’s alpha values were computed for hostile and benevolent sexism subscales to ensure internal consistency. We also computed inter-item correlations and item-total correlations to detect problematic items. For the composite SES index, we assessed internal consistency and inspected the contribution of each indicator.

Power analysis was performed prior to data collection to determine the sample size needed to detect small-to-moderate correlations. Assuming a desired power of .80 to detect an effect size of r = .18 at alpha = .05, the analysis indicated a required sample of approximately 300 participants. Our final sample exceeded this threshold, providing adequate power to detect the hypothesized relationships.

Sensitivity analyses were preplanned. These included repeating key regressions with alternative SES operationalizations, such as using parental education alone or subjective social class alone, and repeating analyses with and without participants identifying as nonbinary due to small subgroup size. We also planned to explore potential nonlinear effects by testing quadratic SES terms in regression models.

All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. We reported two-tailed significance tests and included 95% confidence intervals for key estimates. Effect sizes were reported alongside p-values to provide information about the magnitude of relationships. Missing data were minimal and handled using pairwise deletion for most analyses, with multiple imputation used for robustness checks.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the main variables are reported first. The hostile sexism subscale had a mean of 2.9 on the seven-point scale and a standard deviation of 1.1. The benevolent sexism subscale had a mean of 3.4 and a standard deviation of 1.0. The overall sexism composite had a mean standardized score near zero by construction and an observed standard deviation of 0.96. The composite SES index had a mean of zero and a standard deviation of 0.98 due to z-score standardization of its components. Subjective social class had a mean ladder position of 5.2 with a standard deviation of 1.8. Parental education showed a modal value corresponding to a college degree, while household income was distributed across the intended bands.

Reliability analyses indicated good internal consistency for the sexism measures. Cronbach’s alpha for hostile sexism was .89, indicating high reliability. Cronbach’s alpha for benevolent sexism was .86, also indicating satisfactory internal consistency. Item-total correlations for both subscales were in the acceptable range. The composite SES index showed moderate internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of .63 across the three indicators, reflecting that the SES indicators capture related but not identical constructs.

Initial bivariate correlations addressed the primary hypotheses. The composite SES index correlated negatively with overall sexism, with a Pearson correlation of r = -0.25. This correlation was statistically significant at p < .001. In terms of subscales, SES correlated more strongly with hostile sexism (r = -0.28, p < .001) than with benevolent sexism (r = -0.16, p = .004). These correlations suggest that higher SES is associated with lower levels of sexist attitudes overall, and that the association is somewhat stronger for hostile sexism.

We examined the correlations between individual SES indicators and sexism to assess whether specific indicators drove the observed relationships. Parental education correlated with hostile sexism at r = -0.23 (p < .001) and with benevolent sexism at r = -0.14 (p = .01). Current household income correlated with hostile sexism at r = -0.21 (p < .001) and with benevolent sexism at r = -0.12 (p = .03). Subjective social class correlated with hostile sexism at r = -0.20 (p < .001) and with benevolent sexism at r = -0.11 (p = .04). These results indicate a consistent negative association across SES indicators, with modest effect sizes.

Gender differences were analyzed next. Men reported higher hostile sexism scores on average compared to women. The mean hostile sexism score for men was 3.3 and for women was 2.6. The mean difference corresponded to Cohen’s d = 0.61, indicating a medium effect size. For benevolent sexism, the mean for men was 3.6 and for women was 3.3, with a smaller effect size of d = 0.29. However, when examining the correlation between gender and the SES index, gender was not strongly associated with SES in this sample (r = .06, p = .25), suggesting that gender differences in sexism are not explained by systematic differences in socioeconomic status within this sample.

To examine whether SES predicted sexism above and beyond demographic covariates, we conducted multiple regression analyses. In the first model, overall sexism composite was regressed on the SES index, age, gender, religiosity, and political ideology. The full model explained 18% of variance in overall sexism (R^2 = .18). SES was a significant negative predictor of sexism (β = -0.20, t = -4.05, p < .001) in this model. Gender remained a significant predictor (β = 0.32 for male coded as 1, t = 6.42, p < .001), indicating that men reported higher sexism controlling for SES and other covariates.

In separate regression analyses for hostile and benevolent sexism subscales, SES was a significant predictor of hostile sexism after controlling for covariates (β = -0.23, t = -4.85, p < .001). For benevolent sexism, SES predicted scores more weakly but remained significant in the full model (β = -0.11, t = -2.28, p = .023). These patterns are consistent with the bivariate correlations showing a stronger association between SES and hostile sexism than between SES and benevolent sexism.

We tested for an interaction between SES and gender to assess whether the SES-sexism relationship differed by gender. Interaction terms were added to the hostile and benevolent sexism regression models. The SES × Gender interaction was not significant for hostile sexism (β = 0.04, t = 0.78, p = .44). The interaction was also not significant for benevolent sexism (β = 0.03, t = 0.65, p = .52). These null interaction results indicate that the negative association between SES and sexism did not differ substantially between men and women in our sample.

Additional sensitivity analyses examined alternative SES operationalizations. When parental education alone was entered as the predictor, the pattern of results was similar: parental education predicted hostile sexism (β = -0.19, p < .001) and benevolent sexism (β = -0.10, p = .03) in adjusted models. When subjective social class alone was used, it predicted hostile sexism (β = -0.17, p = .002) but was a weaker predictor for benevolent sexism (β = -0.09, p = .06). These results suggest that the observed SES-sexism association is robust across different SES measures.

We also conducted exploratory analyses considering political ideology and religiosity as potential mediators or moderators. Political conservatism was associated with higher levels of both hostile and benevolent sexism. When political ideology was included in the regression models, SES remained a significant predictor of hostile sexism, although its coefficient was slightly attenuated. Mediation tests suggested that part of the relationship between SES and hostile sexism is statistically accounted for by political ideology, but a direct effect of SES persisted. Religiosity showed similar patterns, in that higher religiosity was associated with greater sexism and partially accounted for some of the SES-sexism relation.

Nonlinear effects of SES on sexism were tested by including quadratic SES terms in regression models. The quadratic terms were not significant for either hostile or benevolent sexism, indicating that the relationship between SES and sexism appeared approximately linear across the observed SES range in this sample.

We assessed potential biases due to missing data and analytic decisions. Multiple imputation of missing SES and sexism items produced results consistent with the main analyses. Excluding participants who identified as nonbinary did not materially change the pattern of results, although the small size of that subgroup limited conclusions about gender diversity. Robustness checks using bootstrapped confidence intervals yielded similar significance patterns for key coefficients.

In sum, the results showed a consistent negative association between socioeconomic indicators and sexist attitudes. The association was stronger for hostile sexism than for benevolent sexism. Gender differences in sexism were present, with men endorsing higher levels of sexism, particularly hostile sexism, but gender did not moderate the SES-sexism relationship. These findings held across multiple analytical approaches and sensitivity checks.

Discussion

The present study investigated the relationship between socioeconomic background and sexist attitudes in an adult sample. We found that higher socioeconomic status was associated with lower levels of sexism, and that this association was more pronounced for hostile forms of sexism than for benevolent forms. Gender differences were observed in mean levels of sexism, with men reporting higher levels of hostile sexism on average. However, gender did not significantly moderate the association between SES and sexism. These findings contribute to the literature on socioeconomic correlates of prejudice and extend prior work to the domain of gender-based attitudes.

The negative association between SES and sexism aligns with theoretical perspectives that emphasize the role of education and economic resources in shaping tolerant attitudes. Education can facilitate exposure to diverse viewpoints and foster critical thinking that reduces reliance on stereotyped beliefs. Higher-SES occupations may involve norms and institutional structures that discourage overt hostility toward outgroups, including women. Economic security tied to higher income levels may reduce perceived threats that can fuel antagonistic attitudes. Our findings are therefore consistent with accounts that situate prejudice reduction within broader socioeconomic processes.

The stronger relationship between SES and hostile sexism compared to benevolent sexism is theoretically informative. Hostile sexism involves explicit antagonism and derogation, which may be more directly targeted by the processes associated with higher SES such as education, contact exposure, and institutional norms. Benevolent sexism, by contrast, involves ostensibly positive but paternalistic beliefs that can be reinforced across socioeconomic levels because they often align with culturally normative ideals about protection and care. The relative resilience of benevolent sexism to SES effects suggests that some gendered attitudes are transmitted through cultural narratives that are less sensitive to economic standing.

The observed gender differences in sexist attitudes, particularly the higher levels of hostile sexism among men, replicate a common pattern in the literature. Men’s higher endorsement of hostile sexism may reflect the maintenance of traditional gender hierarchies and gender-based power dynamics. Nonetheless, the lack of an SES × Gender interaction suggests that the effect of socioeconomic background on sexism operates similarly across men and women in our sample. This finding implies that economic and educational pathways that reduce sexism are likely to be effective for individuals of different genders.

Political ideology and religiosity emerged as important covariates in our analyses. Both factors were associated with higher sexism, and each partially accounted for the SES-sexism relationship. This pattern suggests that SES influences attitudes both directly and indirectly through ideological and religious pathways. Higher SES individuals may be more likely to hold liberal ideologies and lower religiosity, which are associated with lower sexism. These mediating pathways warrant further investigation using longitudinal and causal designs to disentangle directionality.

Several methodological aspects of the present study strengthen confidence in the findings. We used multiple indicators of SES to capture both objective and subjective aspects of socioeconomic background. The sexism measures had strong internal consistency, and reliability analyses supported the use of distinct hostile and benevolent subscales. Preplanned power analyses ensured adequate sample size to detect small-to-moderate associations. Sensitivity analyses affirmed the robustness of the main patterns across analytic choices.

Despite these strengths, key limitations temper the conclusions that can be drawn. The cross-sectional correlational design precludes causal inference. While the results are consistent with the hypothesis that higher SES contributes to lower sexism, it is also plausible that individuals with lower levels of sexism obtain higher socioeconomic attainment, or that third variables influence both SES and sexism. Longitudinal studies tracking individuals across developmental periods would be useful for testing causal pathways.

The reliance on self-report measures introduces the possibility of social desirability bias. Participants may underreport sexist attitudes due to social norms against overt sexism, particularly among higher-SES individuals who may be more attuned to such norms. Implicit measures or behavioral indicators could complement self-report assessments in future research to capture less controlled aspects of sexist attitudes.

The sample was recruited online and, while diverse in SES and education, may not fully represent the broader population. The relatively small proportion of participants identifying as nonbinary limits generalizability to gender-nonconforming populations. Future studies should aim for larger and more representative samples, especially to examine intersectional effects involving race, gender identity, and SES.

The composite SES index showed only moderate internal consistency, reflecting that different SES indicators capture distinct aspects of socioeconomic background. While the composite approach has advantages for capturing multifaceted SES, researchers should remain attentive to which SES component is most theoretically relevant for a given question. Our analyses showed that parental education, income, and subjective social class each related to sexism, but their individual strengths varied.

The finding that benevolent sexism was less strongly related to SES raises questions about the cultural embedding of gendered beliefs. Interventions targeting sexism may need to differentiate strategies for hostile and benevolent sexism. For hostile sexism, increasing education and economic opportunities may be effective. For benevolent sexism, approaches that challenge paternalistic narratives and reshape gender scripts through media campaigns and targeted messaging might be necessary.

Future research could explore mechanisms linking SES to sexism more directly. For instance, studies could examine whether educational content, exposure to diverse networks, economic security, or occupational norms mediate the relationship between SES and sexist attitudes. Experimental designs that manipulate exposure to diversity or present information challenging gender stereotypes could test whether such interventions differentially affect individuals across socioeconomic backgrounds.

Longitudinal designs would provide valuable insight into how changes in SES over the life course relate to changes in sexist attitudes. For example, research could investigate whether upward mobility is associated with reductions in sexism, and whether downward mobility leads to increases. Such studies could inform theories about how socioeconomic trajectories interact with attitude formation and change.

Intersectionality is an important consideration for future research. SES does not operate in isolation from other social categories such as race, ethnicity, and gender identity. The interplay of these statuses may produce unique patterns of prejudice that a unidimensional analysis cannot capture. Studies with larger and more diverse samples could examine whether the SES-sexism association differs across racial or ethnic groups, or whether minority group membership moderates the effects.

Policy implications of the findings deserve careful attention. If higher education and economic resources are associated with lower sexist attitudes, then policies that promote educational access and reduce economic inequality could have downstream benefits for gender equality. However, policy interventions must also target cultural narratives and structural constraints that perpetuate benevolent sexism and gendered division of labor.

The absence of a significant SES × Gender interaction suggests that efforts to reduce sexism through socioeconomic improvements could be broadly beneficial across genders. Educational curricula that emphasize gender equality and critical thinking, workplace policies that promote gender inclusion, and public campaigns that challenge gender stereotypes may have widespread effects regardless of gender.

Another avenue for research involves examining variations across cultural contexts. The relationship between SES and sexism may differ in societies with more traditional gender norms compared to those with progressive gender policies. Cross-cultural comparative research could elucidate the boundary conditions of the observed associations and inform culturally tailored interventions.

Quantitative research could be complemented by qualitative work exploring the lived experiences and narratives that link socioeconomic background to gender attitudes. Interviews and focus groups might reveal the subtle ways in which family practices, community norms, and occupational demands shape beliefs about gender roles. Such qualitative insights could inform the design of more effective measurement instruments and interventions.

Methodologically, future studies might integrate experimental and field methods to test causality and generalizability. For example, interventions that provide economic resources or educational opportunities could be evaluated for their impact on gender attitudes over time. Natural experiments arising from policy changes, such as expanded educational access, could be leveraged to test causal hypotheses at a population level.

In conclusion, the present study provides evidence that socioeconomic background is negatively associated with sexist attitudes, particularly hostile sexism. These findings support the idea that economic and educational pathways can influence prejudice and extend understanding of the SES-prejudice relationship into the domain of gender-based attitudes. The results highlight the importance of considering both socioeconomic and cultural mechanisms in efforts to reduce sexism. Continued research using longitudinal, experimental, and intersectional approaches will be essential to unpack the causal processes and to develop targeted interventions that promote gender equality across socioeconomic strata.
