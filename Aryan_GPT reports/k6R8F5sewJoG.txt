Introduction

Risk-taking behavior is a central topic across psychology, public health, and behavioral economics because of its implications for individual well-being and societal outcomes. Everyday choices about driving, financial decisions, health behaviors, and social interactions often involve trade-offs between potential gains and potential losses, and individuals differ markedly in how they approach such trade-offs. Understanding the determinants of individual differences in risk taking is therefore of both theoretical and practical import. One influential perspective holds that stable dispositional traits, broadly conceived as personality, play a key role in shaping risk-related preferences and behaviors. Under this view, personality dimensions capture enduring patterns of cognition, affect, and behavior that bias individuals toward greater or lesser engagement with risky acts. This trait-based approach complements state-based and situational models of risk taking and helps explain why individuals often show consistent patterns of risk engagement across time and contexts.

Among trait models, Eysenck’s dimensional framework is especially relevant to risk-related outcomes because it explicitly links biologically anchored temperamental dimensions to behavioral tendencies. Eysenck proposed three major dimensions of personality: Psychoticism, Extraversion, and Neuroticism, with an additional Lie scale included to detect response distortion. Psychoticism is conceptualized to encompass impulsivity, aggressiveness, and a tendency toward antisocial or nonconforming behavior, traits that plausibly predispose individuals to higher likelihoods of engaging in behavior that others would consider risky. Extraversion, by contrast, reflects sociability, positive affect, and reward sensitivity, and has logically been linked to behaviors that involve social engagement and reward-seeking. Neuroticism reflects proneness to negative affect and anxiety and might theoretically be expected to suppress risk taking via heightened sensitivity to threat and potential losses. This theoretical logic motivates the examination of these three dimensions as predictors of risk taking, but empirical findings to date have been mixed in ways that merit careful investigation.

Past empirical work has produced a robust association between measures tapping impulsivity and sensation seeking and increased risk taking across multiple life domains. Many measures of impulsivity and sensation seeking map closely onto Eysenck’s psychoticism construct, and studies have reported positive relations between psychoticism-like features and risky behavior. At the same time, extraversion has often been associated with certain kinds of risk taking—particularly social and recreational forms—but not uniformly across domains, and the direction and magnitude of the association have varied across samples and measures. Neuroticism often shows weaker or more complex relations with risk, sometimes correlating negatively with risk taking in domains involving physical danger but showing null or even positive relations in domains where anxiety leads to maladaptive choices. Thus, while theoretical considerations suggest clear hypotheses, empirical results suggest that personality–risk associations are contingent on the measures and contexts used.

Another important consideration in studies of personality and risk taking is the role of age. Lifespan developmental research indicates that risk taking is typically highest in adolescence and young adulthood and declines with age as cognitive control increases and social roles and responsibilities change. The maturation of prefrontal cognitive control systems and shifting reward sensitivities across development are frequently invoked to explain age-related declines in risk taking. Thus, any study that examines personality correlates of risk taking must also consider age as a potentially confounding or moderating variable. Age might act independently to explain variance in risk taking, and age might also interact with personality traits to produce differential effects at different points in the lifespan.

Measurement of risk taking is another substantive issue. Traditional approaches often rely on single-item or global measures that do not capture the domain specificity of risk behavior. The Domain-Specific Risk-Taking (DOSPERT) framework offers a solution by recognizing that risk taking manifests differently across domains such as financial, health/safety, recreational, ethical, and social contexts. This domain-specific perspective implies that personality traits may predict risk taking in some domains but not others; for instance, extraversion might be particularly relevant for social risk taking, whereas psychoticism-related impulsivity might better predict recreational or financial risk taking. Given this possibility, empirical studies that use a domain-general measure risk producing misleading null findings if they do not examine domain-specific patterns.

The use of self-report personality inventories also raises measurement and interpretive issues. For instance, psychoticism scales in early iterations of the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire were criticized for heterogeneity and modest internal consistency, prompting revised scales that attempt to sharpen the construct measurement. The Lie scale is included in many Eysenck instruments to detect socially desirable responding, and inclusion of such checks can improve confidence in self-report data. The DOSPERT instrument similarly rests on self-report assessments of how likely a person is to engage in particular behaviors, which can be influenced by self-awareness, cultural norms, and transient moods. Despite such limitations, both the Eysenck schema and DOSPERT remain widely used because they capture theoretically grounded constructs and permit cross-study comparisons.

Beyond simple trait–behavior associations, contemporary theorizing in cognitive neuroscience provides mechanisms that can integrate personality and risk taking. Dual-process models posit an interplay between relatively fast, affectively driven reward systems and slower, deliberative control systems. Individual differences in the sensitivity of reward circuitry or in the effectiveness of prefrontal control mechanisms could give rise to stable differences in risk willingness, and personality traits could act as psychological markers of these neurobiological differences. For example, high psychoticism might reflect heightened reward sensitivity and lower inhibitory control, leading to a higher propensity for risk taking. Extraversion could index reward responsivity to social stimuli, and neuroticism could index threat sensitivity that reduces or alters the form of risk engagement. These mechanistic accounts point to the potential value of integrating personality measures with cognitive and neuroscientific assessments.

Existing literature also highlights the importance of other individual differences that may shape risk taking, such as perceived control, socioeconomic status, and current affective state. Perceived control can influence whether individuals feel capable of managing potential negative outcomes, thereby modulating risk willingness. Socioeconomic constraints may force or discourage risk taking, depending on whether risk is perceived as an opportunity or a threat to scarce resources. Acute stress and emotional states can temporarily amplify reward sensitivity or narrow attention to immediate outcomes, thereby changing momentary risk-taking tendencies. These contextual factors suggest that personality is only one of multiple contributors to risk-related behavior and that a comprehensive account must consider interactions among traits, states, and situational forces.

Methodologically, many prior studies have been limited by small sample sizes, restricted age ranges (often undergraduate samples), and reliance on narrow measures of risk. Studies that combine a broader age range, reliable measures of Eysenck dimensions (including a revised psychoticism scale), and the DOSPERT measure can advance the field by providing a clearer assessment of trait–risk relations and by testing whether age accounts for or moderates those relations. Furthermore, simultaneous modeling of multiple personality dimensions helps clarify unique contributions of each trait, as traits can be intercorrelated and may confound bivariate associations. Inclusion of control variables such as gender and lie scores strengthens inferences about personality per se rather than response biases.

Given these theoretical and empirical considerations, the present study sought to examine the relations between Eysenckian personality dimensions and self-reported domain-specific risk-taking propensity assessed with the DOSPERT instrument in a diverse adult sample. We focused on a revised psychoticism scale designed to improve measurement precision, and we included extraversion, neuroticism, and the Lie scale to control for response bias. We also considered age as a predictor and potential confounder. Our primary research questions were whether psychoticism, extraversion, and neuroticism each predicted DOSPERT risk-taking scores and whether age accounted for additional variance. Based on theoretical predictions and prior evidence, we hypothesized that higher psychoticism scores would be associated with greater overall risk taking. We further hypothesized that extraversion would predict greater risk taking in social and recreational domains, though the direction of its association with an omnibus risk-taking score was less certain given prior mixed results. We predicted that higher neuroticism would be associated with lower physical and health-related risk taking due to increased perceived threat, but we anticipated the overall relation between neuroticism and global risk-taking propensity might be weak. Finally, we expected that age would be negatively associated with overall risk taking, such that older participants would report lower willingness to take risks.

To test these hypotheses, we administered the revised Eysenck personality inventory and the DOSPERT to a sample with an intentionally broad age range. We used multiple regression analyses to estimate the unique contributions of each Eysenck dimension to DOSPERT scores while adjusting for age, gender, and lie scores. We also conducted domain-specific analyses to evaluate whether trait associations differed across risk domains and exploratory sensitivity checks to evaluate robustness. By taking this multi-faceted approach, the study aimed to clarify which components of Eysenck personality reliably predict self-reported risk-taking propensity, how these relations vary by domain, and how age factors into these associations. The findings would thus have implications for theoretical models that link temperament, cognitive control, and risk-related decision processes, and they could inform future research in developmental, clinical, and applied settings.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures are described in detail because sample composition can affect generalizability. Participants were recruited from a combination of undergraduate research pools, community advertisements, and online crowdsourcing platforms to obtain a diverse sample in terms of age and background. A total of 312 respondents began the survey, and after applying exclusion criteria described below, 287 participants were retained for analysis. The final sample size of 287 was selected because it provided sufficient statistical power to detect small-to-medium effect sizes in multiple regression with six predictors at an alpha of .05 and power above .80. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 65 years old. The sample had a mean age of 29.3 years with a standard deviation of 10.2 years. Gender composition was 62% female, 36% male, and 2% reporting another gender identity; analyses reported here focus on male and female categories for statistical comparisons but include the nonbinary participants in all regression models to avoid unjustified exclusion. Educational attainment varied, with 48% reporting some college, 26% holding a bachelor’s degree, 8% with graduate-level education, and the remainder having high school diplomas or equivalent. The sample included a mix of students and community members, and participants reported a range of occupations and socioeconomic backgrounds.

All participants provided informed consent electronically before beginning the study. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by an institutional ethics board in accordance with standard ethical guidelines for human subjects research. Participants received course credit, a small monetary payment, or entry into a prize draw as compensation for their time depending on their recruitment source. Average completion time for the questionnaire battery was approximately 22 minutes, and participants were free to discontinue at any point without penalty. To ensure data quality, the survey included two attention-check items and basic validation checks such as minimum completion time thresholds; 25 participants were excluded for failing attention checks or completing the survey in less than one-third of the median completion time, and these exclusions left the final analytic sample of 287.

Measures were selected to operationalize both personality and risk-taking propensity with established instruments. The Eysenck Personality Questionnaire – Revised (EPQ-R) format was used, focusing on the psychoticism, extraversion, neuroticism, and lie scales. The psychoticism scale in the revised form includes items intended to capture impulsivity, nonconformity, and a tendency toward aggressive or antisocial attitudes. Each EPQ-R item was answered with a binary yes/no format as per the original scoring conventions for comparability. Sum scores were computed for each subscale in accordance with the instrument guidelines. The psychoticism scale (P) had 24 items in the revised scoring and showed acceptable internal consistency in the present sample (Cronbach’s alpha = .78). The extraversion (E) scale contained 23 items and had an alpha of .81. The neuroticism (N) scale contained 23 items with an alpha of .84. The Lie (L) scale, used as an indicator of socially desirable responding, contained 20 items and had an alpha of .75. All scales were scored so that higher numbers indicated greater levels of the trait.

Risk-taking propensity was measured using the Domain-Specific Risk-Taking (DOSPERT) scale. The DOSPERT is composed of items that ask participants to rate how likely they would be to engage in particular risky activities across five domains: financial (investment), health/safety, recreational, ethical, and social. Each item is rated on a 1–7 Likert scale where 1 indicates “extremely unlikely” and 7 indicates “extremely likely” to engage in the behavior. Following standard practice, we calculated an overall risk-taking score (RTS) by averaging the item scores across all domains, yielding a continuous measure where higher scores indicate greater self-reported willingness to engage in risky activities. We also computed domain-specific averages for secondary analyses. Internal consistency for the overall DOSPERT RTS in our sample was Cronbach’s alpha = .88, and domain-specific alphas ranged from .72 to .84, indicating acceptable reliability for subsequent analyses.

Procedurally, participants completed the EPQ-R and the DOSPERT in randomized order to control for potential order effects. Demographic questions, including age, gender, education, and employment status, were administered at the end of the battery to avoid priming effects. The online survey platform used encrypted connections and data were stored on secure servers. Participants were instructed to answer honestly based on their typical behavior and attitudes. The two embedded attention-check items required participants to select a specific response option; failure on either attention check led to exclusion from the final sample. All participants were debriefed at the end of the survey and provided with contact information for the research team in case of questions.

Statistical analyses were planned in advance and pre-registered to the extent possible within the institutional review process. Primary analyses focused on bivariate correlations and multiple linear regression models predicting the DOSPERT RTS. Bivariate Pearson correlations were computed between RTS and each EPQ-R scale, as well as age and gender. To estimate unique contributions of each predictor, we fit a hierarchical multiple regression model in which demographic variables (age, gender) were entered in Step 1, followed by Lie scale in Step 2, and the EPQ-R personality scales (psychoticism, extraversion, neuroticism) entered simultaneously in Step 3. This hierarchical approach allowed us to estimate incremental variance explained by personality after accounting for demographic and response-bias factors. Effect sizes for regression coefficients were reported as standardized betas, and the overall model fit was summarized by R-squared values and F-tests. Model assumptions were evaluated with residual plots to assess normality and homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity diagnostics including variance inflation factors (VIFs) were computed. Any observed influential cases were examined using Cook’s distance, and sensitivity analyses excluding such cases were reported.

Secondary analyses examined domain-specific DOSPERT subscales to test whether personality predictors had differential associations across risk domains. These domain analyses used the same regression framework but substituted the domain-specific average as the dependent variable. We also conducted exploratory interactions between age and psychoticism to test whether the effect of psychoticism on RTS varied with age, by adding an interaction term to the regression model. All tests used a two-tailed alpha level of .05. Where multiple comparisons were relevant, particularly in the domain-specific tests, we interpreted results with caution and reported adjusted p-values using the false discovery rate (FDR) procedure to control for Type I error inflation.

Finally, to probe robustness, we ran sensitivity analyses that excluded participants with extreme scores on the Lie scale, and that used Winsorized scores for variables showing strong skew. We also examined correlations within age strata (18–25, 26–40, 41+) to assess whether relations differed across life stages. All statistical analyses were performed using standard statistical software, and model diagnostics were carefully reviewed prior to interpreting coefficients. The overall analytic approach was designed to be transparent and reproducible and to provide a clear test of whether Eysenckian personality dimensions, particularly psychoticism, predict self-reported propensity for domain-specific risk taking while accounting for age and potential response biases.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first to characterize the sample and provide context for subsequent inferential tests. The overall DOSPERT risk-taking score (RTS) had a mean of 3.21 with a standard deviation of 0.82 on the 1–7 scale, indicating a moderate average willingness to engage in risk across domains. The revised psychoticism scale had a mean of 10.6 (SD = 4.1) on the 0–24 possible range. Extraversion scores averaged 13.9 (SD = 3.2) and neuroticism scores averaged 12.1 (SD = 3.9). The Lie scale had a mean of 6.8 (SD = 3.0). Age was relatively broadly distributed as noted in the Methods section; the median age was 27 years and the interquartile range for age was 22 to 35 years. Bivariate inspection of scale scores did not reveal extreme skew in the personality measures; the DOSPERT RTS showed mild left skew but was within acceptable limits for parametric testing. No single participant had missing data on the primary variables given the survey design that required completion of key items before proceeding.

We first report bivariate Pearson correlations among the primary variables of interest. Psychoticism correlated positively with DOSPERT RTS (r = .36, p < .001), indicating that participants with higher psychoticism scores reported greater willingness to take risks. Extraversion showed a small, non-significant correlation with RTS (r = .08, p = .18), and neuroticism showed a marginal and non-significant correlation (r = .10, p = .08). The Lie scale was not significantly correlated with RTS (r = −.05, p = .40). Age correlated negatively with RTS (r = −.25, p < .001), indicating that older participants reported less willingness to engage in risky activities. Gender had a small association with RTS, with males reporting slightly higher risk propensity (r point-biserial = .12, p = .04), but gender was not significantly correlated with psychoticism in this sample (r = .07, p = .21). These bivariate associations provide preliminary support for a positive psychoticism–risk link and for an age-related decline in self-reported risk taking.

Given the bivariate associations, we proceeded to hierarchical multiple regression to estimate the unique prediction of DOSPERT RTS by personality scales above and beyond demographic and response-bias variables. In Step 1, age and gender were entered. This demographic model explained R^2 = .07 of the variance in RTS (F(2,284) = 10.69, p < .001). Within this model, age was a significant predictor (standardized beta = −.24, p < .001), while gender was a weak and marginal predictor (standardized beta = .09, p = .07). In Step 2, the Lie scale was added to control for socially desirable responding; the model's R^2 increased slightly to .08 (ΔR^2 = .01, p = .21 for the addition of Lie), and the Lie scale itself was not a significant predictor (beta = −.04, p = .45). In Step 3, the three EPQ-R personality scales (psychoticism, extraversion, neuroticism) were entered simultaneously. The final model (Step 3) explained R^2 = .18 of the variance in RTS (F(6,280) = 10.82, p < .001), indicating that the inclusion of personality dimensions accounted for a meaningful increase in explained variance (ΔR^2 = .10, p < .001 for the addition of the three personality scales).

Within the final model, psychoticism emerged as a clear and significant predictor of RTS (standardized beta = .30, t = 5.10, p < .001), consistent with the bivariate correlation. Age remained a significant negative predictor after accounting for personality (standardized beta = −.20, t = −3.12, p = .002). Extraversion did not significantly predict RTS in the combined model (standardized beta = .05, t = 0.93, p = .35). Neuroticism was not a significant predictor either (standardized beta = .06, t = 1.08, p = .28). The Lie scale was not significant in the final model (beta = −.02, p = .70). Gender was also not significant once personality was taken into account (beta = .06, p = .29). Thus, psychoticism and age accounted for the substantive, significant effects in the multiple regression framework, while extraversion and neuroticism did not uniquely predict global DOSPERT RTS when included concurrently with psychoticism and demographic controls.

We inspected model diagnostics to ensure that standard regression assumptions were met. Residuals approximated normality on visual inspection of Q–Q plots, and the residuals versus fitted values plot did not indicate strong heteroscedasticity. Variance inflation factors for all predictors were low (VIFs < 1.6), indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. Cook’s distance identified two potentially influential cases; sensitivity analyses that excluded those two cases yielded substantively identical results: psychoticism remained a significant predictor (beta = .29, p < .001), and age remained negative and significant (beta = −.19, p = .003), while extraversion and neuroticism remained non-significant. These robustness checks increase confidence in the stability of the primary findings.

Secondary analyses examined whether the pattern of associations differed across DOSPERT domains. We ran separate regression models predicting each domain score from the same set of predictors (age, gender, Lie, psychoticism, extraversion, neuroticism). Psychoticism predicted higher recreational domain risk taking (beta = .33, p < .001) and higher financial-investment domain risk taking at a smaller magnitude (beta = .18, p = .01). Psychoticism was also a modest predictor of ethical risk taking (beta = .16, p = .02). Extraversion did not predict recreational or financial domains, but it was a modest predictor of social risk taking (beta = .20, p = .004) in domain-specific models; however, when we applied FDR correction for multiple domain tests, the extraversion–social domain association fell to marginal significance (adjusted p = .06), suggesting that the extraversion effect is weak and possibly domain-limited. Neuroticism showed a small negative association with health/safety risk taking (beta = −.14, p = .03), consistent with greater threat sensitivity leading to less willingness to engage in risky health behaviors, but this effect did not survive FDR correction. Age showed negative associations across most domains, most strongly with recreational and health/safety risk taking.

We also tested an exploratory interaction between psychoticism and age to evaluate whether the effect of psychoticism on RTS varied across the lifespan. The psychoticism × age interaction term was not significant in the model predicting overall RTS (beta = −.05, p = .36), suggesting that the association between psychoticism and risk taking was relatively consistent across the age range sampled. Stratified correlations within age bands produced similar psychoticism–RTS correlations for 18–25 (r = .34, p < .001), 26–40 (r = .33, p < .001), and 41+ (r = .31, p = .02) groups, indicating stability of the psychoticism–risk relation across life stages in this sample.

Finally, to probe the potential influence of response bias, we ran analyses excluding participants in the top decile of the Lie scale. Results were essentially unchanged: psychoticism remained a significant predictor of RTS (beta = .31, p < .001), and the age effect remained negative and significant (beta = −.22, p = .001). Thus, the relations observed do not appear to be driven by socially desirable responding as measured by the Lie scale. Overall, the results provide consistent evidence that psychoticism scores are positively associated with self-reported domain-general and domain-specific risk taking, that extraversion does not predict global risk propensity in the full model, and that age is associated with lower willingness to take risks.

Discussion

The present study tested theoretical predictions drawn from Eysenckian trait models and domain-specific conceptions of risk taking by examining associations among psychoticism, extraversion, neuroticism, and self-reported risk-taking propensity across domains in a broad adult sample. The central finding is that psychoticism, as measured by a revised EPQ-R scale, reliably predicts higher overall DOSPERT risk-taking scores even after accounting for age, gender, and a lie-scale indicator of response distortion. This finding aligns with theoretical expectations that traits involving impulsivity and nonconformity would predispose individuals toward greater engagement with risky behaviors. Importantly, psychoticism’s predictive effect was observed both in bivariate correlations and in multiple regression models, and it was robust to sensitivity checks that excluded potentially influential data points and participants with high lie-scale scores. These convergent indications strengthen the interpretation that psychoticism is an important dispositional correlate of risk-taking propensity.

The lack of a significant association between extraversion and the global DOSPERT RTS in the primary multivariate model was noteworthy and somewhat unexpected given that extraversion is commonly conceptualized as involving reward sensitivity and approach tendencies that could facilitate risk-taking. In our study, extraversion did show a moderate bivariate association with RTS that did not reach significance, and domain-specific analyses suggested a weak association between extraversion and social risk taking. However, the extraversion–social risk association was not robust to multiple-comparison correction, and extraversion did not predict other risk domains or the overall RTS. These results suggest that extraversion’s relationship to risk taking is more nuanced and possibly domain dependent; extraversion may primarily influence choices that are social in nature or situations where reward is social affiliation rather than material gain. This domain-specificity could explain inconsistent findings in the literature, where some studies report extraversion–risk links and others do not depending on the outcomes and measures used.

Neuroticism did not emerge as a significant unique predictor of overall risk taking in our regression models. Neuroticism showed a small negative bivariate relation with some risk domains, most notably health/safety, which is consistent with the notion that heightened threat sensitivity may curb engagement in physically risky behaviors. However, these associations were weak and did not hold up in corrected domain-level tests. One interpretation is that neuroticism’s effects on risk taking may be complex and contextually modulated. For instance, high neuroticism might reduce some types of risk taking related to physical danger while increasing other types of risky behavior in contexts involving emotional regulation difficulties. The domain-specific complexity may obscure simple linear relations in global measures.

Age was another robust predictor in the current study, such that older participants reported lower willingness to take risks across domains. The age–risk link persisted after controlling for personality dimensions and was observed across several DOSPERT domains. This finding echoes a large body of developmental research indicating that risk taking typically declines from adolescence and early adulthood into middle age and that age-related increases in cognitive control, responsibility, and changes in reward sensitivity likely contribute to this decline. The present cross-sectional data cannot speak to developmental trajectories per se, but they are consistent with theoretical accounts that emphasize maturation and life-course changes in risk propensity.

Taken together, the pattern of findings supports a model in which certain personality dispositions, specifically those captured by psychoticism, confer a stable predisposition toward higher risk-taking propensity, while other traits such as extraversion and neuroticism have weaker, more domain-limited, or context-dependent effects. Psychoticism’s robust association with both the overall RTS and specific domains such as recreational risk taking suggests that traits involving impulsivity and nonconformity exert a broad influence on risk-related choices. This is consistent with mechanistic explanations that posit reduced inhibitory control and heightened impulsive approach tendencies among high-psychoticism individuals. The fact that psychoticism also predicted financial and ethical domains, albeit at smaller magnitudes, implies that its influence is not confined to purely thrill-seeking behaviors but extends to contexts where nonconformity or disregard for rules might facilitate risk-laden decisions.

The null or weak findings for extraversion and neuroticism in predicting global risk propensity raise several plausible explanations. First, measurement differences across studies may contribute to inconsistent results; extraversion, in particular, is a multifaceted construct that includes sociability, assertiveness, and positive affect, and some facets may be more closely related to risk taking than others. The binary yes/no scoring of the EPQ-R might also compress individual differences in extraversion that would be detectable with more nuanced Likert formats. Second, domain specificity likely plays a role: extraversion may be most relevant to social and perhaps recreational risk taking, but these domain-specific effects can be diluted when averaged into a global score. Third, sample characteristics such as age distribution and cultural context can modulate trait–risk relations; our sample included a majority of young adults but extended into older ages, and extraversion’s impact on risk taking might be most visible in adolescent samples where social reward is especially salient.

Several limitations of the current study warrant careful consideration. The cross-sectional correlational design precludes causal inferences; while psychoticism predicted self-reported risk propensity, we cannot determine whether psychoticism causes greater risk taking or whether recurring engagement in risky behaviors might shape self-perceptions reflected in psychoticism scores. Longitudinal designs would be required to disambiguate directionality and to examine developmental trajectories. Second, the reliance on self-report measures for both personality and risk propensity raises concerns about shared method variance and social desirability biases. Although we included a Lie scale to detect response distortion and conducted sensitivity analyses excluding high-Lie individuals, self-report limitations remain. Incorporating behavioral risk tasks, informant reports, and objective outcomes would strengthen causal and construct validity in future research.

Third, although the revised psychoticism scale used here had acceptable internal consistency, psychoticism remains a heterogeneous construct that may conflate impulsivity, antisocial tendencies, and other dispositional features. Future research would benefit from decomposing psychoticism into its constituent facets, for example differentiating impulsivity from callousness or rule-breaking attitudes, to determine which components most strongly drive risk-taking behavior. Using complementary measures such as impulsivity scales, sensation-seeking inventories, and measures of executive function could provide a more nuanced picture.

Fourth, the sample, while broader than many undergraduate-only studies, is not nationally representative. The recruitment sources included students and online respondents, which may bias the sample in ways that affect generalizability to populations with different socioeconomic, cultural, or educational backgrounds. Future studies should seek representative samples or target specific subpopulations to test the robustness of the psychoticism–risk link across contexts.

The findings also suggest several promising directions for future research. A priority is to integrate personality measures with cognitive and neuroscientific assessments that can illuminate underlying mechanisms. For example, combining psychoticism assessment with behavioral inhibition tasks, delay-discounting paradigms, and neuroimaging measures of reward circuitry and prefrontal control would help test whether psychoticism indexes diminished inhibitory control or heightened reward responsivity, and how these neurocognitive features mediate risk taking. Experimental designs that manipulate perceived control, stress, or reward contingencies could examine whether psychoticism moderates sensitivity to these situational factors, thereby clarifying trait × situation interactions.

Longitudinal studies are essential to understand the developmental interplay between personality and risk taking. Tracking individuals across adolescence into adulthood would permit examination of how trait levels and risk behaviors co-evolve, whether early psychoticism predicts later problem behaviors, and whether life events such as employment, parenthood, or legal sanctions alter these trajectories. Such studies would also provide insight into whether interventions that strengthen cognitive control or alter reward valuations lead to changes in both psychoticism-related behaviors and self-reported trait levels.

Another fruitful area for research is the domain-specificity of trait–risk relations. The current results indicate that psychoticism’s effects are more pronounced in recreational and some financial contexts, whereas extraversion may be most relevant to social risk taking. Future work should deliberately target domain-specific hypotheses, perhaps employing more fine-grained assessments or ecological momentary assessment to capture in-the-moment risk choices across daily contexts. This approach could reveal situational moderators, such as peer presence or alcohol use, which might amplify or attenuate trait effects.

From an applied perspective, the results have implications for interventions and risk communication. If psychoticism-related traits robustly predict higher risk propensity, screening for elevated impulsivity and nonconformity could help identify individuals who may benefit from targeted interventions aimed at improving decision-making under risk. Such interventions could focus on strengthening executive control, practicing deliberative decision strategies, or reframing perceived benefits and costs in ways that reduce impulsive choices. Tailoring interventions to age groups could also enhance effectiveness, as age exerts an independent effect on risk willingness; preventive efforts might be especially valuable during late adolescence and early adulthood when risk propensity peaks.

In sum, the study contributes to the literature on personality and risk taking by demonstrating that psychoticism is a reliable predictor of self-reported domain-general and certain domain-specific risk propensities, while extraversion and neuroticism show weaker and more context-dependent associations. Age is also a consistent correlate, with older participants reporting lower risk willingness. These findings support the view that personality dispositions, particularly those involving impulsivity and nonconformity, play a substantive role in shaping risk-related tendencies, but they also highlight the complexity of trait–behavior links and the need for multi-method, longitudinal, and mechanistic research to fully understand the processes at work.
