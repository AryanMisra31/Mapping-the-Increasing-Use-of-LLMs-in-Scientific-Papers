Introduction

Self-determination theory (SDT) provides a prominent framework for understanding human motivation and the conditions under which people engage in and persist at activities for reasons that are internally endorsed and satisfying. According to SDT, intrinsic motivation refers to doing an activity for the inherent satisfaction and interest that the activity itself provides, rather than for separable outcomes or external consequences. Intrinsic motivation is postulated to flourish when three innate psychological needs—competence, relatedness, and autonomy—are met in the social and task environment. The need for competence refers to experiencing effectiveness in one’s interactions and a sense of mastery; the need for relatedness refers to feeling connected to others and experiencing caring reciprocal relationships; and the need for autonomy refers to the sense of volition, choice, and psychological freedom in one’s actions. When these needs are satisfied, individuals are more likely to engage in activities with curiosity, persistence, and enjoyment, and to derive cognitive, affective, and behavioral benefits from their engagement.

The study of individual differences in motivational orientation has a long history, and personality traits are frequently examined as stable dispositional factors that influence how people approach tasks and goals. Personality traits can shape the ways in which people interpret environmental cues, respond emotionally to success and failure, and seek or avoid social interaction. Among traits, extraversion and neuroticism have been the most consistently studied in relation to motivation. Extraversion is characterized by sociability, positive affectivity, assertiveness, and reward sensitivity, and theorists have argued that extraverts may be particularly inclined to approach rewarding situations and to experience higher levels of engagement and intrinsic interest in activities that offer stimulation or social rewards. Neuroticism, by contrast, describes a tendency toward negative affect, anxiety, emotional instability, and vulnerability to stress, and it is theorized to undermine motivation by increasing aversive affective responses, reducing resilience to setbacks, and producing self-doubt that interferes with feelings of competence and autonomy.

Empirical research has yielded mixed findings regarding the relations between these traits and intrinsic motivation across different domains. Some studies with populations engaged in physical exercise contexts, such as gym-goers and sports participants, have reported that extraversion is a strong positive correlate of intrinsic motivation for physical activity. The social and energetic aspects of exercise settings, coupled with the immediate hedonic rewards of physical activation and group participation, may make extraversion particularly relevant in these domains. These findings have been taken to suggest that extraverted dispositions align with the typical affordances of gym environments and group exercise, thereby supporting the satisfaction of relatedness and competence needs in those contexts. Other research, however, has indicated that neuroticism is negatively related to intrinsic motivation across a range of settings. High neuroticism appears to undermine self-determined motivation by generating worry, rumination about potential failure, and affective dysregulation that compromises feelings of autonomy, competence, and relatedness, and thus reduces the appeal and persistence in intrinsically motivating activities.

In addition to extraversion and neuroticism, psychoticism has been posited as another personality dimension that can bear on motivational processes. Psychoticism, conceived in the Eysenckian tradition, captures tendencies toward impulsivity, aggressiveness, coldness, and nonconformity. High levels of psychoticism have been linked to lower prosociality, more antagonistic interpersonal styles, and impulsive decision-making. These characteristics could undermine intrinsic motivation by eroding social bonds and opportunities for supportive feedback, by increasing conflict that thwarts relatedness, and by promoting styles of engagement that are less consistent with the sustained, curious, and mastery-oriented approach that intrinsic motivation embodies. Despite theoretical reasons to expect psychoticism to be relevant, empirical studies of psychoticism in relation to intrinsic motivation have been scarce, particularly in student populations where academic demands and social contexts may interact with personality in domain-specific ways.

Given the domain-sensitivity of motivational processes, it is important to examine how personality traits relate to intrinsic motivation in different spheres of life. Academic settings present a distinct constellation of demands, feedback patterns, and social dynamics compared to physical activity contexts. Academic tasks often involve sustained cognitive effort, delayed rewards, and evaluations from instructors, whereas physical activity can provide immediate sensory stimulation, social interaction, and embodied feedback. Consequently, traits that promote approach tendencies toward social stimulation, like extraversion, might be more relevant in physical domains, whereas traits influencing emotional stability and self-regulation, like neuroticism and psychoticism, may be particularly salient for academic intrinsic motivation. However, the evidence base to adjudicate these possibilities remains limited, and direct comparisons across domains within the same sample are relatively rare.

Students represent a population for whom both academic and physical domains are salient and for whom intrinsic motivation has important implications for learning, mental health, and well-being. Understanding the personality correlates of intrinsic motivation in student samples can inform interventions aimed at fostering self-determined engagement and satisfying basic psychological needs in contexts that matter for developmental outcomes. Previous research with gym-goers suggested that extraversion is the most important personality trait encouraging intrinsic motivation in that sample, and that lower neuroticism is also associated with greater intrinsic motivation. Those findings raise two questions when extended to a student population: first, whether the pattern observed in gym-goers generalizes to students when considering intrinsic motivation in academic and physical domains; and second, whether psychoticism plays a meaningful role in predicting self-determined motivation among students.

The current study was designed to address these gaps by examining how extraversion, neuroticism, and psychoticism relate to self-determined intrinsic motivation in a sample of university students, with explicit attention to domain-specificity. We sought to measure intrinsic motivation toward two domains that are highly relevant for students—academic study and physical activity—and to evaluate whether the trait–motivation relationships differ across these domains. In particular, we tested whether extraversion would be a stronger predictor of intrinsic motivation in the physical domain than in the academic domain, reflecting the social and energetic affordances of exercise contexts. We also tested whether neuroticism would be negatively associated with intrinsic motivation in both domains, given the theorized undermining effects of negative affect and emotion dysregulation on the satisfaction of competence, autonomy, and relatedness needs. Finally, we explored the role of psychoticism as a predictor of intrinsic motivation, hypothesizing that higher psychoticism would be associated with lower intrinsic motivation, particularly in academic settings where collaborative work, adherence to norms, and regulation are emphasized.

Beyond simple trait–outcome associations, we were interested in process-level explanations, and therefore we examined whether basic psychological needs satisfaction might mediate the relations between personality traits and intrinsic motivation. The SDT framework explicitly posits that the satisfaction of competence, relatedness, and autonomy mediates the development of intrinsic motivation. If personality traits shape the likelihood of experiencing needs satisfaction in particular contexts, then needs satisfaction would be expected to account for at least part of the association between traits and intrinsic motivation. For example, high neuroticism might lead to lower needs satisfaction via increased anxiety and threat perception, which in turn would reduce intrinsic motivation. Likewise, high psychoticism might reduce the experience of relatedness and perceived support, which could undermine intrinsic motivational engagement. By testing mediation models, the study aimed to move beyond descriptive correlations to examine plausible mechanisms that link personality to motivated behavior.

We also considered several potential confounding and moderating variables, including gender, age, academic performance, and levels of habitual physical activity. Gender differences in both personality traits and motivational orientations are well documented, and these differences could influence observed associations if not accounted for. Academic performance and habitual physical activity can be both outcomes and antecedents of intrinsic motivation, and they may also correlate with personality traits; accordingly, we included these variables to test the robustness of the trait–motivation associations. Additionally, we explored whether the trait–motivation relations varied by gender or by domain, assessing interaction effects to detect potential moderation.

Given the cross-sectional nature of the study, we approached causal interpretation cautiously, treating our analyses as tests of association and mediation that can suggest but not confirm causal pathways. The study was designed to be comprehensive in its measurement and analytic approach, and to offer a balanced assessment of the relative importance of extraversion, neuroticism, and psychoticism for intrinsic motivation in student populations across academic and physical domains. The primary hypotheses were as follows: (1) Extraversion will be positively related to intrinsic motivation for physical activity, but its relation to academic intrinsic motivation will be weaker or non-significant; (2) Neuroticism will be negatively related to intrinsic motivation in both domains, and this relation will be relatively strong; (3) Psychoticism will be negatively related to intrinsic motivation, especially academic intrinsic motivation; and (4) The relations between personality traits and intrinsic motivation will be partially mediated by basic psychological needs satisfaction, such that traits associated with lower needs satisfaction will predict lower intrinsic motivation.

By addressing these hypotheses within a single, well-characterized student sample, this research sought to clarify the domain-specific and domain-general roles of core personality dimensions in shaping self-determined motivation. The findings have the potential to inform educational practices and health promotion efforts by highlighting personality-related targets for interventions that aim to enhance intrinsic motivation and needs satisfaction. If certain traits consistently undermine intrinsic motivation via pathways involving needs dissatisfaction, then interventions could be designed to bolster emotion regulation, social connectedness, or perceived competence to offset the negative impacts of those traits. Conversely, if extraversion proves context-specific as a predictor of intrinsic motivation, then tailoring environments to better afford social interaction and stimulation could be particularly effective in physical domains. Overall, the study aimed to contribute to a nuanced understanding of how enduring dispositional factors interact with motivational processes across life domains that are central to student development.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures are described first. The sample consisted of 312 undergraduate students enrolled at a medium-sized university. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 25 years, with a mean age of 20.4 years (SD = 1.9). The sample included 187 female students (59.9%), 123 male students (39.4%), and 2 participants who identified as non-binary (0.6%). Students were recruited from introductory psychology courses, via campus email lists, and through flyers posted in academic buildings. Participation was voluntary and students received course credit or a small monetary compensation for completing an online survey. Inclusion criteria were being enrolled as an undergraduate student and being between 18 and 25 years of age. The planned sample size of approximately 300 participants was determined to provide adequate power to detect small-to-moderate effect sizes in correlational and regression analyses and to permit mediation testing using bootstrap methods.

All procedures were approved by the university institutional review board, and participants provided informed consent electronically before beginning the survey. The online survey was administered via a secure platform and participants were instructed to complete the measures in a single sitting. The survey took approximately 25 to 35 minutes to complete. Data quality checks were implemented, including attention-check items and response time monitoring; participants who failed attention checks or whose completion time suggested disengaged responding were excluded from the analyses (n = 8 excluded for failing attention checks, n = 5 excluded for implausibly short completion times). The final analytic sample comprised 299 participants.

Measures included assessments of personality traits, domain-specific intrinsic motivation, basic psychological needs satisfaction, habitual physical activity, and academic performance. Personality traits were assessed using the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire–Revised Short Form (EPQ-R Short), which yields scales for Extraversion (E), Neuroticism (N), and Psychoticism (P). The EPQ-R Short consists of 48 items in true/false format, with 12 or more items per scale in the version used here. Scores were computed as the sum of items keyed for each scale, with higher scores indicating higher levels of the trait. Prior psychometric evaluations support the EPQ-R Short as an efficient measure of the three dimensions; in the present sample the internal consistencies (Kuder-Richardson 20 for dichotomous items) were acceptable, with KR-20 = .78 for Extraversion, KR-20 = .81 for Neuroticism, and KR-20 = .69 for Psychoticism.

Domain-specific intrinsic motivation was measured using two parallel adaptations of the Situational Motivation Scale (SIMS), tailored to the academic and physical activity contexts. The SIMS assesses intrinsic motivation, identified regulation, external regulation, and amotivation, but for the purposes of this study the intrinsic motivation subscale was used in each domain-specific adaptation. The Academic Intrinsic Motivation subscale consisted of four items that asked participants to rate the degree to which they engaged in their academic work for inherent interest and enjoyment (e.g., “I do my schoolwork because I find it enjoyable and satisfying”). The Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation subscale consisted of four analogous items tailored to exercise and sport (e.g., “I do physical exercise because it is fun and enjoyable”). Items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 7 = completely) and were averaged to form subscale scores. Internal consistency was high for the Academic Intrinsic Motivation subscale (α = .88) and for the Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation subscale (α = .86).

Basic psychological needs satisfaction was assessed separately for the academic and physical domains using short-form scales that measure competence, relatedness, and autonomy in the specific context. Each needs subscale contained three items, yielding nine items per context. Items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale and were averaged to form a composite Needs Satisfaction score for each domain. Internal consistency for the Academic Needs Satisfaction composite was satisfactory (α = .90), and for the Physical Needs Satisfaction composite it was likewise satisfactory (α = .88). For mediation tests, the composite needs satisfaction scores were the primary mediators of interest.

Additional measures included a single-item self-report of habitual physical activity frequency (“How many days per week, on average, do you engage in at least 30 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity?”), and self-reported cumulative grade point average (GPA) as an index of academic performance. Gender and age were included as demographic covariates.

The analytic strategy proceeded in several steps. Preliminary descriptive statistics and reliability analyses were computed for all measures. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine bivariate associations among personality traits, intrinsic motivation in each domain, needs satisfaction, and covariates. To assess unique predictive effects, hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted separately for Academic Intrinsic Motivation and Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation as dependent variables. In the first step of each regression, demographic covariates (age, gender) and domain-relevant covariates (GPA for academic analyses and physical activity frequency for physical analyses) were entered. In the second step, the three personality traits (Extraversion, Neuroticism, Psychoticism) were entered simultaneously to examine their unique contributions. Standardized beta coefficients, change in R-squared, and significance tests were reported for each model.

Mediation analyses were conducted to evaluate whether the composite Needs Satisfaction score in each domain mediated the relation between personality traits and intrinsic motivation. For these tests, we used bootstrapped confidence intervals with 5,000 resamples to evaluate indirect effects, following recommended procedures for mediation in cross-sectional data. Separate mediation models were estimated for Neuroticism and Psychoticism because these were hypothesized to have the strongest associations with intrinsic motivation; Extraversion was included in supplementary mediation tests given its expected domain-specificity. All mediation models controlled for the same covariates as in the regression analyses.

Supplementary analyses examined potential moderation by gender by testing interaction terms between each personality trait and gender in predicting intrinsic motivation. Additional robustness checks re-ran primary analyses controlling for both GPA and physical activity frequency simultaneously, and also excluding participants with extreme scores (top and bottom 2.5%) on personality variables to assess sensitivity to outliers.

Data screening procedures ensured that assumptions of normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity were met for regression analyses. Inspection of residuals, plots of predicted versus observed values, and variance inflation factors (VIFs) were used to assess multicollinearity and model fit; VIFs were below commonly used cutoffs, indicating acceptable multicollinearity. Missing data were minimal (below 2% for any variable) and were handled with pairwise deletion for correlational analyses and full-information maximum likelihood estimation for regression tests when appropriate.

Results

Descriptive statistics and reliability estimates are summarized first to provide a context for subsequent analyses. For the final sample of 299 participants, the mean Extraversion score on the EPQ-R Short was 16.8 (SD = 4.2), the mean Neuroticism score was 14.5 (SD = 4.8), and the mean Psychoticism score was 8.9 (SD = 3.6). The mean Academic Intrinsic Motivation score was 5.2 (on the 1–7 scale; SD = 1.0), and the mean Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation score was 4.7 (SD = 1.3). The mean Academic Needs Satisfaction composite was 4.9 (SD = 1.1), and the mean Physical Needs Satisfaction composite was 4.6 (SD = 1.2). Self-reported GPA averaged 3.18 (SD = 0.46), and habitual physical activity frequency averaged 3.4 days per week (SD = 1.8). Reliability coefficients reported earlier (KR-20 or Cronbach’s alpha) indicated acceptable internal consistency across key scales and are reported in the Methods.

Bivariate correlations among the main study variables revealed several notable patterns. Neuroticism exhibited a significant negative correlation with Academic Intrinsic Motivation (r = -0.42, p < .001) and with Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation (r = -0.33, p < .001). Psychoticism was negatively correlated with Academic Intrinsic Motivation (r = -0.28, p < .001) and with Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation (r = -0.21, p < .001). Extraversion showed a small positive correlation with Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation (r = 0.18, p = .002) but had a non-significant correlation with Academic Intrinsic Motivation (r = 0.06, p = .28). These bivariate patterns suggested that Neuroticism had the strongest negative associations with intrinsic motivation across both domains, whereas Extraversion’s relevance appeared more domain-specific and modest in magnitude.

Bivariate correlations between personality traits and needs satisfaction composites also conformed to theoretical expectations. Neuroticism was negatively correlated with Academic Needs Satisfaction (r = -0.46, p < .001) and with Physical Needs Satisfaction (r = -0.36, p < .001). Psychoticism was negatively correlated with Academic Needs Satisfaction (r = -0.30, p < .001) and with Physical Needs Satisfaction (r = -0.22, p < .001). Extraversion showed a positive correlation with Physical Needs Satisfaction (r = 0.21, p < .001) but was not significantly correlated with Academic Needs Satisfaction (r = 0.05, p = .33). Needs Satisfaction composites were strongly correlated with intrinsic motivation in their respective domains: Academic Needs Satisfaction with Academic Intrinsic Motivation (r = 0.61, p < .001), and Physical Needs Satisfaction with Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation (r = 0.57, p < .001).

Hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the unique contributions of personality traits to intrinsic motivation when covariates were controlled. For Academic Intrinsic Motivation, the first step included age, gender, and GPA as covariates; this step accounted for R^2 = .08 of the variance (F(3,295) = 8.60, p < .001), with GPA positively related to Academic Intrinsic Motivation (β = .22, p < .001) and gender not yielding a significant effect. In the second step, the three personality traits were entered simultaneously, producing a significant increment in explained variance (ΔR^2 = .17, p < .001). In this model, Neuroticism was a strong negative predictor of Academic Intrinsic Motivation (standardized β = -0.36, p < .001), Psychoticism was a significant negative predictor (β = -0.19, p = .002), and Extraversion was non-significant (β = .04, p = .21). The full model explained R^2 = .25 of the variance in Academic Intrinsic Motivation (F(6,292) = 16.31, p < .001), indicating that personality contributed substantially above demographic and performance covariates.

For Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation, the hierarchical regression first controlled for age, gender, and habitual physical activity frequency; this initial step accounted for R^2 = .12 of the variance (F(3,295) = 13.46, p < .001), with physical activity frequency positively associated with Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation (β = .29, p < .001). Adding the three personality traits in the second step produced a modest but significant ΔR^2 = .08 (p < .001). In the full model, Neuroticism remained a significant negative predictor (β = -0.28, p < .001), Psychoticism was a smaller yet significant negative predictor (β = -0.12, p = .04), and Extraversion showed a small positive beta that approached conventional significance (β = 0.10, p = .06). The full model explained R^2 = .20 of the variance in Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation (F(6,292) = 12.18, p < .001).

Given the hypothesized mediating role of needs satisfaction, mediation analyses were conducted for the relation between Neuroticism and Academic Intrinsic Motivation with Academic Needs Satisfaction as the mediator. Neuroticism was significantly related to Academic Needs Satisfaction (path a: unstandardized B = -0.14, SE = 0.02, p < .001), and Academic Needs Satisfaction was strongly related to Academic Intrinsic Motivation when controlling for Neuroticism (path b: B = 0.62, SE = 0.05, p < .001). The direct effect of Neuroticism on Academic Intrinsic Motivation remained significant but was reduced in magnitude when needs satisfaction was included (direct effect c': B = -0.21, SE = 0.04, p < .001). The bootstrapped indirect effect (a*b) was significant (indirect effect = -0.09, 95% CI [-0.13, -0.06]), indicating partial mediation. A parallel set of analyses for Physical Needs Satisfaction as mediator of the Neuroticism–Physical Intrinsic Motivation relation likewise revealed significant indirect effects (indirect = -0.07, 95% CI [-0.10, -0.04]) and a reduced but still significant direct effect.

Mediation analyses for Psychoticism revealed that Academic Needs Satisfaction partially mediated the relation between Psychoticism and Academic Intrinsic Motivation. Psychoticism predicted lower Academic Needs Satisfaction (a: B = -0.09, SE = 0.02, p < .001) and Academic Needs Satisfaction predicted Academic Intrinsic Motivation in the expected direction (b: B = 0.61, SE = 0.05, p < .001). The indirect effect was small but statistically significant (indirect = -0.06, 95% CI [-0.09, -0.03]), and the direct effect of Psychoticism on Academic Intrinsic Motivation remained significant (c': B = -0.11, SE = 0.04, p = .002), suggesting partial mediation. Analogous mediation analyses in the physical domain returned smaller indirect effects but consistent patterns (indirect = -0.04, 95% CI [-0.07, -0.02]).

Supplementary moderation analyses tested whether gender moderated the relations between personality traits and intrinsic motivation. Interaction terms between each trait and gender were entered in separate regression models; none of the interaction terms reached significance for Academic Intrinsic Motivation (all ps > .10). For Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation, the Extraversion × Gender interaction approached significance (p = .07), suggesting a trend whereby Extraversion might be a slightly stronger predictor of physical intrinsic motivation for male participants than for female participants, but this trend did not meet conventional thresholds for statistical significance. Sensitivity analyses controlling for both GPA and physical activity frequency produced results that were highly consistent with the primary analyses, with Neuroticism and Psychoticism remaining significant negative predictors of intrinsic motivation and Extraversion remaining non-significant in the academic domain.

Effect sizes and relative importance of predictors were inspected to assess practical significance. Neuroticism had the largest standardized beta in both domain regressions, indicating its relative importance in predicting intrinsic motivation. Psychoticism contributed smaller but meaningful unique variance, and Extraversion contributed little unique variance in the academic domain and modest variance in the physical domain. The mediation analyses suggested that reductions in needs satisfaction partially explained the negative associations of Neuroticism and Psychoticism with intrinsic motivation, accounting for approximately 30–40% of the total effect in the academic domain and a smaller proportion in the physical domain.

Additional exploratory analyses examined the three basic need subcomponents separately to identify whether any particular need drove the mediation effects. When competence, relatedness, and autonomy were entered simultaneously as mediators in multiple-mediator models, the competence subcomponent emerged as the strongest mediator of the Neuroticism → Academic Intrinsic Motivation relation (indirect via competence = -0.05, 95% CI [-0.08, -0.03]), with relatedness and autonomy contributing smaller but significant indirect effects. For Psychoticism, relatedness was the most prominent mediator of its relation with Academic Intrinsic Motivation, consistent with the theoretical link between antagonistic interpersonal styles and reduced social connectedness.

Taken together, the results indicate that in this student sample, Neuroticism and Psychoticism were consistently negatively associated with intrinsic motivation in both academic and physical domains, with Neuroticism showing the strongest effects. Extraversion showed only a modest, domain-specific association with Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation and did not significantly predict Academic Intrinsic Motivation. Needs satisfaction partially mediated the relations of Neuroticism and Psychoticism with intrinsic motivation, particularly in the academic domain, and competence and relatedness emerged as key pathways.

Discussion

The present study examined how three personality traits—Extraversion, Neuroticism, and Psychoticism—related to self-determined intrinsic motivation in a sample of university students, with particular attention to domain-specific differences between academic and physical activity contexts and to the mediating role of basic psychological needs satisfaction. In line with theoretical expectations based on self-determination theory and trait theory, the findings indicated that Neuroticism was a robust negative predictor of intrinsic motivation across both domains, that Psychoticism also predicted lower intrinsic motivation albeit with smaller effects, and that Extraversion’s predictive utility was modest and context-dependent. Mediation analyses suggested that the negative effects of Neuroticism and Psychoticism on intrinsic motivation were partially explained by lower experiences of needs satisfaction, with competence and relatedness playing central roles. These results contribute to a nuanced understanding of how stable dispositional factors interface with situational motivational processes among students.

The finding that Neuroticism emerged as the most important personality predictor of intrinsic motivation aligns with prior conceptualizations of neuroticism as a trait marked by negative affectivity, emotional instability, and heightened sensitivity to stressors. Individuals high in Neuroticism are prone to worry, rumination, and threat-focus, which can undermine experiences of autonomy, competence, and relatedness that are essential for intrinsic motivation. In academic contexts, for example, anxiety and fear of failure may reduce the enjoyment of learning activities, lower perceptions of competence even in the face of objective accomplishments, and restrict willingness to engage in exploratory or challenging tasks. Similarly, in physical activity contexts, high neuroticism may translate into avoidance of group exercise due to social fears or a tendency to interpret physical exertion as aversive rather than enjoyable. The mediation results provide empirical support for this mechanistic account: Neuroticism was strongly associated with lower domain-specific needs satisfaction, and needs satisfaction in turn predicted higher intrinsic motivation. These patterns suggest that interventions aimed at reducing affective distress and enhancing emotion regulation skills could help to remove barriers to needs satisfaction and thereby foster more self-determined engagement among students with elevated Neuroticism.

Psychoticism also showed consistent negative relations with intrinsic motivation, although its effects were smaller than those of Neuroticism. The psychoticism construct captures tendencies toward impulsivity, interpersonal coldness, and nonconformity, which can predispose individuals to behaviors and attitudes that undermine social connectedness and adherence to structured learning demands. The mediation analyses indicated that relatedness in particular mediated the Psychoticism–Intrinsic Motivation link, suggesting that students high in psychoticism may experience less supportive social environments or may be less able to engage in the types of affiliative interactions that promote motivation. The direct effects of Psychoticism on intrinsic motivation that remained after accounting for needs satisfaction imply that other pathways—such as impulsivity-related difficulties in sustained engagement or lower conscientiousness—may also contribute to reduced intrinsic motivation. These findings highlight the value of attending to interpersonal functioning and self-regulatory capacities when designing motivational supports for students.

Extraversion, by contrast, displayed a domain-specific pattern that is theoretically coherent with the characteristics of the trait and the affordances of physical activity contexts. While Extraversion did not significantly predict Academic Intrinsic Motivation in our sample, it showed a modest positive association with Physical Activity Intrinsic Motivation at the bivariate level and a trend toward significance in multivariate analyses when covariates were included. This pattern mirrors findings from prior research in exercise settings showing that extraverts may be drawn to stimulating, social, and reward-rich environments. The social and energizing features of many forms of physical activity may be more congruent with extraverted tendencies, thereby supporting relatedness and immediate hedonic rewards and fostering intrinsic motivation. However, when Neuroticism and Psychoticism were controlled, Extraversion’s unique prediction of physical intrinsic motivation was attenuated, indicating that extraversion may operate in conjunction with other traits and contextual factors rather than as an independent driver of motivation.

The differential roles of these traits across domains emphasize the value of domain-specific measurement in motivational research. Had we examined only a global measure of intrinsic motivation, the relative importance of Neuroticism and Psychoticism might have been less clearly interpreted. By assessing academic and physical domains separately, we were able to detect notable contrasts: Extraversion’s relevance was primarily in the physical domain, whereas Neuroticism and Psychoticism exerted pervasive negative influences across both domains. This finding carries practical implications for interventions: strategies to promote self-determined engagement among students should prioritize reducing anxiety and enhancing stable emotion regulation (targeting Neuroticism-related processes) and should attend to interpersonal skills and prosocial behavior (targeting Psychoticism-related processes), while leveraging extraversion's strengths in contexts where social stimulation and group activities can enhance motivation.

A key theoretical contribution of the present study is the empirical linking of trait-level dispositions to SDT’s proximal mechanisms of needs satisfaction. The mediation analyses showed that a significant portion of the association between personality traits and intrinsic motivation operates through basic psychological needs, consistent with SDT’s conjecture that social-contextual factors that support competence, autonomy, and relatedness foster intrinsic motivation. Importantly, our results suggest that personality traits shape the likelihood of experiencing needs support in situ. Neuroticism, for example, appears to increase susceptibility to experiences that thwart competence and autonomy, possibly by biasing interpretation of feedback toward negative appraisals and by inhibiting approach motivations that facilitate mastery. Psychoticism appears to reduce the capacity for building supportive social relationships that satisfy relatedness needs. By demonstrating these pathways in a student sample, the study shows how trait dispositions and SDT-based mechanisms can be integrated in a process model of motivation.

The practical implications for education and health promotion are multifold. Educators and student support services may consider screening or assessing for elevated levels of neuroticism and psychoticism as part of broader well-being and engagement initiatives. Importantly, such assessments should be used sensitively and ethically, with the goal of identifying students who might benefit from targeted supports rather than stigmatizing individuals. Interventions that cultivate emotion regulation skills (e.g., cognitive reappraisal training, mindfulness, stress management programs) could help students high in Neuroticism to experience greater needs satisfaction and to engage more fully in intrinsically motivating activities. Programs that foster social skills, conflict resolution, and cooperative learning may help ameliorate the interpersonal consequences of higher Psychoticism and thereby enhance relatedness and motivation. In physical activity promotion, creating opportunities for social engagement and stimulation may particularly benefit extraverted students, while providing judgment-free environments and competence-building feedback may be especially important for students prone to neurotic reactions.

While the present study offers several strengths, including a relatively large student sample, domain-specific measurement of intrinsic motivation and needs satisfaction, and mediation testing, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes strong causal inferences; although mediation analyses can be informative about potential mechanisms, they cannot establish temporal precedence. Longitudinal or experimental designs would be necessary to confirm that personality traits lead to changes in needs satisfaction, which in turn lead to changes in intrinsic motivation. Second, the reliance on self-report measures raises the possibility of shared method variance inflating associations and introduces concerns about response biases. Multi-method approaches that include behavioral indicators of engagement, peer or instructor reports, and objective performance metrics would strengthen causal claims and reduce single-method bias. Third, the sample was drawn from a single university and was relatively homogenous in age and education level, which limits generalizability to broader populations, including older adults, non-student samples, or culturally diverse contexts. Fourth, the psychoticism scale in Eysenckian measures has historically exhibited lower internal consistency than other trait scales, and although its reliability in the present sample was acceptable, future research using alternative measures of antagonistic or callous traits might yield complementary insights.

Several avenues for future research arise directly from the present findings. Longitudinal studies that track students across semesters could test whether trait-related differences in needs satisfaction predict trajectories of intrinsic motivation and academic or health outcomes. Intervention studies could experimentally manipulate needs-supportive elements of contexts (e.g., providing autonomy-supportive instruction, competence-affirming feedback, or facilitated social connections) to test whether such modifications attenuate the negative effects of Neuroticism and Psychoticism on intrinsic motivation. Additionally, investigations could examine whether trait-level interventions—such as targeted training in emotion regulation for high-neuroticism individuals—lead to durable increases in needs satisfaction and intrinsic motivation. Research could also explore whether the domain-specificity of extraversion’s relation to intrinsic motivation holds across other domains, such as creative pursuits, music, or volunteer activities, and whether variations in the structure of activities (solo vs. group, immediate vs. delayed feedback) moderate these associations.

It would also be informative to integrate broader personality models, such as the Five-Factor Model, into this line of research to explore whether facets like Conscientiousness and Openness to Experience additionally contribute to intrinsic motivation and interact with Neuroticism and Extraversion. For instance, Conscientiousness may promote sustained effort and goal-directed behavior that support competence, whereas Openness may foster curiosity and intrinsic engagement in learning. Inclusion of neural and physiological measures could further elucidate the mechanisms by which trait dispositions influence motivational processes, for example by assessing stress reactivity or reward sensitivity as mediators.

Finally, it is important to emphasize ethical and developmental considerations when interpreting findings that suggest certain traits are “important to encourage in children” as the abstract language of the initial prompt implied. Personality traits are partially heritable and relatively stable across development, and interventions should focus on fostering adaptive skills and supportive environments rather than attempting to change core personality dispositions. Programs that support emotion regulation, social skills, and environments that promote autonomy, competence, and relatedness are likely to yield benefits for intrinsic motivation without aiming to alter personality traits per se. Encouraging environments that reduce unnecessary stressors, provide constructive feedback, and offer meaningful choice can allow students with a range of dispositional profiles to thrive.

In conclusion, the present study advances understanding of the interplay between personality and self-determined motivation among students by showing that Neuroticism and Psychoticism are meaningful negative predictors of intrinsic motivation across academic and physical domains, that Extraversion’s relevance is modest and domain-specific, and that basic psychological needs satisfaction partially mediates these trait–motivation relations. These findings underscore the importance of addressing affective stability and interpersonal functioning in efforts to support student engagement and well-being, and they suggest fruitful directions for longitudinal, intervention, and multi-method research that can translate theoretical insights into practice.
