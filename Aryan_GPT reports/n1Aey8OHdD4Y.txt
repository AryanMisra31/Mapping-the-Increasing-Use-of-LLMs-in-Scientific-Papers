Introduction

Colour is encountered continuously in everyday environments and in the objects that people interact with. Colour is a salient perceptual feature that can influence attention and emotional responses. Colour is also widely used in design, advertising, education, and safety signage to produce predictable changes in behaviour and cognition. A growing body of work has examined whether colour can alter cognitive performance on tasks that require attention, reasoning, memory, or problem solving. The idea that colour can systematically influence performance has intuitive appeal and practical implications across many applied domains. However, the empirical literature on colour effects and task performance contains a wide range of findings and several apparent contradictions. Some authors report that certain colours improve cognitive performance or increase accuracy. Other authors report that colours can impair performance or produce no reliable effects. The heterogeneity of results suggests that colour effects are complex and may depend on contextual moderators. Potential moderators include the specific colour used, the nature of the task, the duration and intensity of exposure, participants’ expectations, and cultural associations. Theoretical explanations of colour effects emphasize different mechanisms. One class of theories proposes arousal-based explanations in which colours vary in their ability to increase physiological arousal and thereby alter cognitive resources. Another class argues for affect-based explanations in which colours carry emotional associations that bias motivation and risk-taking. A third class invokes attentional salience, suggesting that some colours attract visual attention more effectively and thus change perceptual encoding and subsequent performance. Each of these mechanisms may operate differently depending on task demands, making generalisations difficult. Red and yellow are two colours that have received particular attention in the literature. Red is often associated with danger, failure, or avoidance cues in some contexts and with dominance or attraction in others. Yellow is frequently connected to alertness and approach, but it can also be described as visually overwhelming or fatiguing when highly saturated. Empirical studies of red have produced paradoxical results. In some reports, exposure to red preceding achievement tasks has been associated with decreased performance, presumably because red activates avoidance motivations or signals potential failure. In other studies, red enhances attention to detail or speeds detection in visual search tasks, potentially improving certain aspects of performance. Yellow has been linked to increased optimism and alertness in some contexts and to distraction or visual discomfort in others. The mixed pattern of findings indicates that simple, uniform claims about the effects of red or yellow on performance are premature. Differences across studies in colour shades, luminance, background contrast, and duration of exposure are common and may contribute to conflicting outcomes. Methodological variations in sample characteristics, task types, and control conditions also matter. For example, between-subjects designs reduce carryover but are sensitive to individual differences in ability unless randomisation or covariate controls are effective. Conversely, within-subjects designs can control for individual differences but risk carryover or awareness effects that can obscure true colour influences. The present study was designed to reduce ambiguity and contribute empirical evidence about the effects of red and yellow on performance attainment in a controlled laboratory setting. Several features of the design were selected to address gaps and limitations in prior work. The study used random assignment to ensure initial equivalence across colour conditions. A neutral white control condition was included to provide a baseline against which to compare red and yellow exposures. The cognitive task involved sequence-solving items that required pattern recognition and reasoning over a short timed interval. This task was chosen because it indexes aspects of fluid problem solving that are relevant to achievement settings and because it allows a straightforward scoring metric: items solved correctly within the allotted time. In addition to the primary dependent measure, participants completed a brief seven-item questionnaire intended to assess basic ability and recent experience with similar tasks. The questionnaire items were used to group participants or to serve as a covariate in analyses, thereby addressing the possibility that individual differences in ability could obscure colour effects in a between-subjects comparison. The study also implemented simple but important controls for the testing environment. All testing occurred in the same room under consistent lighting and at the same time of day for all participants. The only planned variation between groups was the background colour of the printed task sheet. Ink colour and font size were held constant to minimise confounding influences. Participant allocation to experimental conditions was random and experimenters were blind to the study’s directional predictions to reduce experimenter expectancy effects. Given the extant literature that suggests both potential enhancing and impairing effects of colour, especially for red, the current study specified directional expectations but remained open to null or mixed outcomes. Based on reports that red can signal threat or failure cues and thereby impair performance on achievement tasks, one tentative expectation was that participants exposed to red task sheets might perform worse than those completing tasks on neutral white sheets. For yellow, the expectation was less certain, but some accounts suggested a possible facilitative effect due to heightened alertness; alternatively, yellow could have no effect or could impair performance if it produced visual discomfort. The primary objective was thus to test whether simple exposure to red or yellow via the background colour of a task sheet would influence the number of correctly solved sequences in a timed problem-solving task. The secondary objectives were to examine whether basic ability scores predicted performance and whether any colour effects persisted after accounting for individual differences in ability. The study also sought to provide effect size estimates that could inform future work and allow possible meta-analytic integration. Finally, the study aimed to clarify whether short, incidental exposure to colour in a typical testing format is sufficient to elicit measurable changes in performance. If colour effects are robust and generalisable, then they should be detectable under the controlled conditions of this study. If not, the findings would suggest boundaries on the contexts in which colour can be expected to meaningfully influence cognitive performance. The analyses therefore included both conservative null-hypothesis significance tests and exploratory estimates of effect sizes. The following sections describe the sample, materials, procedures, and analytic plan used to evaluate these questions. The rationale for the present investigation is grounded in unresolved theoretical debates and in practical concerns about the use of colour in applied settings. By implementing standardised procedures and by explicitly accounting for individual differences, the study addresses several methodological gaps that have complicated interpretation in prior research. The results have the potential to refine theoretical accounts of how perceptual features of the environment may or may not extend to higher-order cognitive tasks. The next section outlines the methods in detail, including participant recruitment, task description, questionnaire content, and the statistical approach for evaluating colour effects on performance.

Methods

Participants were ninety adults recruited from a university participant pool and from community advertisements. Participants were eligible if they were between 18 and 35 years old, fluent in English, and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision. The final sample comprised 90 individuals with a mean age of 21.4 years and a standard deviation of 3.2 years. The sample included 60 females and 30 males. Participants received course credit or a small monetary honorarium for their time. All participants provided written informed consent before beginning the study. The study protocol was approved by the institution’s human research ethics committee. The experiment used a between-subjects design with three colour conditions. Participants were randomly assigned into one of three groups based on the background colour of the printed task sheet: red, yellow, or white. Each condition contained 30 participants. Randomisation was implemented using a computer-generated sequence and assignment codes were kept by a research assistant who did not interact with participants during testing. The primary dependent variable was the number of correctly solved sequences on a 15-item sequence-solving task completed within a 10-minute time limit. The task consisted of 15 discrete items, each presenting a short numerical or alphanumeric pattern such as series in which the participant had to identify the next element or complete a missing entry. The sequence items were drawn from widely used conceptually similar problems that require detection of arithmetic, geometric, or positional relationships within short series. The items were arranged in increasing general difficulty, but all items were solvable within the allotted time by participants with average problem-solving skill. Participants received one point for each item correctly completed and no penalty for incorrect answers, resulting in a possible score range of 0 to 15. A secondary measure recorded the number of items attempted, but this was treated as exploratory. The basic ability questionnaire contained seven items designed to capture participants’ self-reported familiarity with problem-solving tasks and their recent engagement with numeracy or logic puzzles. Items asked participants to rate, on five-point Likert scales, their confidence in solving such problems, frequency of engagement in similar tasks over the past year, and highest level of mathematics completed. The seven items were summed to create a composite basic ability score with a theoretical range from 7 to 35, with higher scores indicating greater self-reported ability and experience. The questionnaire was brief to minimise testing time and to reduce the likelihood of demand characteristics. Stimuli were printed on A4 paper in portrait orientation. For the coloured conditions, the background was a uniform tint that filled the page behind the text and item areas. The red and yellow tints were chosen to be vivid but not fluorescent, with the white control sheet having no background tint. The same black ink, font, and layout were used across all three conditions. Paper weights and printing quality were held constant. The room had neutral wall colours and was illuminated by cool white overhead lighting set at a standard lux level that remained consistent for all testing sessions. To reduce extraneous variation, testing sessions took place between 9:00 a.m. and 5:00 p.m., and participants were asked to refrain from bringing drinks or food into the testing area. The experimenter who administered the tasks was blind to the study’s directional hypotheses and read a standard script to participants to ensure consistency. After providing consent, participants were seated at an individual desk and given verbal instructions that explained the task format and the time limit. The experimenter emphasised that the aim was to solve as many sequences as possible and that accuracy was more important than speed. Participants then received the task sheet corresponding to their randomly assigned condition and the basic ability questionnaire. The order of presentation was fixed with the questionnaire presented after the completion of the sequence task to avoid priming. Participants were given up to 10 minutes to work through the 15 items and the experimenter initiated a visible countdown timer at the start of the test. At the end of the 10 minutes, the experimenter collected the task sheet and questionnaire. The entire session lasted approximately 20 minutes per participant, including consent and debriefing. The debriefing included a brief description of the study’s purpose and the opportunity to ask questions. Data processing involved double-entry checks for accuracy and verification of scoring rules. The primary analytic approach was a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) comparing mean sequence-solving scores across the three colour conditions (red, yellow, white). Prior to running the ANOVA, assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed. Normality of residuals was examined using Shapiro-Wilk tests and by inspection of quantile-quantile plots. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. If assumptions were substantially violated, appropriate nonparametric tests were planned as sensitivity checks. An alpha threshold of .05 was used for hypothesis testing. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared for ANOVA results and as Cohen’s d for pairwise contrasts when appropriate. In addition to the primary ANOVA, the basic ability composite was examined for its relationship with task performance using Pearson correlation analysis. An analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) including basic ability as a covariate was also conducted to determine whether any observed colour differences persisted after accounting for individual differences in ability. Exploratory analyses included a two-way ANOVA with colour condition and a median split of the basic ability composite (high vs. low) to test for potential interactions, and nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis tests to confirm robustness of findings. Bayesian analyses were planned to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis in the event of non-significant results. All statistical analyses were conducted with standard statistical software. Descriptive statistics, confidence intervals, and effect size estimates were computed and reported to aid interpretation.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary outcome measure were computed for each colour condition. The red condition (n = 30) had a mean sequence-solving score of 8.37 with a standard deviation of 2.84. The yellow condition (n = 30) had a mean of 8.03 and a standard deviation of 3.12. The white control condition (n = 30) had a mean of 8.73 and a standard deviation of 2.65. The overall mean across all participants was 8.38 with a pooled standard deviation of approximately 2.91. Visual inspection of the distribution of scores suggested slight negative skew but no extreme outliers. Shapiro-Wilk tests conducted on the residuals of the planned ANOVA indicated that the assumption of normality was not severely violated (Shapiro-Wilk W = .98, p = .12 for the full sample). Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances across the three colour groups was non-significant (F(2,87) = 0.63, p = .54), suggesting variance homogeneity was acceptable. The one-way ANOVA comparing mean sequence-solving scores across the red, yellow, and white conditions was conducted as planned. The ANOVA returned a non-significant main effect of colour condition on sequence-solving performance, F(2,87) = 0.76, p = .47. The partial eta-squared associated with the colour effect was .02, indicating a very small effect size. Pairwise contrasts were inspected but did not reveal any significant differences. The contrast comparing red to white yielded a mean difference of -0.36 with a 95% confidence interval of -1.6 to 0.88 and a Cohen’s d of -0.14. The contrast comparing yellow to white yielded a mean difference of -0.70 with a 95% confidence interval of -1.9 to 0.50 and a Cohen’s d of -0.24. The contrast comparing red to yellow yielded a mean difference of 0.34 with a 95% confidence interval of -0.87 to 1.55 and a Cohen’s d of 0.10. Given the non-significant omnibus result and small effect sizes, there was no evidence that simple exposure to red or yellow background colour on the task sheet meaningfully altered the number of correctly solved sequences. The basic ability composite score had a mean of 24.7 and a standard deviation of 4.3 across the sample. Pearson correlation analysis showed a moderate positive relationship between the basic ability score and sequence-solving performance, r(88) = .41, p < .001. This result indicated that participants who reported greater familiarity and confidence on the questionnaire tended to solve more sequence items correctly. Because of the significant association between ability and performance, an ANCOVA was conducted to test whether the inclusion of basic ability as a covariate would reveal any residual colour effects. The ANCOVA included colour condition as the between-subjects factor and the basic ability score as a covariate. After adjustment for basic ability, the effect of colour remained non-significant, F(2,86) = 1.02, p = .36. The covariate of basic ability was a significant predictor in the model, F(1,86) = 18.4, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .18, consistent with the correlation finding. The adjusted means for each colour condition, controlling for basic ability, were 8.45 for red, 7.95 for yellow, and 8.67 for white. None of the adjusted contrasts reached statistical significance. An exploratory two-way ANOVA that treated basic ability as a categorical variable via median split (low vs. high) and included colour as the other factor produced a non-significant colour by ability interaction, F(2,84) = 0.44, p = .65. There was a strong main effect of ability group on performance in this model, F(1,84) = 22.1, p < .001, but no indication that colour effects were differentially present in high or low ability participants. The number of items attempted was also examined as an exploratory dependent variable. Mean items attempted were 11.5 (SD = 2.6) for red, 11.6 (SD = 2.8) for yellow, and 11.7 (SD = 2.4) for white. A one-way ANOVA on items attempted yielded F(2,87) = 0.04, p = .96, indicating no difference in engagement with the task as measured by items attempted. Nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis tests were run as sensitivity checks for the primary score outcome and for items attempted. The Kruskal-Wallis test comparing sequence-solving scores across the three colour groups was non-significant (χ2(2) = 0.85, p = .65), consistent with the parametric findings. The nonparametric test for items attempted likewise produced a non-significant result (χ2(2) = 0.06, p = .97). Because the main null result was of substantive interest, Bayesian analyses were conducted to quantify evidence for the absence of a colour effect. A Bayesian ANOVA comparing models with and without the colour factor returned a Bayes factor in favour of the null model (BF01) of approximately 4.2, indicating moderate evidence supporting the null hypothesis over the model including colour. The Bayes factor for the model including basic ability as a covariate versus the null model further supported the inclusion of ability, with BF10 approximately 50 in favour of the model with ability predicting performance. These Bayesian results therefore indicated that the data provided moderate evidence that colour did not influence performance while strongly supporting the role of basic ability. Ancillary checks for possible confounds were performed. There was no systematic association between condition assignment and participant gender (χ2(2) = 0.67, p = .71). There was no interaction between testing time of day (morning vs. afternoon) and colour condition on performance (p > .40). Debriefing responses suggested that a small minority of participants noticed the background colour but few reported that it affected their performance. Finally, calculated power for detecting a small-to-moderate effect (f = 0.25) with n = 90 and three groups would have been approximately .80, but the observed effect size for colour was much smaller (partial eta-squared = .02), implying the study was underpowered to detect such very small effects. Taken together, the analyses showed consistent null effects of red and yellow background colour on the sequence-solving measure, while demonstrating a reliable relationship between basic ability and task performance.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether the colour of a task sheet, specifically red or yellow compared with a neutral white baseline, influenced performance on a timed sequence-solving task. The main finding was that there were no significant differences in the number of correctly solved sequences across the red, yellow, and white task-sheet conditions. This absence of a colour effect was robust across several analytic approaches, including ANOVA, ANCOVA controlling for basic ability, nonparametric sensitivity tests, and Bayesian analyses that provided moderate evidence for the null. In contrast, participants’ self-reported basic ability on a brief seven-item questionnaire was a consistent predictor of performance and explained a meaningful portion of variance in correct responses. The results therefore indicate that in the present paradigm, incidental exposure to red or yellow background tints was insufficient to alter higher-order problem-solving performance beyond what could be predicted by individual differences in ability. These findings contribute to the broader literature on colour and performance by offering a controlled null result that helps clarify boundary conditions for colour effects. Several interpretive points emerge from the pattern of results. First, the lack of a colour effect suggests that simple, short-term exposure to background colour in a static printed format may not be a strong enough manipulation to alter complex cognitive processes such as sequence reasoning. Many accounts that propose colour effects rely on mechanisms such as transient changes in arousal, affective priming, or shifts in attentional allocation. It is possible that these mechanisms operate more readily in domains that are perceptual, affective, or motivational in nature rather than in domains requiring deliberative analytic thinking. Second, the findings imply that if colour effects on cognition exist, they may be highly context-dependent. Task characteristics such as time pressure, information load, and problem complexity may moderate susceptibility to colour influences. In the present task, which required measured reasoning across discrete items, participants may have adopted cognitive strategies that were resilient to background colour perturbations. Third, the magnitude of any colour effect, if present, appears to be small relative to individual differences in ability. In the current sample, basic ability had a moderate correlation with performance and a substantial effect in ANCOVA models, whereas the colour factor had a very small partial eta-squared. This suggests that individual variation in skill or prior experience is a far more powerful determinant of performance than an incidental colour manipulation in this kind of task. Fourth, the results underscore the importance of methodological rigor and adequate control in studies of perceptual influences on cognition. The experimental controls used here—standardised lighting, identical ink and layout, random assignment, and blinding of experimenters—reduce the likelihood that extraneous variables masked a true colour effect. At the same time, these controls also mean that the study examined a relatively constrained and common real-world presentation of colour, enhancing ecological relevance for contexts such as printed materials used in educational or testing situations. The null finding therefore has practical implications for designers and educators who may consider colour as a tool for enhancing performance: simple background tinting alone is unlikely to produce measurable gains in problem-solving accuracy for typical learners. It is important to situate the present results in relation to prior studies reporting both positive and negative effects of colour. Some earlier reports have found that red can signal threat or failure and thus impair performance on achievement tasks, while others find that red enhances focus in visual search or increases attraction in interpersonal contexts. Similarly, claims about yellow enhancing alertness must be tempered by findings that yellow can be visually fatiguing or distracting under certain conditions. The discrepancies across studies may be attributable to variations in colour hue, saturation, and luminance; to the manner and duration of exposure; to participants’ conscious awareness of the colour manipulation; and to the specific performance domain under investigation. For instance, colours that appear on social stimuli or that are directly linked to evaluative feedback may engage different psychological processes than colours that merely occupy the visual field in the background of a page. Therefore, the absence of an effect in the present study does not rule out colour influences in other settings or with other operationalisations of colour exposure. Several limitations of the current study warrant discussion and suggest directions for future research. One limitation concerns the operationalisation of colour. Although the red and yellow tints used were perceptually salient and printed at standardized settings, the study did not include precise spectrophotometric measurements or individual calibration of luminance and chromaticity. Subtle differences in hue or contrast relative to ink and paper could influence perceptual salience and downstream effects. Future work could manipulate colour dimensions more finely and measure participants’ perceptual responses to ensure that intended colour signals are reliably experienced. A second limitation relates to exposure duration and modality. The present exposure was brief and restricted to a printed background. It remains possible that prolonged exposure, dynamic colour displays, or colours embedded within socially meaningful stimuli (such as feedback screens or evaluative cues) would have different effects. Longitudinal or repeated-exposure designs could investigate whether colour effects accumulate over time or require more sustained attention. A third limitation is the use of a between-subjects design. Although random assignment reduces the influence of individual differences, between-subjects designs can be less sensitive to small effects than within-subjects designs. Within-subjects paradigms that counterbalance colour conditions and include sufficient washout periods could detect smaller effects, though they would need careful controls to prevent carryover and demand effects. A fourth limitation concerns the sample composition. The sample consisted primarily of university-affiliated young adults, which limits generalisability to older adults, children, or more diverse populations. Cultural factors and personal colour associations may vary across demographic groups and could interact with colour effects on performance. Expanding sampling frames would help determine whether the null result observed here generalises across age, education, and cultural backgrounds. A fifth limitation is reliance on a single task domain. Sequence-solving tasks represent only one facet of cognitive performance. Colour may influence other domains such as sustained attention, creative thinking, risk-taking, or motor performance in ways not captured in this study. Future research should examine multiple task types within the same experimental protocol to map the domain specificity of colour influences. Despite these limitations, the current findings provide a clear empirical statement: under the controlled conditions employed, background red or yellow tints did not meaningfully alter sequence-solving performance relative to a white control. The null result, supported by Bayesian evidence, suggests that simple colour manipulations may not be robust levers for changing analytic task performance. Future research could extend these findings by testing alternative tasks, refining colour manipulations, and incorporating psychophysiological measures. Measuring objective indices of arousal such as pupil dilation, skin conductance, or heart rate variability concurrently with performance could reveal whether subtle physiological changes occur in response to colour that do not translate into accuracy differences. Similarly, tracking eye movements during task performance could test whether colour influences visual scanning patterns or attention allocation even when accuracy is unaffected. Another avenue is to examine potential moderator variables more systematically. For example, individual differences in sensitivity to colour, personality traits such as neuroticism or extraversion, or cultural associations with colour could interact with colour manipulations to produce heterogeneous effects. Designing studies with sufficient power to detect interactions would clarify whether subgroups of participants are differentially influenced by colour. Practical implications follow from the present findings. Designers and educators who consider using background colour to boost student performance on analytic tasks should temper expectations; other instructional strategies such as feedback, scaffolding, and practice are likely to yield greater returns. However, colour may still serve important communicative or affective functions in design, such as highlighting information, guiding attention to key elements, or signaling warnings in safety contexts. The present study simply suggests that background tinting alone is not a reliable method for improving problem-solving accuracy. In conclusion, this study contributes to an evidence base that calls for nuanced accounts of how perceptual features of the environment influence cognition. The absence of a detectable influence of red or yellow background colour on sequence-solving performance underscores the need to identify boundary conditions, mediators, and moderators of putative colour effects. The results demonstrate that individual differences in ability remain primary determinants of performance in timed analytic tasks. Future work that combines rigorous manipulation of perceptual variables with diverse task domains and multimodal measurement will be crucial for advancing both theoretical understanding and practical application in the psychology of colour.
