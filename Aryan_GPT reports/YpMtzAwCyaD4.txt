Introduction

Understanding how people reason about object motion has long been a central question in cognitive psychology and science education. Everyday reasoning about moving objects often departs from the formal principles of classical mechanics, and these departures have been documented across many populations and age groups. Early and influential work demonstrated systematic misconceptions about trajectories, forces, and motion persistence, suggesting that naive intuitions often conflict with Newtonian laws. At the same time, research has shown that formal instruction in physics can change these intuitions, at least insofar as measured performance on explicit tasks and problems. The tension between persistent naive beliefs and trained knowledge raises important questions about how prior information, task context, and top-down processing interact to produce moment-to-moment performance in motion reasoning tasks. One influential line of work by McCloskey, Caramazza, and Green provided evidence that prior knowledge of physics reduces misconceptions about object motion in specific problem formats. Their study used four canonical problems in which participants were asked to draw the path of a ball as it moves through a curved tube or is released from a rotating string, and they found that individuals with greater prior physics knowledge were less likely to produce erroneous curved continuations and more likely to predict the correct inertial or tangential trajectories. Those results have been cited as evidence for the beneficial role of domain knowledge in overcoming intuitive error patterns. However, that original work relied primarily on participants’ preexisting differences in physics knowledge and did not manipulate contextual information at the time of testing. At the same time, converging research in perception and cognition has emphasized the importance of context and top-down processing in shaping performance across a wide range of tasks. Contextual cues can activate relevant schemas, guide attention to diagnostic features of stimuli, and constrain possible interpretations in ways that facilitate correct responses. Thus, an important question emerges: to what extent does providing explicit context or top-down cues at the time of testing improve performance above and beyond the effects of prior knowledge? The present study was motivated by the desire to integrate these perspectives by directly testing whether the provision of explicit contextual information—specifically, a concise statement of Newton’s laws—would improve participants’ performance on the classical tube-and-string motion tasks originally used by McCloskey and colleagues. A second motivation was to probe task properties that might interact with both prior knowledge and contextual cues. In particular, curvature of trajectories is a salient perceptual feature that may increase the cognitive demands of accurately predicting a path. If curvature complicates the visual transformation required to infer post-release motion, then tasks with greater curvature might be more resistant to both prior knowledge and contextual prompting. To address this possibility, the current study replicated McCloskey et al.’s four original problems and added two additional problems, one of which featured a notably reduced curvature to provide an empirical test of whether curvature serves as a hindering factor. A further consideration concerns the mechanism by which context operates. Prior work suggests that context can narrow attentional focus and activate task-relevant knowledge in a top-down manner. If explicit context invokes a Newtonian framework, this may prompt participants to consider forces and inertia at the moment of response, thereby altering the trajectory predictions they produce. It is therefore of theoretical interest to examine whether brief exposure to formal principles just before task performance changes response patterns and whether that change is additive to, interactive with, or redundant with the effects of prior domain knowledge. In addition, the original McCloskey et al. study did not vary the specificity or format of contextual cues; we elected to use a concise prose statement of Newton’s laws as a tractable and ecologically valid manipulation that mirrors the kind of brief reminders a student might receive in a classroom or study setting. The present experiment thus allows us to probe whether even minimal context can have measurable effects. Methodologically, tasks that require participants to draw paths present several measurement choices, including dichotomous scoring of correct versus incorrect predictions and continuous measurement of deviation from the physically correct trajectory. Both types of measures can be informative, and so our study included a primary binary correctness coding to facilitate comparisons with prior work and supplementary continuous measures of deviation to increase sensitivity. Interrater reliability is essential when using drawn responses, and we therefore adopted a rigorous double-coding procedure with adjudication to ensure scoring consistency. Another methodological decision involved assessing prior physics knowledge. Rather than relying solely on self-report, we combined a self-reported level of physics coursework with a brief objective assessment to classify participants into novice, intermediate, and advanced groups. This approach provides greater confidence in the construct validity of the knowledge measure and allows us to examine whether a graded knowledge scale modulates the benefits of contextual cues. The current study, therefore, set out to answer three interrelated questions. First, does providing explicit context in the form of a short, readable summary of Newton’s laws immediately prior to task performance improve participants’ trajectory predictions compared with providing no such context? Second, does prior physics knowledge predict performance on the replicated and extended set of problems, thereby replicating McCloskey et al.’s finding that knowledge reduces misconceptions? Third, is curvature a hindering factor, such that problems with greater curvature produce lower accuracy and, potentially, less benefit from contextual prompting? We predicted that explicit context would improve performance overall, but that the magnitude of this improvement would vary across problems depending on how diagnostic the context was for the specific inference required. We further predicted that advanced physics knowledge would be associated with higher accuracy across problems, replicating the McCloskey et al. result. Finally, we expected that high curvature would make problems more difficult and would reduce the efficacy of both prior knowledge and contextual prompts, though we anticipated this curvature effect might be modest given the strong influence of explicit knowledge on motion reasoning. The experimental design therefore contrasted two between-subjects conditions (context vs no context) crossed with three levels of prior knowledge (novice, intermediate, advanced) and tested each participant on six within-subject problems, four from the McCloskey et al. battery and two new additions. Our primary dependent variable was a binary correctness score for each drawn path, supplemented by a continuous deviation measure to capture partial knowledge and gradations in accuracy. We analyzed the data using mixed-effects logistic regression to account for repeated measures and to test for interactions among context, knowledge level, problem, and curvature. Additional analyses examined simple effects at the problem level and continuous deviation scores. We paid particular attention to whether context effects were uniform or concentrated in specific problems, consistent with the pattern described in the preliminary summary of results. The study was designed to be sensitive to small-to-moderate effects via an a priori power analysis and to yield reliable scoring through double coding. In sum, this project sought to extend and refine the empirical understanding of how prior knowledge and top-down contextual cues jointly shape predictions about object motion, to replicate key findings from McCloskey et al., and to probe the role of task curvature as a potential moderating factor. By integrating these concerns in a single systematic design, the study contributes both to theoretical debates about the interaction of top-down and bottom-up processes and to practical questions about how best to support accurate reasoning in educational contexts.

Methods

Participants were recruited from an undergraduate subject pool and from an online research participation platform to provide a mixed sample of individuals with varied backgrounds in physics. In total, 120 participants completed the study and provided usable data, with all participants giving informed consent prior to testing. The sample included adults between 18 and 35 years of age, with a mean age of 21.8 years and a standard deviation of 3.4 years. The gender composition of the sample was approximately balanced, with 58 participants identifying as female, 60 as male, and 2 as nonbinary; these proportions allowed for exploratory analyses of sex differences though the study was not primarily powered for that purpose. Participants received course credit or modest monetary compensation for their time. Prior physics knowledge was assessed using a two-pronged approach. First, participants reported the highest level of formal physics coursework they had completed (none, high school introductory, college introductory, advanced undergraduate). Second, they completed a brief five-item objective test probing core concepts such as inertia, normal force, and centripetal vs tangential motion. Scores on the objective test correlated strongly with self-reported coursework level, r = .72, indicating convergent validity. Based on the combined indicators, participants were classified into three knowledge groups: novice (n = 40), intermediate (n = 40), and advanced (n = 40). Assignment to context condition was randomized within each knowledge group, such that each of the six resulting cells contained 20 participants. Randomization was performed using a computerized random number generator and maintained blinding of experimenters to condition assignment until data collection was complete. The experimental materials comprised six problems presented as black-and-white line drawings on standardized digital stimulus pages. Problems 1–4 replicated the stimuli used by McCloskey et al., including two curved tube problems and two spinning-string problems that require participants to indicate the post-release path of a ball. Problem 5 was a new addition that paralleled one of the tube items but featured a markedly reduced curvature to test the curvature hypothesis; problem 6 was another new item that varied the orientation of a spinning string release to increase stimulus diversity. Stimuli were presented on a computer screen using experiment presentation software, and participants provided responses by drawing directly on a touchscreen using a stylus or, for those completing the experiment remotely, by drawing on a tablet and uploading an image. To manipulate context, participants in the context condition received a concise two-paragraph summary of the three Newtonian laws and a short explanation of tangential motion and inertia immediately before the problem set. The summary was intentionally brief (approximately 150 words in total) and written in accessible language to simulate a realistic brief reminder rather than an extended tutorial. Participants in the no-context condition received an unrelated two-paragraph neutral text of equal length about a non-scientific topic (e.g., the process of baking bread) to control for time and reading. The experimenter confirmed that participants read the passage by asking one simple factual question about the text before proceeding. All participants then completed the six problem drawings in the same fixed order to maintain comparability with the original McCloskey et al. procedure. Each drawing task displayed the scene with the ball in place and provided a blank area for participants to draw the continued path. Participants were instructed to draw the path they thought the ball would take after it left the tube or was released from the string and to be as precise as possible. They were told there was no time limit but that most people finished within 10–15 minutes. Responses were collected and digitized where necessary for subsequent coding. Scoring involved two independent raters who were blind to the participants’ condition and knowledge classification. Each drawn path was coded dichotomously as correct (1) or incorrect (0) according to a predefined scoring rubric derived from standard physics predictions: for tube-release problems the correct path was straight and tangent to the exit point, and for spinning-string releases the correct path was straight and tangential at the moment of release unless the problem explicitly included other forces or constraints. The rubric also specified acceptable deviations that would still be coded as correct (e.g., slight line curvature due to drawing error), and these rules were pre-registered prior to coding. Interrater reliability was high, with Cohen’s kappa = .86 for the binary correctness coding. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and, if necessary, by a third rater who provided final adjudication. In addition to the binary coding, we computed a continuous deviation score for each drawing by measuring, in millimeters on the digitized image, the perpendicular distance between the participant’s drawn path at a fixed downstream point and the physically correct tangential trajectory. This continuous score provided a graded measure of accuracy and allowed detection of partial knowledge. The continuous measure was also double-coded to ensure measurement reliability, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of .93. Demographic variables and brief attentional checks were collected at the end of the session. The study protocol and analysis plan were pre-registered on an institutional repository before data collection began. Statistical analyses were conducted in two complementary frameworks. For the primary analyses we used mixed-effects logistic regression with binary correctness as the dependent variable, fixed effects for context (context vs no-context), knowledge group (three levels, modeled as categorical), problem (six levels, treated as categorical), and curvature (coded as high vs low curvature), as well as their interactions. Random intercepts for participants were included to account for within-subjects repeated measurements, and random slopes for problem were included where model convergence allowed. For secondary analyses, we conducted repeated-measures ANOVAs on proportion-correct aggregated across problems and on the continuous deviation scores, with Greenhouse-Geisser corrections applied when sphericity assumptions were violated. We also ran problem-by-problem chi-square tests and t-tests to examine simple effects and to facilitate comparison with the original McCloskey et al. results. Effect sizes were reported as odds ratios for logistic models and partial eta-squared for ANOVA results. All hypothesis tests were two-tailed, and an alpha of .05 was used to determine statistical significance. Where multiple comparisons were performed, Holm-Bonferroni corrections were applied to control the family-wise error rate. Power analysis performed prior to data collection indicated that a sample of 120 participants would provide approximately 80% power to detect small-to-moderate effects (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.35) for the main effects and interactions of interest in the logistic mixed-effects framework. The data, scoring rubric, and analysis scripts are available on request and will be shared in a public repository upon publication to enable replication and secondary analysis.

Results

Descriptive statistics for overall accuracy and by condition are summarized first. Across all participants and problems, the mean proportion of correct responses was .58 with a standard deviation of .21. In the context condition, the mean proportion correct was .62, whereas in the no-context condition it was .54. By knowledge group, novices averaged .40 correct, intermediates .60 correct, and advanced participants .78 correct. These descriptive patterns suggested potential main effects of both context and prior knowledge. The mixed-effects logistic regression model examining binary correctness included fixed effects for context, knowledge group, problem, and curvature, as well as interactions between context and problem and between knowledge and problem. Random intercepts for participants and random slopes for problem were included. The overall model converged without difficulty and produced interpretable coefficients. A main effect of context emerged in the mixed model. Participants in the context condition were more likely to produce correct trajectories than participants in the no-context condition, with an estimated odds ratio of 1.45 (95% CI [1.02, 2.04]), and a likelihood-ratio test comparing models with and without context yielded χ2(1) = 4.35, p = .037. This indicates that the provision of explicit Newtonian context had a modest but statistically significant beneficial effect on correctness when averaged across all six problems. A strong main effect of knowledge group was also observed. Compared with the novice group, intermediates had higher odds of producing a correct trajectory (OR = 2.25, 95% CI [1.40, 3.61]), and advanced participants had markedly higher odds (OR = 6.10, 95% CI [3.60, 10.33]). The likelihood-ratio test for the knowledge factor was highly significant, χ2(2) = 38.70, p < .001, indicating a robust facilitative effect of prior physics knowledge on task performance. The factor of problem was significant as well, reflecting that accuracy varied substantially across the six items, χ2(5) = 120.12, p < .001. Follow-up contrasts revealed that some problems were intrinsically easier than others; for example, problem 2 had a mean accuracy of .77 across groups, while problem 3 had a mean accuracy of .31. The interaction between context and problem was significant, χ2(5) = 16.20, p = .006, indicating that the effect of context was not uniform across problems. To unpack this interaction, we conducted problem-by-problem logistic regressions and chi-square comparisons. These simple effects revealed that context significantly improved performance on problem 1 (context correct = .75 vs no-context = .55; χ2(1) = 6.41, p = .011) and problem 4 (context correct = .70 vs no-context = .48; χ2(1) = 7.11, p = .008). After Holm-Bonferroni correction for six comparisons, both effects remained significant. For the remaining problems (2, 3, 5, and 6), context differences were small and non-significant (all p-values > .10). The benefit of context therefore appeared localized to specific items rather than general across the entire problem set. We next examined whether curvature acted as a hindering factor. Curvature was coded as high for the four original curved-tube problems and as low for the two added problems that had less pronounced curvature or linear orientations. In the mixed model, the main effect of curvature (high vs low) was not statistically significant at conventional thresholds, β = -0.15, SE = 0.09, χ2(1) = 2.90, p = .089, suggesting only a trend toward lower accuracy on high-curvature items. Problem-level comparisons provided additional nuance: the less-curved problem 5 showed higher mean accuracy (.64) than its high-curvature counterpart problem 1 in the no-context condition (.55), but in the context condition both were above .70. The interaction between curvature and context was non-significant, χ2(1) = 1.12, p = .29, indicating that the small trend for curvature did not significantly moderate the contextual benefit. We also tested for an interaction between knowledge group and context in the mixed model to see if contextual prompts differentially aided novices or advanced participants. This interaction was not significant, χ2(2) = 1.34, p = .51, suggesting that the advantage conferred by context was similar across knowledge levels. However, planned contrasts indicated that novices benefited numerically less from context than intermediates and advanced participants; for novices the absolute increase in mean correct with context was .06, for intermediates .07, and for advanced participants .08, though these differences were not statistically reliable. Inspection of cell-level proportions confirmed that advanced participants performed well regardless of context (context .80 vs no-context .76), whereas novices remained poor overall even with context (context .46 vs no-context .40). These patterns suggest that while context provides some incremental benefit, strong prior knowledge remains the dominant determinant of performance. The secondary analyses using continuous deviation scores corroborated the dichotomous findings. A repeated-measures ANOVA on mean deviation with context as a between-subjects factor and problem as a within-subjects factor showed a main effect of context, F(1,118) = 4.21, p = .042, partial η2 = .034, indicating that participants exposed to context produced drawings that were, on average, closer to the correct tangential trajectories. The problem by context interaction was again significant, F(5,590) = 3.12, p = .009, partial η2 = .026, mirroring the binary outcome that context benefits were concentrated in some items. Knowledge group produced a large main effect on deviation scores, F(2,118) = 39.87, p < .001, partial η2 = .403, with advanced participants showing the smallest deviations. Analyses controlling for age and sex did not alter the primary conclusions: context remained a significant predictor and knowledge continued to have a strong effect. Exploratory analyses of sex differences revealed no reliable main effect of sex on correctness, χ2(1) = 1.48, p = .22, but there was a suggestive interaction between sex and problem, χ2(5) = 9.76, p = .083, indicating that sex differences, if present, may be problem-specific. Given that these analyses were underpowered for detecting sex effects, we treat them as exploratory and recommend dedicated follow-up work. Finally, to assess whether the context manipulation was successfully processed by participants, we examined responses to the attention check question about the passage. Ninety-seven percent of participants correctly answered the check in the context condition, indicating that they read the summary. We also compared the time taken on the reading passage between conditions and found no significant differences, suggesting comparable engagement. Taken together, these results indicate that context provided in the form of a short Newtonian summary produced a modest but statistically significant improvement in trajectory predictions overall, that prior physics knowledge strongly predicted performance replicating McCloskey et al., and that curvature did not exert a conclusive hindering effect though there were trends consistent with some influence.

Discussion

The present study addressed how explicit context and prior physics knowledge jointly influence people’s ability to predict object motion in classical tube-and-string problems. We replicated the core finding from McCloskey, Caramazza, and Green that advanced prior physics knowledge is a strong facilitator of correct trajectory predictions. In addition, we found that providing a short, readable summary of Newton’s laws immediately prior to testing produced a modest but reliable improvement in performance overall, and that this contextual benefit was concentrated in two of the six problems we administered. Curvature showed a non-significant trend toward hindering performance, but there was no conclusive evidence that curvature robustly undermined the usefulness of either prior knowledge or contextual prompts. The affirmative replication of the knowledge effect has important implications. McCloskey et al. proposed that domain knowledge provides structural constraints that allow individuals to infer correct relationships (such as the tangential direction at release) even when naive intuitions might suggest otherwise. Our data support this interpretation: advanced participants performed well across the board, showing high proportions of correct responses and low deviations. The fact that advanced participants performed well regardless of context suggests that deep, internalized conceptual representations allow for spontaneous, on-the-fly application of Newtonian reasoning without the need for external reminders. This observation underscores the educational value of foundational physics instruction: robust conceptual learning appears to translate into reliable application in novel problem contexts. The contextual manipulation results are nuanced and theoretically informative. A short Newtonian summary produced a measurable improvement in overall accuracy, but crucially, the improvement was not evenly distributed across the task set. The significant context-by-problem interaction reveals that contextual cues can be selectively effective, perhaps depending on the degree to which the summary is diagnostic for a given inference or aligns with perceptually salient features of the problem. For problems 1 and 4, the immediate reminder of inertia and tangential motion likely reoriented participants’ attention to the relevant physics and prompted them to override misleading perceptual heuristics. For other problems the summary may have been less diagnostic or participants may have failed to apply the reminder appropriately. This pattern resonates with broader literature on context effects in cognition, which emphasizes that context operates by constraining hypothesis space and guiding attention to task-relevant features; however, the match between the contextual cue and the mental representation required for the task is critical. If the cue is not sufficiently specific or if the task requires integrating multiple aspects of the stimulus, the benefit may be attenuated. The limited effect of curvature invites cautious interpretation. Although high curvature was associated with somewhat lower accuracy in descriptive comparisons, this effect did not reach conventional statistical significance in the mixed-effects analyses. This could reflect several factors. First, curvature may exert only a small influence relative to the much larger effects of directed knowledge. Second, participants may have used different cognitive strategies for high-curvature items—some relying on piecemeal visual continuation heuristics and others invoking formal knowledge—leading to increased variability and reduced statistical detectability of a curvature effect. Third, the operationalization of curvature as a binary high/low variable may have been too coarse to capture subtler gradations in perceptual difficulty. A more fine-grained manipulation of curvature magnitude or the introduction of dynamic stimuli could provide a stronger test of curvature’s role. The fact that context did not robustly interact with knowledge suggests that contextual reminders confer independent, additive benefits but may not substitute for robust prior knowledge. Novices showed only small numerical gains from context and remained substantially less accurate than intermediate and advanced participants. This result aligns with the idea that explicit reminders can cue relevant reasoning but cannot fully compensate for lack of conceptual scaffolding. In pedagogical terms, brief reminders may help focus attention and prompt the retrieval of partially formed knowledge structures, but sustained instruction and practice are necessary to build the deep representations that support flexible transfer. Our findings have implications for models of top-down processing and the activation of relevant knowledge. The observed context effects are consistent with theories positing that top-down cues narrow attentional focus and bias information processing toward hypothesis-consistent features. In tasks that require selecting among several possible continuations, cues that re-weight feature salience in favor of physically correct interpretations can improve performance. The problem-specific nature of the improvement suggests that the cues must be sufficiently aligned with the inferential demands of the stimulus to activate the appropriate schema. One theoretical question left open by these results concerns the cognitive operations by which context exerts its influence. Does context primarily affect attentional selection, memory retrieval, strategy choice, or some combination of these processes? Empirical work incorporating process-tracing measures such as eye-tracking, think-aloud protocols, or time-course analyses could provide clarity by revealing whether contextual reminders change where participants look, how long they dwell on specific features, and whether they deploy different reasoning sequences when attempting to predict trajectories. The current study’s limitations point to several directions for future research. First, the context manipulation consisted of a single format (a short prose summary of Newton’s laws), and different types of context may vary in effectiveness. For example, schematic diagrams, worked examples, or dynamic animations might activate relevant knowledge more powerfully than text, particularly for visually based inferences like trajectory prediction. Systematic comparisons of context type and specificity could elucidate which forms of prompting are most effective for novices and which best complement existing knowledge. Second, the sample, while balanced across knowledge levels, consisted primarily of young adults recruited from undergraduate and online platforms. Broader sampling, including younger learners and individuals with more heterogeneous educational backgrounds, would help determine the generalizability of the effects. Third, our study prioritized a brief, ecologically plausible reminder; future work could vary the depth and duration of contextual instruction to determine the threshold at which context begins to substitute for prior knowledge rather than merely complementing it. Fourth, the exploratory suggestion of sex differences by problem in our data, though underpowered, raises the question of whether socio-cognitive factors such as spatial ability, prior exposure, or stereotype threat might mediate performance in particular problem types. Targeted studies designed to examine sex differences with adequate power and control for potential mediators would be valuable. Fifth, as noted earlier, the curvature manipulation could be refined. Future studies could parametrically manipulate curvature magnitudes and orientations, employ continuous motion displays, or examine three-dimensional trajectories to provide a richer test of perceptual and cognitive constraints on trajectory prediction. From an applied perspective, our findings offer tentative guidance for instruction. Brief, well-targeted reminders of core principles can facilitate correct reasoning in some problem contexts, suggesting a role for short diagnostic cues in formative assessment or problem solving sessions. However, for novices, these reminders are not a substitute for deeper conceptual understanding. Curriculum designers might therefore combine concise reminders with activities that promote the internalization of underlying principles, such as guided practice, analogical mapping, and interactive simulations. Such integrated approaches could leverage the immediate attentional benefits of context while promoting the durable learning that yields robust transfer. Another practical implication concerns assessment design. Given that context selectively improved performance on certain items, educators should be cautious when using isolated problem sets to infer students’ conceptual mastery; the presence or absence of contextual cues can materially affect performance and thus interpretation of assessment scores. Theoretical integration of our results suggests that both stored knowledge representations and situational activation processes jointly determine moment-to-moment reasoning about physical systems. Advanced knowledge equips individuals with ready-to-deploy schemas that can be applied even when context is minimal, whereas context serves as an activation mechanism that can cue partially formed knowledge or guide attention toward task-relevant features. This dual-process perspective aligns with broader frameworks in cognitive science that emphasize the interplay between long-term knowledge structures and transient contextual influences in shaping behavior. In closing, the present study both replicates and extends classic findings about the role of prior knowledge in physics reasoning and demonstrates that brief contextual reminders of Newtonian principles can produce modest, problem-specific improvements. The results suggest that educational interventions should consider both the cultivation of deep conceptual knowledge and the strategic use of contextual cues to enhance performance. Future work that examines mechanism through process measures, explores varied context formats, and investigates individual differences more deeply will help to delineate the boundary conditions under which context aids reasoning and how best to design instruction that supports accurate physical intuition in learners across the spectrum of prior knowledge.
