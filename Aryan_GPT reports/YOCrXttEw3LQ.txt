Introduction

Memory is a central topic in cognitive psychology, and understanding the factors that influence memory performance has practical and theoretical importance. One of the factors that has received considerable attention is the role of environmental context during encoding and retrieval. Among environmental variables, auditory context, including music, has been proposed to influence memory both positively and negatively. Music is ubiquitous in daily life and is often present in study or work environments, which raises the question of whether music alters the effective encoding and later recall of learned information. A second line of inquiry concerns individual differences in memory performance, and one of the most commonly examined individual differences is gender. Historically, various studies have reported gender differences favoring one sex or the other on particular memory tasks, though such differences are often small and context-dependent. Investigating whether music interacts with gender to affect memory performance therefore stands at the intersection of two important research traditions: context-dependent memory research and individual differences in cognitive function.

There are several theoretical reasons to expect that background music might influence memory. One account is that music alters arousal and mood, and arousal and mood have been shown to modulate encoding and retrieval processes. Moderate arousal can facilitate attention and consolidation processes, which could enhance memory performance under certain conditions. Conversely, music may act as a distractor by capturing attention and consuming cognitive resources necessary for elaborative encoding, particularly for tasks that require focused semantic processing. Another theoretical position draws on the encoding specificity principle, which suggests that overlap in contextual cues between encoding and retrieval benefits memory performance; if the same music is present during both encoding and retrieval, that shared context may serve as a retrieval cue. At the same time, when music is present only during encoding or only during retrieval, the mismatch could harm recall. These competing hypotheses predict different patterns of effects depending on whether music primarily affects arousal and mood, attention and distraction, or contextual cueing.

Empirical findings on the effects of music on memory are mixed. Some studies report facilitative effects of background music, particularly when music is instrumental and matches participants’ preferences or when it increases arousal to an optimal level. Other studies report detrimental effects, especially when music contains lyrics or complex melodic structure that interferes with verbal rehearsal or phonological processing. Still other studies find no effect of background music on certain types of memory tasks, suggesting that the relationship may be moderated by task demands, the type of memory assessed, or individual differences. The diversity of methods and materials across studies contributes to inconsistent findings: some studies use recognition tests, others free recall; word lists vary in semantic relatedness, concreteness, and length; music selections vary in genre, tempo, and presence of vocals; and experimental designs range from between-subjects to within-subjects, with different approaches to counterbalancing and controlling for order effects.

Gender differences in memory have likewise generated a complex literature. Certain studies indicate that women outperform men on verbal memory tasks such as word recall, story memory, and verbal paired associates. Explanations for such findings include differential use of verbal strategies, socialization differences that promote verbal elaboration, or even biological differences that influence brain structures and functional organization. Conversely, other studies find no gender differences or mixed patterns that depend on the specific memory test and population sampled. Moreover, interactions between gender and context, such as stress, arousal, or environmental distraction, have been reported in some studies, implying that gender differences may emerge only under certain situational conditions. For instance, if men and women respond differently to auditory stimulation in terms of changes in arousal or attentional allocation, background music might differentially affect their performance.

The question of whether music and gender interact to influence memory has practical significance. If background music differentially affects men and women, guidance for study or workplace environments could be tailored accordingly. From a theoretical perspective, evidence of an interaction would support models that link individual differences in emotional or attentional reactivity to environmental stimulation with cognitive performance. Conversely, a lack of interaction would suggest that any effects of music on memory are not strongly moderated by gender and may instead depend more on task characteristics or the properties of the music itself.

Despite these motivations, many prior studies suffer from methodological limitations that complicate interpretation. Some studies do not adequately control for the presence of lyrics versus instrumental music, which is crucial given that lyrics can specifically interfere with verbal rehearsal. Others fail to counterbalance the order of conditions when employing within-subjects designs, raising concerns about practice and fatigue effects. Sample sizes are often modest, leaving studies underpowered to detect small effects or interactions. Additionally, the specificity of memory measures varies: free recall, cued recall, and recognition tap different cognitive processes, and lack of consistency across studies makes synthesis difficult.

A further consideration is the type of memory tested. Much of the literature on music and memory focuses on verbal short-term or long-term memory using word lists, story recall, or paired associates. However, music may have stronger effects on episodic memory tied to autobiographical events, where music can serve as a potent contextual cue. Similarly, music might affect procedural or implicit memory in different ways that are not captured by explicit recall tasks. Understanding the boundary conditions for music effects therefore requires careful matching of task demands to theoretical mechanisms.

Given these mixed findings and methodological gaps, there is a need for controlled studies that use standardized materials and procedures to probe the effects of music on verbal memory and to examine whether gender moderates those effects. A within-subjects design wherein each participant experiences both a no-music condition and a music condition can provide sensitive tests of main effects and interactions while controlling for individual differences. Counterbalancing order across participants controls for sequence effects, and using short exposure times for word lists can approximate common study behaviors where learners glance through information before attempting recall. Selecting word lists that combine compound nouns and abstract nouns allows examination of memory for items that vary in concreteness and semantic organization, which can influence ease of encoding and recall.

The present study was designed to test two primary questions. First, does background classical instrumental music affect free recall of short word lists when participants study each list for a fixed brief interval and then attempt recall after a brief delay? Second, are there gender differences in recall performance on such lists, and do men and women show differential sensitivity to background music? To address these questions, participants studied two matched lists of 20 words each, viewing each list for 30 seconds before a one-minute retention interval followed by free recall. One list was presented in silence and the other while classical instrumental music played during the encoding interval and the retention interval. The order of conditions was counterbalanced across participants to control for practice and order effects.

Several predictions were considered. If music facilitates encoding via arousal or mood enhancement, participants should recall more words in the music condition than in silence. If music primarily serves as a distractor that competes with verbal encoding processes, participants should recall fewer words under music. If the effect of music depends on gender, then an interaction would be observed whereby one sex benefits or suffers more from music than the other. Alternatively, music may have little or no effect on this type of memory task, resulting in similar recall across conditions and no gender differences. The present study was intended to provide a well-controlled test of these competing possibilities and to contribute evidence about the robustness and generalizability of prior findings.

Methods

Participants in the present study were recruited from an undergraduate subject pool at a midsize university. A total of sixty participants completed the experiment. The sample included thirty female participants and thirty male participants. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 24 years. All participants reported being native speakers of English. Participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing. None reported a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. Participants gave informed consent before beginning the study and received course credit for participation. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the university's institutional review board, and the study conformed to established ethical guidelines for human subjects research.

Materials consisted of two word lists, each containing twenty words. The words were selected to include a mixture of compound nouns and abstract nouns to ensure variety in semantic content. Examples of compound nouns included terms such as "toothpaste," "newspaper," and "sunflower," while examples of abstract nouns included terms such as "justice," "freedom," and "truth." The words were chosen to be of similar frequency and length across lists to minimize item-related differences in memorability. The two lists were matched for average word length and for the proportion of compound versus abstract words. The order of words within each list was randomized for each participant to prevent position effects that could arise from fixed sequences.

The auditory stimulus in the music condition was a classical instrumental piece selected to be pleasant, melodic, and without lyrics. The piece was edited to a standardized thirty-second excerpt for the encoding interval, with the same excerpt continuing at the same volume during the one-minute retention interval and into the recall period so that the presence of music continued until participants began writing. The music was played through over-ear headphones that were calibrated to present sound at a comfortable and consistent volume level of approximately 65 decibels, measured at the ear with a sound level meter prior to data collection. Headphones were used to ensure that the music did not vary in intensity due to room acoustics, and to minimize confounding noise. The no-music condition involved the same procedure but with the headphones off and no auditory input.

The experimental design was within-subjects with one repeated factor: auditory condition (music vs silence). Each participant completed two study-test trials, one for each list, with one list presented in silence and the other with music. The order of conditions was counterbalanced across participants so that half the participants experienced the music condition first and the other half experienced the music condition second. This counterbalancing was implemented using a simple alternating assignment as participants were scheduled for sessions. The lists themselves were also counterbalanced across participants such that for half the participants List A was in the music condition and List B was in the silence condition, and for the other half List B was paired with music and List A with silence. This fully counterbalanced arrangement aimed to control for potential list-specific effects or order effects.

The procedure for each trial began with the participant being instructed that they would see a list of words on the computer screen for a brief interval and that they should try to remember as many words as possible for a later recall test. The experimenter emphasized that they should not attempt to write the words down while they were being displayed. Following the instructions, the participant initiated the trial. The twenty words of the list were then presented on the computer screen simultaneously in a clear sans-serif font, with a viewing duration of thirty seconds. In the music condition, the musical excerpt began one second before the list appeared on the screen and continued throughout the thirty-second viewing period. After the thirty-second viewing period, the screen went blank and participants underwent a one-minute retention interval. During this interval, participants were instructed to sit quietly and not rehearse the words. Music continued during the retention interval in the music condition. Following the retention interval, participants were given a sheet of paper and a pen and were told to write down as many words from the list as they could remember; they were given two minutes for this free recall period. The music continued into the beginning of the recall period in order to maintain consistent auditory context between the retention interval and the initiation of recall. The same sequence was repeated for the second list, with the appropriate auditory condition applied.

Scoring of responses was straightforward: each correctly recalled word from the target list was scored as one point. Minor spelling errors were accepted provided the intended word was clearly recognizable, and synonyms or semantically related words that were not on the list were scored as incorrect. Intrusions—words not on the list—were tallied separately but did not contribute to the correct recall score. The primary dependent variable for each trial was the number of correctly recalled words out of a possible twenty. Secondary variables included the number of intrusions and response latency for the first recalled item, though response latency measures were treated as exploratory because the experiment was designed primarily to measure recall accuracy.

Prior to the main analyses, data were screened for accuracy and completeness. No participant failed to complete both trials. Data from all sixty participants were included in the analyses. Descriptive statistics, tests of normality, and checks for outliers were conducted to ensure that the assumptions of parametric tests were reasonably satisfied. Paired-samples t-tests were used to compare recall performance between the music and silence conditions. A mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) was then conducted with auditory condition as the within-subjects factor and gender as the between-subjects factor to test for main effects and interactions. Effect sizes including Cohen's d for paired comparisons and partial eta squared for ANOVA effects were computed to provide information about the magnitude of any observed effects. Alpha was set at .05 for determining statistical significance for all tests. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software.

Participants were debriefed at the conclusion of the experiment. During debriefing the purpose of the study was explained, and participants were given the opportunity to ask questions. They were informed that the focus was on the effects of background music and gender on memory performance. Participants were thanked for their time and given contact information for the research team in case they had additional questions. Data were anonymized and stored securely in accordance with institutional data handling policies.

Results

Descriptive statistics for recall performance under each auditory condition were calculated first. For the silence condition, participants on average recalled 9.8 words out of 20, with a standard deviation of 3.2. For the music condition, participants on average recalled 9.5 words out of 20, with a standard deviation of 3.4. The distributions for both conditions approximated normality according to visual inspection of histograms and quantile-quantile plots. Shapiro-Wilk tests for normality were conducted for both conditions and yielded p-values above .05, indicating no significant deviation from normality. Inspection of boxplots did not reveal any extreme outliers that would unduly influence mean estimates, and the range of scores was similar across conditions.

A paired-samples t-test was conducted to compare the mean number of words recalled in the silence versus music conditions. The mean difference in recall (silence minus music) was 0.3 words. The paired t-test indicated that this difference was not statistically significant, t(59) = 1.02, p = .312. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference ranged approximately from -0.4 to 1.0 words, indicating that the true mean difference could plausibly be slightly negative or slightly positive but was near zero. The effect size for the paired comparison, Cohen's d for paired samples, was 0.13, which is conventionally interpreted as a small effect. These results suggest that, on average, background classical instrumental music did not produce a measurable change in free recall for these short word lists.

Given the a priori interest in gender differences and potential interactions with auditory condition, a mixed-design ANOVA was performed with auditory condition (silence, music) as the within-subjects factor and gender (male, female) as the between-subjects factor. The main effect of auditory condition was not significant, F(1,58) = 1.05, p = .310, partial eta squared = .018. The main effect of gender on overall recall collapsed across conditions was also non-significant, F(1,58) = 0.03, p = .862, partial eta squared = .001. The mean overall recall for female participants was 9.7 words (SD = 3.3) and for male participants was 9.6 words (SD = 3.3), reflecting minimal difference. The interaction between auditory condition and gender was not significant, F(1,58) = 0.45, p = .505, partial eta squared = .008. The pattern of means showed that in the silence condition, females recalled on average 10.0 words and males recalled 9.6 words, whereas in the music condition females recalled on average 9.4 words and males recalled 9.6 words. These minor differences did not approach statistical significance.

Order effects were examined to ascertain whether performance differed depending on whether participants experienced the music condition first or second. An independent-samples t-test compared overall recall across participants who had the music condition first versus those who had the silence condition first. There was no significant difference in overall recall based on order, t(58) = 0.56, p = .577. Additionally, a supplementary mixed ANOVA including order as an additional between-subjects factor revealed no significant three-way interaction among auditory condition, gender, and order, F(1,56) = 0.22, p = .641. These analyses suggest that order did not confound the primary comparisons.

Because intrusion counts were recorded, these were analyzed as a measure of potential interference introduced by the music condition. The mean number of intrusions in the silence condition was 0.6 (SD = 0.9), and in the music condition the mean number of intrusions was 0.7 (SD = 1.0). A paired t-test comparing intrusion counts across conditions indicated no significant difference, t(59) = 0.65, p = .519. Intrusions did not differ by gender; females averaged 0.6 intrusions and males averaged 0.7 intrusions, t(58) = 0.45, p = .654. These low and similar intrusion rates across conditions indicate that the presence of music did not substantially increase the production of extralist words.

We examined the latency to the first recalled item as an exploratory measure of retrieval initiation. The mean latency to the first recalled item in the silence condition was approximately 12.3 seconds (SD = 6.5) and in the music condition was approximately 12.9 seconds (SD = 6.9). A paired t-test on latency values indicated no significant difference, t(59) = 0.78, p = .438. Gender did not reliably influence latency; females had a mean latency of 12.1 seconds and males 13.1 seconds, t(58) = 0.68, p = .499. These findings do not support the idea that music altered the speed with which participants could initiate recall under the present task constraints.

Because effect size estimates were small, we conducted a post hoc sensitivity analysis to estimate the smallest detectable effect given our sample size and the within-subjects design. For a paired t-test with N = 60 and alpha = .05, the study had approximately .80 power to detect an effect size (Cohen's d) of around 0.36. The observed paired effect size (d = 0.13) was well below this threshold, suggesting that if a true effect exists it is likely small in magnitude. The partial eta squared values from the ANOVA likewise indicated minimal variance explained by auditory condition or gender.

To ensure that the matching of word lists did not obscure potential item effects, we compared recall for List A versus List B across participants regardless of condition assignment. Mean recall across lists did not differ significantly, t(59) = 0.42, p = .676, confirming that the lists were well matched in difficulty. This supports the interpretation that observed null effects are not due to one list being systematically easier or harder.

Given the null results for the primary comparisons, we also explored whether participant-reported familiarity or liking of classical music moderated performance in the music condition. Participants completed brief ratings after the session indicating whether they liked the music piece and whether they reported listening to classical music regularly. Split-sample comparisons based on median splits for liking and familiarity revealed no consistent pattern; participants who reported liking the music did not recall significantly more words in the music condition than participants who did not, t(58) = 0.94, p = .352. Similarly, self-reported frequency of listening to classical music was not associated with memory performance in the music condition, t(58) = 0.72, p = .475.

In sum, the statistical analyses provided no evidence that background classical instrumental music affected free recall of short word lists under the present conditions. There was also no evidence of gender differences in recall, nor of an interaction between music and gender. Secondary measures such as intrusion counts and latency to first recall likewise showed no significant differences across conditions or gender groups. Effect sizes were small, and post hoc power analysis suggested limited sensitivity to detect very small effects. Overall, the pattern of results indicates that neither the presence of background classical music nor the participant's gender exerted a meaningful influence on free recall performance in this experimental setup.

Discussion

The present study sought to evaluate whether background classical instrumental music influences free recall of short word lists and whether gender modulates any such effect. Using a within-subjects design with thorough counterbalancing, participants studied two matched lists of twenty words each, one under silent conditions and one while music played. The core findings were clear and consistent across multiple analyses: there was no significant difference in the number of words recalled in the music condition compared with the silence condition, and there were no significant gender differences or interactions between gender and auditory condition. Secondary measures including intrusions and latency to first recall similarly revealed no reliable effects. Effect size estimates were small, and the confidence intervals around mean differences included zero, indicating that if any effect exists it is likely to be small in magnitude for this type of memory task.

These results contribute to an ongoing literature that presents mixed findings regarding the cognitive effects of background music. On the one hand, accounts emphasizing arousal or mood effects might predict facilitation under music, whereas accounts emphasizing distraction or interference predict impairment. The null outcomes observed here are compatible with the possibility that opposing influences—mood-related facilitation and attentional distraction—may cancel each other out under certain circumstances, leading to net null effects. Alternatively, the specific characteristics of the stimulus materials and the task may render them relatively insensitive to modulation by background music. Free recall of word lists that are presented for only 30 seconds and probed after a short delay may rely heavily on immediate semantic encoding and rehearsal processes that are robust to modest variations in background auditory input.

The absence of gender differences in the present study also aligns with several prior studies that have failed to find substantial gender effects on basic verbal recall tasks. While some prior research has reported female advantages on certain verbal memory measures, such effects are not universal and may depend on the type of material (e.g., narrative versus isolated words), the depth of encoding, or other moderating variables such as strategy use. The lack of an interaction between gender and music suggests that, at least for short-term free recall of this kind, men and women respond similarly to the presence of classical instrumental music. This finding reduces the likelihood that gender-specific arousal or attentional responses to background music are major determinants of recall performance in this paradigm.

Several methodological features of the current study constrain the generalizability of these null findings and suggest avenues for future research. First, the music used in this experiment was a single instrumental classical excerpt presented at a moderate volume. This choice was intended to minimize confounds associated with lyrics and extreme tempos, but it also limits the scope of inference. Different genres, tempos, complexity levels, or the presence of vocal lyrics could produce different effects. For example, music with lyrics may more directly interfere with verbal working memory via competition for phonological processing resources, potentially leading to larger negative effects on recall. Conversely, highly preferred music or music that significantly elevates positive mood for an individual could enhance encoding and yield facilitative effects in certain contexts.

Second, the memory task focused on free recall of relatively decontextualized words consisting of compound and abstract nouns. It is possible that music exerts stronger effects on other forms of memory. Episodic memory for autobiographical events, for example, is frequently linked to music through emotionally charged cueing, and studies have shown that hearing a familiar song can evoke vivid recollections of personal experiences. Similarly, recognition memory tasks may be more sensitive to subtle encoding differences because they reduce retrieval effort and rely more on familiarity processes. Future studies should vary both the memory domain (e.g., episodic vs. semantic) and the memory measure (free recall vs. recognition) to map the boundary conditions of music effects.

Third, the exposure duration and timing of music might be critical. In this experiment, music began immediately before encoding, continued through a short retention interval, and persisted briefly into recall. Different temporal patterns—such as continuous background music throughout a study session, or music present only during retrieval—could create distinct contextual cueing effects consistent with the encoding specificity principle. The encoding specificity principle suggests that when the same contextual cues are present at encoding and retrieval, recall may benefit; the present design did not systematically manipulate encoding-retrieval context match beyond maintaining continuity into the beginning of the recall period. Future designs could test fully crossed conditions with music present at encoding only, retrieval only, both, or neither to evaluate contextual cueing hypotheses.

Fourth, individual differences beyond gender may moderate music effects on cognition. Although exploratory analyses in the present study did not find consistent relationships with self-reported liking or familiarity with classical music, more fine-grained measures such as personality traits (e.g., extraversion, openness to experience), attentional control, working memory capacity, or habitual study practices might better predict who benefits from background music. Neurophysiological measures of arousal, such as heart rate variability or electroencephalography, could also clarify the mechanisms by which music influences cognitive processes. Future research that integrates behavioral, self-report, and physiological measures would be well-positioned to elucidate mediating pathways.

Fifth, the sample size and statistical power deserve consideration. While the sample of sixty participants provided reasonable power to detect medium-sized effects in a within-subjects design, it was not sufficient to reliably detect very small effects. The observed effect size for the music manipulation was small (Cohen's d = 0.13), suggesting that any true effect, if present, is likely small. Determining whether such small effects are theoretically meaningful or practically important requires larger samples and meta-analytic synthesis. Researchers aiming to detect small but consistent effects across populations should plan correspondingly larger sample sizes.

Sixth, task strategies and participant expectations may modulate outcomes. Participants were instructed not to rehearse during the viewing period and to recall freely after the retention interval, but individual differences in spontaneous encoding strategies—such as covert rehearsal, imagery, or semantic grouping—could influence recall independently of auditory condition. The use of concurrent articulatory suppression tasks in future work could help to isolate phonological rehearsal mechanisms and determine whether music interferes specifically with such strategies.

Seventh, ecological validity is an important consideration. Laboratory tasks using isolated word lists may not capture the complexities of learning in real-world settings, where individuals often engage with longer texts or multimodal materials and where background music may be part of a broader multitasking environment. Studies that employ more ecologically valid materials, such as passages, lectures, or problem-solving tasks, could provide complementary insights into how music affects everyday learning and memory.

Eighth, the possibility of null effects being due to cancellation of opposing influences should be considered. Music may simultaneously induce slight increases in mood and arousal that facilitate consolidation while also imposing a mild attentional load that interferes with encoding. If these processes operate with comparable strength in opposite directions, net effects may appear null despite underlying changes in cognitive dynamics. Measuring intermediary variables such as affective state, subjective distraction, and attentional allocation could reveal such countervailing processes.

Ninth, cultural and contextual factors might influence music’s impact on cognition. The present sample consisted of university students from a particular cultural setting, and cross-cultural differences in music preferences, familiarity with classical music, and norms around background music may affect generalizability. Studies in diverse cultural contexts and age groups would clarify whether the present results hold more broadly.

Finally, the practical implication of the present findings is that, for brief focused study tasks involving isolated word memorization, background classical instrumental music at moderate volume does not appear to have a large or systematic effect on free recall performance. This suggests that students or workers who prefer playing quiet instrumental music while briefly reviewing lists of items may not experience meaningful harms or benefits in this specific context. However, given the limitations discussed above and the variety of possible musical and task parameters, blanket recommendations about music and study behavior are premature.

In conclusion, the current study provides evidence that background classical instrumental music does not significantly alter free recall of short lists of compound and abstract nouns, nor does gender significantly moderate this effect under the tested conditions. These null findings refine our understanding of the conditions under which music may or may not influence memory and highlight the importance of considering task type, music characteristics, individual differences, and study design in future research. Further work that manipulates genre, lyrical content, timing relative to encoding and retrieval, and task domain, as well as studies employing larger and more diverse samples and multimodal measures, will help to chart the contours of the relationship between music and memory.
