Introduction

Human decision making in everyday life often relies on multiple sources of information. People routinely combine what they hear from others with what they have experienced personally. For example, drivers may integrate a traffic report on the radio with their past experience of traffic patterns to decide which route to take. The psychological processes that underlie how description and experience inform decisions have attracted substantial interest. A body of work has contrasted decisions from description—where outcome probabilities and magnitudes are explicitly presented—with decisions from experience—where people learn about outcomes by sampling or observing. Research contrasting these two modes of decision making has uncovered systematic differences that are important for theory and applied settings. The paradigmatic finding in this literature is the so-called description–experience gap. The description–experience gap refers to the observation that people often behave differently when probabilities are explicitly described compared with when they are learned from experience. One consistent aspect of this gap is that people tend to overweight rare events when they are explicitly described, but to underweight rare events when they are learned from experience. This asymmetry has practical implications for risk communication, medical decision making, and public policy, because rare events often carry significant consequences despite their low probability. Theoretical explanations for the description–experience gap have invoked memory limitations, sampling error, and different cognitive processes engaged during deliberation versus learning. When decisions are made from experience, people may sample only a limited number of outcomes and may fail to encounter or remember rare events, which can lead to systematic underestimation of rare-event probabilities. By contrast, when probabilities are given in description, they are salient and can be used directly in deliberative calculations. Prospect theory, a leading descriptive account of choice under risk, predicts systematic risk aversion for gains and risk seeking for losses based on value functions that are concave for gains and convex for losses. Prospect theory also asserts that outcomes are weighted nonlinearly by a probability weighting function, which often implies overweighting of low probabilities. However, prospect theory was developed based largely on choices from description and does not straightforwardly account for the experience-based underweighting of rare events that has been documented. The mismatch between descriptions of probability weighting and empirical findings from experience-based tasks provides a compelling challenge to unified theories of risky choice. Further, this challenge raises questions about the factors that moderate the description–experience gap and about individual difference variables that might predict susceptibility to it. Personality traits are one potential moderator that has been underexplored. Dispositional optimism, defined as a general expectation that good outcomes will occur in the future, is a stable individual difference that influences perception, emotion, and decision making. Optimism has been associated with increased approach behavior, higher risk tolerance in some domains, and differences in affective responses to outcomes. Optimists might, for example, downplay the likelihood or impact of negative rare events because their general expectancies bias processing. Conversely, optimists might be more willing to take risky options that offer potential gains. Because optimism affects both cognitive appraisal and affective responses, it is plausible that optimism could interact with the mode of information presentation to influence risky choice. Specifically, optimistic individuals might be especially prone to underweighting rare negative events when making decisions from experience, because they may pay less attention to negative samples or encode them less strongly in memory. Alternatively, optimism might have a more general effect on risk preference irrespective of information mode. Empirical work examining the role of optimism in the description–experience gap is sparse. Some research has examined related traits such as trait anxiety or sensation seeking, but the literature is not extensive and findings are mixed. The lack of clear evidence leaves open whether optimism moderates the description–experience effect, whether it affects baseline risk preferences, and whether it interacts with outcome valence (gain versus loss). Outcome valence is another factor that could influence how information mode interacts with choice. Classic findings suggest that people are risk averse for gains and risk seeking for losses. However, work on the description–experience gap has sometimes produced results that diverge from prospect theory predictions, especially in experience-based tasks. The combination of valence (gain vs loss) and information mode may yield complex patterns of choice that are not fully captured by current models. For instance, experience-based underweighting of rare events might reduce both risk aversion in gains and risk seeking in losses, thereby compressing valence-dependent differences. Alternatively, experience-based learning might interact with emotion in ways that amplify valence effects, depending on memory and attention. The current study was motivated by three primary aims. The first aim was to replicate the description–experience gap using a controlled experimental paradigm that included both gain and loss problems. The second aim was to examine whether dispositional optimism predicts risky choice and whether optimism moderates any description–experience differences. The third aim was to explore valence effects in the context of description and experience, to see whether the classical gain–loss asymmetry replicates and whether it interacts with information mode. To address these aims, we designed a mixed experimental study. Participants were randomly assigned to either a description condition, in which probabilities and outcomes were explicitly presented, or to an experience condition, in which probabilities had to be inferred from sampling. Each participant encountered both gain and loss problems, allowing within-subject assessment of valence effects. A standardized measure of dispositional optimism was administered to capture trait-level expectancies. Based on the literature, we articulated several hypotheses. First, following prior findings, we hypothesized that participants in the experience condition would underweight rare events and therefore choose the risky option more often than participants in the description condition when rare events would normally discourage risky choice. Second, we hypothesized that participants would show classic prospect-theory-consistent valence effects, with relatively more risk aversion in the gain domain and more risk seeking in the loss domain. Third, we hypothesized that higher dispositional optimism would be associated with greater risk taking, particularly in the experience condition and particularly for losses, reflecting a tendency to downplay negative rare outcomes. We also planned exploratory analyses to probe the robustness of any description–experience differences across trial-level features and to examine whether sampling behavior in the experience condition predicted subsequent choices. To provide a rigorous test of these hypotheses, the design included comprehension checks, standardized instructions, and a sample size expected to provide adequate statistical power. The experimental tasks were designed to be principled analogues of canonical description and experience paradigms while being amenable to online data collection. By combining a replication focus with the exploration of optimism as a potential moderator, the study sought to contribute to theory by clarifying whether personality shapes the way people use descriptive versus experiential information in risky choice. The study also sought to inform applied contexts in which people must make consequential decisions based on limited personal experience and on external descriptions. If optimism were found to moderate the description–experience gap, interventions could be targeted to individuals in domains such as health or finance. Conversely, if optimism has little influence, then universal design principles for risk communication may be appropriate. The need for such clarity drove the methodological choices and analytic strategy described below. We preregistered key aspects of the design and analysis plan to reduce researcher degrees of freedom and to produce a transparent test of hypotheses. The preregistration focused on the primary between-subjects comparison and on the planned covariate analyses involving optimism. Secondary and exploratory analyses were designated as such. Overall, the study aimed to be a careful empirical contribution to understanding how information mode and personality jointly shape risky decision making across gains and losses.

Methods

Participants were recruited via an online recruitment platform and were remunerated at a standard rate for participation. A target sample size of 120 participants was selected based on an a priori power calculation for detecting a small-to-moderate effect in a between-subjects comparison with 80% power. Participants were eligible if they were adults aged 18 or older, fluent in English, and located in the study country. Individuals who had previously participated in similar decision-making experiments run by the research group were excluded to reduce practice effects. Data collection continued until the target sample size had been reached, with additional participants tested to allow for potential exclusions. A total of 142 participants completed the study. After excluding participants who failed comprehension checks, who completed the study in implausibly short times, or who provided incomplete data on the optimism measure, the final analytic sample comprised 130 participants. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions using an automated randomization algorithm. Sixty-five participants were assigned to the description condition and sixty-five participants were assigned to the experience condition. The between-subjects assignment ensured that participants did not experience both modes and thus avoided contamination from cross-condition learning. The experiment used a mixed factorial design with information mode (description vs experience) as a between-subjects factor and valence (gain vs loss) as a within-subjects factor. The primary dependent variable was the proportion of trials in which a participant chose the risky option. The risky option was operationalized consistently across gain and loss problems as the option with higher outcome variance and a low-probability extreme outcome. In gain problems, the risky option offered a large monetary gain with low probability and a smaller outcome otherwise. In loss problems, the risky option offered a large monetary loss with low probability and a smaller loss otherwise. Each participant completed a set of twenty choice problems, comprising ten gain problems and ten loss problems. The order of gain and loss problems was pseudorandomized for each participant to mitigate order effects. Within each valence category, problem parameters were held constant across participants, and problem order was counterbalanced. In the description condition, each problem was presented with explicit probability information and precise outcome magnitudes. Probabilities were presented as percentages and as frequency statements to enhance clarity. Participants in the description condition were instructed to assume that the presented probabilities were accurate and that outcomes were drawn according to those probabilities if the gamble were played. In the experience condition, participants learned about the outcome distributions through sampling. For each problem, participants were given the opportunity to draw 20 samples from each option before making their final choice. The sampling was implemented so that each draw produced an outcome consistent with the underlying probability distribution, and participants were free to inspect the sequence of outcomes as they were sampled. Participants in the experience condition were explicitly told that the sampling was representative and that the underlying probabilities remained the same across samples. The sampling regime of 20 draws per option was selected as a compromise between giving participants enough evidence to form judgments and maintaining reasonable task length for online data collection. To allow for analysis of sampling behavior, the system recorded the sequence of sampled outcomes, the number of times extreme (rare) outcomes were observed, and the time taken during sampling. The gambling problems were designed so that the risky option involved a rare outcome with probability 0.10 and a common outcome with probability 0.90. In gain problems, the risky option paid $8 with probability 0.10 and $1 with probability 0.90, while the safe option paid $1.25 for sure. In loss problems, the risky option incurred a $8 loss with probability 0.10 and a $1 loss with probability 0.90, while the safe option incurred a $1.25 loss for sure. The expected values of the two options were close but not identical, and participants were instructed to make choices as if they were real. To increase engagement, a small proportion of participants were randomly selected to receive one of their choices as an incentive payoff at the end of the experiment, with the payoff implemented in real money terms. Prior to the main task, participants completed a practice block that contained two neutral trials to familiarize them with the interface. Participants in the description condition practiced reading probability statements and making choices. Participants in the experience condition practiced the sampling mechanism and made choices after sampling. Following the choice task, all participants completed a standardized dispositional optimism questionnaire composed of ten items. Items asked participants to indicate agreement with statements about general expectancies for positive versus negative outcomes. Responses were collected on a five-point Likert scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Optimism scores were computed by averaging the relevant items after reverse-scoring negatively worded items, yielding a scale with a possible range from 1 to 5. Higher scores indicated greater dispositional optimism. The optimism scale has been widely used in behavioral research and has acceptable internal consistency; in the present sample the internal consistency was acceptable. In addition to the optimism scale, participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire including age, gender, education level, and subjective familiarity with monetary gambles. Participants also completed comprehension checks that required them to state the probability of the rare event in a sample problem and to identify which option was riskier in a practice trial. Participants who failed the comprehension checks were excluded from analysis. The main statistical analyses were planned prior to data collection. The primary test for the description–experience gap involved a mixed ANOVA with information mode as a between-subjects factor and valence as a within-subjects factor, with the dependent variable being the proportion of risky choices. In addition, optimism was tested as a continuous covariate in an ANCOVA model to examine its main effect and interactions with information mode and valence. Trial-level analyses using mixed-effects logistic regression were also preplanned to examine choices as a function of trial-level predictors including valence, condition, optimism, and number of rare outcomes observed during sampling for the experience group. These trial-level models accounted for the nested structure of trials within participants by including random intercepts for participants. Secondary analyses explored sampling behavior descriptively and correlated the number of rare events observed with subsequent risky choices. All statistical tests used an alpha of 0.05 for determining statistical significance. Effect sizes were reported for key findings. Data processing and analysis were conducted using standard statistical software, and syntax for the primary analyses was archived to ensure reproducibility. The study was approved by an institutional review board, and participants provided informed consent electronically before beginning the experiment.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the final sample are presented first. The final sample comprised 130 participants after exclusions. The mean age of participants was 32.4 years with a standard deviation of 10.7 years. Fifty-six percent of participants identified as female, forty-two percent as male, and two percent as other or preferred not to say. The median education level corresponded to completion of a college degree. Mean optimism score in the sample was 3.28 on the 1–5 scale, with a standard deviation of 0.74. Internal consistency of the optimism scale in this sample, estimated by Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.78. Participants in the description and experience conditions did not differ significantly on age, gender distribution, education level, or optimism score, which suggests that randomization produced comparable groups. The mean proportion of risky choices across all trials and participants was 0.42 with a standard deviation of 0.19. Breaking down by valence, the mean proportion of risky choices on gain trials was 0.45 with a standard deviation of 0.24, whereas the mean proportion on loss trials was 0.38 with a standard deviation of 0.23. These descriptive differences suggested somewhat lower risk taking for losses compared with gains. Figure-like descriptive summaries indicated that the pattern was similar across the two information modes, although point estimates varied slightly. In the description group, mean risky choice proportion on gain trials was 0.46 and on loss trials was 0.39. In the experience group, mean risky choice proportion on gain trials was 0.44 and on loss trials was 0.37. To formally test the hypotheses, a mixed ANOVA was conducted with information mode (description vs experience) as a between-subjects factor and valence (gain vs loss) as a within-subjects factor. The ANOVA revealed no significant main effect of information mode on the proportion of risky choices, F(1,128) = 1.02, p = 0.31, ηp^2 = 0.008. This result indicates that participants in the experience condition did not choose the risky option at a significantly different rate than participants in the description condition. The ANOVA did reveal a significant main effect of valence, F(1,128) = 9.45, p = 0.0026, ηp^2 = 0.069. This effect reflected fewer risky choices on loss trials than on gain trials across both conditions. The interaction between information mode and valence was not significant, F(1,128) = 0.12, p = 0.73, ηp^2 = 0.001. Together, these findings indicated a valence effect but no reliable description–experience difference and no interaction. To examine the role of dispositional optimism, optimism scores were entered as a continuous covariate in an ANCOVA model with the same factorial structure. The main effect of optimism on risky choice proportion was not significant, F(1,127) = 1.04, p = 0.31, ηp^2 = 0.008. The optimism-by-condition interaction was not significant, F(1,127) = 0.56, p = 0.46, ηp^2 = 0.004. The optimism-by-valence interaction was also not significant, F(1,127) = 0.77, p = 0.38, ηp^2 = 0.006. The three-way interaction among optimism, condition, and valence was likewise non-significant, F(1,127) = 0.23, p = 0.63, ηp^2 = 0.002. To provide converging evidence at the level of individual choices, we fitted a mixed-effects logistic regression predicting the probability of choosing the risky option on each trial. The model included fixed effects for valence (coded gain = 0.5, loss = -0.5), condition (description = 0.5, experience = -0.5), optimism (mean-centered), and their interactions, and included a random intercept for participant. The logistic regression showed a significant fixed effect for valence, odds ratio = 0.74, 95% CI [0.62, 0.88], p = 0.001, indicating reduced odds of choosing the risky option in loss trials compared with gain trials. The fixed effect for condition was not significant, odds ratio = 0.95, 95% CI [0.80, 1.14], p = 0.57. The interactions involving optimism were non-significant in the mixed model as well. The logistic model therefore corroborated the ANOVA results. Because the description–experience gap is often linked to underweighting of rare events in experience-based tasks, we explored sampling behavior in the experience condition. On average, participants observed 2.1 rare events per risky option during the 20 draws, with a standard deviation of 1.4. The number of observed rare events varied across participants and trials due to stochastic sampling. We computed the correlation between the total number of rare events observed by a participant across all trials and that participant’s overall risky choice proportion. This correlation was small and non-significant, r = 0.10, p = 0.41. We also examined whether observing a rare event during the sampling phase of a particular trial predicted a reduced likelihood of choosing the risky option on that trial. A trial-level mixed-effects logistic regression that included a predictor for whether a rare event had been observed during sampling revealed a modest negative effect, odds ratio = 0.88, 95% CI [0.76, 1.02], p = 0.09, which did not reach the conventional significance threshold. These exploratory analyses suggest that sampling exposure to rare outcomes had limited influence on subsequent choices in this paradigm. To probe whether there might be subtle condition differences obscured by aggregate analyses, we compared the description and experience groups on several secondary indices. Reaction times during the final choice phase did not differ reliably between groups, t(128) = 0.91, p = 0.36, indicating similar deliberation times. The distribution of choices across problems showed no consistent pattern favoring the description or experience group. We also examined whether education level or age moderated the effect of condition; neither variable produced significant interactions. To provide a more direct test of the preregistered replication aim, we compared our effect sizes with those typically reported in the literature. The between-group effect size in our sample was small (Cohen’s d = 0.18) and did not replicate a robust description–experience difference. Given our sample size, the observed effect was below the threshold detectable with the planned power for medium effects. Finally, we conducted sensitivity analyses excluding participants with extreme optimism scores and excluding participants with very few observed rare events during sampling. These sensitivity analyses produced similar patterns of results, with a consistent valence effect and no reliable condition or optimism effects. In summary, the results showed a clear valence effect with fewer risky choices in losses than gains, but no evidence for a main effect of information mode nor for an influence of dispositional optimism on risky choice in this task.

Discussion

The present study examined how information mode—description versus experience—and dispositional optimism influence risky choice across gain and loss domains. We had three primary hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that experience-based decision making would produce underweighting of rare events relative to description-based decision making, leading to greater risk taking in experience. Second, we hypothesized that valence would modulate choice in line with prospect theory, with more risk seeking in losses and more risk aversion in gains. Third, we hypothesized that dispositional optimism would predict greater risk taking overall and would interact with condition and valence. The data did not support the first hypothesis. There was no significant difference in risky choice frequency between participants who received explicit description of probabilities and those who learned probabilities via sampling. The absence of a description–experience difference in our study contrasts with some prior reports of robust differences. The literature on the description–experience gap includes many studies with varying effect sizes, and the gap is not always observed in every setting. The lack of replication in our data suggests that the description–experience effect may be sensitive to task parameters, sampling regimes, participant population, and other contextual factors. Several methodological factors may account for the null result regarding condition. One possibility is that the sampling regime used in the experience condition provided too much information for participants to reliably infer rare-event probabilities. In our design, participants were allowed and in fact given 20 draws per option prior to making choices. This relatively generous sampling could have allowed many participants to observe rare events at least once, reducing the underweighting that would otherwise follow from sparse sampling. Another possibility is that the presentation format of the description condition made probabilities less salient than in other studies. We presented probabilities both as percentages and as frequency statements to enhance clarity, but differences in salience or framing could still have been smaller than in previous experiments that find the description–experience gap. It is also possible that the incentive structure influenced behavior. We used a partial incentive scheme in which a subset of participants received one choice as an actual payoff, which could affect the degree to which participants treated the task as consequential. If participants treated the task as low stakes, they may have been more exploratory in both conditions, thereby attenuating condition differences. Another plausible explanation for the null condition effect lies in sample characteristics. Our participants were drawn from an online population that may have more experience with probabilistic tasks or with online sampling mechanisms than typical lab samples. Such familiarity could reduce the differences between description and experience by making sampling more accurate or by encouraging more deliberative processing even in the experience condition. The second hypothesis received partial support but in an unexpected direction. We observed a significant valence effect: participants chose the risky option less frequently in loss problems than in gain problems. This pattern indicates greater risk aversion for losses than for gains in our data, which is the opposite of what prospect theory typically predicts. Classic prospect theory posits risk seeking for losses and risk aversion for gains. Our observed pattern suggests that participants were more inclined to avoid risky options when facing potential losses, opting for the smaller but certain loss option more often than for the corresponding certain gain in gain problems. Several interpretations may account for this reversal. One possibility is that the particular outcome magnitudes and probabilities used in our problems created a context in which the certain loss was perceived as more psychologically tolerable than the potential for a large loss, leading to conservative behavior. Loss aversion—that people weight losses more heavily than equivalent gains—could produce preferring certainty to avoid the rare large loss. If participants viewed the rare large loss as particularly salient or aversive, they might avoid the risky option even if prospect theory suggests risk seeking. Another possibility relates to affect and regret. Considering potential losses may elicit stronger negative affect and anticipated regret, promoting choices that minimize the chance of experiencing a large loss. In contrast, potential gains may generate excitement that favors taking gambles. The emotional salience of losses in our task could therefore have pushed choices toward greater risk aversion. It is also worth noting that description–experience distinctions can interact with valence in complex ways. In some prior work, experienced losses may be over-remembered or attended to more strongly than experienced gains, which could counteract the underweighting of rare events in experience. If the memory of negative rare events is particularly vivid, even limited sampling could produce strong aversive responses that promote risk avoidance. Thus, the valence-related processes of attention, memory, and affect might explain why we found fewer risky choices in loss problems. The third hypothesis concerning dispositional optimism was not supported. Optimism did not reliably predict the frequency of risky choices, nor did it interact with information mode or valence. This null result suggests that trait optimism, at least as measured by the instrument used here, was not a major driver of decision differences in this paradigm. Several interpretations are possible. One is that dispositional optimism influences broad life outcomes and coping strategies but does not strongly impact micro-level choices in laboratory-style gambles. The types of cognitive processes engaged in the experimental task—such as probability encoding, sampling behavior, and rapid choice—may be relatively insulated from general expectancies about the future. Another possibility is measurement sensitivity. The optimism scale used in the study captures general expectancies, but specific beliefs about the likelihood of negative events in a particular domain might be more relevant to choice. Domain-specific pessimism or domain-specific risk perceptions could be better predictors of risk taking in specific tasks than general optimism. A related consideration is the potential for optimism to interact with other traits such as sensation seeking, impulsivity, or numeracy. If optimism’s effect on risky choice is moderated by these traits, a simple bivariate association may not capture its influence. Future studies could include broader individual difference batteries to test for such moderation. It is also possible that optimism influences choice primarily under conditions of ambiguity or under long-term uncertainty rather than in tasks with clear probabilistic structure. Because our tasks presented either explicit probabilities or provided the opportunity to sample a substantial number of draws, the uncertainty may have been perceived as low, reducing the role of optimism. Beyond the main hypotheses, our exploratory analyses provided additional insights. Sampling behavior in the experience condition did not show a strong relationship with subsequent risky choices. The number of rare events observed per participant did not correlate significantly with overall risky choices, and observing a rare event on a particular trial only weakly predicted avoidance of the risky option on that trial. These findings suggest that, within the constraints of our sampling regimen, exposure to rare events was not a dominant determinant of choice. One interpretation is that participants integrated sampling information in a noisy or unreliable way, perhaps due to limited attention or memory constraints. Another possibility is that participants used heuristics based on local patterns rather than on formal estimates of rare-event probability. If so, the number of rare events observed may not capture the psychological variable driving choice. These considerations point to the importance of measuring cognitive mediators directly in future work, such as soliciting probability estimates, confidence ratings, or memory probes for sampled outcomes. The null findings regarding the description–experience effect raise questions about the boundary conditions of the phenomenon. It may be that the description–experience gap emerges most clearly under conditions of very sparse sampling, when participants are unlikely to observe rare events in the experience condition and when probabilities in the description condition are presented in a particularly salient manner. By contrast, when sampling is generous or when descriptive probabilities are not emphasized, the gap may disappear. Experimental manipulations that systematically vary sample size, the salience of descriptive probabilities, and the concreteness of outcomes would help delineate these boundaries. In addition, varying the extremity of rare outcomes and the stakes involved may reveal conditions under which description–experience differences are more pronounced. The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the online sample and the partial incentive scheme may limit generalizability to high-stakes real-world decisions. Laboratory studies with fully incentivized choices might yield different patterns. Second, although the sample size was adequate for detecting small-to-moderate effects, the observed effect sizes for condition differences were smaller than expected, and a larger sample might be required to detect subtle effects. Third, the optimism measure was a self-report instrument that may not fully capture the facets of expectancy that are relevant to risky choice. Using multiple measures of optimism, including behavioral indices or state manipulations, could provide a more nuanced picture. Fourth, our sampling regime in the experience condition was fixed at 20 draws per option. While this design choice had practical benefits, it differed from many prior studies that use free sampling or different numbers of draws, and thus comparison across studies requires caution. Future research should explore different sampling rules, including free-sampling paradigms, to test how sampling behavior relates to choice. Despite these limitations, the study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, it provides an example of a rigorous attempt to replicate the description–experience gap with a balanced design including gains and losses and an individual difference measure. The failure to find a condition effect suggests that the description–experience gap may be more context-dependent than sometimes assumed. Second, the robust valence effect observed—fewer risky choices for losses than for gains—highlights that valence can interact with other task features to produce outcomes that diverge from classic prospect theory predictions. This finding adds nuance to our understanding of valence effects in risky choice and suggests the need for models that can accommodate context-dependent reversals. Third, the lack of association with dispositional optimism suggests that trait expectancies may not be primary drivers of micro-level gamble choices in contexts like those we studied. This null result points researchers toward alternative moderators and mediators, such as numeracy, affective responsiveness, memory for outcomes, or domain-specific beliefs. Several avenues for future research emerge from these findings. One promising direction is to manipulate sampling intensity systematically in the experience condition, ranging from very sparse to very dense sampling, to map how sampling availability shapes probability estimation and choice. Another direction is to incorporate memory probes and probability judgments to test whether underweighting of rare events stems from failure to sample, failure to recall, or biased weighting in judgment. A third direction is to probe the role of affect by measuring physiological responses or self-reported affective reactions to sampled outcomes, especially negative ones, to see whether affective salience mediates valence effects. A fourth direction is to examine whether optimism interacts with task framing or stakes; for example, manipulating current mood or inducing optimistic expectancies experimentally could reveal causal effects that trait measures do not capture. Finally, exploring the neural correlates of description versus experience processing using neuroimaging could reveal distinct circuits involved in deliberative processing versus sampling-based learning and might help bridge cognitive and neural accounts. In conclusion, the present study did not replicate a description–experience difference in risky choice but did reveal a robust valence effect inconsistent with classic prospect theory predictions, and it found no association between dispositional optimism and risky choice. The results underscore the complexity of risky decision making and the need to specify boundary conditions for prominent phenomena such as the description–experience gap. By highlighting task features and individual differences that may moderate these effects, the study suggests concrete experimental manipulations and measurement strategies for future work. Continued research that systematically varies sampling parameters, captures cognitive mediators, and explores affective and personality moderators will be essential for developing a more nuanced and generalizable theory of how people make risky choices from description and from experience.
