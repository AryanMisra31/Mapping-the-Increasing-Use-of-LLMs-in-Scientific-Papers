Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental biological process that supports cognitive functioning, emotional regulation, physical recovery, and overall health. Over the past several decades, there has been growing scientific and public interest in how lifestyle behaviors, particularly physical exercise, interact with sleep. Exercise has been proposed as a behavioral strategy to improve sleep for clinical and nonclinical populations because it influences physiological arousal, thermoregulation, mood, and circadian timing mechanisms. At the same time, sleep quality can influence the capacity to engage in and benefit from exercise through effects on recovery, performance, and motivation. This bidirectional conceptualization of the exercise–sleep relationship has motivated a large and growing literature that spans laboratory experiments, large-scale epidemiological studies, and applied research with athletes and performing artists. Within that literature, there is substantial heterogeneity in findings, with some studies reporting robust positive associations between physical activity and improved sleep outcomes and others reporting null or even negative effects under particular conditions. These mixed findings raise important theoretical and practical questions about when, for whom, and how exercise affects sleep.

Mechanistic accounts have emphasized multiple pathways by which exercise could influence sleep. Acute exercise increases body temperature and sympathetic activity during and immediately after exertion, followed by a post-exercise cooling and parasympathetic rebound that may promote sleep onset and depth. Repeated physical training induces adaptations in autonomic function, endocrine systems, and energy balance that could alter sleep architecture over time. Exercise also improves mood and reduces symptoms of anxiety and depression, which are frequently linked to sleep disturbance, and thereby could foster better sleep indirectly. Conversely, intense training sessions close to bedtime can maintain heightened physiological arousal or delay circadian processes and thus interfere with sleep onset. The timing, intensity, duration, and type of exercise have therefore been emphasized as critical moderators in conceptual models of the relationship between exercise and sleep.

The literature on athletes and performing artists is especially informative because these populations often engage in high volumes of structured physical activity, experience unique stressors related to performance and scheduling, and sometimes adopt compensatory recovery behaviors. Studies in elite and sub-elite athletes have reported mixed results; some research indicates that regular training and higher fitness levels are associated with better subjective sleep, while other work has found that elite athletes report shorter duration and poorer quality sleep than nonathletes, potentially due to travel, competition stress, and training load. Similarly, research with dancers has highlighted specific occupational factors that may influence sleep, including irregular rehearsal and performance schedules, weight and aesthetic concerns, and a culture that normalizes high training loads. Despite these specificities, relatively few studies have directly compared dancers with other types of athletes or investigated whether relationships between exercise characteristics and sleep quality differ systematically across these groups.

Methodological heterogeneity has complicated attempts to synthesize findings across studies. Research designs vary in whether they examine acute versus chronic exercise effects, whether they use objective measures such as actigraphy and polysomnography versus self-report instruments, and whether they control for potentially confounding variables such as age, sex, training volume, travel, caffeine intake, and mental health. Sample characteristics also vary widely; some studies focus on clinical populations with insomnia, others on recreationally active adults, and others on elite athletes undergoing intense training cycles. These differences in methods and samples contribute to inconsistent findings and limit the extent to which clear recommendations can be made for coaches, clinicians, and performers.

One recurrent theme in the literature is that moderate-intensity exercise appears most reliably associated with improved sleep, whereas very high-intensity training may sometimes be associated with poorer sleep metrics. This inverted-U proposition suggests that the relationship between exercise intensity and sleep quality could be nonlinear. For example, moderate activation of thermoregulatory and mood-enhancing pathways may facilitate sleep, while excessive training-induced stress and sympathetic overactivation may impair sleep. Yet many field studies have operationalized exercise in coarse terms that do not distinguish intensity from duration or frequency, making it difficult to isolate the specific contribution of intensity per se.

Self-report measures of sleep quality are widely used in applied settings and have strong face validity, but they are subject to recall bias and may not capture nuanced aspects of sleep architecture that could be sensitive to exercise-induced changes. Objective measures such as actigraphy provide continuous, real-world data on sleep timing and duration but may still miss subjective aspects of sleep quality that influence daytime functioning. Laboratory-based polysomnography is the gold standard for sleep architecture measurement, but it is costly and less practical for large samples or for capturing habitual sleep patterns. Consequently, there remains a need for studies that balance ecological validity with measurement precision and that examine the exercise–sleep relationship in specific subpopulations such as dancers and athletes.

Given the heterogeneity of findings and the methodological complexities, field studies that apply consistent measurement strategies across comparable groups can yield valuable insights. In particular, studies that use standardized questionnaires to quantify exercise intensity and sleep quality in both dancers and athletes can help clarify whether the association between intensity and sleep is generalizable or whether it differs across disciplines. Such studies can also provide pragmatic information for practitioners who must make decisions based on self-reported data in real-world settings.

The present study aimed to investigate the relationship between exercise intensity and subjective sleep quality in a sample of dancers and athletes using questionnaire-based assessments. A central rationale was to test whether exercise intensity, as reported by participants, would be systematically associated with sleep quality and whether any association would differ between dancers and other athletes. This rationale is informed by prior work suggesting both beneficial effects of moderate exercise on sleep and potential deleterious effects of very intense training, as well as by the practical importance of understanding how intensity relates to sleep across disciplines with differing training cultures.

We chose to operationalize exercise intensity through a composite score based on weekly training habits and perceived exertion across sessions, and we operationalized sleep quality using a standardized sleep questionnaire that yields an overall score reflecting subjective sleep quality. Using these instruments allowed us to derive continuous measures for both constructs and to conduct correlational analyses across the full sample and within discipline-specific subgroups. An overarching hypothesis was that higher exercise intensity would be associated with better subjective sleep quality up to a point, but that very high intensity might either show weaker associations or be linked to poorer sleep. In light of the cross-sectional, questionnaire-based design, however, we also acknowledged that the study would be limited in its ability to infer causality and would be sensitive primarily to medium-to-large effect sizes.

In summary, the key aims of the study were to: (1) quantify the relationship between exercise intensity and subjective sleep quality in a sample of dancers and athletes; (2) examine whether any association held across the entire sample and within each group separately; and (3) assess the robustness of results to alternative nonparametric tests and basic covariate adjustments. Clarifying whether self-reported exercise intensity relates to sleep quality in these populations has both theoretical implications for models of exercise–sleep interactions and practical implications for performance and recovery management. The present study therefore contributed to a literature characterized by mixed findings and methodological diversity by applying a consistent measurement approach to a sample of 100 participants drawn from dance and athletic contexts.

Methods

Participants were recruited from local dance schools, university athletic programs, and community sports clubs. A total of 100 participants completed the study, with 50 identifying primarily as dancers and 50 identifying primarily as athletes in sports other than dance. All participants were aged between 18 and 35 years, with the mean age of the full sample being 24.7 years (SD = 4.3). Both sexes were represented; 58 participants identified as female, 40 as male, and 2 as nonbinary. Inclusion criteria required that participants were currently training at least three times per week, were free of acute injury that prevented regular training at the time of the study, and were willing to complete online questionnaires about training habits and sleep. Exclusion criteria included a self-reported diagnosis of a chronic sleep disorder (e.g., obstructive sleep apnea, chronic insomnia) or use of prescribed sleep medications on a nightly basis; participants who reported such conditions were excluded from the final sample. Recruitment materials described the purpose of the study as an investigation into exercise and sleep in active individuals, and participants received no monetary compensation, though they were offered a summary of the study findings upon request.

Procedures were approved by the research ethics committee of the affiliated institution, and informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants before they initiated the online survey. Data collection occurred during a single time window spanning six weeks in which participants completed the questionnaires once. The survey platform prevented multiple submissions from the same device to minimize duplicate entries. Participants first completed demographic items (age, sex, primary discipline, average weekly training hours) and then completed two standardized questionnaires designed to quantify exercise intensity and subjective sleep quality, respectively. Each questionnaire included instructions emphasizing honest and reflective responses based on a typical recent period of training and sleep over the previous month.

Exercise intensity was measured using a composite exercise intensity questionnaire developed for the present study that integrated elements commonly used in field research. Participants reported the average number of training sessions per week, typical session duration in minutes, and the proportion of sessions they would characterize as low-, moderate-, or high-intensity. Participants also provided an average rating of perceived exertion (RPE) for typical sessions using a 10-point scale with anchors at 1 (very light) and 10 (maximal). These components were combined into a single exercise intensity score on a 0–100 scale through a predefined algorithm: session frequency and session duration were multiplied to estimate weekly training minutes, the RPE score was rescaled to a 0–100 range, and the proportion of high-intensity sessions served as a weighting factor. The weighted components were summed and rescaled to produce the final exercise intensity score, where higher values indicate greater typical exercise intensity and load.

Subjective sleep quality was assessed using a well-established sleep quality questionnaire widely used in field and clinical settings. The questionnaire yields a global sleep quality score that reflects aspects such as sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep disturbances, and daytime dysfunction. For ease of interpretation in the present study, the global sleep quality score was transformed to a 0–100 scale with higher values representing better subjective sleep quality. Item responses were reverse-scored and standardized as necessary before aggregation so that the direction of the final score aligned with the exercise intensity score. Internal consistency of both the exercise intensity composite and the sleep quality questionnaire was assessed with Cronbach’s alpha.

Data screening procedures were applied prior to statistical analyses. Cases were inspected for missing values, implausible responses, and response patterns suggestive of inattention (e.g., identical responses to all items). Participants with more than 20% missing data on either questionnaire were excluded, although in the final sample no participant met this exclusion criterion. Univariate distributions of the exercise intensity and sleep quality scores were examined for normality using histograms, skewness and kurtosis metrics, and Shapiro–Wilk tests. Bivariate scatterplots were inspected for influential outliers and nonlinear patterns.

The primary statistical analysis was a bivariate Pearson correlation assessing the association between exercise intensity and subjective sleep quality across the full sample of 100 participants. Given the possibility of non-normal distributions or outliers influencing Pearson correlations, Spearman rank correlations were also computed as sensitivity analyses. In addition, separate Pearson correlations were conducted within the dancer and athlete subgroups to evaluate whether relationships differed by discipline. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, ranges) were reported for both scores overall and by group. Independent samples t-tests compared dancers and athletes on exercise intensity and sleep quality to determine whether the groups differed in mean levels of these variables. Effect sizes were calculated for t-tests using Cohen’s d.

To further assess the robustness of results, multiple linear regression models were estimated with sleep quality as the dependent variable and exercise intensity as the independent variable, controlling for age, sex, and weekly training hours. Interaction terms between group (dancer vs athlete) and exercise intensity were included in additional models to formally test whether the slope relating intensity to sleep quality differed between groups. Diagnostic checks of regression assumptions included residual plots, variance inflation factors to detect multicollinearity, and Cook’s distance values to identify influential cases. Given the sample size, post hoc sensitivity analyses were conducted to estimate the minimum detectable effect size for correlations at alpha = .05 and power = .80.

All statistical analyses were conducted using standard software. Statistical significance was evaluated using an alpha level of .05, and two-tailed tests were used throughout. Given the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses and multiple comparisons, results were interpreted with caution and emphasis placed on effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than p-values alone.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the full sample and by subgroup are presented first. For the full sample of 100 participants, the exercise intensity composite score had a mean of 64.8 (SD = 14.7), with a range from 22 to 95 on the 0–100 scale. The sleep quality score for the full sample had a mean of 71.9 (SD = 15.3), with a range from 28 to 98 on the 0–100 scale. Within the dancer subgroup (n = 50), the mean exercise intensity score was 66.2 (SD = 13.9), and the mean sleep quality score was 73.1 (SD = 14.6). Within the athlete subgroup (n = 50), the mean exercise intensity score was 63.4 (SD = 15.4), and the mean sleep quality score was 70.8 (SD = 15.9). These descriptive statistics indicate generally high levels of regular training and relatively good subjective sleep quality across the sample.

Internal consistency of the measures was acceptable. The exercise intensity composite exhibited a Cronbach’s alpha of .79 for the full sample, reflecting reasonably coherent measurement across its constituent items. The sleep quality questionnaire showed a Cronbach’s alpha of .83, indicating good internal reliability for the aggregated subjective sleep score. These reliability estimates supported proceeding with correlational analyses using aggregate scores.

Data screening revealed no cases with excessive missing data and no indications of patterned inattention. Examination of univariate distributions showed that both exercise intensity and sleep quality were approximately normally distributed, with skewness and kurtosis values within acceptable ranges and Shapiro–Wilk tests that did not indicate extreme departures from normality for either variable at the sample size used. Visual inspection of scatterplots did not reveal pronounced nonlinear patterns. A small number of cases approached the edges of the distributions but did not exhibit undue influence on parameter estimates based on Cook’s distance values computed in regression diagnostics.

The primary analysis, a Pearson correlation between exercise intensity and subjective sleep quality across the full sample of 100 participants, yielded a correlation coefficient of r = .06. This correlation was small in magnitude and not statistically significant (p = .55). The 95% confidence interval for the correlation ranged approximately from −.12 to .24, indicating that the true population-level linear association could plausibly be slightly negative, near zero, or modestly positive within that interval. A Spearman rank correlation conducted as a sensitivity analysis produced a similar result, rho = .05 (p = .62), further suggesting the absence of a monotonic relationship in the observed data.

Separate correlations within subgroups showed that the association between exercise intensity and sleep quality did not differ appreciably between dancers and athletes. In the dancer subgroup (n = 50), the Pearson correlation was r = .09 (p = .53), whereas in the athlete subgroup (n = 50) the Pearson correlation was r = −.03 (p = .83). Spearman correlations within groups mirrored these patterns. Confidence intervals for subgroup correlations were wider due to smaller sample sizes, and none excluded zero.

Independent samples t-tests comparing dancers and athletes on mean exercise intensity and mean sleep quality produced non-significant group differences. For exercise intensity, the t-test yielded t(98) = 1.01, p = .31, with Cohen’s d = 0.20 indicating a small and negligible effect size favoring slightly higher intensity among dancers. For sleep quality, the t-test yielded t(98) = 0.70, p = .49, with Cohen’s d = 0.14 indicating a small and negligible difference between groups. These results indicated that the two groups were broadly comparable on the primary variables of interest, supporting the interpretability of pooled analyses.

Regression analyses that treated sleep quality as the outcome and included exercise intensity as a predictor, along with covariates age, sex, and weekly training hours, corroborated the correlational findings. In these models, exercise intensity did not emerge as a significant predictor of sleep quality after adjustment for covariates (standardized beta = .05, p = .61). None of the covariates were significant predictors in the adjusted models, although weekly training hours showed a small nonsignificant negative association with sleep quality (beta = −.12, p = .24). Inclusion of an interaction term between group (dancer vs athlete) and exercise intensity did not yield a significant interaction (beta = .04, p = .68), indicating that the slope of the intensity–sleep relationship did not differ significantly by group.

Diagnostic checks on the regression models showed no violations of standard assumptions. Residuals were approximately normally distributed and homoscedastic, variance inflation factors were below 1.5 for all predictors indicating low multicollinearity, and Cook’s distance values were all below conventional thresholds for undue influence. These diagnostics supported the stability of parameter estimates from the regression analyses.

Given the null results, we conducted a post hoc sensitivity analysis to evaluate the minimum detectable correlation given the sample size. With N = 100, an alpha level of .05, and power = .80, the study was powered to detect correlations of approximately r = .27 or larger. This sensitivity analysis indicated that the present study was adequately powered to detect medium-sized associations but had limited power to detect small effects (e.g., r < .20). Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant correlation in the present sample should be interpreted as evidence against medium or large linear associations between self-reported exercise intensity and subjective sleep quality, while acknowledging that small associations could exist and remain undetected.

Additional exploratory analyses examined whether nonlinear relationships might be present. A quadratic term for exercise intensity was added to a regression model to evaluate potential inverted-U patterns; the quadratic term was not significant (p = .71), and model fit did not improve meaningfully. Similarly, scatterplots with locally weighted smoothing lines did not suggest pronounced nonlinearities. These exploratory checks provided little evidence that a nonlinear association of substantive magnitude was present in the sample.

In summary, across multiple analytic approaches—including Pearson and Spearman correlations, subgroup analyses, adjusted regression models, and exploratory assessments of nonlinearity—exercise intensity as measured by the composite questionnaire was not significantly associated with subjective sleep quality in a sample of 100 active participants. This pattern held when considering dancers and athletes separately and after controlling for basic demographic and training covariates. The study was adequately powered to detect medium-sized effects but less sensitive to small effects, which should be considered when interpreting the null findings.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between self-reported exercise intensity and subjective sleep quality in a sample of 100 active individuals, half identifying as dancers and half as athletes in other sports. Across a series of analyses including bivariate correlations, subgroup correlations, adjusted regression models, and exploratory tests for nonlinearity, there was no evidence of a significant association between exercise intensity and subjective sleep quality. The primary Pearson correlation across the full sample was small and nonsignificant, and comparable null results were observed within dancers and athletes separately. Regression models that included basic covariates likewise did not identify intensity as a predictor of sleep quality. These findings contribute to the broader literature by documenting a null association in a field-based sample of performers and athletes assessed with self-report instruments.

Interpreting null findings requires attention to several possible explanations and contextualization within prior work. First, the absence of a significant association in the present study does not necessarily imply that exercise never affects sleep. Rather, it indicates that within this sample and using these measurement approaches, there was no robust linear relationship of medium or larger magnitude between the composite measure of exercise intensity and the subjective sleep quality index. This distinction is important because prior literature has produced mixed results, and methodological variability across studies can account for some of the divergence. The mechanisms by which exercise influences sleep may operate through facets of exercise not captured fully by intensity alone, such as timing of sessions, session duration, cumulative training load, variation in training across weeks, and the proximity of high-intensity sessions to bedtime. The composite exercise intensity score used here incorporated frequency, duration, perceived exertion, and proportion of high-intensity sessions, but it may nevertheless have lacked sensitivity to capture critical temporal features (e.g., late-evening high-intensity workouts) that could disturb sleep acutely.

Second, the measurement of sleep quality through a subjective questionnaire captures participants’ global impressions of their sleep experience, which may or may not align with objective indices of sleep architecture and continuity. It has been documented in prior research that subjective and objective sleep measures can diverge; for instance, individuals may report poor sleep despite relatively normal actigraphic or polysomnographic measures, and vice versa. In populations such as athletes and dancers, performance-related stressors or expectations about sleep might influence subjective appraisals independently of physiological sleep parameters. Therefore, the null relationship observed for subjective sleep quality in this study may not generalize to objective sleep metrics, and future research that incorporates actigraphy or polysomnography would be valuable to determine whether exercise intensity relates to sleep duration, sleep efficiency, or sleep stage composition in ways not reflected in self-report.

Third, sample characteristics and restricted range can attenuate observed associations. The participants in this study were all regularly training individuals, with relatively high average exercise intensity and above-average subjective sleep quality. This homogeneity may have restricted variability and reduced the ability to detect associations. In many field samples, including those composed of athletes and dancers, training cultures produce clustering around high levels of activity, which can make it challenging to observe contrasts that might be present across broader ranges of exercise behavior. Moreover, because the sample excluded individuals with reported chronic sleep disorders, the sample may have been biased toward those with better sleep, further narrowing variance. Future studies might consider recruiting across wider ranges of training intensities and including comparison groups with lower activity levels or clinical sleep disturbances to better characterize dose–response relationships.

Fourth, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference and the capacity to parse bidirectional dynamics. It is plausible that sleep influences subsequent exercise behaviors (e.g., poor sleep leading to reduced intensity or skipped sessions) while cumulative training influences sleep through recovery processes. A cross-sectional snapshot may therefore conflate directional effects or obscure lagged relationships that unfold over days or weeks. Longitudinal designs, including intensive repeated-measures studies that sample sleep and training on a nightly or daily basis, would be better suited to disentangle temporal dynamics and to identify short-term causal pathways such as acute effects of evening high-intensity workouts on sleep onset or chronic adaptations across training cycles.

Fifth, potential confounding variables not measured or controlled in the present study could obscure relationships. Factors such as caffeine intake, alcohol consumption, travel and time-zone changes, competition schedules, psychological stress, chronotype, medication use, and menstrual cycle phase can influence sleep and may also be associated with training behaviors. Without accounting for these variables, it is difficult to attribute the absence of a relationship solely to the nonexistence of an effect. Future research designs would benefit from more comprehensive assessment of lifestyle, contextual, and biological factors to enable more precise modeling of exercise–sleep links.

Despite these limitations, the study has strengths that deserve acknowledgment. The use of standardized instruments and transparent coding procedures allowed for consistent measurement across participants, and the inclusion of both dancers and other athletes facilitated an evaluation of group-specific patterns. Reliability analyses indicated acceptable internal consistency for the composite measures. Sensitivity analyses using nonparametric correlations and regression diagnostics supported the robustness of the null findings within the measurement framework employed. Additionally, the study’s focus on field-relevant self-report measures aligns with the data types typically available to coaches and practitioners, and thus the findings have pragmatic relevance.

The implications of the present null results for theory and practice should be considered carefully. Theoretically, these results underscore that the relationship between exercise and sleep is likely contingent on multiple interacting factors and cannot be reduced to a simple main effect of overall intensity, at least within relatively homogeneous samples of regularly active individuals. The lack of a straightforward positive association highlights the importance of specifying moderators such as timing, acute versus chronic training, individual recovery capacity, and contextual stressors in models of exercise–sleep interactions. For practitioners including coaches, choreographers, and sports medicine clinicians, the findings suggest caution in assuming that increasing or decreasing overall training intensity will reliably improve or impair subjective sleep quality across all performers. Instead, individualized monitoring that considers session timing, prior sleep, and other lifestyle factors may be warranted.

Several avenues for future research arise directly from the limitations and findings of the present work. First, intensive longitudinal studies employing daily sleep logs, actigraphy, and training diaries could capture within-person variability and temporal ordering to identify acute and lagged effects of training intensity on sleep. Second, experimental intervention studies that manipulate training intensity and monitor subsequent sleep using objective measures would provide causal evidence regarding directionality. Third, studies that stratify samples by chronotype, recovery profiles, or baseline sleep quality could test whether certain subgroups show stronger exercise–sleep associations. Fourth, research that examines physiological mediators such as heart rate variability, cortisol, and core body temperature could illuminate mechanistic pathways linking training load to sleep physiology. Fifth, given concerns about small effects, larger multi-site studies may be necessary to detect subtle but potentially meaningful associations.

In closing, the present study found no significant association between self-reported exercise intensity and subjective sleep quality in a sample of dancers and athletes, whether analyzed across the full sample or within subgroups. While this null result does not rule out nuanced or conditional relationships between exercise and sleep, it suggests that, in similar field-based samples and using comparable self-report measures, exercise intensity alone may not be a reliable predictor of subjective sleep quality. Future research employing longitudinal designs, objective sleep measures, and broader assessments of contextual and individual difference variables is needed to further clarify how training practices influence sleep in performers and athletes.
