Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental biological process that is essential for physical health, cognitive functioning, and emotional regulation. Poor sleep quality has been consistently linked to a broad range of adverse outcomes, including impaired attention and memory, mood disturbance, reduced occupational and academic performance, and increased risk for chronic health conditions. The prevalence of sleep disturbance in modern populations has raised substantial concern among health professionals and researchers alike, prompting investigation into behavioral and environmental contributors to sleep disruption. One behavioral domain that has received increasing attention as a potential correlate and contributor to disturbed sleep is the use of social media. Social media platforms are now pervasive across age groups, and usage patterns have changed rapidly over the last decade. Many individuals engage with multiple platforms throughout the day and night, and the integration of social media into daily life creates novel opportunities for cognitive and emotional stimulation at hours when the brain historically engaged in restorative processes. Given the public health significance of sleep and the ubiquity of social media, exploring the relationship between social media use and sleep quality is a timely and important endeavor.

There are several plausible mechanisms by which social media use might affect sleep. First, the exposure to blue-spectrum light emitted by screens can influence circadian rhythms by suppressing melatonin secretion, thereby delaying sleep onset and reducing sleep depth. Second, social media interactions can increase cognitive and emotional arousal; engaging content, social comparison, and emotionally charged communications can elevate physiological and psychological activation in ways that impede the winding down process prior to sleep. Third, the temporal displacement hypothesis suggests that time spent on social media directly reduces time available for sleep, particularly when social media use occurs in the late evening or during the night. Finally, the content and social rewards associated with social media may create conditioned behaviors that make it difficult for users to disengage, thereby increasing the likelihood of continued device use at night. Each of these mechanisms points to potential pathways from social media behavior to poorer sleep quality, and the mechanisms are not mutually exclusive.

Empirical research examining associations between social media use and sleep quality has proliferated, and a consistent pattern has emerged in many, though not all, studies. Many investigations report positive associations between the amount of time spent on social media or the intensity of engagement and indices of poorer sleep quality, including longer sleep latency, shorter total sleep time, increased sleep fragmentation, and greater subjective daytime dysfunction. Studies have also identified associations between evening or bedtime social media use and delayed sleep onset and later wake times. In contrast, some research has found more nuanced results, with the type of social media activity and the timing of use moderating the relationship with sleep outcomes. For example, passive consumption of social feeds may have different effects on sleep than active social interaction; similarly, emotionally stimulating content may be a stronger predictor of sleep disturbance than neutral content. Given this pattern of findings, a more detailed examination of the relationship between general social media use and perceived sleep quality remains necessary.

Gender differences in both social media behavior and sleep patterns have also been reported in the literature. Some research indicates that women are more likely than men to use certain social media platforms or to engage in specific social behaviors online. Women have been reported to use a wider range of social media sites and to engage more in social and communicative aspects of social media, whereas men may be more likely to use platforms for information-seeking or gaming. In terms of sleep, sex differences have also been observed in prevalence rates of insomnia symptoms and in self-reported sleep quality, although findings are heterogeneous across samples and measurement approaches. Factors such as hormonal fluctuations, caregiving responsibilities, and psychosocial stressors may contribute to observed differences in sleep patterns by gender. Given the potential intersection of gendered patterns of social media use and gendered patterns of sleep, it is important to consider whether relationships between social media behavior and sleep differ by gender or whether gender independently predicts social media use or sleep quality.

Despite a growing body of research, gaps and inconsistencies remain. Many studies employ convenience samples that are heavily skewed toward young adult or college populations, limiting generalizability to broader age ranges. Reliance on single-item or crude measures of social media exposure (e.g., total screen time) does not always capture the multifaceted nature of social media behavior, which includes the number of platforms used, the purposes for use, and the emotional valence of interactions. Similarly, while objective measures of sleep such as actigraphy and polysomnography provide detailed information, many studies rely on subjective reports of sleep quality due to feasibility constraints. The use of validated multi-item scales for both social media engagement and sleep quality is therefore valuable when examining associations in cross-sectional datasets.

In addition to measurement considerations, interpretive challenges arise because most studies are correlational, making causal inferences precarious. Poor sleep may lead individuals to use social media more (e.g., wakefulness during the night leading to device use), social media use may lead to poorer sleep, or both may be influenced by shared third variables such as stress, psychopathology, or lifestyle factors. Longitudinal and experimental work has been called for to disentangle these possibilities, but cross-sectional correlational studies remain useful for establishing the presence and magnitude of relationships that merit further investigation.

The present study seeks to address some of these considerations by employing validated self-report instruments that capture general social media engagement and overall sleep quality in a balanced sample of men and women. Specifically, the study uses a Social Media Usage Questionnaire (SMUQ), which assesses intensity of use, mood-related use, and interference with daily functioning, together with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), which yields a global index of perceived sleep quality over the preceding month. Inclusion of a count of the number of social media platforms used allows for an examination of breadth of platform engagement in addition to intensity. By recruiting a sample with an equal number of male and female participants, the study is positioned to examine gender differences in social media behaviors and sleep quality as well as to assess whether gender is associated with the number of platforms used.

Based on the existing literature, several hypotheses were specified. First, it was hypothesized that total social media usage as indexed by the SMUQ would be positively associated with global PSQI scores, indicating that greater social media engagement would be associated with poorer sleep quality. Second, it was hypothesized that gender would be associated with the number of social media sites used, with women expected to report use of a greater number of platforms than men. Third, it was hypothesized that there would be no significant gender differences in global sleep quality, reflecting mixed findings in prior research and the possibility that social media use may be a more proximal correlate of sleep than gender per se. Finally, exploratory analyses were planned to consider whether the number of social media sites was associated with PSQI scores and whether associations between SMUQ and PSQI persisted after controlling for demographic factors.

The rationale for these hypotheses rests on both mechanistic and empirical grounds. Mechanistically, intensity and breadth of social media engagement may increase opportunities for late-night exposure, cognitive/emotional arousal, and circadian disruption, all of which are expected to worsen sleep quality. Empirically, previous studies that measured aspects of social media intensity and timing have found associations with sleep disturbance, lending support to the expectation that a validated self-report measure of social media use would correlate with a well-established sleep quality instrument. The gender hypothesis draws on patterns observed in prior surveys showing that women often engage with a larger number of social platforms and use social media for social connection, which may drive the count of platforms used. Finally, the lack of consistent gender differences in global sleep quality across studies suggests that an absence of gender disparity in the current sample is plausible.

To test these hypotheses, a correlational design with standardized questionnaires was implemented. The design allows for assessment of associations between continuous measures of social media use and sleep quality, as well as for group comparisons based on gender. The cross-sectional nature of the design precludes causal claims, but the balanced sampling by gender and the use of validated instruments allow for robust examination of the patterns of association in the target sample. The study thereby contributes to the literature by offering an empirically grounded snapshot of the relationships among gender, social media engagement, and perceived sleep quality in a moderate-sized sample of adults.

Methods

Participants were one hundred adults recruited through a combination of online advertisements on social media platforms and postings at a university subject pool. The sample comprised 50 self-identified males and 50 self-identified females. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years of age and fluent in the study language; there were no upper age limits. Exclusion criteria included self-report of a diagnosed sleep disorder that was currently untreated (e.g., untreated obstructive sleep apnea) and current shift work that involved regular night shifts, as these factors could introduce substantial confounding influences on sleep patterns. Individuals who endorsed current night-shift employment were screened out. Data collection occurred over a two-month period, and interested individuals were provided with an online informed consent form prior to participation. Participants completed the questionnaires in a single session via a secure online survey platform.

Demographic information collected included age in years, gender (male, female), educational attainment (high school, some college, college degree, postgraduate), employment status (student, employed full-time, employed part-time, unemployed), typical bedtime and wake time, and self-reported health status (excellent, good, fair, poor). Participants were also asked to list which social media platforms they used regularly from a checklist that included major platforms and an open-ended "other" option; a simple count of platforms endorsed was computed to represent number of social media sites used. The decision to use a count of platforms was intended to capture breadth of platform engagement, which may reflect both diversity of social media exposure and varied functional uses.

The Social Media Usage Questionnaire (SMUQ) was administered to assess participants’ intensity of and attitudes toward social media engagement. The SMUQ used in the present study comprised 20 items that probed frequency of use, time spent, mood-related use (e.g., using social media when feeling lonely or anxious), and interference with daily activities and pre-sleep routines. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree/never) to 5 (strongly agree/very often) depending on the item stem, and higher total scores indicated greater problematic or intensive social media engagement. The SMUQ provided a continuous score that was used in correlational and regression analyses. Prior to analyses, internal consistency of the SMUQ within the sample was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha.

Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a widely used self-report instrument that yields a global sleep quality score based on seven component scores: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component contributes to a global score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating worse sleep quality. The PSQI global score was the primary sleep outcome for correlation and group comparison analyses. Component scores were also examined in exploratory analyses to identify which aspects of sleep quality were most strongly associated with social media measures.

Procedure details included an initial screening for eligibility followed by consent and completion of the online battery. Participants were instructed to answer items based on their typical behavior and experiences over the previous month. The survey took approximately 20 minutes to complete on average. No monetary compensation was provided; participants could optionally enter a raffle for one of five gift cards as an incentive. All procedures were approved by an institutional review board prior to data collection.

Statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, ranges) were calculated for age, SMUQ total score, PSQI global score, and number of social media platforms used. Internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) was calculated for the SMUQ and for the PSQI global measure. The primary analyses included Pearson product-moment correlation to test the association between SMUQ total score and PSQI global score. The relationship between gender and number of social media platforms used was tested using an independent samples t-test comparing the mean number of platforms endorsed by men versus women; in addition, a point-biserial correlation was computed treating gender as a dichotomous variable coded 0/1 for analytic completeness. To examine gender differences in sleep quality, an independent samples t-test was performed comparing PSQI global scores across male and female participants.

Assumptions for parametric tests were evaluated. Normality of continuous variables was assessed via skewness, kurtosis, and visual inspection of histograms. Homogeneity of variance for t-tests was evaluated using Levene’s test; where Levene’s test was significant, Welch’s t-test correction was applied. Outliers were considered via inspection of boxplots and z-scores; no extreme outliers were removed unless clearly indicative of recording error. Given the sample size of 100, the analyses had adequate power to detect medium-sized effects. Effect sizes were reported, including Pearson’s r for correlations and Cohen’s d for t-tests, to aid interpretation of the magnitude of observed effects.

To further probe the robustness of the association between social media use and sleep quality, a multiple linear regression analysis was conducted with PSQI global score as the dependent variable and SMUQ total score as the primary predictor. Age and gender were entered as covariates in the model to account for potential demographic confounds. Multicollinearity diagnostics were examined through variance inflation factors (VIFs). Residuals were inspected to ensure homoscedasticity and linearity assumptions were reasonable. Statistical significance was evaluated at the conventional alpha level of .05 for primary hypotheses; exploratory analyses were interpreted with caution, and emphasis was placed on effect sizes and confidence intervals.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary study variables are presented first. The sample mean age was 24.6 years (SD = 6.1), with ages ranging from 18 to 46 years. The SMUQ total score had a mean of 48.7 (SD = 12.3) on a potential range of 20 to 100, indicating moderate variability in social media engagement across participants. The PSQI global score had a mean of 7.0 (SD = 3.2), with scores ranging from 1 to 17. By conventional PSQI interpretation, a global score greater than 5 is commonly taken to indicate poor sleep quality; in this sample, 62% of participants had PSQI scores above this threshold. The number of social media platforms used ranged from 1 to 7, with an average of 3.45 platforms (SD = 1.35). The distribution of the number of platforms was approximately symmetric, with a slight positive skew. Descriptive breakdown by gender showed that women reported a slightly higher mean number of platforms and a marginally higher SMUQ score, whereas mean PSQI scores were similar across genders; these observations are examined in inferential analyses below.

Reliability analyses indicated acceptable internal consistency for the SMUQ in the present sample, with Cronbach’s alpha = .88. The PSQI global score also demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha = .77. Given these reliability estimates, the scales were deemed suitable for correlational and regression analyses.

The primary correlation of interest—between SMUQ total score and PSQI global score—was performed using Pearson correlation. The SMUQ total score was positively and significantly correlated with PSQI global score, r(98) = .42, p < .001. This correlation indicates that higher self-reported social media engagement and problematic use were associated with poorer perceived sleep quality. The strength of the relationship can be interpreted as a moderate effect size according to conventional benchmarks. Inspection of the scatterplot revealed a positive association across the range of observed values without evidence of gross non-linearity; no single case appeared to unduly influence the correlation.

A multiple regression analysis was conducted to examine whether the association between SMUQ and PSQI remained after controlling for age and gender. The overall model with SMUQ, age, and gender as predictors of PSQI global score was significant, F(3, 96) = 12.1, p < .001, and accounted for approximately 27% of the variance in PSQI scores (adjusted R^2 = .27). Within the model, SMUQ was a significant predictor of PSQI (standardized beta = .40, t = 4.60, p < .001), indicating that higher social media engagement was associated with worse sleep quality even after controlling for demographic covariates. Age was not a significant predictor in the model (beta = -.09, t = -0.95, p = .34), and gender did not reach significance as a predictor of PSQI when SMUQ and age were included (beta = .05, t = 0.56, p = .58). Multicollinearity diagnostics showed VIFs below 1.2 for all predictors, suggesting multicollinearity was not a concern. Examination of residuals indicated homoscedasticity and approximate normality; therefore, regression assumptions were reasonably satisfied.

The relationship between gender and the number of social media platforms used was examined next. An independent samples t-test comparing the mean number of platforms used by women and men indicated that women reported using a greater number of social media platforms on average. Women (n = 50) reported a mean of 3.80 platforms (SD = 1.20), whereas men (n = 50) reported a mean of 3.10 platforms (SD = 1.30). The t-test showed this difference to be statistically significant, t(98) = 2.89, p = .005, with a medium effect size, Cohen’s d = 0.58. Levene’s test for equality of variances was non-significant, suggesting equal variances could be assumed. Consistent with this group comparison, the point-biserial correlation between gender (female coded as 1, male coded as 0) and number of platforms was r_pb = .28, p = .004, indicating a modest positive association such that identifying as female was associated with endorsing a greater number of social media platforms.

The association between gender and PSQI global score was also examined. Men had a mean PSQI score of 6.9 (SD = 3.1) and women had a mean of 7.1 (SD = 3.3). An independent samples t-test revealed no significant difference in PSQI global scores between men and women, t(98) = 0.37, p = .71, Cohen’s d = 0.06. Levene’s test was non-significant for this comparison as well. The point-biserial correlation between gender and PSQI global score was negligible (r_pb = .04, p = .71), reinforcing the lack of a gender effect on sleep quality in the present sample.

Exploratory analyses considered whether the number of social media platforms used was associated with PSQI global score. The Pearson correlation between number of platforms and PSQI was positive but modest, r(98) = .18, p = .07, which did not reach conventional statistical significance at the .05 level. However, when number of platforms was entered into the regression model alongside SMUQ, it did not account for significant incremental variance in PSQI scores beyond that explained by SMUQ. In the regression, SMUQ remained a significant predictor (beta = .39, p < .001) whereas number of platforms showed a non-significant beta (beta = .10, p = .28). This pattern suggests that intensity and problematic aspects of social media use as captured by SMUQ are more strongly related to sleep quality than simply the breadth of platform engagement.

Given the possibility that particular components of the PSQI might be differentially associated with social media use, exploratory correlations were computed between SMUQ total and PSQI component scores. Significant positive correlations were observed between SMUQ and subjective sleep quality component (r = .35, p < .001), sleep latency component (r = .30, p = .002), and daytime dysfunction component (r = .41, p < .001). Correlations between SMUQ and other components such as sleep duration and sleep disturbances were smaller and did not reach significance after correction for multiple comparisons. These results suggest that social media engagement is more closely associated with perceived sleep quality and difficulties falling asleep and with daytime functional consequences than with objective duration indicators reported on the PSQI.

To ensure robustness, nonparametric Spearman rank correlations were also computed for primary associations. The Spearman correlation between SMUQ and PSQI was rho = .40, p < .001, consistent with the Pearson correlation. The nonparametric comparison of the number of platforms across gender via Mann-Whitney U test also yielded a significant result (U = 901.5, p = .006), corroborating the t-test outcome. No substantial differences in outcome patterns emerged from nonparametric approaches.

Finally, supplemental analyses examined whether employment status or educational attainment were associated with PSQI or SMUQ scores. Neither employment status nor educational attainment was significantly correlated with PSQI global score or with SMUQ total score after controlling for age. These supplemental tests suggest that the primary associations observed were not easily attributable to simple demographic confounds in the present sample.

In summary, the results indicated a statistically significant moderate positive association between social media usage intensity as assessed by SMUQ and poorer perceived sleep quality as assessed by PSQI. Women reported using a greater number of social media platforms than men, but there were no significant gender differences in global sleep quality. Breadth of platform use had a weaker and non-significant association with PSQI when considered alongside SMUQ, indicating that problematic or intensive engagement rather than mere number of platforms is likely to be the more salient correlate of poor perceived sleep in this sample.

Discussion

The present study examined associations among social media engagement, sleep quality, and gender in a balanced sample of 100 adults. The principal findings were that greater social media usage intensity and problematic engagement—as reflected in higher SMUQ scores—were associated with poorer perceived sleep quality on the PSQI, and that women reported using a larger number of social media platforms than men. No gender differences in overall sleep quality were observed. These results contribute to a growing literature suggesting that patterns of social media engagement are meaningfully related to self-reported sleep outcomes, and they also highlight that gender differences may be more evident in aspects of social media behavior (e.g., breadth of platform use) than in sleep quality per se.

The positive association between SMUQ and PSQI is consistent with theoretical perspectives and empirical work that emphasize behavioral, physiological, and cognitive pathways linking social media engagement to sleep disruption. The observed pattern—stronger associations with subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, and daytime dysfunction—suggests that social media engagement may particularly affect the subjective experience of sleep and the ability to initiate sleep, as well as the perceived daytime consequences of poor sleep. These outcomes are congruent with mechanisms such as heightened cognitive and emotional arousal prior to bedtime and the delay of the sleep period due to device use. Physiological mechanisms related to blue light exposure and consequent circadian phase delays may also contribute to difficulty falling asleep, which was captured by the sleep latency component association.

The findings that the number of social media platforms used was higher among women align with prior reports characterizing gender differences in the ways social media is used. Women in this sample endorsed using an average of roughly 3.8 platforms relative to men's 3.1 platforms, a difference that was statistically and practically meaningful. This observation may reflect gendered patterns of social interaction and platform selection; women may be more likely to engage across multiple contexts for social connection and communication, which could translate into the use of a broader array of platforms. However, it is important to emphasize that the number of platforms did not show a robust relationship with sleep quality when the intensity of social media engagement was accounted for. Thus, breadth of platform use alone may not be a primary driver of poor sleep; rather, how individuals use social media—frequency, timing, and emotional involvement—appears to be more consequential.

The absence of gender differences in PSQI global scores in this study is noteworthy. Although some prior work has reported higher rates of insomnia symptoms in women, other studies have found more nuanced or negligible gender distinctions, particularly when samples are young and otherwise healthy. It is possible that gender-related influences on sleep emerge in interaction with other life circumstances (e.g., caregiving burden, chronic illness, hormonal status) that were not prevalent in the current sample. Alternatively, the effects of social media use on sleep may operate similarly across genders such that any baseline gender disparities are attenuated. The regression analyses showing that gender did not uniquely predict PSQI scores after accounting for SMUQ and age further support the idea that social media engagement may be a more proximal correlate of sleep quality than gender itself in this sample.

The moderate effect size observed between SMUQ and PSQI (r ≈ .42) indicates that while social media engagement is a meaningful correlate of perceived sleep quality, it accounts for a minority of the variance in sleep outcomes. This is consistent with the multifactorial nature of sleep quality, which is influenced by physiological, psychological, environmental, and behavioral factors. Stress, mental health conditions, caffeine and alcohol intake, physical activity, and other screen-based activities are among many contributors to sleep quality that were not assessed in depth in this study. Hence, while social media use appears to be a relevant factor, it should be considered within a broader constellation of determinants of sleep.

Several practical implications follow from these findings. First, interventions aimed at improving sleep might incorporate components that address social media behavior, particularly evening use and emotionally engaging activities online. Sleep hygiene recommendations that encourage winding down routines free from device interaction are consistent with the mechanisms implicated here. Behavioral strategies such as setting specific limits on nighttime social media use, using device features that reduce blue light emission, and establishing consistent sleep schedules could be useful adjuncts to broader sleep interventions. These approaches may be especially pertinent given the high proportion of participants in this study whose PSQI scores exceeded commonly used thresholds for poor sleep.

Second, public health messaging could benefit from emphasizing not only total screen time but also the timing and emotional context of social media use. Users who engage in social media primarily for social reassurance or to cope with negative mood states may be particularly vulnerable to sleep disruption if these behaviors are concentrated in the evening hours. Mental health professionals and primary care providers might consider screening for problematic social media behavior when patients present with sleep complaints, and brief counseling around technology use could be integrated into clinical practice.

Third, the gender difference in number of platforms used suggests that interventions and public health campaigns may need to be tailored to patterns of use that differ by demographic groups. Women’s broader engagement across platforms might require nuanced guidance that addresses cross-platform switching and the cumulative effects of multiple streams of social input. However, it is important to avoid stereotyping and to recognize substantial within-gender variability; personalization of recommendations based on individual usage patterns is likely to be more effective than gender-targeted prescriptions alone.

Despite these informative findings, the study has important limitations that constrain the inferences that can be drawn. Foremost, the cross-sectional and correlational design precludes causal conclusions. It remains possible that poor sleep leads to increased social media engagement—particularly during nocturnal awakenings—or that bidirectional effects operate. Longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to clarify temporal ordering and causality. For example, randomized trials that manipulate evening social media access could provide stronger evidence regarding causal effects on sleep latency and quality.

Another limitation relates to measurement. Although the SMUQ and PSQI are validated and reliable self-report instruments, they are subject to biases including recall error and social desirability. Objective measures of sleep, such as actigraphy or polysomnography, and objective monitoring of device use would complement self-report measures and increase confidence in findings. Passive sensing methods that capture timing and duration of social media use could help disentangle whether late-night use is particularly detrimental relative to daytime use. Similarly, more fine-grained measurement of social media content (e.g., emotionally arousing content, passive versus active use) could elucidate which aspects of social media engagement are most disruptive to sleep.

Sample characteristics also limit generalizability. The sample was relatively young on average and recruited predominantly through online and university-related venues. Findings may not extend to older adults, adolescents, or clinical populations with sleep disorders. The balanced gender composition strengthens internal comparisons by gender but does not capture non-binary or gender diverse experiences; future research should expand inclusivity in gender measurement and sampling.

In addition, the study did not assess potential third-variable confounds comprehensively. Variables such as anxiety and depressive symptoms, caffeine and alcohol consumption, physical activity levels, and chronic health conditions could account for part of the association between social media use and sleep. Incorporating broader assessments and statistical controls in future work would help isolate the unique contribution of social media behavior.

Future research directions are plentiful. Longitudinal panel studies could track changes in social media behavior and sleep quality over time to investigate directional effects and potential cumulative impacts. Experimental studies that manipulate social media access, particularly in the evening, could test causal hypotheses and examine mechanisms such as arousal and circadian effects. Studies using objective monitoring of both sleep and device use would provide stronger evidence and allow for precise temporal mapping of social media exposure to sleep onset and awakenings. Research that examines vulnerable subgroups—such as adolescents whose sleep physiology and social media engagement patterns differ from adults—could identify populations at elevated risk and inform targeted prevention efforts.

Investigations into potential moderators are also needed. For instance, individual differences in emotion regulation, susceptibility to social comparison, chronotype (morningness-eveningness preference), and baseline sleep vulnerability may moderate the social media–sleep relationship. Understanding these moderators could help tailor interventions to those most likely to benefit. Additionally, research into content- and platform-specific effects could reveal whether certain types of platforms or activities (e.g., live streaming, video content, messaging) are particularly disruptive.

Policy implications should be considered cautiously. While the current evidence supports recommendations to limit evening social media use as part of sleep hygiene promotion, broad regulatory or platform-level interventions would require stronger causal evidence and consideration of feasibility, acceptability, and potential unintended consequences. However, platform designers might consider features that facilitate healthier sleep behaviors, such as optional quiet hours, reminders to disconnect before bedtime, and more robust tools for managing notifications in the evening.

In conclusion, the present study found that greater intensity of social media engagement was associated with poorer perceived sleep quality in a sample of adults, and that women reported using a greater number of social media platforms than men. These findings align with theoretical and empirical work suggesting that social media behaviors—particularly those involving intense or problematic engagement—may contribute to sleep difficulties. While causality cannot be established from the present data, the observed associations underscore the importance of considering social media use as a behavioral domain relevant to sleep health. Future longitudinal, experimental, and objective measurement studies are needed to clarify causal pathways, identify moderators, and inform targeted interventions aimed at preserving sleep health in an increasingly connected world.
