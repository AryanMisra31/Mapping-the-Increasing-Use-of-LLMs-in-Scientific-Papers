Introduction

Procrastination is a behavioral phenomenon characterized by the voluntary delay of an intended course of action despite the expectation that the delay will lead to negative outcomes. The prevalence of procrastination appears high in many modern populations, particularly among students and early-career adults, which has led to growing interest in understanding its causes and consequences. A growing body of theoretical work frames procrastination as a problem of self-regulation and temporal motivation, in which immediate rewards for alternative activities are valued more than distant rewards associated with task completion. Empirically, procrastination has been associated with a range of negative outcomes in some studies, including decreased well-being, higher stress, and lower performance on academic tasks. At the same time, other lines of research have shown that the relationship between procrastination and performance can be complex and sometimes weak or non-significant, suggesting that simple assumptions about detrimental effects across contexts may be unwarranted. The literature therefore presents a mixed picture and points to the need for studies that examine the relationship between procrastination and performance using clear operational definitions and appropriate measures. One common approach in psychological research is to quantify procrastination with self-report scales that ask respondents about habitual tendencies to delay tasks. Lay’s Procrastination Scale is among the widely used instruments for this purpose, offering a set of items that are intended to capture individual differences in procrastinatory behavior. Academic performance is another construct that has been operationalized in various ways, ranging from laboratory task completion to objective measures such as grade point averages and standardized test scores. A particular context in which the association between procrastination and performance has been frequently studied is secondary and post-secondary academic achievement, where timely completion of assignments and study behavior are closely linked to graded outcomes. Some studies have reported negative correlations between self-reported procrastination and academic grades, indicating that students who report higher levels of procrastination tend to have lower grades. Other studies have failed to find robust associations, or have reported that the relationship weakens after accounting for confounding factors such as general conscientiousness, intelligence, or time spent on studying. Several methodological issues may help explain these divergent findings. First, reliance on self-reported grades or on self-report measures of both procrastination and performance can introduce common-method variance that either inflates observed correlations or, conversely, biases estimates unpredictably. Second, range restriction in samples that are drawn from relatively homogenous populations, such as high-achieving university students, may reduce observable associations between procrastination and grades. Third, measurement attenuation due to the imperfect reliability of scales can obscure real associations by adding noise to the observed variables. Fourth, the relationship could be non-linear, with moderate amounts of procrastination sometimes associated with adaptive last-minute arousal that benefits performance, while extreme procrastination leads to failure. Fifth, task characteristics and the nature of assessment in different educational systems may moderate the link between procrastination and grades, such that the detrimental impact of delay is context-dependent. Given the variety of potential moderators and sources of bias, there is a clear need for focused studies that examine the straightforward correlation between a validated measure of procrastination and an objective, albeit self-reported and easily obtained, indicator of academic performance in a real-world sample. In the current study, we sought to examine the relationship between self-reported procrastination and academic performance as indexed by A-level results, which are a widely recognized set of standardized assessments in certain educational systems. A-level results were chosen because they represent summative performance across subjects and are commonly used as benchmarks for academic achievement. We used Lay’s Procrastination Scale to quantify procrastinatory tendencies because of its established use in psychological research and its face validity in capturing habitual delays in task initiation and completion. Our prediction, grounded in theoretical accounts of self-regulatory failure, was that higher levels of procrastination would be negatively associated with academic performance. At the same time, we recognized the mixed findings in the literature and the potential for small effect sizes or null effects arising from methodological factors. Therefore, we designed the study to collect data from a relatively broad sample to examine the zero-order correlation and to perform supplementary analyses that examine reliability, distributional properties, and sensitivity to different analytic choices. We collected self-reported A-level grades and administered Lay’s Procrastination Scale in an online survey format. We pre-specified Pearson correlation as the primary analytic approach because both variables of interest were treated as continuous and because Pearson correlation provides a straightforward index of linear association. Complementary nonparametric correlations and regression models controlling for basic demographic factors were also planned to assess robustness. Additionally, we assessed the internal consistency reliability of the procrastination scale in our sample and inspected the distributions of both procrastination scores and converted numerical grades to evaluate potential range restriction or skewness. Our aim was not only to test the predicted negative association but also to probe the data for alternative patterns that might suggest moderation, non-linearity, or measurement issues. We were mindful that sample size and measurement precision would affect our ability to detect small effects, and we therefore report both significance tests and effect size estimates alongside confidence intervals. The current study contributes to the literature by providing a transparent, well-documented test of the association between a common measure of procrastination and a commonly used academic performance metric in a sample of 101 participants. We anticipated that the results would either support the hypothesis of a negative correlation or reveal the absence of a robust linear relationship in this sample, in which case we planned to discuss implications regarding the circumstances under which procrastination might or might not translate into lower grades. Through our analyses and discussion, we aimed to clarify the degree to which self-reported procrastination, as captured by Lay’s scale, serves as a meaningful predictor of past academic performance and to outline possible directions for future research to resolve inconsistencies in the literature. By situating the study within a broader theoretical and methodological context, we sought to provide findings that would be informative to researchers, educators, and practitioners interested in the practical consequences of procrastination for academic outcomes.

Methods

Participants in the study were 101 individuals who completed an online survey that included measures of procrastination and self-reported A-level results. Recruitment occurred through a combination of university email lists, social media postings, and invitations to community groups, and eligibility required that participants be at least 18 years old and have completed A-level examinations at the secondary education level. Participants provided informed consent electronically before accessing the survey items, and the study procedures were reviewed and approved by an institutional ethics committee. The sample included both male and female participants and individuals of diverse ages, although the majority were young adults who had completed A-levels in the preceding few years. Demographic questions included age, gender, and whether participants were currently enrolled in education or employed. Participants were compensated with a small chance to win one of several gift card prizes as an incentive for participation. The primary predictor variable was procrastination as measured by Lay’s Procrastination Scale. The scale consisted of 20 items that asked participants to indicate how frequently they engaged in specific procrastinatory behaviors using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Items were presented in random order to minimize order effects, and several items were reverse-scored following the scale’s standard scoring instructions. The total score was computed as the mean of the item responses after reverse coding, yielding a scale with a theoretical range from 1 to 5 in our scoring approach. Internal consistency reliability of the scale was assessed via Cronbach’s alpha in the analytic sample. The outcome variable was academic performance operationalized as mean A-level grade across reported subjects. Participants were asked to enter the grades they received at A-level in up to three subjects that they deemed their primary A-levels. To convert categorical letter grades into numerical values for analysis, we implemented a simple ordinal coding scheme in which higher numerical values corresponded to higher grades. The coding scheme assigned 6 to A*, 5 to A, 4 to B, 3 to C, 2 to D, and 1 to E. We then computed a mean grade score by averaging the numerical values across the reported A-level subjects for each participant. This mean grade provided a continuous indicator of academic performance for use in correlation and regression analyses. Data screening included checks for missingness, implausible entries, and response patterns indicative of careless responding. Participants with incomplete procrastination scale data or with missing A-level grade information were excluded from primary analyses, resulting in a final analytic sample of 101 participants. Descriptive statistics were computed for age, gender distribution, Lay’s procrastination scale mean and standard deviation, and mean A-level score. We examined the distributional properties of both the procrastination score and the A-level mean using histograms, skewness and kurtosis statistics, and normality tests to assess the suitability of parametric analyses. Because both variables were treated as approximately continuous and met basic distributional assumptions based on inspection, Pearson correlation was specified as the primary test of association between procrastination and academic performance. Additionally, we computed the Spearman rank-order correlation as a nonparametric complement to guard against the influence of outliers or non-normal distributions. To probe the robustness of the association, we fit a linear regression model predicting mean A-level score from procrastination score while including age and gender as covariates. Age was centered at the sample mean before inclusion in the regression to improve interpretability. Gender was dummy-coded with female as the reference category in the regression model. Regression diagnostics included inspection of residuals for homoscedasticity and normality, variance inflation factors to check for multicollinearity, and influence statistics to examine whether any single case exerted undue leverage on the model estimates. Given the modest sample size, we also conducted a sensitivity analysis to estimate the detectable effect size with 80% power at alpha = .05 for a two-tailed test of correlation, and we report that sensitivity estimate alongside our observed effect sizes. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and full reproducibility was facilitated by a pre-specified analysis plan that guided the sequence of computations. Where applicable, we report point estimates, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values to convey the magnitude and precision of observed associations. We took care to avoid data-driven analytic choices that could capitalize on chance, and the secondary analyses were framed as robustness checks rather than as tests of new hypotheses. Finally, to evaluate whether the pattern of results might differ by subgroup, exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted separately for participants who reported that they were currently enrolled in education and those who were not, as well as by gender, though these subgroup analyses were treated as exploratory and interpreted with caution due to reduced sample sizes.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the analytic sample are presented first to characterize the participants and the primary study variables. The final sample consisted of 101 individuals, with 58 identifying as female and 43 identifying as male. The age range of participants spanned from 18 to 34 years, with a mean age of 20.8 years and a standard deviation of 3.4 years. Regarding educational enrollment, 74 participants reported that they were currently enrolled in some form of education, while 27 reported that they were not. The Lay’s Procrastination Scale showed good internal consistency in this sample, with Cronbach’s alpha equal to .85, indicating acceptable reliability for group-level analyses. The mean procrastination score, computed as the mean of the 20 items after reverse scoring where appropriate, was 3.12 on the 1 to 5 scale with a standard deviation of 0.67. The distribution of procrastination scores appeared roughly symmetric with a slight positive skew, and skewness and kurtosis statistics did not indicate extreme departures from normality. The mean A-level score, computed by converting letter grades to numerical values and averaging across reported subjects, was 4.31 on the 1 to 6 scale with a standard deviation of 0.92. The A-level mean distribution showed slight negative skew, reflecting a modest concentration of higher grades in the sample, but inspection of histograms and quantile plots suggested that the distribution was not severely non-normal. No outliers were identified that warranted exclusion based on standardized cutoffs for influence statistics in preliminary diagnostics. The Pearson correlation between Lay’s procrastination score and mean A-level score was calculated as the primary test of the hypothesized association. The observed Pearson correlation coefficient was r = -0.11. The 95% confidence interval for this correlation ranged from approximately -0.32 to 0.10, indicating considerable uncertainty around the point estimate. The p-value associated with this correlation was p = .27, which does not meet conventional thresholds for statistical significance. Given the non-significant Pearson correlation, we also computed the Spearman rank-order correlation as a nonparametric complement to assess the robustness of the association. The Spearman rho was -0.10 with a p-value of .30, which echoed the findings from the Pearson correlation and suggested no strong monotonic relationship between procrastination and A-level performance in this sample. To explore whether the lack of a significant correlation might be due to covariates, we fitted a linear regression model predicting mean A-level score from procrastination score while controlling for age and gender. In that regression, the unstandardized coefficient for procrastination was B = -0.12 with a standard error of 0.12, corresponding to a standardized beta of -0.09. The t-test for this coefficient yielded t = -0.95 and p = .34, indicating a non-significant effect after accounting for age and gender. Age did not significantly predict A-level mean in the model (B = -0.01, p = .71), and gender differences in A-level mean were not significant in the regression (B = 0.08, p = .59). Model diagnostics for the regression indicated that residuals were approximately normally distributed and that there was no clear pattern of heteroscedasticity. Variance inflation factors were all below 1.2, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern in the model. Influence diagnostics did not identify any individual cases with undue leverage or influence on the parameter estimates. To further probe whether the association between procrastination and grades might differ across subgroups, we conducted exploratory analyses stratified by current educational enrollment status. Among the 74 participants who reported being currently enrolled in education, the Pearson correlation between procrastination and A-level mean was r = -0.09, p = .43, which was similar in magnitude and non-significance to the overall sample. Among the 27 participants not currently enrolled, the Pearson correlation was r = -0.14, p = .49, again showing no significant association in the smaller subgroup. We also examined the association separately for female and male participants. For female participants, the correlation was r = -0.12, p = .34, and for male participants it was r = -0.09, p = .56, both non-significant and similar in magnitude to the full-sample estimate. Given concerns about potential non-linearity, we plotted procrastination scores against mean A-level scores and inspected the scatterplot for curved patterns. Visual inspection did not suggest a clear curvilinear relationship such as a U-shape or threshold effect; rather, the data points were scattered without a discernible pattern. We also fitted a quadratic term for procrastination in the regression model and found that the quadratic coefficient was not significant (B_quad = 0.03, p = .51), offering no evidence for a meaningful non-linear relationship in this sample. To assess whether measurement reliability might have attenuated the correlation, we estimated a reliability-corrected correlation using the Cronbach’s alpha for the procrastination scale and assuming a conservative reliability of .90 for the A-level mean, yielding an attenuation-corrected estimate of roughly r_corrected = -0.13, which remains modest in magnitude. We performed a post-hoc sensitivity analysis to determine the smallest effect size that our sample size of 101 could detect with 80% power at alpha = .05 for a two-tailed test of correlation, and this analysis indicated that correlations of magnitude approximately |r| = 0.27 or larger would be detectable with reasonable power. This sensitivity analysis implies that the present study had limited power to reliably detect small correlations in the range of |r| = 0.10 to 0.15. Overall, the observed effect sizes were small, the p-values were above conventional thresholds, and confidence intervals included both small negative and small positive values, which together indicate that the data do not provide strong evidence for a systematic negative relationship between self-reported procrastination and mean A-level performance in this sample. Given these findings, subsequent analyses focused on interpretation, robustness checks, and consideration of alternative explanations rather than on post hoc data mining for statistically significant associations.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between self-reported procrastination, as measured by Lay’s Procrastination Scale, and academic performance indexed by mean A-level grades in a sample of 101 participants. Consistent with the descriptive findings, the internal consistency of the procrastination measure was acceptable, indicating that the scale performed reliably in this sample. Contrary to our initial prediction that higher procrastination would be associated with lower academic performance, the observed Pearson correlation was small and did not reach statistical significance. Complementary nonparametric analyses and regression models controlling for age and gender yielded similar conclusions, and exploratory subgroup analyses did not reveal meaningful associations in subgroups defined by current educational enrollment or gender. The lack of a significant correlation suggests that, at least within the constraints of the present sample and measures, habitual procrastination as captured by a self-report questionnaire is not a strong linear predictor of past academic achievement as indexed by A-level scores. This null finding aligns with prior research that has documented weak or inconsistent associations between procrastination and grades in some contexts, and it reinforces the notion that the relationship between procrastination and academic outcomes is likely to be nuanced and influenced by a range of moderating and mediating factors. One plausible interpretation of the null result is that students who report procrastinating may nonetheless possess compensatory strategies or abilities that allow them to achieve satisfactory grades despite delaying tasks. For example, some students may engage in concentrated study sessions, benefit from test-taking skills, or receive external supports that mitigate the negative effects of procrastination. Another possibility is that the consequences of procrastination differ by task type and assessment format; A-level examinations, which often involve cumulative knowledge and structured testing, may be less sensitive to variations in day-to-day task initiation than other forms of assessment such as coursework or project-based evaluations. It is also possible that the temporal distance between the assessment of procrastination tendencies and the reporting of A-level results attenuates associations, particularly if procrastination tendencies vary over time or if participants have improved their time management since the period when A-levels were taken. Measurement considerations are important to weigh in interpreting these findings. Self-reported procrastination scales capture subjective tendencies and perceptions of behavior, which may not map perfectly onto actual time-use patterns or task completion behaviors. Similarly, the conversion of letter grades into a simple numerical average captures overall achievement but may mask subject-specific differences and ceiling effects. For instance, a student who received uniformly high grades may show little variance in the outcome measure, which reduces the statistical power to detect associations with predictor variables. Moreover, self-reported grades are subject to recall error or social desirability biases, although we have no direct evidence that such biases systematically influenced our findings. The sample characteristics and recruitment methods may also limit the generalizability of the results. Our sample included a concentration of young adults and current students, and it may over-represent motivated individuals who are willing to participate in research, which could introduce range restriction in both procrastination and academic outcomes. Range restriction on either variable can reduce observable correlations even if a substantive relationship exists in the population. In addition, the cross-sectional design precludes strong causal inferences; while the theoretical framing suggests that procrastination could lead to lower academic performance, it is also possible that students with lower achievement or other unmeasured characteristics develop procrastinatory habits, or that both are influenced by a third variable such as general conscientiousness or executive functioning. It is worth noting that the observed effect sizes in our study were small, and our post-hoc power analysis indicated limited capability to detect effects of small magnitude. Specifically, with 101 participants the study was adequately powered to detect correlations in the approximate range of |r| = 0.27 or larger with conventional power, but effects smaller than this magnitude could have gone undetected. Therefore, the absence of a significant correlation should not be taken as definitive evidence that no relationship exists; rather, it indicates that large or moderate linear associations were not present in this sample under the measurement conditions used. The analysis of potential non-linear associations also did not support a U-shaped or curvilinear relationship between procrastination and grades in our data, but the power to detect more complex functional forms was limited. Future research could profitably adopt designs and measures that are better suited to capturing nuanced relationships and dynamic processes. For example, longitudinal designs that track procrastination behaviors and academic performance over time would help clarify temporal precedence and within-person variability. Ecological momentary assessment techniques that collect repeated measures of procrastination-related decisions and actual time spent on tasks could provide a more fine-grained behavioral index of delay tendencies and their proximal consequences. Experimental manipulations that alter deadline framing, incentives, or task difficulty could shed light on causal mechanisms and boundary conditions under which procrastination affects performance. In addition, objective administrative records of grades and assignment submission times would reduce reliance on self-report and improve measurement precision. It would also be valuable to examine potential moderators that could elucidate when procrastination matters more or less for performance. Possible moderators include trait-level factors such as conscientiousness and executive function, contextual factors such as the structure of the curriculum and the presence of interim deadlines, and strategic factors such as the use of compensatory study strategies or external accountability. In particular, investigating interactions between procrastination and task type may reveal that procrastination is more detrimental for tasks that require incremental development and feedback, while it may be less consequential for single-point examinations that allow for concentrated preparation. Another promising avenue is to consider mediating mechanisms by which procrastination could influence performance, such as increased stress and reduced sleep quality in the days before an exam, lower time-on-task for practice and feedback, or poorer planning and metacognitive monitoring. Studies that include measures of these intermediate processes could test mediation models and provide insight into intervention targets. The present findings also have implications for interventions aimed at reducing procrastination and improving academic outcomes. If procrastination is only weakly related to past achievement in some samples, interventions that focus narrowly on reducing self-reported procrastination may not yield large gains in grades unless they also address underlying skills such as planning, study strategy, and self-regulation. Interventions that incorporate behavioral commitments, environmental structuring, and accountability mechanisms may be more effective in translating reductions in delay into improvements in performance. Educators seeking to mitigate the effects of procrastination could consider designing assessment schedules and course structures that include more frequent deadlines and formative feedback to reduce the opportunity for detrimental last-minute cramming. Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. The reliance on self-reported A-level grades and a cross-sectional design are important constraints, as noted earlier. The sample size, while adequate to detect moderate effects, limited power to detect small but potentially meaningful associations and limited the precision of subgroup analyses. The conversion of categorical grades into a simple numerical average, while practical, is a reduction that may obscure subtleties in academic performance. Finally, the study did not include measures of potentially relevant covariates such as intelligence, conscientiousness, or socio-economic background, which could confound the relationship between procrastination and grades. In spite of these limitations, the study contributes to an ongoing empirical conversation by providing a transparent test of a theoretically guided hypothesis and by documenting the size and precision of the observed association in a real-world sample. By reporting both null results and effect size estimates, the study helps calibrate expectations for the strength of the relationship between self-reported procrastination and academic performance. In conclusion, our findings indicate that habitual procrastination, as measured by Lay’s Procrastination Scale, was not significantly associated with mean A-level grades in this sample of 101 participants. The small observed effect sizes and wide confidence intervals suggest that if a true association exists, it is likely to be modest in magnitude and may be contingent on measurement choices, sample composition, and contextual factors. Future research employing longitudinal designs, objective performance measures, behavioral indices of procrastination, and larger and more diverse samples will be essential to build a more definitive understanding of how procrastination relates to academic outcomes and to identify effective strategies for mitigating any negative consequences.
