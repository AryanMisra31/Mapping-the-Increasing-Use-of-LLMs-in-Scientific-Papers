Introduction

Stereotypes about social groups are pervasive and exert measurable influence on perceivers’ judgments and behavior. These stereotypes can shape expectations about competence, leadership potential, and suitability for particular roles, often operating outside of conscious awareness. Over several decades, psychologists have documented robust associations between gender and leadership in many cultures, with men often being perceived as more suited to managerial and leadership roles than women. This pervasive expectation has been succinctly captured by researchers in the formulation known as the “think manager, think male” paradigm. The “think manager, think male” paradigm summarizes the observation that when people are asked to imagine a prototypical manager, they are likely to endorse traits and images that overlap more strongly with stereotypic male characteristics than stereotypic female characteristics. The implications of this association are substantial because they are thought to contribute to the underrepresentation of women in senior management and executive positions. Stereotype research therefore has not only theoretical import for understanding cognitive representations but also practical relevance for interventions aimed at increasing gender equity in organizations. The literature further suggests that stereotypic associations are not immutable and that they can be altered under certain conditions. Notably, several streams of research have shown that exposure to counterstereotypic exemplars or information that contradicts a stereotype can attenuate or sometimes eliminate its effects. For example, when perceivers encounter vivid, salient examples of women succeeding in leadership roles, their associations between gender and leadership may weaken. Laboratory studies have used brief counterstereotypic priming procedures, biographical vignettes, and media portrayals to demonstrate that stereotypes can be shifted, at least temporarily. This body of experimental research suggests a promising avenue for designing interventions that reduce gender bias in leadership judgments by providing counterstereotypic information. At the same time, not all attempts to alter stereotypes are equally effective, and the boundary conditions that determine when counterstereotypic information produces change remain incompletely specified. The robustness of the “think manager, think male” stereotype can depend on features of the manipulation, characteristics of the perceiver, and the measurement context. For instance, manipulation strength, the degree of identifiability of the exemplar, and whether the manipulation engages controlled versus automatic processes can all influence the magnitude and duration of stereotype change. Moreover, participant characteristics such as gender, political ideology, prior exposure to counterstereotypic information, and occupational experience can moderate how people respond to interventions. In light of this complexity, studies that attempt to apply counterstereotypic manipulations directly to measures of managerial judgments provide important, practical tests of the paradigm. These studies can reveal whether brief interventions that might be feasible in organizational training settings produce meaningful changes in perceptions of leadership potential. The current investigation is framed by this applied motivation and seeks to test whether a brief counterstereotypic article about a successful female businesswoman can alter judgments in a simple task that mimics real-world personnel evaluations. The investigation also seeks to examine the replicability and boundary conditions of the “think manager, think male” effect by using a mixed factorial design that measures judgments of both male and female targets. Previous experimental work in this domain typically involves manipulations that vary in intensity and format. Some studies present short biographies of a counterstereotypic target, others rely on photographs paired with descriptions, and still others use news articles or media clips. The choice of stimulus matters because depth of processing and perceived credibility can influence whether participants integrate the counterstereotypic information into their mental models of leadership. Therefore, one central question in applied research is whether a single short article read immediately before an evaluative judgment task is sufficient to shift managerial ratings. Another relevant factor is whether the measurement instrument is able to detect changes in stereotype-consistent responding. Many prior studies have used multi-item scales that index perceived managerial traits, such as assertiveness, decisiveness, and strategic thinking. These scales often show good internal reliability and sensitivity to experimental manipulations. However, the internal structure of such scales and potential ceiling or floor effects can influence the ability to detect change. Researchers therefore recommend choosing trait items thoughtfully and conducting pilot testing to ensure that items are discriminating in the target sample. A further issue concerns the role of within-subjects exposure to female and male targets. Within-subjects designs for target gender allow direct comparison of responses to male and female targets within the same individual, which improves power and controls for between-subject variance. At the same time, within-subjects designs can introduce contrast or demand effects: when participants see both a male and a female target, they might infer the experimental purpose and alter their responses. Past studies have used both within- and between-subjects target manipulations; each approach has tradeoffs. The current study uses a within-subjects manipulation for target gender to maximize sensitivity to differences in ratings of male and female targets, while using a between-subjects manipulation for exposure to counterstereotypic information in order to preserve some ecological validity of the intervention. The primary hypothesis based on the “think manager, think male” framework was that, in the absence of counterstereotypic information, participants would rate a male character as more managerial than a female character. The secondary hypothesis was that exposure to a credible article describing a successful female business executive would reduce or eliminate this male advantage, such that the interaction between article condition and target gender would be significant. A null interaction would suggest either that the “think manager, think male” stereotype did not manifest in this sample or that the manipulation did not have the intended effect on perceivers’ judgments. The current study aimed to test these competing possibilities with an experimental design that attempted to balance methodological rigor and applied relevance. Beyond the primary hypotheses, the investigation also considered several exploratory questions that can inform interpretation of any observed effects. One exploratory question concerned participant gender: whether male and female participants might differ in their baseline ratings of male and female targets and whether they might respond differently to the counterstereotypic article. Another exploratory question concerned order effects: because the within-subjects target was presented in two successive trials, it was important to examine whether the order in which participants rated the male and female targets influenced the pattern of results. These supplemental analyses help clarify whether observed effects are robust or contingent on procedural details. The present study therefore contributes to the literature by evaluating whether a brief, easily implemented counterstereotypic intervention can change managerial judgments measured on a sensitive within-subjects task. The study also provides evidence about current levels of stereotype endorsement in a sample of adult participants recruited from a nonclinical, community-based pool. Given social changes over recent decades, it is possible that the strength of the “think manager, think male” association has attenuated in some populations. This possibility motivates an open-minded approach to interpretation that considers both the possibility of stereotype change through specific manipulations and the possibility that baseline attitudes have shifted. The methodological choices made in this study were intended to align with prior experimental paradigms while testing an intervention that could, in principle, be used in training or educational contexts. The paper proceeds by describing the method in detail, reporting the primary and exploratory analyses, and then discussing the implications of the findings for theory and practice, along with suggestions for future research designed to clarify boundary conditions and enhance intervention efficacy.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and from a local online participant recruitment platform. The study recruited 210 adults between the ages of 18 and 65. Participants were offered a small monetary payment or course credit for their time. All participants provided informed consent prior to beginning the study. The sample size of 210 was determined prior to data collection to provide adequate power (approximately .80) to detect small-to-moderate interaction effects in a mixed-design analysis, based on conventional power calculations for F tests. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two between-subjects conditions: the counterstereotypic article condition or the control article condition. Random assignment was achieved using a computerized randomization routine embedded in the online study platform that was used for data collection. The final analytic sample consisted of 210 participants after excluding six participants who failed attention checks or did not complete the primary dependent measures. Attention checks included simple reading-comprehension items on the article and a check to ensure both target ratings were completed. The task used a mixed factorial design. The between-subjects factor was article condition, with two levels: counterstereotypic female-leader article versus a neutral control article about a nonwork topic. The within-subjects factor was character gender, with two levels: a female character and a male character presented in separate brief vignettes. The dependent variable was a managerial-trait rating scale completed for each character. The counterstereotypic article described the career trajectory and recent accomplishments of a fictitious successful female businesswoman who had achieved a senior executive position in a large organization. The article was approximately 450 words in length and was written in the style of a business news profile. The content emphasized strategic decision-making, effective leadership in complex organizational contexts, and the executive’s visible successes that were directly attributable to leadership skills. The article also included a photograph-like headshot of the protagonist to increase vividness and perceived credibility. The control article was similar in length and format but described a neutral topic unrelated to work or leadership, specifically a short popular-science piece about advances in urban gardening. The control article was included to balance reading time and format while remaining neutral with regard to managerial stereotypes. The vignettes used for the target characters were standardized and brief, each consisting of a single-paragraph description that provided a name, job title (non-managerial baseline to avoid confounding with implied role), and two neutral factual details about work responsibilities. One vignette described “Sally,” a female character, and the other described “John,” a male character; names were chosen to be common and culturally neutral within the participant pool. Each vignette intentionally avoided explicit references to past leadership roles or overtly stereotypic trait words to ensure that participant ratings would reflect their spontaneous application of managerial schema rather than reading comprehension of leadership content. For each character, participants completed a nine-item managerial-trait scale adapted for the purposes of the experiment. The items included traits such as “decisive,” “strategic,” “assertive,” “competent in motivating others,” “organized,” “confident,” “visionary,” “task-focused,” and “respected by colleagues.” Participants rated each item for each character on a 7-point Likert scale anchored at 1 = “Not at all” and 7 = “A great deal.” The items were summed and averaged to produce a composite managerial rating for each character, with possible scores ranging from 1 to 7. The scale was designed to capture the constellation of traits typically associated with managerial prototypes and was pilot-tested in a separate sample to ensure clarity and adequate variance. The order of presentation of the male and female character vignettes was counterbalanced across participants to control for order effects. Half of the participants rated the female target first and the male target second, and the other half rated the male target first and the female second. The composite managerial ratings for each target were the primary dependent measures. In addition, participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire that included age, gender, highest educational attainment, and prior managerial experience. Prior managerial experience was coded as a dichotomous variable indicating whether the participant had ever held a managerial or supervisory role in employment. The procedure began with informed consent and brief instructions describing the study as an investigation of impressions formed from short readings. Participants first read either the counterstereotypic article or the control article according to their random assignment. After reading, participants were given two short comprehension questions about the article to encourage attentive reading and to provide an attention check. Participants who failed both comprehension questions were excluded from primary analyses. After the reading phase, participants were asked to complete an unrelated filler task for two minutes (a simple working-memory digit recall task) to create a short temporal separation between the manipulation and the dependent measures. This filler interval was intended to more closely approximate real-world exposure effects in which people do not immediately make evaluations after encountering a single news item. Following the filler task, participants proceeded to the vignettes and completed the managerial trait ratings for both characters. The order of the vignettes was counterbalanced as described. After completing the rating scales, participants were asked several manipulation-check questions about their perceptions of the article content. They also completed a brief measure of socially desirable responding to assess whether participants might be altering responses due to perceived experimental demand. Finally, participants were debriefed and thanked for their participation. Data analysis followed a pre-registered plan that specified primary analyses using a repeated-measures general linear model with managerial ratings as the dependent variable, character gender as the within-subjects factor, and article condition as the between-subjects factor. Where appropriate, follow-up paired-samples t-tests and independent-samples t-tests were used to unpack significant effects. Effect sizes for ANOVA were reported as partial eta-squared and for t-tests as Cohen’s d. Additional exploratory analyses examined moderation by participant gender and prior managerial experience. All statistical tests were two-tailed and a conventional alpha level of .05 was used to determine statistical significance. Reliability of the managerial trait composite was computed using Cronbach’s alpha prior to the main analyses to ensure the scale was internally consistent. The data, analysis scripts, and experimental materials were archived on a secure server and made available upon reasonable request in the interests of transparency.

Results

Preliminary data screening showed that the managerial-trait composite had high internal consistency. Cronbach’s alpha for the nine-item composite across both targets and conditions was .88, indicating good reliability for the scale. Descriptive statistics were examined for distributional assumptions and outliers. Inspection of the composite scores revealed approximate normality and no extreme outliers beyond 3 standard deviations. The analytic sample included 210 participants after exclusions for failed attention checks and incomplete data. The sample was 58% female and had a mean age of 29.6 years (SD = 10.8). Approximately 24% of participants reported prior managerial experience. There were no significant differences between the article and control groups on demographic variables or prior managerial experience, confirming successful randomization. Table 1 presents the means and standard deviations for managerial ratings by condition and target. (Note: Table omitted in this text format.) In the counterstereotypic article condition (n = 105), the female target (Sally) had a mean managerial rating of 5.20 (SD = 0.90) while the male target (John) had a mean of 4.80 (SD = 1.00). In the control article condition (n = 105), the female target had a mean of 5.05 (SD = 0.95) and the male target had a mean of 4.75 (SD = 1.05). These descriptive statistics suggested a pattern opposite to the classic “think manager, think male” expectation: across conditions, the female target received higher managerial ratings than the male target. The primary statistical test was a repeated-measures ANOVA with character gender as the within-subjects factor and article condition as the between-subjects factor. The main effect of character gender was statistically significant. Across both conditions, female targets were rated as more managerial than male targets, F(1,208) = 12.57, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .057. The main effect of article condition (counterstereotypic article vs control article) was not statistically significant, F(1,208) = 1.83, p = .177, partial eta-squared = .009. Critically, the interaction between character gender and article condition was not significant, F(1,208) = 0.72, p = .397, partial eta-squared = .003. This non-significant interaction indicates that reading the counterstereotypic article did not differentially affect the relative ratings of the female versus male target. To further examine the gender effect within each condition, paired-samples t-tests were conducted separately for the article and control groups. In the counterstereotypic article group, participants rated the female target higher than the male target: t(104) = 3.60, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.35. In the control group, participants also rated the female target higher than the male target: t(104) = 2.80, p = .006, Cohen’s d = 0.27. Thus, the female advantage in managerial ratings was evident in both conditions. An independent-samples t-test comparing the female target’s ratings between article conditions showed a small, non-significant difference: participants who read the counterstereotypic article rated the female target slightly higher (M = 5.20, SD = 0.90) than those who read the control article (M = 5.05, SD = 0.95), t(208) = 1.26, p = .209, Cohen’s d = 0.17. An analogous comparison for the male target also showed a non-significant difference between article conditions, t(208) = 0.59, p = .555, with means of 4.80 (SD = 1.00) in the article condition and 4.75 (SD = 1.05) in the control condition. The absence of a clear article effect suggested that the manipulation did not substantially shift ratings beyond a possible small, non-significant upward shift for the female target. Exploratory analyses examined whether participant gender moderated any of the primary effects. A mixed ANOVA including participant gender as an added between-subjects factor revealed a small main effect of participant gender, with female participants giving slightly higher managerial ratings overall than male participants, F(1,206) = 4.12, p = .044, partial eta-squared = .019. However, participant gender did not interact with character gender or with article condition in any meaningful way; the three-way interaction was not significant, F(1,206) = 0.30, p = .585. Follow-up simple effects showed that both male and female participants rated the female target higher than the male target, and the magnitude of that difference did not differ substantially by participant gender. Another exploratory analysis tested whether prior managerial experience affected ratings. Participants with prior managerial experience (n ≈ 50) gave slightly higher ratings to both targets, but prior managerial experience did not interact with character gender or article condition. The two-way interaction between prior managerial experience and character gender was non-significant, F(1,208) = 0.92, p = .339. The order in which targets were presented (female-first vs male-first) was examined for potential order effects. A repeated-measures ANOVA including presentation order as a between-subjects factor showed no main effect of order and no significant interactions involving order. Thus, order did not appear to drive the observed female advantage. Given the non-significant interaction between article condition and character gender, we conducted a complementary Bayesian analysis to estimate evidence in favor of the null interaction. The Bayesian mixed-model analysis yielded a Bayes factor indicating that the data were approximately 4.2 times more likely under the null model without the interaction than under a model including the interaction, providing moderate evidence against a meaningful interaction effect. Finally, manipulation checks confirmed that participants in the article condition attended to and comprehended the article; mean comprehension question accuracy was 87% in the article condition, compared with 85% in the control condition on their respective comprehension items. Ratings of perceived credibility of the article content were moderate to high for both the counterstereotypic and control articles, with no substantial differences. Taken together, the results indicate a robust main effect in which the female target was rated as more managerial than the male target across both conditions and a lack of evidence that the counterstereotypic article differentially influenced ratings as predicted by the “think manager, think male” framework. This pattern was consistent across various sensitivity analyses and was not moderated in meaningful ways by participant gender, prior managerial experience, or presentation order.

Discussion

The present experiment sought to test whether brief exposure to a counterstereotypic exemplar—a short article profiling a successful female business executive—would reduce the typical association between male gender and perceived managerial capacity. The rationale followed from previous work showing that counterstereotypic information can attenuate or eliminate stereotype-consistent judgments. Contrary to the specific prediction derived from the “think manager, think male” paradigm, the current study did not find evidence that the counterstereotypic article interacted with target gender to alter managerial ratings. Instead, and unexpectedly, participants rated the female character more managerial than the male character across both the counterstereotypic and control conditions. The absence of an interaction suggests that the experimental manipulation, as implemented, did not produce the hypothesized differential effect, while the main effect favoring the female target suggests a more complex pattern in the current sample than the classic stereotype would predict. Several important points emerge from these findings that are relevant to theory, methodology, and applied practice. First, the observed female advantage in managerial ratings challenges a simple, universal interpretation of the “think manager, think male” heuristic. It is possible that cultural shifts, at least among the kind of participants recruited for this study, have changed the default prototypical image of a manager such that female candidates are now readily perceived as managerial. If this interpretation is correct, it suggests that stereotype strength can decline over time and that observed patterns of bias are not static. This possibility is consistent with broader social changes in gender norms and increased visibility of women in leadership roles in many sectors. However, caution is warranted in extending this interpretation too broadly because the sample in the present study may not be representative of the broader population. Participants were relatively young on average and included a large proportion of women, both of which may be associated with more egalitarian attitudes toward gender and leadership. Second, an alternative interpretation is that procedural aspects of the experimental design produced the observed pattern. The vignettes used to describe the targets were intentionally neutral with respect to leadership; yet participants may have used implicit associations or inferred leadership-relevant traits from the non-leadership details differently for the female and male names. For example, a name like “Sally” might have elicited a more favorable response in this sample due to unmeasured cultural connotations or due to momentary context effects unrelated to the “manager” stereotype. Likewise, because the female vignette was positioned after reading any article (counterstereotypic or control), small contextual cues could have inflated the female ratings. Although order was counterbalanced and did not show significant effects in analyses, subtle contextual influences that are not captured by order alone may still have played a role. Third, it is also possible that demand characteristics and social desirability influenced participants to avoid appearing biased against women. As awareness of gender bias has increased, participants—especially those participating in research contexts—may respond in a way that aligns with perceived normative expectations. In the current study, measures of socially desirable responding were collected and did not indicate a strong correlation with the difference in ratings, but such measures are themselves imperfect. A related possibility is that the vignette format and multi-item scale encouraged participants to reflect and provide socially acceptable responses rather than reveal automatic, stereotype-consistent associations. This suggests the possibility that explicit measures of managerial judgments may sometimes underestimate implicit stereotype endorsement. Fourth, with respect to the failure of the counterstereotypic manipulation to produce a significant interaction, several explanations merit consideration. It is possible that the manipulation was too weak or too brief to produce measurable change beyond any existing attitudes in the sample. Although the article was designed to be vivid and credible, a single short read—and with a brief filler interval—may not be sufficient to induce durable changes in applied judgments. Prior work that has produced more robust changes in stereotype-related responses often uses repeated exposure, longitudinal interventions, or more immersive manipulations such as video profiles or interactions with exemplars. The lack of a strong manipulation effect suggests that interventions intended to shift real-world personnel evaluations may require more intensive or repeated exposure. Fifth, measurement factors may have limited the ability to detect the intended interaction. The managerial-trait composite, while reliable in the present sample (alpha = .88), may have been susceptible to ceiling effects on some items for the female target. If many participants already endorsed high levels of managerial capacity for the female target, there would be less room for the manipulation to increase ratings further. Inspection of item-level distributions suggested that some items (e.g., “competent in motivating others”) had high average endorsements across both targets, reducing sensitivity. Future studies could improve sensitivity by including items with a wider range of difficulty or by using forced-choice paradigms that reduce social desirability and allow more direct assessment of relative stereotypes. Sixth, the possibility of sample-specific characteristics influencing the results should be acknowledged. The sample was skewed toward younger adults and included a higher proportion of female participants than might be present in other contexts. If younger participants or women are more likely to see females as managerial, then the observed pattern could reflect demographic changes rather than the collapse of the stereotype in the population at large. It is therefore important for future work to replicate these findings in more diverse and representative samples, including older adults and individuals with a broader range of occupational experiences. Seventh, the exploratory analyses provided little evidence that participant gender or prior managerial experience meaningfully moderated the results. Both male and female participants exhibited the female advantage, and prior managerial experience did not shift the pattern. These null moderation findings suggest that the female advantage was not confined to a particular subgroup in the sample, although statistical power for these interactions was lower and definitive conclusions should be reserved. The Bayesian analysis that provided moderate evidence in favor of the null interaction model lends some weight to the conclusion that the manipulation did not have a substantive moderation effect on the relationship between target gender and managerial ratings. Taken together, the pattern of results supports several tentative conclusions. Brief single-exposure manipulations in the form used here may be insufficient to alter managerial judgments in a measurable way when explicit ratings are used as the outcome. At the same time, the unexpected female advantage invites further research into current levels of gendered managerial perception in different populations and contexts. For applied practice, the findings imply that brief awareness-raising articles alone may not be a reliable strategy to shift personnel evaluations in organizations; more extended and active interventions are likely necessary. Theoretical implications of the current findings include the need to view stereotype strength as dynamic and context-dependent rather than as a fixed attribute. The “think manager, think male” heuristic has been a useful descriptive generalization for decades, but the present data suggest that its applicability may vary across time, groups, and measurement contexts. The results therefore call for a more nuanced theoretical model that integrates social change, sample composition, measurement modality, and manipulation strength when predicting when gendered leadership stereotypes will emerge or be attenuated. Several clear avenues for future research arise from this study. First, direct replication with a larger and more diverse sample is an essential first step to determine the generalizability of the female advantage observed here. Replications should include participants from different age cohorts, occupational backgrounds, and cultural contexts. Second, experimental manipulations of varying intensity and format should be compared systematically. For example, researchers could compare a single short article, multiple articles over time, video profiles of female leaders, and interactive contact interventions to determine which formats produce the most durable shifts in managerial judgments. Third, measures that tap implicit associations—such as implicit association tests or response-time based paradigms—should be used alongside explicit rating scales to assess whether different facets of stereotype endorsement are differentially influenced by interventions. It is possible that implicit associations remain male-biased even when explicit ratings do not, and such dissociations would have important theoretical and practical implications. Fourth, interventions that combine counterstereotypic exemplars with perspective-taking exercises, structured decision-making rubrics, or accountability prompts may prove more effective than exemplars alone. Applied work in organizational contexts often suggests that structural changes to evaluation processes produce more reliable reductions in bias than brief informational interventions. Fifth, researchers should examine potential mechanisms that might lead to a female advantage in managerial ratings, including media exposure patterns, shifting cultural norms, and selection processes in the sample. Qualitative work could help elucidate why participants perceive female targets as more managerial in some contexts. Sixth, future studies should pretest target vignettes and names to ensure that they do not carry unintended valence or cultural connotations that could bias results. Pretesting can prevent idiosyncratic name effects or subtle narrative cues from confounding the intended manipulation. Seventh, longitudinal designs would be valuable to determine whether any manipulation effects are transient or durable. Short-term changes that fade quickly may be insufficient for long-term organizational change, whereas interventions that produce sustained shifts would be more promising. Methodological refinements can also improve clarity about the boundary conditions of stereotype change. For example, factorial designs that cross exemplar exposure with accountability instructions or with group-status feedback could reveal interactive effects that inform theory. Finally, the ethical implications of interventions designed to alter stereotypes deserve attention. Interventions intended to reduce bias must be evaluated not only for efficacy but also for unintended consequences, such as backfire effects or resistance among some subgroups. In conclusion, the present study found no evidence that a single short counterstereotypic article differentially altered managerial ratings of male and female targets and unexpectedly observed a female advantage in managerial ratings across conditions. These results point to the need for more nuanced theoretical accounts of gendered leadership stereotypes that account for social change and emphasize the importance of stronger, multi-faceted interventions when the goal is to alter personnel evaluations. Future research that addresses the limitations identified here and systematically varies manipulation strength, measurement modality, and sample composition will be essential to clarify when and how gendered leadership stereotypes can be reliably changed.
