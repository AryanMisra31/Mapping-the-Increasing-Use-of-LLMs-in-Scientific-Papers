Introduction

Empathy is a core component of social cognition that facilitates interpersonal understanding and prosocial behavior. Empathy allows individuals to infer and resonate with the emotional states of others, and it supports effective social interaction across a variety of contexts. Because empathy contributes to relationship quality, moral reasoning, and social cooperation, understanding the factors that influence empathy has been a topic of sustained interest in psychology. Researchers have examined stable dispositional correlates of empathy, such as temperament and personality, as well as more malleable influences, including socialization, education, and media exposure. Among proposed malleable influences, engagement with fictional narratives has received increasing attention as a potential source of change in empathic capacity. The idea that reading fiction could shape empathy is grounded in the notion that narratives simulate social experience and invite readers to inhabit the perspectives of characters. By repeatedly engaging in perspective-taking within fictional contexts, readers may practice cognitive and affective processes that are relevant to real-world empathic responding. Several studies have reported positive associations between the amount of fiction reading and measures of trait empathy or theory of mind, and experimental work has suggested that short-term exposure to literary fiction can temporarily enhance performance on certain perspective-taking tasks. These findings have fueled the hypothesis that narrative exposure, particularly through reading, can function as an informal form of empathy training. However, the literature is not unanimous, and replication attempts have produced mixed results, which points to the importance of examining boundary conditions and alternative forms of narrative exposure.

Alongside the focus on reading, fictional narratives are consumed in many other formats in contemporary media environments, including films and television programmes. Visual narrative media differ from written narratives in modality, pacing, sensory richness, and the balance between showing and telling, and these differences could hypothetically influence their effects on empathy-related processes. Films and television provide audiovisual cues to character emotion and context, and they often use actors’ facial expressions and vocal prosody to present inner states that readers must infer through text. It is plausible that for some consumers, visual narratives might reduce the need to generate mental-state inferences actively, because emotions and intentions can be conveyed more directly on screen. On the other hand, visual narratives may also promote empathic engagement through vivid portrayal of characters and dynamic situations that capture viewers’ attention and emotional investment. The net effect of visual fiction on empathy is therefore theoretically ambiguous and empirically under-explored in comparison to print fiction.

Beyond the modality of the narratives themselves, individual difference variables such as gender have been robustly associated with empathy in numerous studies. On average, females have been found to report higher levels of self-reported empathy and to perform better on a variety of emotion-recognition and empathic concern measures. Explanations for gender differences in empathy have ranged from socialization and gender roles to biological factors and differential opportunities for practicing empathic behavior. Given the consistent mean-level difference in empathy by gender, it is important to consider gender not only as an independent predictor of empathy but also as a potential moderator of media effects on empathy. In other words, the impact of narrative exposure on empathic functioning may differ for males and females, either because of baseline differences in empathy, differing media use patterns, or differences in cognitive and affective processing of narrative content.

Despite the theoretical relevance of gender, relatively little empirical work has explicitly assessed whether gender moderates the relationship between fictional engagement and empathy, and even less work has focused on visual narrative formats. Studies that have examined reading often control for gender statistically, but few have tested interaction effects or conducted stratified analyses by gender. Similarly, research that has addressed films or television has tended to focus on short-term experimental effects of specific content rather than naturalistic patterns of viewing and their associations with dispositional empathy. Naturalistic associations are important to assess because they capture variation in habitual media exposure and may reveal accumulative or long-term relationships distinct from short-term experimental effects.

In addition to modality and gender, the operationalization of narrative exposure varies widely across studies and may influence observed associations. Some studies measure frequency of reading or viewing in broad categories, whereas others ask about specific titles, genres, or intensity of engagement. Frequency measures such as hours of fictional viewing per time unit capture a broad behavioral index of exposure but do not directly measure cognitive engagement, narrative transportation, or identification with characters—all of which are theorized to mediate media effects on empathy. Therefore, research that uses simple frequency measures is valuable for assessing everyday behavior, but it must be interpreted with caution regarding mechanisms.

The current study was designed to address several gaps in the literature simultaneously. First, it focused explicitly on fictional viewing in visual formats—films and television programmes—measured as self-reported hours of fictional viewing per fortnight. Second, it examined the relationship between this naturalistic measure of fictional viewing and trait empathy in a community sample. Third, it tested whether gender affected the relationship between fictional viewing and empathy by examining mean differences and stratified associations and by including an interaction term in multivariate models. This design allowed us to assess both overall relationships and gender-specific patterns.

Based on prior research reporting a positive association between narrative exposure and empathy, we initially expected that greater amounts of fictional viewing would be associated with higher self-reported empathy. Given the robust sex differences in measures of empathy favoring females, we also expected to find higher mean empathy scores among female participants. However, because the mechanisms by which visual narratives might influence empathy are less well established than for print narratives, and because gender could plausibly moderate those mechanisms in complex ways, we did not make a strong directional prediction regarding the interaction between gender and fictional viewing. Instead, we tested whether the association between fictional viewing and empathy differed for males and females and whether any such difference was robust to control for demographic covariates.

In addition to the primary analyses, we planned sensitivity checks to examine the influence of potentially confounding variables such as age and education. By controlling for these factors, we aimed to determine whether observed relationships were independent of demographic covariates that are known to correlate with both media use patterns and empathic self-reports. We also examined distributions of the key variables to assess whether non-normality or outliers could unduly influence statistical tests.

The theoretical contribution of this study is twofold. First, it seeks to clarify whether habitual exposure to fictional films and television is associated with trait empathy in a manner similar to the associations often reported for print fiction. Second, it investigates whether gender serves as a moderator of this association, thereby informing theories about individual differences in media effects on socio-cognitive outcomes. Empirical clarity on these points can contribute to broader debates about the potential of everyday narrative engagement to shape social cognition and about whether interventions aimed at increasing empathy might benefit from designing gender-tailored content or delivery channels.

Finally, the study adopts a cautious interpretive stance, recognizing the cross-sectional nature of the data and the limitations of self-report measures for inferring causal relationships and internal processes. We treat the observed associations as suggestive rather than definitive and emphasize the need for complementary longitudinal and experimental studies to test causal pathways. Nevertheless, documenting naturalistic relationships between media exposure and empathy remains important for generating hypotheses and informing the design of future research and applied interventions.

Methods

Participants were recruited through a combination of university participant pools, social media advertisements, and community bulletin postings. The recruitment materials invited persons aged 18 and older to take part in an online survey about media habits and social attitudes. A total of 205 individuals began the survey, and after applying pre-specified inclusion criteria and attention/check questions, data from 192 participants were retained for analysis. The final sample included 192 participants because 13 participants either failed attention checks or provided incomplete data on the primary variables. The sample included 92 self-identified males and 100 self-identified females, and participants who selected a non-binary or other gender identity were asked to indicate this, but due to small numbers in these categories the primary analyses focus on male and female groups; data from non-binary participants were retained for descriptive reporting but were not included in gender-stratified hypothesis tests because of insufficient power for reliable inference. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 65 years, with a mean age of 28.4 years and a standard deviation of 9.2 years. Educational attainment varied; approximately 56% of participants reported current enrollment in or completion of undergraduate studies, 28% reported completion of postgraduate education, and the remainder reported high school or vocational qualifications. The sample was ethnically and socioeconomically diverse within the constraints of an online convenience sample.

All participants provided informed consent by selecting an online consent agreement before beginning the questionnaire. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by an institutional ethics committee, and the research conformed to relevant ethical standards regarding voluntary participation and data confidentiality. Participants were compensated with a small voucher or course research credit, depending on their recruitment route.

Measures

Trait empathy was measured using a widely used self-report instrument that assesses multiple components of empathy. The chosen instrument consisted of 28 items scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Higher total scores indicated greater self-reported empathy. The scale included items tapping both affective empathy (e.g., empathic concern) and cognitive empathy (e.g., perspective taking), and the total score was used as the primary dependent variable for consistency with past work that aggregates across components to estimate dispositional empathy. Internal consistency of the scale in the present sample was high, with Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88.

Fictional viewing was assessed using a self-report quantity measure that asked participants to estimate the average number of hours they spent watching fictional films or television programmes in a typical fortnight (a two-week period). The item explicitly asked participants to exclude news, documentaries, and non-fictional reality formats, and to include only films and television programmes that primarily depict fictional narratives. Participants entered a numeric value; values greater than 168 hours were flagged and checked for plausibility as part of data cleaning. Responses ranged from 0 to 56 hours per fortnight, with the vast majority of values under 30 hours. Because viewing hours were positively skewed, distributional characteristics were examined and sensitivity checks using transformed and non-parametric methods were planned.

Demographic variables included self-identified gender, age, highest level of education completed, and self-reported ethnicity. Participants were also asked two attention-check questions embedded within the survey to ensure data quality, and responses that failed these checks were excluded from analysis. Participants were additionally asked about general media use patterns, such as hours of non-fictional viewing and reading habits, to enable supplementary analyses examining specificity of any observed associations.

Procedure

Participants completed the measures online via a secure survey platform. After consenting, participants provided demographic information and then completed the empathy measure, followed by the fictional viewing item and supplementary media use questions. The order of the empathy and viewing questions was fixed to reduce unnecessary variability; presenting the empathy items first reduced the likelihood that participants would artificially inflate or deflate empathy responses in reaction to the viewing assessment. The entire survey took approximately 15 minutes to complete. Data were stored on a secure server and were de-identified prior to analysis.

Data preparation and statistical analysis

Data cleaning involved screening for missing values, implausible entries, and failures on attention checks. Participants with incomplete data on the primary variables or who failed attention checks were excluded. For the primary analyses, descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables, and distributions were visually inspected. Because the distribution of fictional viewing hours exhibited positive skew, we examined both raw and log-transformed versions of the variable for sensitivity analyses. However, primary analyses are reported using the raw measure for interpretability, and any differences observed using transformed data are reported as sensitivity checks.

To address the study aims, we conducted a set of pre-planned analyses. First, we examined the simple Pearson correlation between hours of fictional viewing per fortnight and trait empathy for the full sample. Second, we compared mean empathy scores between males and females using an independent-samples t-test. Third, to test whether the relationship between fictional viewing and empathy differed by gender, we conducted stratified Pearson correlations separately for males and females. Fourth, we estimated a linear regression model predicting trait empathy from fictional viewing hours, gender, and the interaction between viewing hours and gender. This interaction term tested statistical moderation by gender. Age and education were included as covariates in follow-up regression models to assess the robustness of the findings. We report effect sizes (Cohen’s d for the t-test and Pearson’s r for correlations) and 95% confidence intervals for key estimates, and we consider an alpha level of .05 as the threshold for statistical significance. Sensitivity analyses included non-parametric Spearman correlations and analyses using log-transformed viewing hours to verify that results were not driven by skew or outliers.

Prior to data collection, a power analysis based on conventions for detecting small-to-moderate correlations indicated that a sample of approximately 190 participants would provide 80% power to detect a correlation of around r = .20 at alpha = .05. The actual sample of 192 therefore provided reasonable power to detect small-to-moderate effects, although it would be less well powered for detecting very small effects or interactions of modest magnitude. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and assumptions for statistical tests (e.g., homoscedasticity for regression) were evaluated using diagnostic plots.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. For the full sample of 192 participants, the mean trait empathy score was 86.2 (SD = 12.9) on the 28-item 5-point scale sum, indicating a moderate-to-high average level of self-reported empathy. The distribution of empathy scores approximated normality with a slight negative skew; skewness statistics did not exceed conventional thresholds that would invalidate parametric tests. For fictional viewing hours per fortnight, the sample mean was 9.8 hours (SD = 6.7), and the median was 8 hours. The distribution of viewing hours was positively skewed, with skewness approximately 1.2; most participants reported between 0 and 20 hours, and a small number reported higher values up to 56 hours. Because of the skew, we examined both raw and log-transformed viewing hours in sensitivity checks; transforming the variable produced similar substantive results and is reported where relevant.

When comparing gender groups, female participants (n = 100) had a mean empathy score of 90.1 (SD = 11.3), whereas male participants (n = 92) had a mean empathy score of 81.8 (SD = 13.1). An independent-samples t-test indicated that this difference was statistically significant, t(190) = 5.01, p < .001, with a Cohen’s d of 0.67, indicating a medium-to-large effect size. The mean difference of 8.3 points and its 95% confidence interval suggested a reliable difference in reported empathy favoring females in this sample. The difference remained significant when controlling for age and education in a linear regression model predicting empathy from gender, age, and education; gender remained a significant predictor (b = 8.1, SE = 1.6, p < .001).

For hours of fictional viewing, the mean for females was 10.2 hours (SD = 6.5) and for males was 9.3 hours (SD = 6.8). The difference in viewing hours between genders was not statistically significant, t(190) = 0.86, p = .39. Thus, although females reported higher empathy on average, males and females in this sample reported similar amounts of fictional viewing in hours per fortnight.

The primary correlation of interest was the association between fictional viewing hours and trait empathy across the full sample. The Pearson correlation between viewing hours and empathy was r = .08, 95% CI [-.05, .20], p = .21, indicating a small positive association that did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance. The Spearman rank correlation yielded a similar result, rho = .07, p = .25, and the correlation using log-transformed viewing hours was r = .09, p = .18. These sensitivity checks suggest that the non-significant overall association was not attributable to skew or outliers in the viewing variable.

Given the strong mean-level gender difference in empathy and the theoretical possibility of gender moderation, we examined correlations separately by gender. For male participants (n = 92), the Pearson correlation between fictional viewing hours and empathy was r = .28, 95% CI [.07, .46], p = .01, indicating a small-to-moderate positive association. For female participants (n = 100), the correlation was r = .01, 95% CI [-.15, .17], p = .92, indicating no observable association. The difference between these two correlations was examined using Fisher’s Z transformation. The comparison yielded a Z = 1.72, p = .09, which suggests that the difference between male and female correlations approached but did not reach conventional statistical significance at the alpha = .05 level when tested directly. This indicates that although the association was statistically significant for males alone and not for females, the formal test of moderation via difference in correlations was inconclusive under conventional thresholds.

To provide a more direct test of moderation, we estimated a linear regression predicting trait empathy from fictional viewing hours, gender (coded 0 = male, 1 = female), and the interaction between viewing hours and gender. In this model, the main effect of viewing hours on empathy was not significant (b = 0.11, SE = 0.09, p = .21). Gender was a significant predictor (b = 8.37, SE = 1.67, p < .001), consistent with the t-test result. The interaction term for viewing hours x gender had a coefficient of b = -0.23, SE = 0.13, p = .08, representing a negative interaction that approached but did not reach statistical significance at the .05 level. The direction of the interaction indicated that the slope relating viewing hours to empathy was more positive for males than for females, aligning with the stratified correlations, but the interaction did not meet the conventional criterion for statistical significance.

We conducted follow-up regression models that included age and education as covariates to assess robustness. In the covariate-adjusted model, the main effect of viewing hours remained non-significant (b = 0.09, SE = 0.09, p = .30), gender remained significant (b = 7.92, SE = 1.70, p < .001), and the interaction term was similar in magnitude and significance level (b = -0.21, SE = 0.13, p = .11). Age was not a significant predictor in these adjusted models, and education level showed a small non-significant association with empathy. These results indicate that the pattern of findings was not meaningfully altered by inclusion of demographic covariates.

Because the finding of a significant correlation in males but not females could be influenced by different variances in the two groups, we examined the variance of empathy within genders. Variance in empathy among males (SD = 13.1) was slightly larger than among females (SD = 11.3), but the difference in variance did not appear large enough to fully account for the observed pattern of associations. We also examined potential non-linear relationships by fitting quadratic terms for viewing hours, but these terms were non-significant in the full sample and within gender groups. This suggests that the relationship between viewing hours and empathy in males was approximately linear within the observed range.

As an additional sensitivity check, we re-ran the male-only correlation after excluding three male participants who reported extremely high viewing hours (>40 hours per fortnight). The correlation in males decreased slightly but remained statistically significant (r = .25, p = .02), indicating that the male association was not driven solely by a small number of high-frequency viewers. For females, excluding outliers had no effect on the near-zero correlation.

We also examined whether reported hours of non-fictional viewing were related to empathy to assess specificity. Across the full sample, hours of non-fictional viewing were not significantly correlated with empathy (r = .02, p = .78), and stratified analyses by gender showed no meaningful associations. This pattern supports the idea that any observed association between media exposure and empathy may be specific to fictional content, although the non-significant overall result cautions against strong conclusions.

Finally, exploratory analyses examined whether self-reported engagement measures, gathered for a subset of participants who completed additional items about narrative transportation and character identification, mediated the association between viewing hours and empathy. In this smaller subsample, character identification was correlated with both empathy and viewing hours for males but not for females, and mediation models suggested partial mediation of the male viewing-empathy association by identification. However, these analyses were exploratory, based on a reduced sample, and should be interpreted cautiously.

In summary, the primary analyses showed no significant association between hours of fictional viewing per fortnight and trait empathy in the full sample. Females reported significantly higher empathy than males. When analyses were stratified by gender, a small-to-moderate positive association between viewing hours and empathy emerged for males but not for females. A formal test of the interaction between viewing hours and gender in regression approached but did not reach statistical significance. Sensitivity analyses suggested that the pattern was not driven by distributional outliers or demographic covariates.

Discussion

The present study assessed whether habitual engagement with fictional films and television, measured as hours per fortnight, was associated with trait empathy and whether gender influenced this relationship. The findings contribute to a nuanced picture of media-empathy associations in several ways. First, contrary to a straightforward hypothesis that more fictional viewing would be associated with higher empathy across the board, the study found no significant association in the full sample. Second, consistent with a large body of literature, females reported higher trait empathy than males. Third, and most interestingly, stratified analyses revealed a small-to-moderate positive correlation between fictional viewing and empathy for males but not for females. However, the formal statistical test for moderation via an interaction term in regression analysis was not statistically significant at conventional levels, even though it was in the direction predicted by the stratified correlations.

Interpreting these findings requires consideration of several theoretical and methodological points. The lack of an overall association between fictional viewing hours and empathy suggests that simple quantity of exposure to visual narratives may not be a robust predictor of dispositional empathic capacity when measured cross-sectionally. This contrasts with some prior work that has reported links between reading fiction and empathy, and it highlights the importance of modality in media effects. Visual narratives differ from written narratives in ways that may reduce the extent to which they cultivate the practice of imagining others’ mental states. For example, a film or television programme can present a character’s emotions directly through actors’ performances, music, and cinematography, thereby reducing the need for viewers to generate inner-state inferences. If the mechanism by which narrative exposure increases empathy is practice in mental-state inference, then modalities that make inference more effortful—such as literary fiction—might be more effective at producing spillover effects on dispositional empathy than modalities that more fully depict internal states.

Another possibility is that key mediators were not captured by the primary measure of viewing quantity. Theories that link narrative exposure to empathy often emphasize cognitive and affective engagement processes such as transportation into the narrative, identification with characters, and reflective processing of moral dilemmas. Hours of viewing per se do not measure how deeply viewers engage with content; a person might spend many hours passively watching episodic television without experiencing strong narrative transportation or identification. The exploratory mediation findings in the present study’s subsample suggest that identification may play a role, at least for males, but these results are tentative and require replication in larger samples with more comprehensive measures of engagement.

The gender-specific pattern—where males showed a positive association between fictional viewing and empathy while females did not—raises several interpretive hypotheses. One straightforward interpretation is that males, who reported lower mean empathy, may have more room for variation in empathy related to external experiences such as media exposure. In statistical terms, a floor or ceiling effect could make it easier to detect variability-linked correlates in the lower-mean gender group. If a ceiling effect limits upward variation in empathy among females, then any positive association with viewing might be attenuated or harder to detect. However, inspection of the distributional statistics indicated that the female empathy scores did not exhibit extreme ceiling effects; thus, this explanation is only partial.

Another interpretation is that males and females may differ in the types of fictional content they choose, and content differences could drive differential associations with empathy. For instance, some genres may be more likely to foster perspective-taking and empathic concern (e.g., character-driven dramas) than others (e.g., action-oriented entertainment). If males in the sample tended to watch more of the empathy-enhancing genres, while females watched a broader or different mix that did not further increase empathy, this could explain the observed gender-specific association. Unfortunately, the primary measure in this study did not capture genre or qualitative characteristics of viewed content, which limits the ability to test this explanation empirically. Future work should include content coding and genre differentiation to probe this possibility.

A third possibility relates to differential processing styles or interpretations of narrative cues. Males and females might differ in how they interpret audiovisual emotional cues such as facial expressions and vocal tone. If males engage more actively in cognitive processing to interpret such cues, their viewing experiences might entail a form of mental-state practice that translates into higher dispositional empathy. Conversely, if females, on average, rely on or automatically perceive such cues more effectively at baseline, additional exposure may not alter dispositional empathy measurably. These speculations about processing styles would benefit from converging evidence using behavioral tasks or neurocognitive measures.

The fact that the interaction term in the regression model did not reach conventional significance levels despite the stratified male correlation being significant highlights an important statistical nuance. A significant association within a subgroup does not necessarily imply a significant difference between subgroups. The Fisher’s Z test of difference between correlations in this study yielded a p-value that approached significance, and the regression interaction term had a p-value in the marginal range. These patterns suggest that while gender differences in the relationship may exist, the current sample size provided only limited power to detect modest interaction effects with high confidence. Future studies aiming specifically to test moderation by gender should be powered to detect interactions of small-to-moderate effect size and consider pre-registering analysis plans to avoid selective reporting.

Methodological limitations of the present study constrain the strength of causal and generalizable conclusions. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; it is equally plausible that more empathic males choose to consume greater amounts of fictional media, that greater fictional viewing increases empathy for some males, or that a third variable influences both media use and empathy. Longitudinal or experimental designs would be required to disentangle directionality. Similarly, self-report measures of empathy are subject to biases, including social desirability and introspective limits. Behavioral measures of empathic accuracy, physiological indices of affective resonance, and multi-informant reports could complement self-reports to provide a richer assessment of empathic functioning.

The measurement of fictional viewing as hours per fortnight also has limitations. Self-reported time estimates can be imprecise and influenced by recall bias. Moreover, hours alone do not capture the quality of engagement, narrative complexity, character development, or the presence of morally challenging situations—factors that may be central to any empathy-enhancing effects. Incorporating more nuanced measures of media engagement, such as diaries, passive sensing of viewing behavior, or experience sampling methods that assess in-the-moment engagement, could improve measurement precision and illuminate mechanisms.

Despite the limitations, the study has several strengths. The sample was diverse in age and educational background relative to student-only samples, and the use of multiple analytic approaches, including sensitivity checks and covariate-adjusted models, bolstered confidence in the robustness of the core findings. The inclusion of both basic descriptive comparisons and formal tests of moderation provided a balanced analytic perspective. Additionally, the exploratory mediation analyses—while preliminary—offered useful hypotheses about identification and transportation that can inform future targeted studies.

Theoretical implications stem from the differential findings for males and females. If replicated, the pattern suggests that the relationship between narrative exposure and empathy is more complex than a simple, generalizable effect of consumption quantity. Theories that posit a general training effect of narrative exposure might need to specify boundary conditions related to modality, engagement depth, and individual differences such as gender. It may be that narrative media contribute to empathy in interaction with personal predispositions and content features; such an interactionist account would be richer and more realistic than a uniform exposure model.

Practical implications also follow. If certain forms of fictional viewing can be associated with higher empathy for specific groups, media-based interventions could be designed to target those groups and optimize content features that promote perspective-taking and identification. For instance, curated film-based programs for male audiences that emphasize complex character development and moral dilemmas might be more effective in promoting empathic growth than broad encouragement to watch more fiction. Educational and therapeutic applications of narrative media would benefit from tailoring content and delivery to audience characteristics rather than relying solely on exposure volume.

Future research directions are numerous. First, longitudinal panel studies that track changes in media use and empathy over time would help establish temporal precedence and inform causal hypotheses. Second, randomized experiments that manipulate content features (e.g., perspective-taking prompts, character perspective depth, genre) and measure empathic outcomes with both self-report and behavioral indices would clarify mechanisms. Third, multimodal measurement approaches that combine objective viewing logs, content coding, and in-the-moment engagement assessments would disentangle quantity from quality of narrative exposure. Fourth, research should examine whether the observed male-specific association replicates across cultures and age cohorts and whether it extends to other demographic categories such as socioeconomic status or personality traits.

Fifth, neurocognitive investigations could explore whether repeated engagement with fictional visual narratives leads to measurable changes in brain regions implicated in social cognition and emotion processing, and whether such changes differ by gender. Sixth, studies that directly compare modalities—reading versus audiovisual narratives—using matched content could test whether modality per se accounts for differences in empathy-related outcomes. Seventh, given the exploratory suggestion that character identification may mediate effects in males, future work should measure identification and related constructs systematically and test mediation models with adequate power.

In conclusion, this study found no significant overall relationship between hours of fictional films and television viewing and trait empathy in a community sample, while replicating the robust mean-level gender difference in empathy favoring females. A notable pattern emerged in which males—but not females—showed a positive association between fictional viewing hours and empathy, though formal moderation tests were only marginally significant. The findings suggest that the relationship between narrative exposure and empathy is complex and may depend on modality, engagement processes, and individual differences. The results underscore the need for more nuanced and methodologically rigorous research to unpack how and for whom narrative media can contribute to empathic development.
