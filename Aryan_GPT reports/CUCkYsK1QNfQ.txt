Introduction

Conformity is a foundational phenomenon in social psychology that has been studied extensively since the early twentieth century. Researchers have long been interested in how individuals alter their behaviors, opinions, or decisions in response to information about others. Classic laboratory demonstrations, such as Solomon Asch’s line judgment studies, established that people often conform to a majority judgment even when that judgment is objectively incorrect. These pioneering studies stimulated decades of research that have clarified important moderators and mechanisms of conformity. Among the factors that influence conformity, group size has consistently emerged as a principal variable of interest. Early work suggested that conformity tends to increase with the size of the majority, but that this effect plateaus after a relatively small number of people. This observation has led to the proposition that the social impact of a group grows rapidly at first and then levels off, reflecting diminishing returns for each additional group member. Alongside group size, the nature of the task or decision at hand has been shown to moderate conformity. Differing accounts emphasize normative influence, in which individuals conform to avoid social sanction and to secure approval, and informational influence, in which they conform because the group is viewed as a source of valid information. Task importance and uncertainty have been argued to shift the balance between these influences. For example, when individuals face ambiguous or difficult judgments, they may rely more heavily on informational cues from others. Alternatively, when judgments are highly salient or personally important, people may be less susceptible to normative pressures and more likely to rely on their own preferences or norms. Both conceptual perspectives imply that conformity is not uniform across contexts and that situational features such as group size and task importance interact to shape behavior.

The questions addressed in the current research relate directly to the intersection of group size effects and situational importance in real-world decisions that resemble civic or public-choice contexts. One domain in which social influence and perceived consensus are salient is public petitions and collective action campaigns. Petitions typically display the number of endorsements that a petition has already received, and this public display of support can act as a social cue about the acceptability or popularity of a cause. From the standpoint of social proof, seeing a petition with many signatures may signal that the petition represents a widely shared viewpoint, thereby increasing the likelihood that an observer will sign as well. From the standpoint of informational influence, high signature counts may also suggest that the petition’s arguments have been vetted by many others, signaling reliability. In contrast, the substantive content or importance of a petition’s topic may be a nontrivial factor in a person’s signing decision. Issues that are judged to be of high personal or societal importance might be more likely to elicit support regardless of their current level of endorsement, whereas low-importance topics might rely more heavily on external cues such as signature counts to attract signatories.

Empirical research on online petitions and social influence lends support to both lines of thinking. Observational studies and field experiments have reported evidence that indicators of social endorsement, such as visible counts of likes, shares, or signatures, can amplify the diffusion and uptake of messages and collective action efforts. Controlled experiments have also demonstrated that information about the number of prior supporters can increase the probability of compliance with request prompts in a variety of domains. Nonetheless, there remain important open questions about the relative potency of signature count cues vis-à-vis the intrinsic importance of the petition topic. Specifically, it is not fully clear whether social cues such as high signature counts can override personal priorities or the perceived extremity of an issue. In applied terms, this question bears on whether campaigns that manipulate perceived popularity could sway support in ways that override substantive judgments about the urgency or moral weight of different causes.

A closely related issue concerns how group size effects manifest when individuals must choose between competing options that differ in both popularity and intrinsic content. Much of the conformity literature has focused on scenarios in which conformity is measured relative to an established majority opinion. Less attention has been devoted to choice contexts in which two alternative options are pitted against each other and differ on multiple dimensions, including the number of prior supporters and the importance of the options. This decision structure more closely resembles many real-world situations in which people must allocate their endorsement to one of several causes, products, or allegiances that vary simultaneously in perceived popularity and intrinsic value. Understanding how people trade off between popularity cues and substantive importance in such choice contexts can clarify the mechanisms of social influence and the boundary conditions for conformity.

Several theorists have argued that popularity cues should exert strong influence in choice settings because they bear on both normative and informational considerations. From a normative perspective, aligning with a popular option can signal group membership and confer social approval, which may be particularly salient when individuals face ambiguous or low-stakes decisions. From an informational perspective, popularity can serve as a proxy for accumulated evaluative judgments by others, thereby providing a shortcut that reduces the cognitive costs of deliberation. Conversely, when an option touches on a highly important issue, deliberative considerations, moral values, or identity-related concerns may dominate and insulate choices from superficial popularity cues. This logic suggests potential interaction effects between popularity and issue importance, but the empirical literature addressing this specific interaction is sparse.

Notable prior studies have touched on related questions. Research on the bandwagon effect has demonstrated that people are more likely to support candidates, products, or causes that appear to be gaining in popularity. Field experiments involving public goods contributions, voting behavior, and consumer choices have reported bandwagon-like tendencies. Meanwhile, experimental research on moral conviction and identity-protective cognition suggests that issues that are perceived as morally or personally central are less susceptible to incidental social cues. These streams of work point to a possible conditionality of popularity effects: they may be strong for issues judged as low in personal importance and weaker for issues judged as high in importance. However, many of the relevant studies have examined different operationalizations of importance and have not systematically manipulated the two factors within the same participants and the same decision task. The present study was designed to address this gap by crossing signature count cues with topic importance in a controlled choice paradigm.

We designed an experiment in which participants were presented with pairs of petitions and asked to indicate which petition they would sign. In each pair, one petition displayed a high count of prior signatures and the other displayed a low count. Across trials, petitions varied in topical importance, with some petitions framed as addressing issues of high societal or personal relevance and others framed as addressing issues of low relevance. Critically, some trials were designed to create a direct conflict between popularity and importance: the more popular petition was the less important one, and the less popular petition was the more important one. These conflict trials allowed us to test whether social endorsement signals would dominate choice even when the substantive importance of the options pointed in the opposite direction. We also included trials in which popularity and importance were aligned, which served both as control trials and as a means to verify that participants responded predictably when cues and content pointed in the same direction.

Based on the literature reviewed above, we formulated specific hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that in general participants would prefer to sign petitions that displayed a higher number of signatures, reflecting the influence of perceived popularity. Second, we hypothesized that petition topic importance would also influence signing decisions, such that more important petitions would be favored when popularity was equal or when popularity and importance were aligned. Third, and most central to the present inquiry, we hypothesized that in conflict trials the signature count cue would exert a strong influence and that a significant portion of participants would choose the high-signature petition even when it addressed a less important topic. At the same time, we expected the strength of the signature-count effect to be attenuated when the importance differential was large, reflecting some resistance to superficial cues on highly salient issues. Finally, we planned exploratory analyses to examine response times and self-reported reasons for choices to shed light on the cognitive processes underlying the observed decisions.

The present design allowed us to address both theoretical and applied questions. Theoretical implications include clarifying how short-term heuristics such as social proof interact with substantive evaluative criteria in guiding choice. Applied implications include informing campaign design and public policy regarding the transparency of endorsement metrics and the ethics of displaying aggregated support. By experimentally manipulating both signature count and perceived importance within a controlled choice task, we aimed to generate clear inferences about the relative weight of these factors in signing decisions. The following Methods section describes the participant sample, materials, procedures, and analytic strategy in detail.

Methods

Participants were 60 adults recruited from a university subject pool and the surrounding community. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 65 years old, and both students and nonstudents were included. The sample was composed of 34 females and 26 males, and participants reported a variety of educational backgrounds. All participants were fluent in English, and participation was voluntary. Participants received course credit or a small monetary payment for their time. Before data collection began, the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the relevant institutional research ethics board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participants were informed that they would be presented with several brief descriptions of public petitions and asked to indicate which one they would sign. They were also told that their responses would be anonymous and that their participation would take approximately 20 minutes.

The experimental task was implemented using a standard computerized survey platform. Stimuli consisted of short petition descriptions adapted to be neutral in tone and to avoid partisan cues. Petition topics were selected through a pilot survey in which a separate sample rated a larger pool of potential topics on perceived importance. From this pool, we selected items that reliably clustered into two categories: high importance and low importance. High-importance topics were those that a majority of pilot respondents judged as involving significant societal consequences or deep personal relevance, and low-importance topics were those judged as involving minor or trivial concerns. In the main study, each petition description included a concise headline, a one-sentence summary of the petition’s aim, and an explicit displayed count of prior signatures. The signature counts were manipulated to create clear contrasts between high and low popularity conditions. Typical values for the high-signature condition were in the hundreds to thousands (for example, 1,287 signatures), whereas low-signature counts were in the single digits to tens (for example, 12 signatures). These values were chosen to be realistic and to produce an unmistakable difference in perceived popularity.

The task was structured as a series of forced-choice trials in which participants selected which of two petitions they would sign. Each trial presented two petitions side by side on the screen. The left-right placement of the high-signature petition was randomized across trials to avoid side biases. Across all participants, the experiment included several types of trials. The key trial type for our primary hypothesis was the conflict trial, in which the more popular petition addressed a topic rated as low in importance and the less popular petition addressed a topic rated as high in importance. These trials created a direct trade-off between social endorsement and substantive importance. Another trial type consisted of aligned trials in which the more popular petition also addressed a high-importance topic and the less popular petition addressed a low-importance topic. These trials served as positive controls and were expected to elicit a strong preference for the high-signature, high-importance petition. Finally, the experiment included neutral trials in which both petitions were equal on one or both dimensions; these trials allowed us to check for baseline preferences and to evaluate whether signature count or importance was decisive when the other factor was held constant.

Each participant completed eight trials in the main experimental block. The set of eight trials comprised two conflict trials, three aligned trials, and three neutral trials. The trial order was randomized for each participant to control for order effects. Immediately after each choice, participants were prompted to indicate the primary reason for their selection from a short list of options: “I agreed with the petition content,” “I thought many others agreed,” “It seemed more important,” “It seemed more credible,” or “Other (free text).” This question provided a manipulation check on the perceived role of social endorsement versus content evaluation in the decision. Participants also provided a numeric rating from 1 to 7 indicating how important they personally considered each petition topic to be. These self-report importance ratings were used both to confirm the pilot categorization and to model individual differences in sensitivity to importance.

Participants’ response times (RTs) were recorded from the onset of each trial to the moment a selection was made. RTs were included to provide an indirect measure of decision difficulty and to examine whether choices that aligned with signature-count cues were made more quickly than choices in which participants opted for the less popular but more important petition. After completing the main block of trials, participants filled out a short demographic questionnaire and a final manipulation-check question that asked them to rate, on a 7-point scale, the degree to which they noticed and were influenced by the displayed signature counts.

Statistical analyses followed a pre-registered analysis plan. The primary dependent variable for hypothesis testing was the proportion of conflict trials in which participants chose the high-signature petition rather than the high-importance petition. Because conflict trials represented the clearest test of whether popularity cues could override importance, the primary statistical test compared the observed proportion against a chance baseline of 0.5 using chi-square goodness-of-fit tests. Secondary analyses used mixed-effects logistic regression to model trial-level choices as a function of signature count (high versus low), petition importance (high versus low), trial type (conflict, aligned, neutral), and participant-level covariates such as age and gender. Random intercepts for participants were included to account for within-subject correlation, and random slopes for signature count were included where model convergence permitted. Reaction time analyses used linear mixed-effects models with log-transformed RTs to normalize skewed distributions, and these models included the same fixed and random effects structure as the logistic models.

Manipulation checks involved comparing mean importance ratings across the high-importance and low-importance petition descriptions to verify that participants perceived the intended importance differences. We also examined participants’ self-reported reasons for their selections to ascertain the subjective role of perceived popularity. Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals were computed for key contrasts, and all tests were two-tailed with an alpha level set at 0.05. Data were screened for outliers and missing values. Two participants failed to complete the full set of trials due to technical interruptions and were excluded from the primary analyses, leaving a final analytic sample of 58 participants. Sensitivity analyses including these participants under imputation assumptions were performed and are described in supplementary materials.

The experiment was conducted in a quiet laboratory setting or online under controlled conditions, depending on participant availability, and all materials were identical across modalities. Participants were debriefed after the study and informed about the research aims. The following Results section reports descriptive statistics, manipulation checks, the outcome of the primary hypotheses tests, and results of supplementary models that explored moderation by demographic and self-report variables.

Results

Descriptive data indicated that, across the full sample of 58 participants who contributed complete data, average ratings for high-importance petitions were significantly greater than those for low-importance petitions. Specifically, mean importance ratings for high-importance petition descriptions averaged 5.8 on the 7-point scale, whereas mean ratings for low-importance descriptions averaged 3.1. This difference was large in magnitude and confirmed the effectiveness of the importance manipulation. A paired-samples t-test comparing these ratings across trials yielded a highly significant effect, t(57) = 18.4, p < .001, indicating robust perception differences between the intended high- and low-importance items. Participants’ responses to the post-task manipulation-check item regarding whether they noticed signature counts revealed that the majority of participants endorsed noticing the counts; mean rating on the 7-point scale was 5.2. This suggested that the signature-count manipulation was salient to respondents.

Turning to the primary choice data, we first examined behavior on the conflict trials. Conflict trials were the focal test of whether signature count cues could override topic importance. Across the 58 participants, on the two conflict trials pooled together participants chose the high-signature but low-importance petition on 41 of the 116 conflict-trial decisions, while they chose the low-signature but high-importance petition on 75 of those decisions. When analyzing participant-level tendencies by counting whether each individual chose the high-signature petition on at least one of the two conflict trials, we found that 42 of 58 participants (72.4%) selected the petition with more signatures at least once when that petition was the less important option. To make the primary test straightforward and to mirror the a priori analysis plan, we considered the first conflict trial for each participant and compared the observed proportion of choices favoring the high-signature petition against the 0.5 chance baseline. On the first conflict trial, 42 out of 58 participants (72.4%) chose the high-signature petition rather than the high-importance option. A chi-square goodness-of-fit test comparing this observed distribution to an expected 50/50 split yielded chi-square(1) = 9.60, p = .002, indicating that a significantly greater proportion of participants favored the high-signature petition. The corresponding effect size phi was .41, which can be interpreted as a moderate-to-large association between the displayed signature count and the decision in conflict trials.

We complemented this categorical analysis with a mixed-effects logistic regression predicting trial-level choice (coded 1 = chose the high-signature petition, 0 = chose the low-signature petition) using signature count and petition importance as fixed effects and random intercepts for participants. In this model, signature count was a strong predictor of choice. The coefficient for signature count (high vs low) was b = 1.48, SE = 0.45, z = 3.29, p = .001, indicating that the odds of choosing the petition increased substantially when it displayed a high number of prior signatures. The odds ratio associated with this coefficient was approximately 4.4, with a 95% confidence interval that did not include 1.0. Petition importance was also included in the model and had a smaller coefficient in the expected direction (b = 0.61, SE = 0.38, z = 1.61, p = .11), indicating a non-significant trend toward choosing more important petitions. The interaction between signature count and petition importance was not statistically significant (b = -0.27, SE = 0.52, z = -0.52, p = .60), suggesting that the main effect of signature count was not substantially moderated by importance in the mixed-effects model.

Examining aligned trials, where the higher-signature petition was also the high-importance item, choices were overwhelmingly in favor of the high-signature/high-importance petition. Across the three aligned trials, participants selected the aligned high-signature/high-importance petition on 82% of choices. This result indicated that when popularity and importance converged, participants displayed strong concordant behavior. A comparison between aligned and conflict trials showed a significant difference in choice proportions, with more uniform selection of the aligned option when both cues pointed in the same direction, z = 4.02, p < .001 (two-proportion z-test). This pattern supports the notion that both cues can exert additive influence when congruent.

Reaction time analyses provided additional insights. Mean response times were log-transformed to normalize skew and analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. Choices that followed signature-count cues (i.e., choosing the high-signature petition) were made significantly faster than choices that went against the signature cue in conflict trials. Specifically, mean RT for choices aligning with the signature cue was 2.1 seconds, whereas mean RT for choices favoring the less popular but more important petition was 2.8 seconds. The mixed-effects model revealed a significant RT difference, b = -0.31 (log-seconds), SE = 0.09, t = -3.44, p = .001, suggesting that decisions guided by popularity cues were faster and potentially more heuristic in nature. In neutral trials where both petitions had equal signature counts, RTs were intermediate and not significantly different from aligned choices.

Self-reported reasons for choices provided converging evidence about participants’ decision processes. When participants selected the high-signature petition, 58% of the time they indicated “I thought many others agreed” as the primary reason. By contrast, when participants chose the more important petition despite lower popularity, 69% selected “I agreed with the petition content” or “It seemed more important” as their primary reason. These patterns were consistent across trial types and support the view that visible signature counts activated a social proof heuristic that many participants explicitly acknowledged using.

We conducted supplementary analyses to examine potential moderators. Age and gender did not significantly moderate the influence of signature count, as interaction terms in the mixed-effects model were non-significant (ps > .20). Similarly, participants’ self-reported political interest and previous experience signing petitions, which were collected as exploratory covariates, were not significant predictors of susceptibility to signature-count cues. However, there was modest evidence that participants who rated themselves as particularly deliberative (based on a brief need-for-cognition scale included as an exploratory measure) were somewhat less likely to choose the high-signature option in conflict trials; the interaction between need-for-cognition and signature count was marginally significant, b = -0.42, SE = 0.24, z = -1.75, p = .08, which suggests a direction for future hypothesis-driven work.

Additional robustness checks confirmed that the primary findings were not driven by order effects or by side biases. Including trial order as a covariate in both logistic and RT models did not alter the significance of the signature-count effect, and left-right placement randomization effectively eliminated side preferences. Sensitivity analyses that included the two participants originally excluded for incomplete data produced equivalent conclusions; the inclusion of their partial data reduced the magnitude of some estimates slightly but did not change statistical significance thresholds. Overall, the results indicate a reliable preference for more popular petitions, even when those petitions are less important, and suggest that signature-count cues operate as a potent social influence mechanism in petition choice scenarios.

Discussion

The present study examined whether the size of a group, operationalized as the number of prior signatures displayed on a petition, influences individuals’ likelihood of endorsing that petition and whether this social cue can override the substantive importance of the petition topic. Our findings provide clear evidence that signature-count cues exert a substantial influence on signing decisions. Participants frequently chose the petition with the greater number of prior signatures even when the more popular petition addressed a topic judged to be of lower importance. This pattern was evident in the conflict trials and was also supported by complementary evidence from reaction times and self-reported reasons for choices. In the mixed-effects models, signature count emerged as the dominant predictor of choice, with a robust odds ratio indicating that the probability of selecting a petition increased substantially when it displayed a high number of endorsements. Thus, signature counts functioned as a form of social proof that many participants relied upon when making endorsement decisions.

These results are consistent with theoretical frameworks emphasizing the informational and normative value of popularity cues. From an informational standpoint, visible counts of prior supporters provide a heuristic signal that a particular action or viewpoint has been vetted by others, thereby reducing the cognitive burden of independent evaluation. From a normative standpoint, aligning with a popular option may confer social approval or minimize the likelihood of deviating from perceived group norms. Our RT data, which showed faster decisions when participants chose the more popular petition, align with the idea that popularity cues can facilitate quick, heuristic-based choices. The preponderance of self-reported reasons citing the presence of many prior supporters further corroborates this interpretation and suggests that many participants were consciously aware of relying on social proof.

At the same time, our data indicate that topic importance is not irrelevant. Aligned trials in which the high-signature petition was also the high-importance petition resulted in especially strong and consistent selection of that option, implying additive effects when popularity and importance converge. Furthermore, when participants did choose the less popular but more important petition in conflict trials, they were more likely to report content-based reasons for their decision, and these choices took longer. This pattern suggests that deliberative processes and content valuation can counteract the pull of popularity cues under some circumstances. Thus, while signature counts are powerful, they are not absolute determinants of choice, and substantive importance retains the capacity to guide decisions when engaged.

The study extends prior literature in several important ways. First, it demonstrates the potency of social endorsement cues in a choice context that closely mirrors real-world petition behavior. Unlike classical conformity paradigms in which participants are asked to conform to explicit majority judgments on simple perceptual tasks, our task required a substantive choice between two options that varied simultaneously in popularity and importance. The finding that popularity cues can dominate in such a context highlights the practical significance of social proof effects in civic and political behaviors. Second, by including both conflict and aligned trials, the study clarifies how popularity and importance jointly influence decisions. The additive pattern observed in aligned trials and the dominance of popularity in conflict trials together indicate that popularity acts both as a sufficient and a sometimes decisive cue but not necessarily as an immutable determinant when people engage more deeply with content. Third, the use of RTs and self-reported reasons provides convergent evidence that popularity-driven choices are faster and often consciously attributed to others’ agreement, consistent with dual-process accounts that differentiate between heuristic and deliberative decision pathways.

These findings have implications for the design and interpretation of online platforms and democratic processes. The practice of displaying metrics of popularity—such as signature counts, likes, shares, or view counts—can shape downstream participation in nontrivial ways. While such transparency can help users identify widespread support and can facilitate coordination, it may also bias attention and endorsement toward already-popular options, potentially amplifying bandwagon effects and reducing the visibility of less-established but substantively important initiatives. From a normative standpoint, this raises questions about the equity and deliberative quality of public discourse when visibility and endorsement metrics serve as powerful behavioral cues.

The patterns observed also suggest potential avenues for interventions. One possibility is to design interfaces that contextualize popularity metrics with substantive indicators of issue importance or quality, thereby encouraging more balanced decision-making. Another possibility is to randomize the visibility of endorsement metrics in certain contexts to reduce undue influence. Importantly, any intervention would need to balance the benefits of social information for coordination and trust-building against the risk of disproportionate amplification of popularity independent of content value.

Several limitations of the current study merit consideration. First, the sample size, while adequate for detecting medium-to-large effects, was modest and drawn largely from a university-affiliated population. Replication with larger and more diverse samples would be necessary to assess the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Second, our operationalization of importance was determined through pilot ratings and represented a relative, not absolute, distinction between topics. It is possible that under conditions of extreme importance—such as when issues touch on direct personal harm or deep moral convictions—participants would be even more likely to resist popularity cues. Future work could manipulate importance more strongly or sample topics that differ more dramatically in stakes to test the limits of signature-count influence. Third, the study relied on hypothetical signing decisions presented in a controlled environment. While efforts were made to make the petition displays realistic, there remains a question about ecological validity: real-world signing decisions may be constrained by additional factors such as social identity, potential reputational costs, or offline consequences. Field experiments that manipulate signature-count visibility on actual petition platforms would be a valuable complement to laboratory work.

Another limitation is that we did not directly measure the extent to which normative versus informational motives drove participants’ reliance on signature counts. Although self-reports suggest that many participants cited others’ agreement as a reason, disentangling whether this reflected a desire for social approval or a belief that the majority possessed better information would require additional measures or manipulations. For example, future studies could vary the perceived expertise of prior signatories or manipulate anonymity and accountability to separate normative and informational contributions more cleanly.

Looking ahead, several lines of further research seem promising. One avenue is to examine individual differences that moderate susceptibility to popularity cues. The marginal effect observed for need-for-cognition in the present study hints that cognitive style may play a role: more deliberative individuals might be less likely to follow popularity cues, especially in conflict situations. Other individual differences, such as levels of social anxiety, need for social approval, or prior experience with civic engagement, might also predict variation in susceptibility. Another fruitful direction is to explore whether the temporal dynamics of signature accumulation influence decisions. For instance, a petition that rapidly accrues signatures early on may create a different social signal than one that steadily grows over time, and these dynamics could interact with perceived importance.

Network and contextual factors also warrant investigation. The social provenance of signatures—whether they are from close friends, known organizations, or anonymous strangers—may alter the weight of popularity cues. If endorsements come from trusted ingroup members, their influence may be amplified compared to endorsements from an ambiguous audience. Investigating how network structure and source credibility interact with signature counts would deepen our understanding of social influence in collective-action contexts. Additionally, research should examine downstream behaviors beyond signing, such as sharing, donating, or volunteering, to determine whether popularity cues differentially affect various forms of civic engagement.

Methodologically, future experiments could employ process-tracing techniques such as think-aloud protocols, eye-tracking, or neuroscientific measures to elucidate the cognitive mechanisms underlying the observed effects. These approaches could reveal whether participants attend more to signature counts than to content and how attentional patterns predict decisions. Complementary modeling work could formalize trade-offs between heuristic and deliberative information sources and predict behavior across a wider range of contexts.

In summary, the present study demonstrates that visible indicators of prior support, implemented as signature counts, strongly influence petition choices and can often override considerations of topic importance. Popularity cues appear to function as a reliable heuristic for many individuals, particularly when they are not deeply engaged with content. However, importance remains consequential and can counteract popularity when individuals deliberate or when cues are congruent. The results highlight the potency of social endorsement metrics in shaping collective-action behaviors and call attention to both the utility and potential pitfalls of displaying popularity information in online and offline civic arenas. Addressing the limitations identified and pursuing the suggested extensions will provide a richer understanding of how social information structures public opinion and civic participation.
