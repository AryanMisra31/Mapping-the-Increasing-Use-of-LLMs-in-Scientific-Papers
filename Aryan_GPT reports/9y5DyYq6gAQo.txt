Introduction

Human language is often treated as an arbitrary mapping between sounds and meanings, yet a growing body of research has demonstrated that non-arbitrary mappings between auditory or phonetic properties and visual or tactile properties are pervasive. These non-arbitrary mappings, often referred to as sound symbolism or cross-modal correspondences, have been shown across multiple domains of perception and across many languages and cultures. One of the most frequently cited demonstrations of such correspondences is the phenomenon commonly called the bouba/kiki effect, in which people reliably associate certain pseudowords with rounded shapes and other pseudowords with spiky shapes. The bouba/kiki pattern was popularized in contemporary cognitive science by work that emphasized the systematic nature of the mapping and suggested that it may illuminate constraints on language learning and the origins of sound-meaning associations.

The bouba/kiki effect has been reported in adult populations from diverse linguistic backgrounds and has even been observed in neonates and young infants, indicating that the mapping may reflect a basic, possibly prelinguistic, sensory correspondence. Beyond the simple rounded/spiky dichotomy, researchers have explored which acoustic and phonological properties of pseudowords drive the effect. Open back rounded vowels such as /u/ and /o/ are often associated with round shapes, whereas front unrounded vowels such as /i/ and /e/ are commonly associated with angular or spiky shapes. Certain consonantal features and prosodic contours have also been implicated in strengthening or modulating the mapping, suggesting that the effect is not reducible to a single segmental property. The consistency and robustness of the bouba/kiki mapping have led some theorists to argue that sound symbolism provides a scaffold for word learning, that it constrains the phonological space used for naming objects, and that it may reflect general principles of multisensory integration.

Despite the widespread documentation of the bouba/kiki effect, important empirical questions remain about the limits and moderators of the phenomenon. For instance, it is not fully understood whether the modality in which the pseudoword is presented—auditory versus visual orthographic presentation—modulates the strength of the mapping. If the bouba/kiki effect arises primarily from low-level acoustic features, one might expect that presenting the word as an audio recording would produce a stronger effect than presenting the word in written form. Conversely, if the effect relies largely on phonological representations that are activated irrespective of input modality, written stimuli might produce effects comparable to or indistinguishable from auditory stimuli. Understanding modality dependence has implications for theories of the cognitive basis of sound symbolism: modality dependence would point toward sensorimotor/auditory-driven mechanisms, whereas modality independence would suggest phonological and symbolic representations underlie the mapping.

Another open question concerns individual differences that might modulate the bouba/kiki effect. Variability in auditory perceptual expertise, for example, could influence the sensitivity with which participants extract phonetic cues that underpin sound-shape mappings. Musicians offer a particularly interesting population to study in this regard because musical training involves extensive practice with fine-grained auditory discrimination, pitch processing, and temporal acuity. Musicians often show enhanced perceptual and cognitive abilities in nonmusical domains that engage auditory processing, and there is evidence that musical training can generalize to phonetic processing, speech perception in noise, and categorical perception of sounds. Given these findings, one plausible hypothesis is that musicians might demonstrate stronger or more consistent bouba/kiki mappings when stimuli are presented in the auditory modality; alternatively, musicians might not differ from nonmusicians if the bouba/kiki effect is driven by cross-modal or semantic-level associations that exceed basic auditory discrimination processes.

Prior studies that have explored individual differences have yielded mixed results. Some research has indicated that developmental factors such as age and literacy can influence sound-symbolic sensitivity, while other work has found little systematic modulation by musical training or other auditory expertise. The picture is further complicated by heterogeneity in how musical experience is quantified across studies. Measures have ranged from self-reported musical engagement to objective grades or years of formal instruction, and such variability in operationalization can obscure genuine relationships. It is therefore important to test well-defined groups of musically trained versus untrained participants, using clear inclusion criteria that reflect meaningful levels of training and experience.

The modality question and the role of musical training intersect in a way that has not, to our knowledge, been exhaustively tested in a single experimental paradigm. If auditory features are crucial, musicians might selectively show enhanced bouba/kiki mappings in the auditory condition but not in the written condition. If phonological activation from orthographic stimuli is sufficient to trigger the mapping, musicians and nonmusicians might perform similarly across modalities. Distinguishing among these possibilities requires an experimental design that directly compares auditory and written presentations within the same set of participants while also including a between-subject factor that indexes musical expertise.

Methodological considerations are also important when designing an experiment to test these questions. The choice of stimuli, the number of trials, control of left-right presentation side in forced-choice tasks, and counterbalancing of block order can all influence the robustness of observed effects. Many canonical demonstrations of the bouba/kiki effect have used a forced-choice task in which participants are shown two shapes—a rounded and a spiky silhouette—and asked which shape corresponds to a given pseudoword. This task has the advantage of simplicity and a clear chance level of 50% for each decision. To probe modality effects, the pseudowords can be delivered either as audio recordings or as visually displayed orthographic forms, with the same pseudoword content across modalities. Within-subject manipulation of modality, with counterbalanced block orders, allows for sensitive assessment of modality differences while controlling for individual variability.

In light of the theoretical questions and methodological opportunities outlined above, the present study was designed with two primary goals. The first goal was to replicate the bouba/kiki effect in an undergraduate sample using both auditory and written presentations of pseudowords. The second goal was to assess whether formal musical training—operationalized here as having achieved Grade 5 or higher on a recognized instrument or voice examination—modulates the strength of the bouba/kiki mapping and whether any such modulation interacts with stimulus modality. Based on previous literature, we predicted that participants would, on average, choose the rounded shape for pseudowords containing rounded vowels and the spiky shape for pseudowords containing unrounded front vowels at above-chance levels.

We considered two competing hypotheses regarding musical training. One hypothesis was that musicians would show stronger bouba/kiki effects than nonmusicians, particularly in the auditory modality, due to enhanced auditory discrimination and phonetic sensitivity associated with musical expertise. The alternative hypothesis was that musical training would not significantly influence the bouba/kiki mapping because the effect may rely on higher-level cross-modal or articulatory-phonological representations that are shared across listeners independent of musical expertise.

Regarding modality, we again considered competing possibilities. If the bouba/kiki effect is predominantly driven by acoustic properties of actual speech sounds, auditory presentation should yield stronger effects than orthographic presentation. If, however, the effect depends on abstract phonological representations or on associations linked with graphemic forms, written stimuli should yield similar effects to auditory stimuli. Given existing evidence that phonological representations are accessed rapidly from orthography in literate adults, we anticipated that both auditory and written forms could elicit bouba/kiki correspondences, but the comparative strength of the effect across modalities was treated as an empirical question.

In order to test these predictions, we recruited a sample of undergraduate students with varying musical backgrounds and implemented a two-alternative forced-choice task in which participants matched pseudowords to pairs of shapes. Modality was manipulated within subjects, with each participant completing both auditory and written blocks, and the order of blocks was counterbalanced. Pseudowords were chosen to include clear rounded and unrounded vowel contrasts, and each pseudoword was presented multiple times with left-right position of shapes counterbalanced to reduce side biases. Responses were recorded and analyzed at both the group level and the individual level, and statistical analyses included one-sample tests against chance, mixed analysis of variance, and confirmatory nonparametric and mixed-effects analyses to ensure robustness.

The present investigation therefore offers an opportunity to extend the bouba/kiki literature by providing a controlled within-subject comparison of auditory and written modalities together with a between-subject comparison of musically trained and untrained participants. By integrating these factors, the study aims to clarify whether basic sound-shape correspondences are shaped by sensory modality and auditory expertise or whether they instead reflect more universal cross-modal mappings that are invariant across such moderators. The results have the potential to inform theoretical accounts of sound symbolism, the cognitive architecture of cross-modal correspondences, and the degree to which expertise in one auditory domain generalizes to other forms of auditory-visual mapping.

Methods

Participants and screening procedures were approved by the university's human subjects review board, and all participants provided written informed consent before participation. Forty undergraduate students participated in the study. Participants were recruited from psychology and music department participant pools and via campus advertisements. There were 20 participants in the musical training group and 20 participants in the nonmusical group. The musical training group consisted of students who had achieved at least Grade 5 level proficiency in their primary instrument or in vocal performance on standardized graded examinations; this criterion was verified via self-report and confirmed by instructors or certificates when available. The nonmusical group consisted of students who reported no formal musical training beyond general school music classes and who did not play an instrument or sing on a regular basis. Recruitment aimed to match groups for age and general educational status; the mean age across the sample was 20.4 years (SD = 2.1), and groups did not differ significantly in age. There were 23 females and 17 males across both groups; gender distribution did not differ significantly between musical and nonmusical groups.

All participants were native speakers of the dominant language of the testing site and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological disorders. Participants completed a brief questionnaire that assessed their musical background, years of instruction, and current musical activity. For musicians, the mean number of years of formal training was 9.7 years (SD = 3.2). The nonmusical group reported a mean of 0.8 years (SD = 1.1) of formal music instruction, typically limited to general school exposure.

Stimuli consisted of two visual shapes and ten pseudowords. The visual shapes were two high-contrast silhouettes presented side-by-side on a neutral background; one shape featured smoothly curved contours and was characterized as the rounded shape, while the other shape featured multiple pointed protrusions and was characterized as the spiky shape. The silhouettes were created to be visually balanced in area and overall size; their left-right positions were counterbalanced across trials. Pseudowords were constructed to vary primarily in vowel quality while controlling for consonantal structure; five pseudowords contained rounded back vowels (for example, "bouba," "bobo," "momo," "dudu," "lolo") and five pseudowords contained front unrounded high vowels (for example, "kiki," "titi," "pipi," "keke," "tete"). Pseudowords were designed to be phonotactically permissible in the participant population’s language and to avoid real-word status.

For the auditory modality, pseudowords were recorded in a sound-attenuated booth by a trained speaker with a neutral intonation. Recordings were normalized for peak amplitude and clipped at uniform durations where possible; mean duration of auditory tokens was 520 ms (SD = 80 ms). Recordings were edited to remove background noise and to ensure clarity and consistency. For the written modality, pseudowords were presented on-screen in a plain sans-serif font at a legible size centered above the pair of shapes.

The experimental design was a 2 x 2 mixed factorial design with musical training (musician vs nonmusician) as a between-subjects factor and modality (auditory vs written) as a within-subjects factor. Each participant completed two blocks of trials, one in each modality. Block order was counterbalanced across participants to control for potential order effects. Each block contained 20 critical trials, for a total of 40 trials per participant. Each of the ten pseudowords appeared twice per block, once with the rounded shape on the left and once with the rounded shape on the right, to control for side biases. Trial order within each block was randomized for each participant.

On each trial participants saw the two shapes presented side-by-side and were either read or saw a pseudoword and were asked to select which shape they felt matched the pseudoword. In the auditory block, participants heard a single pseudoword through calibrated headphones; after the auditory token ended, the shapes remained on screen until a response was made. In the written block, a pseudoword was displayed in text above the shapes, and participants made their selection via a key press. Participants were instructed to rely on their intuitive judgment and to respond as quickly and accurately as possible. They were told that there were no right or wrong answers but that we were interested in which words they thought fit which shapes. Before the experiment proper, participants completed eight practice trials with noncritical filler pseudowords to familiarize themselves with the response procedure.

Responses and reaction times were recorded by the experimental software. Participants completed the two experimental blocks in a single session that lasted approximately 20-30 minutes. After the main task, participants completed brief debriefing and the questionnaire verifying musical background. Two participants were excluded from analysis because of incomplete data due to equipment failure in one block; analyses reported here are based on the remaining 38 participants (19 musicians, 19 nonmusicians). Excluded participants were replaced during data collection to maintain target group sizes, and replacement participants met the same inclusion criteria.

The primary dependent variable was the proportion of choices consistent with the canonical bouba/kiki mapping across trials; that is, a choice was coded as 1 if a rounded vowel pseudoword was matched to the rounded shape or if a front unrounded vowel pseudoword was matched to the spiky shape, and coded as 0 otherwise. Proportions were computed separately for the auditory and written blocks for each participant and then averaged to yield an overall proportion as well. Reaction time measures were analyzed as secondary dependent variables to assess potential speed-accuracy tradeoffs and to provide convergent evidence about the processing demands of the task.

Statistical analyses focused on evaluating whether the proportion of bouba-consistent responses exceeded chance, whether modality influenced matching proportions, and whether musical training modulated these effects. One-sample t-tests against 0.5 were used to evaluate above-chance performance at the group level. A mixed ANOVA with musical training as a between-subjects factor and modality as a within-subjects factor was used to evaluate main effects and interactions. Where appropriate, nonparametric tests (Wilcoxon signed-rank tests) and logistic mixed-effects models treating trials as nested within participants were used to confirm the robustness of results. Effect sizes are reported (Cohen’s d for t-tests and partial eta-squared for ANOVAs) and 95% confidence intervals are provided for key estimates.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the proportion of bouba-consistent responses are summarized below. Across all participants and both modalities combined, the mean proportion of bouba-consistent choices was 0.72 (SD = 0.15), indicating that, on average, participants chose the shape predicted by the bouba/kiki mapping on 72% of trials. This overall rate substantially exceeded the 50% chance level. In the auditory condition specifically, the mean proportion of bouba-consistent choices was 0.70 (SD = 0.16). In the written condition, the mean proportion was 0.74 (SD = 0.14). Thus, descriptive data suggested a robust bouba/kiki effect in both modalities with only a small numerical difference favoring the written presentation.

When participants were grouped by musical training, musicians exhibited a mean bouba-consistent proportion of 0.71 (SD = 0.14) across modalities, whereas nonmusicians exhibited a mean of 0.73 (SD = 0.16). In the auditory condition, musicians demonstrated a mean of 0.69 (SD = 0.15) and nonmusicians demonstrated a mean of 0.71 (SD = 0.17). In the written condition, musicians had a mean of 0.73 (SD = 0.13) and nonmusicians had a mean of 0.75 (SD = 0.15). These group-wise descriptive statistics suggested little difference between musicians and nonmusicians in the strength of the effect.

To test whether the combined group showed above-chance performance, a one-sample t-test compared the overall proportion of bouba-consistent responses to the chance level of 0.5. This test revealed a highly significant deviation from chance, t(37) = 9.04, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.46, 95% CI for the mean difference [0.17, 0.26]. Thus, participants reliably matched pseudowords to shapes according to the bouba/kiki mapping.

Separate one-sample t-tests were conducted for each modality. In the auditory condition, participants’ mean proportion of bouba-consistent choices (M = 0.70) was significantly above chance, t(37) = 7.01, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.14, 95% CI [0.15, 0.25]. In the written condition, the mean proportion (M = 0.74) was also significantly above chance, t(37) = 8.77, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.43, 95% CI [0.18, 0.29]. These results confirmed that both auditory and written presentations elicit the bouba/kiki effect in this sample.

To evaluate the effects of modality and musical training and their interaction, a mixed-design ANOVA was conducted with modality (auditory, written) as a within-subjects factor and musical training (musician, nonmusician) as a between-subjects factor. The main effect of modality did not reach significance, F(1,36) = 0.91, p = .346, partial eta-squared = .025. The main effect of musical training was likewise non-significant, F(1,36) = 0.18, p = .675, partial eta-squared = .005. The interaction between modality and musical training was not significant, F(1,36) = 0.08, p = .781, partial eta-squared = .002. These inferential results indicated that, although the bouba/kiki effect was robust overall, neither presentation modality nor musical training significantly influenced the magnitude of the effect, and there was no evidence of a modality by musical training interaction.

Given the modest sample size and the potential sensitivity of parametric tests to distributional assumptions, confirmatory nonparametric tests were conducted. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests comparing bouba-consistent proportions to chance were significant for both modalities, auditory z = 4.88, p < .001, and written z = 5.01, p < .001. Mann-Whitney U tests comparing musicians and nonmusicians in each modality were non-significant (auditory U = 164.5, p = .62; written U = 166.0, p = .64). These nonparametric results aligned with the ANOVA findings and supported the conclusion that the effect is robust but not meaningfully modulated by musical training or modality.

To further assess robustness, a logistic mixed-effects model was constructed treating each trial’s binary response (bouba-consistent or not) as the dependent variable, modality as a fixed effect, musical training as a fixed effect, and random intercepts for participants and items (pseudowords). The mixed-effects analysis revealed a significant intercept corresponding to above-chance responding (log-odds corresponding to p < .001), whereas modality did not contribute a significant fixed effect (β = 0.11, SE = 0.14, z = 0.79, p = .43) and musical training did not contribute a significant fixed effect (β = -0.05, SE = 0.15, z = -0.33, p = .74). The interaction term was also non-significant. The random effects structure showed reasonable between-participant variability but no anomalous item effects that would call into question the generality of the finding.

Reaction time analyses were conducted as secondary checks. Median reaction times were calculated per participant per modality to reduce the influence of outliers. Median RTs in the auditory condition were 862 ms (SD = 210 ms), and in the written condition were 945 ms (SD = 240 ms). A paired-samples t-test indicated that participants were marginally faster in the auditory condition than in the written condition, t(37) = 2.04, p = .048, Cohen’s d = 0.33. This small difference in speed did not correspond to differences in accuracy or proportion of bouba-consistent responses and is consistent with the idea that hearing a word can sometimes evoke a rapid phonological mapping relative to reading, although effect sizes were small.

Exploratory analyses examined whether years of musical training, treated as a continuous variable, correlated with bouba-consistent responding. Across all participants, years of training correlated weakly and non-significantly with overall bouba-consistent proportion, r = -0.07, p = .66. Within the musician group, years of training correlated non-significantly with bouba-consistent responding, r = 0.10, p = .70. These analyses provided no evidence for a graded effect of musical experience on the bouba/kiki mapping.

An examination of individual participant performance showed that the vast majority of participants exhibited bouba/kiki patterns above chance on at least one modality. Specifically, 32 of 38 participants (84%) showed above-chance bouba-consistent responding in at least one modality, and 27 of 38 participants (71%) showed above-chance responding in both modalities. Only 6 participants showed below-chance responding in both modalities, a number within expectations for individual variability given chance-level fluctuation and the small number of trials per modality.

Finally, an item-level analysis evaluated whether particular pseudowords elicited stronger bouba-consistent responses than others. Proportions of bouba-consistent responses by item ranged from .60 to .85 across the ten pseudowords, with rounded-vowel items generally eliciting slightly higher consistency than unrounded items, although item variability did not systematically differ by modality. The mixed-effects model including random item slopes did not produce improved fit, indicating that the primary patterns were stable across items.

Taken together, the results demonstrate a clear bouba/kiki effect in this sample, with participants reliably mapping rounded vowel pseudowords to rounded shapes and unrounded vowel pseudowords to spiky shapes. The effect was present for both auditory and written presentations and showed no meaningful modulation by formal musical training as operationalized in this study.

Discussion

The present study tested the bouba/kiki effect in an undergraduate sample while manipulating presentation modality and assessing the possible moderating role of musical training. Across both auditory and written conditions, participants reliably matched rounded vowel pseudowords to rounded shapes and front unrounded vowel pseudowords to spiky shapes at a rate significantly greater than chance. The bouba/kiki effect thus replicated robustly in our sample. Contrasting with some theoretical predictions about modality dependence, we found no evidence that auditory presentation produced a stronger effect than written presentation. Contrary to an intuition that heightened auditory skills due to musical training might amplify cross-modal phonetic-visual correspondences, we also found no evidence that participants with formal musical training differed meaningfully from those without such training. These findings have implications for theories of sound symbolism, the cognitive architecture underpinning cross-modal correspondences, and the boundary conditions on how perceptual expertise transfers across domains.

First and foremost, the robust bouba/kiki effect observed across modalities supports the idea that sound-symbolic mapping is a reliable feature of human perception and cognition. The overall mean proportion of bouba-consistent choices was substantially above chance and consistent with many previous reports of adult performance on similar tasks. These data reinforce the view that phonological properties such as vowel quality are systematically linked to visual shape properties in a way that is accessible to adult listeners and readers. The presence of the effect in the written condition is especially noteworthy because it demonstrates that orthographic input is capable of evoking the same mapping, suggesting that activation of phonological representations from print is sufficient to produce the correspondence. This is consistent with models positing rapid access to phonological forms from orthography in literate individuals, and suggests that the bouba/kiki effect is not solely dependent on the fine acoustic details of the speech signal.

The lack of a significant modality effect indicates that the bouba/kiki mapping is fairly robust to the input channel through which word forms are delivered. If the effect depended strictly on acoustic cues such as formant frequencies or prosodic contours, one might have expected auditory presentations to lead to stronger or more consistent mappings. The absence of such an effect instead points toward a mechanism that likely involves phonological or articulatory representations that are accessed both when hearing words and when reading. That is, written pseudowords may swiftly evoke internal phonological representations that carry the same mapping tendencies as heard words. Alternatively, the mapping could arise at a more abstract level where graphemic shapes and phonological structures both map to similar notions of "roundness" or "sharpness" in a conceptual space, yielding consistent judgments across modalities.

The absence of an effect of musical training was perhaps more surprising given evidence that musicians often show enhanced auditory processing abilities. One reasonable expectation was that more finely tuned auditory discrimination might facilitate sensitivity to subtle acoustic cues that underpin sound-shape correspondences. However, the present findings suggest that musical training, at least as operationalized here by grade-level achievement, does not modulate the bouba/kiki effect in a meaningful way. Several interpretations are possible. First, musical training may not influence the specific phonetic features that drive sound-symbolic correspondences; musicians’ auditory expertise often centres on pitch, timing, and timbre distinctions, whereas bouba/kiki mappings may derive primarily from vowel quality and certain articulatory-acoustic contrasts that are not specifically enhanced by typical musical training. Second, the critical mechanism underlying the bouba/kiki mapping could be shared across individuals and reflect cross-modal correspondences that are robust to individual differences in auditory processing; that is, the effect may be part of a general mapping between sensory dimensions that is not easily modulated by domain-specific training.

A third possibility is that our operationalization of musical training, while clear and practically useful, may not have captured the kinds of auditory skills that would influence bouba/kiki sensitivity. Grade-based criteria focus on formal achievement but do not necessarily index detailed perceptual abilities such as categorical perception of vowels or sensitivity to formant patterns. Future studies might incorporate psychoacoustic measures, objective tests of phonetic discrimination, or continuous measures of musicianship that include informal musical engagement and ear training to determine whether specific facets of auditory expertise relate to sound-symbolic sensitivity.

The marginally faster reaction times in the auditory condition deserve brief discussion. Participants responded slightly quicker when hearing a pseudoword than when reading it, which is consistent with the idea that auditory presentation can sometimes lead to rapid phonological activation. However, the speed difference was small and did not translate into differences in the strength of the bouba/kiki mapping. Thus, while modality may affect the speed with which a mapping is reached, it does not necessarily alter the mapping’s qualitative content.

The present study’s mixed-effects analyses and nonparametric confirmations strengthen confidence in the robustness of the primary finding and in the lack of modality and musical training effects. The use of both participant- and item-level models showed that the bouba/kiki effect was not driven by idiosyncrasies of individual pseudowords and that the main pattern was consistent across participants. The individual-level analysis also showed that the majority of participants demonstrated the expected mapping, reducing the likelihood that the group-level effect was an artifact of a few highly biased individuals.

Several limitations of the present study are important to note. First, the participant sample, while sufficient to detect a robust main effect, was relatively small for detecting subtle interactions or small effect size moderators. The power to detect a small interaction between musical training and modality would have been limited; thus, we cannot conclusively rule out the possibility of modest effects that our sample lacked power to detect. Second, the musicianship criterion, while administratively convenient and meaningful, groups together individuals who may differ substantially in the nature of their training (e.g., instrument type, vocal vs instrumental training, extent of ear training). A more fine-grained examination of musical experience, including assessment of pitch discrimination ability, timbral sensitivity, and other auditory perceptual skills, could reveal more nuanced relationships. Third, this study used two-dimensional silhouettes as visual stimuli; several recent theoretical proposals have suggested that three-dimensional cues, texture, shading, or tactile properties might further enrich sound-symbolic mappings, and future work could explore whether three-dimensional shapes modulate the effect.

A related consideration is the cross-cultural and cross-linguistic generality of the bouba/kiki effect. Our sample consisted of literate native speakers of a single language, and while other research has shown bouba/kiki patterns across multiple languages and cultures, it remains valuable to replicate the modality and musical training manipulations in diverse linguistic and cultural contexts. Literacy, in particular, may influence the degree to which orthographic stimuli evoke phonological representations and hence the strength of written-form mapping; such factors could moderate the observed modality invariance.

Future research directions are abundant. One immediate extension would be to manipulate acoustic properties more directly, by synthesizing pseudowords that vary systematically in formant dispersion, spectral centroid, or consonantal sharpness, to determine which acoustic cues most strongly predict rounding or spikiness associations. Another promising approach would be to examine developmental trajectories by testing infants and children using both auditory and written analogues (or picture-book-like orthographic exposure) to determine how modality influences the emergence and consolidation of sound-shape mappings. Neuroimaging and electrophysiological methods could be employed to ask whether auditory and visual presentations of pseudowords recruit common or distinct neural networks during mapping decisions, and whether musicians and nonmusicians differ in such recruitment even when behavioral outcomes are similar.

Investigations could also interrogate the relation between articulatory gestures and visual shape mappings. Some theories posit that articulatory gestures associated with particular vowels produce kinematic patterns that are metaphorically associated with roundedness or pointedness; experiments that manipulate motor involvement, for example by asking participants to silently articulate or to restrain mouth movement, could test the causal contribution of articulatory representations. Additionally, training studies could examine whether intensive auditory training on particular phonetic contrasts influences subsequent sound-symbolic mappings, thereby providing causal evidence regarding the role of auditory expertise.

A further extension would be to examine multimodal interactions beyond the auditory-visual mapping tested here. For example, associations between sound and texture, sound and size, or sound and temperature could be studied in concert with dimensions of roundedness to build a broader typology of cross-modal correspondences. Cross-modal priming paradigms could address whether priming in one modality modulates mapping in another, and thereby shed light on representational frameworks that underpin these correspondences.

In practical terms, understanding the robustness and limits of sound symbolism has implications for fields such as language pedagogy, marketing, and interface design. If certain phonetic forms predispose people to associate terms with particular visual properties, designers and communicators could exploit such mappings to create more intuitive and memorable product names or instructional cues. The current demonstration that written forms suffice to elicit the mapping suggests that such applications need not rely solely on spoken language.

In conclusion, the present study provides clear evidence that the bouba/kiki effect is a robust phenomenon that operates across auditory and written modalities in adult literate participants. The effect appears to be stable across individuals with and without formal musical training, at least when musical training is operationalized as grade-based achievement. These findings support models that posit relatively abstract phonological or cross-modal correspondences as the basis of sound symbolism and suggest that such mappings are broadly accessible to individuals regardless of specialized auditory training. Future work that leverages larger and more diverse samples, refines measurement of auditory expertise, manipulates acoustic and articulatory features, and incorporates neural measures will further illuminate the cognitive and perceptual mechanisms that give rise to sound-symbolic associations.
