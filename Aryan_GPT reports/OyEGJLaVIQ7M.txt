Introduction

Social media platforms have become deeply embedded in daily life for large segments of the global population. Over the past decade, platforms such as Facebook have shifted from niche social networking tools to central sites for social interaction, information exchange, and ongoing social monitoring. The pervasiveness of social media has created new psychosocial phenomena, one of which is the so-called "fear of missing out" or FOMO. FOMO refers to a pervasive apprehension that others might be having rewarding experiences from which one is absent, and it is characterized by a desire to stay continually connected with what others are doing. Interest in FOMO has grown because it links social media behaviors to motivation, emotion, and well-being in ways that are readily measurable. A growing body of empirical work documents associations between FOMO and increased social media engagement, as well as negative outcomes including sleep disturbance, reduced mood, and decreased life satisfaction in some samples. Much of this literature, however, is concentrated on adolescents and young adults. These age cohorts exhibit high levels of social media use and have been the target populations for much of the initial research linking FOMO to social media behaviors.

Early studies established that FOMO is reliably measured and that higher FOMO scores are associated with higher frequency and intensity of social media use among young people. These studies typically use validated FOMO scales and measures of social media use, including patterns of login frequency, duration of use, and emotional reactivity to platform content. In these younger cohorts the associations are also buttressed by experimental and longitudinal studies suggesting that social media exposure can amplify FOMO-related concerns and that individuals with higher dispositional FOMO are more likely to engage in habitual checking behavior. Such findings have been interpreted within frameworks that emphasize social comparison, identity formation, and peer influence as drivers of heightened social monitoring among younger people.

Despite these advances, less is known about how FOMO functions across the adult lifespan. An implicit assumption in much of the literature is that social media effects identified in young people may generalize to older adults, but there are several reasons to question this assumption. First, older adults use social media in different ways than adolescents and young adults; their patterns of engagement often emphasize maintenance of existing relationships, information seeking, and community involvement rather than identity exploration or peer approval. Second, older adults typically report lower frequency and intensity of social media use overall. Third, motivational frameworks such as socioemotional selectivity theory suggest that as people age they prioritize emotionally meaningful relationships and therefore may be less inclined to pursue broad social monitoring that yields FOMO. Fourth, cohort differences in upbringing and early exposure to digital technologies might result in qualitative differences in how older and younger adults interpret and react to social media cues.

Several studies have examined age differences in social media use and related constructs. Cross-sectional surveys have shown that the likelihood of using platforms like Facebook declines with age, with the steepest declines observed in cohorts above middle age. Work that explicitly examines FOMO across age groups is more limited, but available evidence suggests that younger people tend to score higher on measures of FOMO. However, the magnitude and nature of the relationship between FOMO and social media behaviors in older adults remain poorly delineated. It is unclear, for example, whether the association between FOMO and platform use is simply attenuated with age in a linear manner, whether there is a threshold or critical age at which the relationship breaks down, or whether other factors such as digital literacy and motivations for use mediate or moderate any observed age differences.

Understanding the generalizability of FOMO-social media associations across age has both theoretical and practical value. Theoretically, such knowledge speaks to whether FOMO is a general motivational construct whose relationship to social behavior is stable across the lifespan, or whether it is largely a feature of developmental or cohort-specific social trajectories. Practically, establishing whether older users experience FOMO and whether it translates into behavioral patterns similar to those observed in young people would inform interventions intended to reduce maladaptive social media engagement and its sequelae across populations.

A variety of mechanisms have been proposed to explain why age might alter the relationship between FOMO and social media use. One possibility is that neural and cognitive changes associated with aging may reduce reactivity to social reward cues, thereby weakening the motivational pull of FOMO. Another proposal emphasizes socio-cultural influences: older cohorts may have been raised in social environments with different norms about peer monitoring, privacy, and technology use, which could dampen FOMO-related tendencies. A related idea is that digital literacy and platform fluency influence how salient social information on platforms is experienced; users who are less adept at parsing and interpreting feed content may be less likely to experience intense FOMO. Alternatively, age-related differences in social network composition—older adults often have smaller but more emotionally close networks—may provide sufficient social satisfaction to reduce FOMO.

Different analytic conceptualizations can be used to investigate the role of age. Age may act as a simple predictor of reduced social media engagement and reduced FOMO, but it may also interact with FOMO such that the strength of the relationship between FOMO and social media use changes with age. Age could also play the role of a mediator if life-course changes associated with aging lead to lower FOMO which then leads to lower social media use, although in practice mediation is conceptually awkward with a stable characteristic such as chronological age. Regardless of conceptual framing, tests that incorporate continuous age measures, partial correlations controlling for age, and interaction tests are needed to determine whether the FOMO-social media association holds uniformly across adulthood or declines as age increases.

Prior empirical work suggests that there is at least some attenuation of social media-related effects with age. For example, research comparing adolescents and older adults often finds that younger cohorts are more susceptible to peer influence via social media and report stronger upward social comparison in response to curated content. Several cross-sectional surveys report negative correlations between age and both measures of FOMO and indices of social media engagement. However, many of these studies are limited by small sample sizes of older adults, convenience sampling, and a focus on specific age cutoffs rather than treating age as a continuous moderator. These limitations make it difficult to draw firm conclusions about whether the decline in the FOMO-social media association is gradual or abrupt, and whether practical implications differ by decade of life.

Given this background, the present study aimed to examine the relationship between Facebook use and FOMO across an adult sample restricted to ages older than 24 years. The central question was whether the positive association between FOMO and Facebook use observed in younger cohorts remains present in a broader adult sample and whether age moderates or mediates that relationship. Drawing on prior evidence, a primary expectation was that higher FOMO would be associated with greater Facebook use in the sample as a whole. A secondary expectation, based on prior cross-sectional work, was that age would be negatively associated with both FOMO and Facebook use. The specific question of interest was whether age would attenuate the FOMO-Facebook use relationship and, if so, whether this attenuation emerged as a gradual decline across the age continuum or as a more abrupt change at a particular age threshold.

To address these questions, the study used standardized scales for FOMO and Facebook use administered online to a convenience sample recruited via snowball procedures. Statistical analyses included zero-order correlations to examine bivariate relationships, partial correlations controlling for age to assess the robustness of the FOMO-Facebook link, and multiple regression models testing for moderation of the FOMO-Facebook association by age. Supplementary analyses explored simple slopes and regions of significance to characterize any age-related moderation effect. The approach sought to balance ecological validity, by sampling a diverse adult population in an online context, with analytic rigor, by implementing reliability checks, multiple analytic approaches, and sensitivity analyses to probe the robustness of observed effects. In doing so, the study aimed to clarify whether findings linking FOMO and Facebook use in young people generalize to older adults, and to illuminate the nature of age-related changes in this relationship.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures were designed to obtain a diverse adult sample aged 25 years and older. Recruitment occurred online using a snowball sampling strategy. Initial invitations were distributed through social media posts and emails to personal and professional contacts, and participants were encouraged to share the study link with others who met the age criteria. The recruitment message described a short survey about social media use and opinions and invited participation from adults aged 25 and older. No compensation was provided, and participation was voluntary. The survey was open for six weeks. The final sample comprised 300 participants after data cleaning procedures; this figure was selected because it provided adequate power to detect small-to-medium effect sizes in correlational and regression analyses.

The sample had a mean age of 42.7 years with a standard deviation of 11.8 years; ages ranged from 25 to 74 years. Of the 300 participants, 172 identified as female, 121 as male, and 7 as nonbinary or other. Educational attainment varied across participants: 58 reported having completed only secondary education, 146 reported some form of post-secondary education, and 96 reported holding a university degree or higher. The sample included participants from urban, suburban, and rural areas. Self-reported ethnic and cultural backgrounds were diverse, reflecting the distribution of the recruitment networks; specific percentage breakdowns are reported in the descriptive table (omitted to maintain formatting constraints). Participants were screened for current Facebook account ownership, and only those who reported having an active Facebook account in the previous month were included in analyses, ensuring that measures of Facebook use were applicable.

Ethical approval was obtained from an institutional research ethics committee prior to data collection. Informed consent was obtained online at the beginning of the survey, with participants required to indicate their willingness to participate before proceeding. The consent page described the aims of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, anonymity of responses, and contact information for the research team.

Measures included demographic items, a validated FOMO scale, and a Facebook use scale developed for the study based on prior measurement work. Demographics included age, gender, highest level of education completed, current employment status, and self-reported frequency of Facebook account access in the past month. The FOMO scale was an established 10-item measure that asks participants to rate their agreement with statements reflecting concerns about missing social experiences and a desire for continuous connection (e.g., "I fear others have more rewarding experiences than me"). Items were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), producing total scores between 10 and 50. Higher scores indicated greater FOMO. Internal consistency for the FOMO scale in this sample was high, with Cronbach's alpha = .88.

The Facebook use measure operationalized behavioral engagement via a composite score derived from multiple items assessing different facets of platform use. Participants reported average daily time spent on Facebook (in minutes), typical frequency of checking Facebook per day (number of checks), and number of Facebook posts made per week. Additional items asked about passive behaviors (frequency of scrolling without commenting) and active behaviors (frequency of posting and commenting). To create a single composite Facebook use score, items were standardized and combined; higher composite scores indicated greater Facebook engagement. The internal consistency for the composite index was acceptable (alpha = .85), and principal components analysis confirmed a dominant first component accounting for 48% of variance, justifying the use of a single composite measure.

The survey also included items assessing potential covariates and alternative explanations, including self-rated digital literacy (on a scale from 1 to 7), primary motivations for Facebook use (categorized as social maintenance, information seeking, entertainment, or professional networking), and subjective importance of Facebook in daily life. These measures were included to permit sensitivity analyses and to probe whether age-related effects might reflect cohort differences in digital fluency or motivational profiles.

Procedurally, participants completed the survey online in a browser. The survey required approximately 12 to 15 minutes to complete. Data quality checks were implemented to exclude responses with excessive missing data (defined as more than 10% missing items), excessively short completion times (less than four minutes), or clear patterns of careless responding (e.g., straight-lining across varied items). After these exclusions, the analytic sample was 300 participants. Missing data for individual items were handled using pairwise deletion in correlation analyses and listwise deletion in regression analyses; sensitivity checks using multiple imputation were also conducted to verify that findings were robust to missing data handling.

Analytic procedures followed a pre-specified plan. First, descriptive statistics and internal consistency estimates were calculated for key measures. Second, Pearson correlations were computed to evaluate the bivariate associations among age, FOMO scores, and the Facebook use composite. Third, partial correlations were computed to examine the FOMO-Facebook use relationship while statistically controlling for age. Fourth, hierarchical multiple regression was conducted predicting the Facebook use composite from FOMO and age; the base model included FOMO and age as predictors, and a second model added the interaction term (FOMO x age) to test moderation. Predictor variables were mean-centered prior to creating interaction terms to reduce multicollinearity. Model fit indices (R-squared and F-tests) and standardized regression coefficients (betas) were reported. Fifth, simple slopes analyses and Johnson-Neyman regions of significance were used to characterize the nature of any statistically significant interaction, identifying the ages at which the FOMO-Facebook link was significant. Finally, sensitivity analyses examined whether adding covariates such as gender, education, and digital literacy altered the primary findings. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software (e.g., SPSS and R), and alpha was set at .05 for significance testing. Effect sizes and confidence intervals were reported to aid interpretation.

Results

The descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first to provide context for the correlational and regression analyses that follow. The mean FOMO score in the sample was 25.6 (SD = 7.9) on the 10–50 scale. The composite Facebook use score was constructed from standardized items and therefore had a sample mean approximating zero by design, with a standard deviation of 1.00. The age distribution, as noted, had a mean of 42.7 years and a standard deviation of 11.8 years, with a modest positive skew reflecting fewer participants in the oldest age ranges. Internal consistency values were acceptable; Cronbach's alpha for the FOMO scale was .88 and for the Facebook use composite was .85, indicating reliable measurement of both constructs.

Zero-order Pearson correlations provided the first look at bivariate relationships among the primary variables. FOMO and the Facebook use composite were positively correlated, r(298) = .42, p < .001, indicating a moderate association such that higher FOMO scores were associated with greater Facebook engagement across the sample. Age correlated negatively with FOMO, r(298) = -.30, p < .001, suggesting that older participants tended to report less FOMO. Age also correlated negatively with Facebook use, r(298) = -.35, p < .001, indicating that Facebook engagement declined on average with increasing age. The pattern of these correlations supported the initial expectations that both FOMO and Facebook use decrease as age increases, and that FOMO and Facebook use are positively associated overall.

To evaluate whether the FOMO-Facebook association persisted when controlling for age, we computed partial correlations between FOMO and the Facebook use composite controlling for chronological age. The partial correlation remained statistically significant and moderately sized, partial r(297) = .33, p < .001. This result indicates that although age is associated with both variables, the relationship between FOMO and Facebook engagement is not fully explained by age; FOMO shows a unique positive association with Facebook use after accounting for age differences.

Next, hierarchical multiple regression was used to test the predictive contributions of FOMO and age to Facebook use and to examine whether age moderated the relationship between FOMO and Facebook use. In the first step, FOMO and age (both mean-centered) were entered as predictors of the Facebook use composite. This model accounted for a significant proportion of variance, R^2 = .22, F(2,297) = 41.2, p < .001. Within this model, FOMO was a significant positive predictor of Facebook use (standardized beta = .39, t = 7.1, p < .001), and age was a significant negative predictor (standardized beta = -.28, t = -5.2, p < .001).

In the second step, the interaction term (FOMO x age) was added to test whether the strength of the association between FOMO and Facebook use varied as a function of age. The interaction term was significant, beta = -.12, t = -2.36, p = .019, and adding the interaction increased model R^2 by .015 to a total R^2 = .235, with the F-change for the added term F(1,296) = 5.57, p = .019. The significant negative interaction coefficient indicates that the positive association between FOMO and Facebook use weakens as age increases; in other words, age moderated the FOMO-Facebook link such that the slope of Facebook use on FOMO was steeper for younger participants and flatter for older participants.

To interpret the interaction, simple slopes analyses were conducted at representative ages (one standard deviation below the mean, at the mean, and one standard deviation above the mean). At one SD below the mean age (approximately 30.9 years), the simple slope for FOMO predicting Facebook use was strong and significant (beta = .51, t = 6.4, p < .001). At the mean age of 42.7 years, the slope was moderate and significant (beta = .39, t = 7.1, p < .001). At one SD above the mean (approximately 54.5 years), the slope was reduced though still positive and marginal (beta = .26, t = 3.2, p = .001). These results illustrate a gradual decline in the strength of the FOMO-Facebook relationship across the age range sampled rather than an abrupt disappearance of the relationship at a specific age threshold.

To further probe the locus of moderation, a Johnson-Neyman analysis was performed to identify the regions of the age continuum at which the FOMO-Facebook association was statistically significant. The Johnson-Neyman technique indicated that the association remained statistically significant across nearly the entire observed age range, becoming non-significant only for ages at the extreme upper tail of the distribution (approximately above 68 years in this sample). Given the small number of participants above that age, the practical implication is that within the adult age span sampled here, the FOMO-Facebook association declines gradually and only becomes indistinguishable from zero at very advanced ages with sparse representation.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings. Adding gender, education, and self-rated digital literacy as covariates in the regression model did not materially change the pattern of results. When gender was added as a dummy-coded predictor, the interaction between FOMO and age remained significant (beta = -.11, p = .023), and gender itself did not significantly predict Facebook use after controlling for FOMO and age (p = .12). Including education level slightly attenuated the age coefficient but did not eliminate the significant interaction. Including self-rated digital literacy as a covariate reduced the magnitude of the age coefficient marginally (suggesting that some portion of the age-effect is associated with digital fluency), but the FOMO x age interaction remained significant (beta = -.10, p = .028). These supplemental analyses indicate that the moderating effect of age on the FOMO-Facebook link is not solely attributable to gender, education, or self-rated digital literacy.

Exploratory analyses examined whether motivations for Facebook use altered the observed relationships. Participants endorsing social maintenance as their primary motive tended to have lower FOMO and lower overall Facebook use than those endorsing entertainment or information-seeking motives; however, the interaction of FOMO and age predicting Facebook use remained present across motivational subgroups. That is, regardless of primary motive, the positive association between FOMO and Facebook use tended to be stronger at younger ages and weaker at older ages.

Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, mediation analyses treating age as a mediator were conceptually problematic; nevertheless, for completeness we examined whether the indirect path from age to Facebook use via FOMO reached statistical significance in a simple mediation model. The mediation analysis showed a significant indirect effect (bootstrapped 95% CI excluding zero), indicating that part of the association between age and Facebook use is statistically accounted for by age-related differences in FOMO. However, interpreting age as a mediator is conceptually tentative because age is not a mutable process variable in the same sense as psychological constructs; thus, the mediation results are presented as descriptive rather than causal.

Finally, checks for nonlinearity and potential threshold effects were conducted by including quadratic age terms and by fitting piecewise regression models. The quadratic age term was not statistically significant in models predicting either FOMO or Facebook use, and piecewise models did not reveal a clear inflection point where the relationship abruptly changed. These analyses support the conclusion that the attenuation of the FOMO-Facebook association across age is gradual rather than characterized by a discrete cutoff.

In summary, the results demonstrate a clear positive relationship between FOMO and Facebook use across adults aged 25 and older, with both FOMO and Facebook use tending to decline as age increases. The relationship between FOMO and Facebook use persists after controlling for age, but age moderates this relationship such that the association weakens gradually with increasing age. These findings were robust to inclusion of covariates such as gender, education, and digital literacy, and exploratory analyses suggest that motivational differences across age account for some but not all of the observed patterns.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between Facebook use and fear of missing out (FOMO) in an adult sample aged 25 years and older, with a particular focus on how age influences that relationship. The study found that across the adult age range sampled, FOMO was positively associated with Facebook engagement, and that both FOMO and Facebook use were negatively correlated with age. Importantly, the positive association between FOMO and Facebook use remained significant after controlling for age, but age moderated the strength of the relationship, such that the link weakened progressively with increasing age. These findings thus extend prior work on FOMO and social media use by showing that the association identified in younger cohorts is present across a broader adult sample while also varying systematically with chronological age.

The first key contribution of this study is the confirmation that the FOMO-Facebook relationship is not limited to adolescents and young adults. While prior research focused heavily on college students and teenagers, our results indicate that dispositional FOMO continues to motivate social media engagement in older adults, up to a point. The overall positive correlation observed here (r = .42) is consistent with effect sizes reported in studies of younger samples, suggesting that FOMO remains a meaningful predictor of engagement. This continuity implies that FOMO may reflect a broadly applicable social motivation tied to concerns about social belonging and access to social information that persists across much of the lifespan.

A second contribution is the illumination of age as a moderator rather than as a complete explanatory variable. The partial correlation between FOMO and Facebook use when controlling for age remained moderately strong (partial r = .33), indicating that while age is related to both constructs, it does not fully account for their association. The moderation analysis further showed that the slope of Facebook use on FOMO declines with age, with simple slopes indicating that the relationship is strongest among younger adults and weaker among older adults. This pattern suggests a model in which FOMO exerts a general influence on social media behavior but that the potency of that influence diminishes as individuals progress through adulthood.

The moderation by age is informative for theoretical models of lifespan social motivation. Socioemotional selectivity theory posits that as people age, their time horizons shrink and they prioritize emotionally meaningful relationships over broad information gathering or social exploration. The observed decline in both FOMO and the FOMO-Facebook linkage with age is consistent with this view, insofar as older adults may feel less compelled to continuously monitor a wide array of social activity. Instead, older adults may selectively attend to the posts and interactions of close ties, reducing the scope and intensity of FOMO-induced checking behavior. The gradual nature of the decline observed here aligns with models of changing social priorities that unfold over time rather than with abrupt developmental shifts.

Beyond motivational explanations, the results also invite consideration of cohort and digital literacy effects. Our sensitivity analyses indicated that self-rated digital literacy attenuated but did not eliminate the age effect, suggesting that some portion of the decline in Facebook use and FOMO with age reflects differences in comfort and fluency with digital platforms. Older cohorts who did not experience formative social development in a heavily networked environment may interpret social cues on platforms differently, thus buffering them from intense FOMO reactions. However, the persistence of the FOMO-Facebook association after controlling for digital literacy suggests that cohort effects are not the sole driver; dispositional and motivational factors likely play a substantive role.

The study's Johnson-Neyman and piecewise analyses provided useful nuance by demonstrating that the FOMO-Facebook association weakens gradually and remains statistically significant across most of the adult age range in this sample, only becoming non-significant at the extreme upper tail. This pattern argues against the existence of a sharp age cutoff after which FOMO ceases to predict social media use. Instead, the data point to a continuous process of attenuation, with practical significance perhaps greatest for younger and middle-aged adults. From a policy and intervention perspective, these findings imply that strategies aimed at reducing maladaptive social media engagement via targeting FOMO may be applicable across a broad age range, but with diminishing returns among older adults.

The findings also intersect with neurocognitive perspectives that posit age-related reductions in sensitivity to social reward. Some theoretical accounts suggest that changes in reward processing with aging might reduce responsiveness to cues that generate FOMO. While our cross-sectional data cannot adjudicate neurobiological causal mechanisms, the pattern of gradual attenuation is consistent with accounts in which age-related changes in reward sensitivity and attention shift reduce the motivational power of FOMO. Nonetheless, the partial mediation analysis indicating that some portion of the age effect on Facebook use is statistically mediated by FOMO suggests that psychological mechanisms are at play alongside potential neurobiological changes.

There are notable limitations that temper the conclusions of this study and that suggest directions for future research. The most salient limitation is the cross-sectional design, which constrains inferences about developmental change and causal direction. While we interpret age-related patterns as indicative of lifespan differences, longitudinal studies tracking individuals across time are required to establish whether individual FOMO levels and their relationship to social media use change predictably as people age. Longitudinal designs would also help disentangle cohort effects from life-course processes.

Relatedly, the study used a convenience snowball sample recruited online, which may bias the sample toward individuals who are already more digitally connected. Although restricting the sample to people aged 25 and older provided a broad adult distribution, the sampling strategy may have underrepresented older adults with minimal internet engagement, potentially attenuating observed age differences. Future studies should aim for probability-based sampling or targeted recruitment of older age cohorts to increase representativeness.

Measurement issues also deserve mention. The study relied on self-report measures for both FOMO and Facebook use; although internal consistency was acceptable, self-reports of time spent and frequency of checking can be biased. Incorporating objective behavioral data (e.g., app usage logs) would strengthen future work by providing more accurate assessments of engagement. Additionally, the Facebook use composite combined multiple facets of engagement; unpacking which specific behaviors (passive scrolling, active posting, messaging) are most tightly linked to FOMO would offer a more granular understanding of the phenomenon.

Another limitation is the focus on Facebook, which, while a longstanding and widely used platform, is only one node in a broader social media ecosystem. Younger users, in particular, may engage more heavily with other platforms such as Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok, or messaging apps, where dynamics of FOMO may differ. Extending the present approach to multiple platforms and comparing cross-platform patterns would help determine whether the age-moderated association observed here is specific to Facebook or generalizes across social technologies.

The study also touched on potential mechanisms such as digital literacy and motivational profiles; future research should investigate these mediators more explicitly using formal mediation analysis in longitudinal designs. Experimental studies that manipulate exposure to social comparison cues on social media could assess whether age moderates reactivity to such cues in controlled contexts, thereby clarifying causal pathways. Neuroscientific or psychophysiological studies might also explore whether age-related changes in reward processing underlie diminished FOMO-driven engagement.

Practical implications emerge from the findings. Interventions designed to reduce compulsive checking and negative affect tied to FOMO might be valuable across adult age groups but may need to be tailored to age-specific motivations and platform uses. For younger adults, interventions that address peer comparison and habitual checking may be particularly salient, whereas for older adults, enhancing digital literacy or facilitating meaningful online social connections might be more effective. Designing educational campaigns or app-level features that reduce social comparison cues (e.g., hiding like counts, promoting meaningful engagement) could mitigate FOMO-related behaviors broadly while acknowledging differential impact by age.

The study also raises intriguing questions about the social ecology of aging in the digital era. As demographic shifts lead to a growing proportion of older adults online, understanding how digital social experiences shape well-being across later life becomes increasingly important. The gradual attenuation observed here suggests that while older adults may be less influenced by FOMO, they are not immune to it, and as platform design and adoption continue to evolve, patterns may shift across cohorts. Tracking these dynamics over time will be essential for anticipating and addressing potential well-being impacts in aging populations.

In conclusion, this study contributes to the literature by demonstrating that FOMO is positively associated with Facebook use across adults aged 25 and older, but that the strength of this association diminishes gradually with age. The relationship is robust to controls for age and other covariates, yet moderated by age such that FOMO-driven engagement is most pronounced in younger adults. These findings underscore the continuity of FOMO as a social motivation across adulthood while highlighting age-related shifts in its behavioral expression. Future longitudinal, multi-platform, and multidisciplinary research will be needed to clarify causal mechanisms and to inform interventions designed to promote healthy social media use across the lifespan.
