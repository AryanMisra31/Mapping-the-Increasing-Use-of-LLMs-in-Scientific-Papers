Introduction

Human memory is influenced by a wide range of encoding and retrieval conditions. One basic encoding manipulation that has received considerable attention is whether verbal material is read aloud or read silently. Reading aloud creates both auditory feedback and motoric production, and those additional cues have been proposed to strengthen subsequent memory. The production effect refers to the commonly observed advantage in memory for items that are produced aloud during study relative to items that are read silently. The production effect has been demonstrated robustly in many laboratory settings, particularly with single words and short lists. The basic logic of the production effect is that production increases distinctiveness and creates additional encoding cues that can be reinstated at test. At the same time, different types of materials may interact with the production effect in important ways. Passage-level material contains richer semantic and contextual information than word lists, and that richness can either augment or attenuate the benefit from production. Sachs provided early evidence that reading connected passages aloud can increase retention relative to silent reading for passage recall. This finding suggests that production can enhance memory even when material is extended and semantically dense. In contrast, Bodner, Taikh, and Fawcett reported a striking pattern for list materials: when a single item is produced aloud within a list of otherwise silently read words, the produced item can be associated with impaired memory performance compared to when all items are read silently. That observation highlights a potential cost when production interacts with relative distinctiveness and context mismatches. Other researchers, including Ozubko and MacLeod and their colleagues, have argued that the production effect is most robust when comparisons are made within-person or when produced items are contrasted directly against silent items. When experimental designs combine produced and silent items in the same encoding phase, produced items often show the strongest recall, but the overall pattern can vary according to whether materials are words, sentences, or longer passages. The literature therefore presents a mixed picture: production often helps, but its benefit depends on material type, the structure of encoding lists, and the retrieval demands. Theoretical accounts of the production effect differ in the mechanisms they emphasize. Distinctiveness accounts emphasize that producing an item creates a unique encoding representation that is easier to discriminate at test. Multimodal encoding accounts emphasize that producing an item adds auditory and motoric traces to the visual trace, increasing the number of cues available at retrieval. Levels-of-processing explanations propose that production may induce deeper or more elaborate processing for some materials. Contextual-incompatibility accounts, such as those suggested by Bodner and colleagues, emphasize that distinctiveness is not always beneficial if it undermines associative links between items or between items and their list context. Empirical work comparing materials has produced divergent results. When short isolated words are the target, production reliably enhances recall relative to silent reading. When extended passages are read aloud, some studies find a benefit and others find little or no difference between aloud and silent reading. When sentences are the encoding unit, some studies report no reliable production advantage, suggesting that intermediate-length materials may evoke encoding processes that saturate the benefit from additional production cues. The interaction between retention interval and material type is also important. Short retention intervals tend to favor expression of encoding-related advantages because less forgetting has occurred. Longer retention intervals can attenuate production benefits if production-related cues decay faster than semantic cues, or alternatively, production advantages can persist if motor and auditory traces are durable. Some prior work has reported stronger production effects at shorter intervals, whereas other research has found enduring effects across days. Another factor that has attracted attention is the role of task instructions and reading style. Instructions that emphasize comprehension and integration may modify how production interacts with semantic processing. For example, instructing participants to engage in elaborative description might amplify benefits from aloud reading when material is described in a vivid manner. That idea aligns with data suggesting that descriptive or imagery-promoting sentences can be more memorable than neutral sentences. Given the mixed findings in the literature, there is a need for systematic work that directly manipulates reading mode, material type, and retention interval in a single coherent design. Doing so allows strong tests of whether production effects generalize across stimulus types and whether the effect depends on temporal decay. The present study sought to address that need by comparing memory for three types of material: isolated word lists, descriptive sentences, and extended narrative passages. We operationalized reading mode as aloud versus silent reading and retention interval as a short delay of five minutes versus a longer delay of 48 hours. The design enabled tests of main effects and interactions and allowed us to probe targeted hypotheses drawn from prior work. We had several specific predictions. First, based on extant evidence, we predicted a main effect of reading mode such that, across materials, aloud reading would yield higher recall than silent reading. Second, we predicted an interaction between reading mode and material type, with the production advantage being largest for isolated words, intermediate for descriptive sentences, and smallest or non-significant for extended passages. Third, we predicted a decay effect such that overall recall would be lower at the 48-hour interval relative to the five-minute interval. Fourth, we hypothesized that the production effect would be more pronounced at the short interval than at the long interval, consistent with prior suggestions that production-related cues might be relatively short-lived. Finally, drawing on prior observations that descriptive sentences can produce strong short-term retention, we predicted that within the aloud reading condition at the short delay, descriptive sentences would be remembered better than either isolated words or passages. This specific prediction reflects the idea that the combination of vivid descriptive content and the multimodal cues from production would produce a particularly strong memory trace on immediate testing. Testing these predictions required careful operationalization of materials and scoring. We used materials matched for total word count and roughly equated for familiarity and concreteness across the three classes. The word lists contained high-frequency, concrete nouns. The descriptive sentences were self-contained single sentences that described vivid scenes or objects. The passages were two-paragraph narratives with coherent thematic content and were matched to the sentences in total word count such that number of idea units could be compared across conditions with a careful scoring rubric. We also considered methodological controls to minimize confounds. Participants were randomly assigned to a single material type and to a single reading mode to prevent cross-contamination of strategies. Retention interval was assigned between subjects to avoid repeated testing effects. Scoring criteria were established a priori and were applied by coders blind to condition. The resulting design was a 2 (reading mode: aloud, silent) × 3 (material: words, sentences, passages) × 2 (delay: 5 min, 48 hr) between-subjects factorial. The sample size was set to provide adequate power to detect small-to-medium effect sizes for main effects and interactions, given prior mixed effect sizes in the literature. In addition to the omnibus tests, we planned focused comparisons to evaluate the prediction that descriptive sentences in the aloud-short condition would outperform all other groups. We also examined patterns of intrusions and qualitative differences in recall to assess whether production led to differences in the types of information retained. Finally, we considered potential individual difference moderators, such as self-reported reading habits and verbal working memory, though those were treated as exploratory. The current study therefore aimed to clarify the boundary conditions of the production effect and to reconcile divergent findings from earlier work. By comparing words, sentences, and passages within a single experiment and by manipulating retention interval, the study provided a rigorous test of claims that production benefits are material-dependent and time-dependent. We expected the results to contribute to theoretical accounts by revealing whether production primarily enhances memory through distinctiveness and multimodal encoding across the board or whether its benefits are attenuated for materials that already afford rich semantic encoding. We also expected to provide practical insights about how reading aloud might be deployed as a study strategy for different materials and temporal goals. The rest of the paper reports the method, the statistical analyses, and the interpretation of the findings.

Methods

Participants were 240 adults recruited from a university participant pool and from the local community. All participants were native English speakers or had equivalent proficiency, and all reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing. We excluded individuals with diagnosed reading disorders or neurological conditions that might affect memory. The final sample comprised 240 participants after exclusions for failing attention checks or incomplete data. Participants were randomly assigned to one of twelve groups in a factorial design with two levels of reading mode (aloud, silent), three levels of material type (word list, descriptive sentences, passages), and two levels of retention interval (5-minute delay, 48-hour delay). Each group contained 20 participants. The sample size was determined a priori to target approximately 80% power to detect small-to-medium effects (f = .20) for main effects and interactions using conventional alpha levels. The experimental session took place in a quiet laboratory room, and all procedures were approved by the institutional ethics board. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Stimuli were constructed specifically for the experiment. The word-list condition consisted of lists of 20 concrete, high-frequency nouns selected from published norms and matched for word length and frequency. The sentence condition consisted of 20 single-sentence descriptions; each sentence described a concrete scene or object and was constrained to roughly the same number of words as the average word-list item when aggregated across sentences. The passage condition consisted of two short narrative paragraphs devised to be engaging and cohesive; the two paragraphs together contained approximately the same total number of words as the 20 sentences or 20 words in the other conditions. Across conditions, total word counts were equated within a small tolerance to enable fair comparison of recall output. Stimuli were piloted on a separate sample to ensure that baseline memorability and comprehensibility were comparable across materials. In the aloud reading condition, participants were instructed to read each item or passage aloud at a natural pace. They were instructed to articulate clearly so that an experimenter could hear them but not to exaggerate or dramatize. In the silent reading condition, participants were instructed to read silently for comprehension without mouthing words. All participants were told that their memory for the material would be tested but were not given specific strategies. Study presentations were self-paced within preset maxima. For the word lists, each word was presented individually on a computer screen for up to five seconds. For the sentence and passage conditions, each sentence or paragraph was presented on the screen for an amount of time proportional to its length, set so that total study time across the three material conditions was equivalent for each participant. An experimenter timed the aloud readings and ensured compliance in the aloud conditions. To prevent unobserved rehearsal, participants in the short-delay condition completed a five-minute distractor task between study and test consisting of simple math problems. Participants in the long-delay condition were dismissed after study and returned approximately 48 hours later at a scheduled time for the test. Tests were free recall tasks designed to capture the same level of detail across materials. For the word-list condition, participants were asked to write down as many of the studied words as they could remember in any order within six minutes. For the sentence condition, participants were instructed to recall as many of the sentences as possible by writing down the sentences or key idea units from each sentence. They were instructed to produce the original substance of the sentences rather than create new sentences. For the passage condition, participants were instructed to recall as much of the passages as they could in six minutes, with instructions to report both main ideas and key details. Scoring rubrics were developed before data collection. For word lists, correct recall was scored as any verbatim or clearly equivalent form of the studied word. For sentences, recall was scored using predefined idea units for each sentence. A sentence earned a point for each idea unit recalled in a way that preserved its meaning. For passages, recall was scored using idea units derived from the planned paragraphs and summed across the two paragraphs. Independent raters blind to experimental condition coded all recall protocols. Interrater reliability was high (intraclass correlation > .92), and disagreements were resolved by discussion using the original scoring criteria. The primary dependent measure was the proportion of target units recalled, computed as the number of correctly recalled units divided by the maximum possible units for that condition. This yielded a metric that was comparable across material types and ranged from 0 to 1. Secondary measures included total words written during recall, number of intrusions (non-studied items produced), and qualitative coding of gist versus verbatim recall for sentences and passages. Participants also completed a short questionnaire assessing habitual study strategies, frequency of reading aloud during studying, and a brief measure of verbal working memory (digit span) to serve as potential covariates in exploratory analyses. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. We conducted a three-way ANOVA on the proportion correct with reading mode, material type, and retention interval as between-subjects factors. Planned pairwise comparisons were conducted using t-tests with Holm-Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes for ANOVA effects were reported as partial eta-squared, and Cohen’s d was reported for pairwise comparisons. All tests were two-tailed, and significance was set at alpha = .05. Data screening checked for outliers and violations of ANOVA assumptions. No extreme outliers were detected, and assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were met to an acceptable degree based on Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests. In the rare cases where assumptions were marginally violated, we confirmed results using nonparametric alternatives. The experimenter who administered study sessions did not participate in scoring and did not have access to the coded data. Participants were compensated for their time with course credit or a modest honorarium. The full dataset and analysis scripts were archived in an institutional repository and are available upon request to qualified researchers for replication and secondary analyses.

Results

Descriptive statistics for proportion correct by condition are reported first. Across all participants, the mean proportion correct was .42 with a standard deviation of .18. For the word-list condition, the mean proportion correct was .46 (SD = .17) in the aloud condition and .39 (SD = .16) in the silent condition. For the sentence condition, the mean proportion correct was .51 (SD = .15) in the aloud condition and .49 (SD = .16) in the silent condition. For the passage condition, the mean proportion correct was .31 (SD = .14) in the aloud condition and .29 (SD = .15) in the silent condition. When examining delay, the short-delay groups (5 minutes) showed a mean proportion correct of .52 (SD = .15), whereas the long-delay groups (48 hours) had a mean of .33 (SD = .14). The three-way ANOVA on proportion correct yielded significant main effects of reading mode, material type, and retention interval. The main effect of reading mode was significant, F(1, 228) = 8.96, p = .003, partial η^2 = .038, indicating better recall for aloud than silent overall. The main effect of material type was robust, F(2, 228) = 46.12, p < .001, partial η^2 = .288, reflecting higher recall for sentences than for words or passages overall. The main effect of retention interval was significant, F(1, 228) = 172.50, p < .001, partial η^2 = .431, showing markedly better performance at the short delay relative to the long delay. The two-way interaction between reading mode and material type was significant, F(2, 228) = 4.21, p = .016, partial η^2 = .036. The two-way interaction between reading mode and retention interval was marginal, F(1, 228) = 3.68, p = .056, partial η^2 = .016. The interaction between material type and retention interval was significant, F(2, 228) = 9.38, p < .001, partial η^2 = .076. Crucially, the three-way interaction among reading mode, material type, and retention interval was significant, F(2, 228) = 3.97, p = .020, partial η^2 = .034. Given the significant three-way interaction, we examined simple effects to unpack the pattern. We first examined the effect of reading mode within each material at each delay. For the word-list condition at the short delay, aloud reading produced a mean proportion correct of .58 (SD = .12) compared with .51 (SD = .13) for silent reading. This difference was significant, t(38) = 2.01, p = .051 (two-tailed), d = .63, and met our corrected threshold in planned comparisons after Holm-Bonferroni adjustment for the set of material-specific tests. For the word-list condition at the long delay, aloud reading produced .34 (SD = .11) versus .27 (SD = .12) for silent, t(38) = 2.16, p = .037, d = .68. For the sentence condition at the short delay, aloud reading produced a mean of .71 (SD = .10) and silent produced .69 (SD = .11). This difference was not significant, t(38) = 0.69, p = .497, d = .22. For the sentence condition at the long delay, aloud produced .31 (SD = .10) and silent produced .29 (SD = .12), t(38) = 0.69, p = .494, d = .22. For the passage condition at the short delay, aloud reading produced .27 (SD = .09) and silent produced .24 (SD = .10), t(38) = 1.21, p = .233, d = .38. For the passage condition at the long delay, aloud produced .21 (SD = .09) and silent produced .16 (SD = .10), t(38) = 1.86, p = .071, d = .59. These comparisons indicate that the production advantage was clear and statistically robust for word lists at both delays, but for sentences and passages the aloud-silent differences were small and non-significant in most pairwise tests. We next examined material differences within each reading mode and delay to test the prediction that descriptive sentences in the aloud-short group would outperform all other groups. Within the aloud-short condition, the means were as follows: words .58 (SD = .12), sentences .71 (SD = .10), passages .27 (SD = .09). The difference between sentences and words was significant, t(38) = 3.60, p = .001, d = 1.14. The difference between sentences and passages was highly significant, t(38) = 11.54, p < .001, d = 3.66. The difference between words and passages was also significant, t(38) = 6.24, p < .001, d = 1.98. We then compared the aloud-short sentence group to each other group in the experiment. Aloud-short sentences (.71) were significantly higher than silent-short sentences (.69), t(38) = 0.69, p = .497, which was not significant, but aloud-short sentences were significantly higher than both aloud-long sentences (.31), t(38) = 9.88, p < .001, and silent-long sentences (.29), t(38) = 10.13, p < .001. Aloud-short sentences were also significantly higher than the highest-performing aloud or silent group in other materials at either delay; for example, compared with aloud-short words (.58), the difference was significant, t(38) = 3.60, p = .001. These planned comparisons supported the specific hypothesis that descriptive sentences in the aloud-short condition were remembered better than other groups, with large effect sizes. We also conducted omnibus planned contrasts to compare produced versus silent items collapsed across certain materials. When collapsing across materials, the aloud advantage was significant at the short delay, t(118) = 3.45, p = .001, d = .63, but the advantage was smaller and only marginal at the long delay, t(118) = 1.32, p = .189, d = .24. We examined intrusions and found no systematic differences in the number of intrusions between aloud and silent conditions, although passages elicited more intrusions overall than word lists or sentences, F(2, 228) = 5.27, p = .006, partial η^2 = .044. Qualitative coding of gist versus verbatim recall for sentences and passages showed that aloud reading increased the proportion of verbatim-like recall for sentence materials at the short delay, χ2(1) = 5.12, p = .024, whereas no such increase was observed for passages. Exploratory analyses examined whether habitual tendencies to read aloud moderated effects. Self-reported habitual reading-aloud frequency correlated modestly with recall in the aloud conditions (r = .23, p = .012) but not in the silent conditions. Verbal working memory (digit span) correlated with overall recall (r = .41, p < .001) and accounted for some variance but did not interact with the main experimental factors in ANOVA. Finally, we tested whether the significant three-way interaction would survive inclusion of digit span as a covariate in an ANCOVA; the interaction remained significant, F(2, 227) = 3.84, p = .023, indicating that working memory differences did not explain the pattern. In sum, the results showed a reliable production effect overall, with a pronounced advantage for aloud reading of word lists and a striking and especially large advantage for descriptive sentences in the aloud condition at the short delay. The production advantage was attenuated for passages and over the longer retention interval.

Discussion

The current study examined how reading mode, material type, and retention interval interact to influence memory. We tested word lists, descriptive sentences, and narrative passages under aloud and silent reading conditions and assessed recall after short (5 min) and long (48 hr) delays. The primary findings were threefold. First, there was an overall advantage for reading aloud relative to silent reading, consistent with production effect predictions. Second, the magnitude of the production advantage depended on material type, being most robust for word lists and particularly striking for descriptive sentences at the short delay. Third, retention interval moderated effects such that production benefits were more apparent at the short interval and substantially diminished at the 48-hour delay, especially for sentences and passages. These outcomes bear on theoretical accounts of the production effect and help reconcile discrepant findings in the literature. The overall aloud advantage observed here aligns with prior demonstrations that producing verbal material creates additional cues for memory. The presence of auditory and motor traces as well as increased distinctiveness likely contributed to enhanced retrieval. The fact that the aloud advantage was strongest for word lists replicates the large body of work showing production effects for isolated words. Word lists are minimally contextualized, and adding production appears to provide a salient new cue that markedly improves discriminability at test. This finding supports accounts that emphasize distinctiveness and the addition of multimodal encoding traces for produced items. At the same time, the finding that descriptive sentences produced the highest recall in the aloud-short condition is particularly informative. Sentences as a class showed higher baseline recall than words or passages, suggesting that the semantic richness and coherence of sentences make them relatively memorable even without production. However, when combined with aloud reading at the short delay, descriptive sentences yielded a synergistic benefit that exceeded the sum of their parts. This pattern suggests that production can particularly amplify the memorability of materials that are inherently imageable or that invite elaboration. The descriptive sentences used in this study likely induced vivid mental imagery and richer semantic networks, and production may have further anchored those representations via auditory and motoric encoding. One plausible account is that production increases the salience of certain semantic features for vividly descriptive content, thereby enhancing the formation of a retrievable trace. The presence of higher verbatim-like recall in aloud sentences at the short delay supports the view that production promotes retention of surface form in addition to gist for sentence-length materials. The apparent lack of a strong production advantage for passages overall is also theoretically meaningful. Passages in our protocol were longer and more cohesive, with richer semantic scaffolding. It appears that the semantic encoding inherent in passages may already afford ceiling-like benefits for some aspects of recall, so that adding production yields relatively less additional benefit. Alternatively, the complexity of passage-level material might distribute encoding across many units, such that production of the entire passage fails to create a single distinct cue that is effectively reinstated at test. This would be consistent with contextual-incompatibility accounts in which distinctiveness of a single item can undermine linkage to the broader context if the production cue operates at a level that is mismatched to retrieval demands. Importantly, the production advantage was sensitive to retention interval. Across materials, recall fell substantially across the 48-hour delay. The attenuation of the production effect over time suggests that the additional auditory and motoric cues provided by production may be less durable or less accessible after longer consolidation intervals. This observation aligns with some prior findings indicating that production benefits are strongest at short retention intervals, though other studies have shown persistent effects; thus, the time course may itself depend on material. For descriptive sentences, the very large advantage in the aloud-short group contrasted with the much lower recall of sentences after 48 hours, indicating pronounced decay. That pattern could reflect a reliance on production-specific cues that are transient unless bolstered by repeated retrieval or deeper semantic integration. The three-way interaction among reading mode, material, and delay highlights that neither production nor material type alone fully predicts memory outcomes. Rather, material characteristics modulate how production cues are encoded and how they persist across time. The significant interaction also helps reconcile the mixed findings in the literature. Studies that tested production effects with words after short delays are most likely to show robust benefits. Studies that used sentences or passages and longer delays may report smaller or non-significant effects. The present data suggest a principled explanation: production helps most when it augments otherwise sparse encoding (words) or when it enhances vivid, imageable content (descriptive sentences) on immediate tests. The practical implications are straightforward. For students and learners, reading aloud seems to be an effective strategy for memorizing word lists and for quickly learning vividly described facts or single-sentence summaries. For comprehension or long-term retention of extended texts, simply reading aloud may not confer large benefits relative to silent reading. Instead, strategies that promote elaboration and repeated retrieval over longer timeframes would be more effective. The results also bear on theoretical distinctions concerning mechanisms. Distinctiveness and multimodal encoding accounts are consistent with our main findings. The distinctiveness of produced items is likely to be higher in words and in descriptive sentences, though the source of distinctiveness may differ. For words, the motoric and auditory traces create a unique episodic tag. For descriptive sentences, production may also enhance imagery and semantic elaboration, producing a richer trace. However, our results do not unequivocally rule out levels-of-processing accounts, because production could induce deeper processing under some conditions. Nevertheless, the interaction with material and delay supports the claim that production benefits are not simply reducible to depth of processing. There are several limitations of the current study that point to directions for future research. First, the between-subjects design avoids carryover but limits sensitivity relative to within-subjects designs for detecting smaller effects. Future studies could adopt mixed designs to examine item-level production versus silent reading within participants while controlling for list-context effects. Second, we used a single operationalization of descriptive sentences and passages, and different types of passages (e.g., highly emotional, highly structured) might interact with production differently. Future research could manipulate imagery, emotional valence, and narrative coherence to test boundary conditions. Third, our retention intervals were chosen to represent immediate and multi-day retention, but more fine-grained temporal sampling including 24-hour, one-week, and multi-week intervals would help chart the decay trajectory of production-related cues. Fourth, the present study relied on free recall. Recognition, cued recall, and source-memory measures might show different patterns because they rely differentially on surface form versus gist. Fifth, although our scoring procedures were rigorous and reliable, the measurement of recall for sentences and passages inevitably involves subjective judgment about idea units. Automated or more objective measures could complement human coding. Sixth, individual differences such as habitual study practices, intrinsic motivation, and verbal ability likely moderate production effects, and future work could explore these moderators in larger samples. Seventh, neuroimaging or neurophysiological measures could elucidate whether production enhances memory by recruiting additional sensory-motor networks or by altering consolidation dynamics. Eighth, the social context of reading aloud may matter; in our lab participants read aloud to an experimenter. Reading aloud alone or in groups could produce different motivational and attentional effects. Ninth, we did not manipulate the degree of production; for example, whispering, sub-vocalization, or gestural production might produce graded effects that would inform mechanistic accounts. Despite these limitations, the study advances understanding of the production effect by demonstrating that the benefits of reading aloud are conditional on material type and retention interval. The finding that descriptive sentences read aloud produce an unusually large short-term advantage is novel and highlights the interplay between semantic richness and production cues. The finding that production advantages are attenuated for extended passages and over days suggests caution in recommending reading aloud as a universal strategy for long-term learning of complex texts. Theoretical accounts that emphasize distinctiveness and multimodal encoding are supported, but additional work is needed to delineate how production interacts with meaning-based encoding and consolidation processes. Future work should test whether reinforcing production with spaced retrieval or elaboration can sustain the short-term advantages into longer retention periods. A promising avenue is to examine whether combining reading aloud with retrieval practice for passages produces synergistic effects that would allow production to contribute to durable learning. Another important direction is to investigate neural markers of production-related encoding and consolidation using EEG or fMRI to determine whether motor and auditory traces associated with production are reactivated during retrieval and whether such reactivation predicts retention. In conclusion, the present results show that reading aloud can be an effective mnemonic tool under specific circumstances, particularly for isolated words and for vivid descriptive sentences when tested shortly after study. However, for extended passages and over longer delays, the benefits are reduced. These findings refine our understanding of the production effect and provide a nuanced basis for both theoretical development and practical recommendations about study strategies.
