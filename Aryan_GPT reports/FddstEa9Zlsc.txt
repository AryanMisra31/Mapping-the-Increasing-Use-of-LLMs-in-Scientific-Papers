Introduction

Humans are storytellers by nature and have long used narrative media to share experiences and convey social information. Engagement with narrative forms, whether written, visual, or auditory, has been proposed to cultivate social and emotional capacities by exposing consumers to diverse perspectives and affective situations. One line of inquiry has examined the relationship between reading fiction and empathy, with several studies reporting positive associations between the extent of fiction reading and higher scores on various empathy measures. The proposed mechanisms for this relationship include increased practice in perspective taking, greater exposure to mental states, and narrative transportation that temporarily places the reader in the position of a protagonist. These mechanisms suggest that narrative engagement could strengthen empathic skills through repeated cognitive and affective simulation of others’ experiences. However, the bulk of the existing literature has focused on textual fiction, and comparatively little is known about whether similar associations hold for other narrative modalities such as film and television. Film and television are visual and auditory narrative forms that can produce immersive experiences and portray social information in ways that differ from text. They provide dynamic cues such as facial expression, tone of voice, and music, and these cues may either amplify empathic responses or, in some instances, reduce the cognitive demand on viewers to infer mental states. In principle, visual fiction could have effects on empathy that are similar, stronger, or qualitatively different from those of reading fiction, depending on the processes engaged by the medium.

The theoretical frameworks that link narrative exposure to empathy draw on multiple strands of psychological theory. Theory of mind research emphasizes the role of representing others’ beliefs and intentions for social cognition. Perspective-taking theories emphasize deliberate cognitive shifts into another person’s vantage point. Affective empathy theories emphasize the emotional resonance that arises in response to others’ affective states. Narrative exposure may engage all of these processes to varying degrees. For example, literary fiction has been shown in some experimental studies to improve performance on measures of theory of mind immediately after exposure, suggesting that content which foregrounds internal states may temporarily enhance cognitive empathy. Other work indicates that longitudinal patterns of fiction consumption may correlate with trait empathy measures, which are construed as relatively stable dispositional characteristics. These different findings complicate a simple causal story and point to the need for nuanced investigation of both modality and temporal dynamics.

Despite the conceptual plausibility of an association between fictional viewing and empathy, empirical investigations into audiovisual media have produced mixed results. Some studies suggest that movie viewing, particularly of character-driven films that encourage identification with protagonists, can elicit empathic responses and increase recognition of emotional states in others. Other studies have highlighted that the passive nature of some screen-based engagement, especially with fast-paced or sensational content, may not foster the same depth of social-cognitive processing as literary fiction. Television, as a heterogeneous medium encompassing serialized drama, comedy, documentary, and reality formats, presents further complexity because different genres may differentially influence empathic outcomes. For example, long-form serialized drama that allows for character development over time could foster sustained perspective-taking, whereas short-form or montage-driven content could emphasize spectacle over internalization.

Methodological issues also complicate conclusions about the relationship between fictional media exposure and empathy. Many studies rely on self-report measures of both media consumption and empathic disposition, which raises concerns about shared method variance and measurement bias. Experimental designs often use brief exposures to specific stimuli and then assess short-term changes, which speaks to state-level effects rather than enduring trait-level shifts. Correlational designs can document associations across individuals but cannot resolve questions of causality or directionality, such as whether empathic people choose more fiction-oriented media or whether fiction consumption promotes empathy. Additionally, demographic variables such as age, gender, socioeconomic status, and education may moderate or confound observed associations, and not all studies adequately control for these factors. The variety of empathy measures used across studies, from performance-based tasks to multidimensional self-report scales, further complicates cross-study comparisons.

Gender differences in empathy are one robust empirical regularity reported in the literature, with many studies finding that females score higher on average on both cognitive and affective empathy measures. Several explanations for this pattern have been proposed, including socialization processes that encourage expressive emotional behavior among females, biological factors, and differences in media consumption preferences that could influence empathy measures indirectly. If females are more likely to engage with certain types of narrative content that promote perspective taking, this could contribute to observed gender differences. Alternatively, gender differences may persist independent of media habits, reflecting broader dispositional or socialized tendencies. Therefore, any study exploring media-empathy links should consider gender both as an independent predictor and as a potential moderator of media effects.

The Basic Empathy Scale (BES) is one of several self-report instruments developed to assess trait empathy and is commonly used in research contexts due to its bifurcated assessment of cognitive and affective components. The BES asks respondents to rate their agreement with statements that reflect understanding of and resonance with others’ emotions. While self-report inventories are practical for large-sample studies, they inevitably capture an individual’s self-concept regarding empathic tendencies rather than direct behavioral indicators. This tension between measurement practicality and construct validity is particularly relevant for studies that examine the correlates of empathy across diverse behavioral domains such as media consumption.

Given these theoretical, empirical, and methodological considerations, there remains a meaningful gap in understanding the relationship between audiovisual fictional media consumption and trait empathy. The current study was designed to address this gap by examining the association between the number of hours of fictional film and television viewing over a two-week period (a fortnight) and scores on the Basic Empathy Scale. A central motivation for focusing on fortnightly viewing time was to capture a short-term snapshot of habitual viewing behavior that is less susceptible to recall bias than long-term retrospective estimates. The decision to aggregate viewing time across fictional films and television programs was made to examine narrative exposure in audiovisual modalities as a class, while acknowledging that genre-level differences might be an important focus for future research.

The study adopted a correlational design because the primary aim was to explore natural variability in media exposure and its relation to empathy rather than to test causal effects via experimental manipulation. Correlational analyses are appropriate for identifying associations that may subsequently motivate experimental or longitudinal investigation. In addition to the primary correlation analysis, the study examined gender differences in empathy scores to contextualize any media-empathy associations within known demographic patterns. If a significant association between fictional viewing and empathy had been observed, secondary analyses would have considered whether that association differed by gender. Because prior literature suggested a likely positive association between narrative exposure and empathy based on findings from reading studies, the study hypothesized a positive relationship between hours of fictional audiovisual viewing and BES scores. A secondary hypothesis was that females would score higher on the BES than males, consistent with extensive prior findings.

Several practical and theoretical contingencies were borne in mind when designing the study. One practical challenge was reliance on self-reported viewing hours, which can be noisy and imprecise; however, questionnaires that anchor prospective estimates to specific and recent time windows, such as a fortnight, can reduce error relative to more distant recall. A theoretical consideration was that the mechanisms that operate for reading fiction might not translate wholesale to audiovisual fiction. For example, if reading requires more active mental simulation, the act of reading might differentially train cognitive empathy compared to the more externally provided social cues in film and television. Conversely, audiovisual media might provide richer affective cues that enhance emotional resonance. The present study focused on aggregate viewing time and trait empathy, which limits the ability to resolve these mechanistic distinctions but offers an initial test of whether quantity of fictional viewing is associated with dispositional empathy.

Another design consideration was the demographic composition of the sample. The study was conducted using a convenience sample drawn primarily from a university population and the surrounding community, which yields participants who are relatively young and educated. While such a sample enables efficient data collection and can be informative about adult patterns of media consumption and empathy within this demographic, it constrains the generalizability of the findings. Recognizing this limitation, the study included basic demographic questions to allow for descriptive contextualization and exploratory control analyses.

In sum, the present study sought to extend the literature on narrative media and empathy by examining whether self-reported hours of fictional film and television viewing over a fortnight were positively associated with scores on the Basic Empathy Scale, and whether empathy scores differed by gender. The study used a correlational design with self-report measures of media consumption and empathy, and it aimed to provide an initial empirical test of whether audiovisual fiction shows patterns similar to those documented for reading fiction. Given prior work showing positive associations for reading, the primary expectation was a positive correlation between viewing hours and empathy, and the secondary expectation was a replication of the commonly reported gender difference with females scoring higher on the BES.

Method

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from community advertisements in a mid-sized metropolitan area. Two hundred adult volunteers participated in the study. The sample comprised 100 self-identified females and 100 self-identified males. Participant ages ranged from 18 to 55 years, with a mean age of 24.7 years and a standard deviation of 6.2 years. All participants reported sufficient fluency in English to complete the questionnaires. There were no inclusion criteria based on media consumption habits, and participants who reported zero hours of fictional viewing in the preceding fortnight were retained in the sample. The study received approval from the university research ethics committee prior to data collection, and all participants provided informed consent before beginning the questionnaires. Participants were not compensated monetarily but were entered into a prize draw for participation credit or a small voucher, consistent with local ethics guidelines.

The Basic Empathy Scale (BES) was used to assess trait empathy. The BES consists of items that tap into cognitive empathy (the capacity to understand others' emotions) and affective empathy (the capacity to resonate with others' emotions). For the present study, the total BES score was used as the primary outcome variable, with secondary analyses considering the cognitive and affective subscales separately. Items were presented with a Likert response format ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), and higher scores indicate higher self-reported empathy. Scale scores were calculated by summing item responses and converting to a standardized scale for descriptive reporting. Internal consistency of the BES in the current sample was assessed using Cronbach's alpha.

Media consumption was assessed using a brief questionnaire developed for the study that asked participants to estimate the number of hours they spent watching fictional films and television programmes combined over the preceding fortnight. The questionnaire provided definitions and examples of fictional content, including feature films, episodic dramas, sitcoms, animated fiction, and other narrative-based television, to help participants distinguish fictional viewing from documentary or news viewing. Participants were instructed to provide an estimate of total hours across the two-week period and were asked to include both live viewing and recorded or streamed content. To reduce the influence of outliers and misreporting, participants were given the option to indicate if they were unsure of their estimate, but otherwise numerical responses were requested. Responses were recorded in hours and were treated as a continuous variable for analysis. The media questionnaire also collected basic information about preferred genres and the proportion of viewing that was film versus television, although these data were used only for descriptive purposes and exploratory analyses.

The study was administered online using a secure survey platform to allow participants to complete the questionnaires remotely. The survey began with demographic questions followed by the media consumption questionnaire and then the BES items. The order of the BES items was randomized by the survey platform to reduce response set effects. Completion time averaged approximately 12 minutes. Data collection occurred over a period of six weeks. The online administration was chosen for its convenience and to facilitate a larger sample size than would have been feasible in a single laboratory session.

Data screening procedures were implemented prior to primary analyses. Responses were inspected for missing data, implausible values, and duplicate entries. Participants who completed less than 90% of the BES items were excluded from analyses. No participant was excluded for excessive missingness in the final dataset. Media consumption estimates were inspected for extreme outliers; values greater than three standard deviations above the mean were examined and retained if they represented plausible viewing habits, but sensitivity analyses excluding these extreme values were also conducted to assess robustness. Normality of the BES scores and media hours was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis statistics and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Because hours of viewing can be positively skewed, both parametric and nonparametric correlation measures were planned.

The primary analysis examined the bivariate correlation between hours of fictional audiovisual viewing in the preceding fortnight and total BES scores. Pearson's correlation coefficient was used as the primary index given its interpretability for continuous variables, but Spearman's rank correlation was also computed as a robustness check. The alpha level for statistical significance was set at .05 for primary analyses. Secondary analyses compared BES scores between males and females using independent-samples t-tests with equal variances assumed unless Levene's test indicated otherwise. Effect sizes were computed as Cohen's d for group comparisons and as r for correlation coefficients, with 95% confidence intervals reported where appropriate. Additional exploratory analyses included separate correlations between viewing hours and the cognitive and affective subscales of the BES, an examination of genre preferences as moderators, and a simple linear regression predicting BES scores from viewing hours and gender.

All statistical analyses were conducted using the statistical software package SPSS version 25. Data and syntax used for the analyses are archived on a secure server and are available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author. The study adhered to open-science practices insofar as the analytic plan was pre-registered before data collection concluded, and deviations from the pre-registered plan are reported below in the Results section. Power considerations for the correlational analysis were guided by conventional recommendations; with a sample size of 200, the study had adequate power (.80) to detect small-to-moderate correlation coefficients around r = .20 or larger at an alpha of .05.

Results

Data screening indicated that the dataset was complete for the vast majority of participants. All 200 participants provided usable BES data with fewer than 10% missing responses per person. Missing item-level responses on the BES were infrequent and were handled using mean imputation of the participant's valid responses for that subscale when necessary. Inspection of media consumption estimates revealed a range from 0 hours to 76 hours in the two-week period. The mean number of hours of fictional viewing reported was 13.2 hours, with a standard deviation of 9.1 hours. Median viewing time was 11 hours, and the distribution showed a modest positive skew, with a skewness statistic of 1.02. Three participants reported viewing hours greater than three standard deviations above the mean (greater than approximately 40 hours); sensitivity analyses were planned to examine the influence of these outliers.

Total BES scores ranged from 22 to 88 in the sample, with a mean of 43.0 and a standard deviation of 8.6. The cognitive empathy subscale had a mean of 21.8 (SD = 4.7) and the affective subscale had a mean of 21.2 (SD = 5.1). Cronbach's alpha for the BES total score in the present sample was .88, indicating high internal consistency. The cognitive and affective subscales had alphas of .81 and .83 respectively. Visual inspection of the BES score distribution indicated approximate normality, with a skewness statistic of -0.21 and kurtosis close to zero.

Given the skew in the media hours variable, both Pearson's and Spearman's correlations were computed as planned. The Pearson correlation between hours of fictional viewing in a fortnight and total BES score was r = .09. This correlation corresponded to a p-value of .18, which did not meet the pre-specified alpha of .05 for statistical significance. The 95% confidence interval for the Pearson correlation ranged from approximately -0.04 to 0.22, indicating that the data were compatible with small negative to small positive associations. The Spearman rank-order correlation yielded a similar pattern, with rho = .08 and p = .21, providing convergent evidence for a weak non-significant association between viewing hours and BES scores.

To assess the robustness of the correlation to extreme values, sensitivity analyses were conducted excluding the three participants with viewing hours greater than 40. With these outliers removed (n = 197), the Pearson correlation between hours of viewing and BES scores was r = .10, p = .15, which remained non-significant. Thus, the presence of extreme high-viewing cases did not materially influence the conclusion of no statistically significant association. Additional exploratory analyses examined log-transformed viewing hours to reduce skew; the correlation between log-transformed hours and BES was r = .11, p = .12, again non-significant.

Because gender is commonly associated with empathy, group comparisons were carried out. Females in the sample had a mean BES total score of 45.0 (SD = 8.0), whereas males had a mean of 41.0 (SD = 9.0). An independent-samples t-test comparing BES scores by gender indicated a statistically significant difference, t(198) = 3.33, p = .001. Levene's test for equality of variances was not significant (p = .34), supporting the equal variances assumption for the t-test. The observed difference corresponded to a Cohen's d of 0.47, which is a medium-sized effect according to conventional benchmarks. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference ranged from approximately 1.6 to 6.5 points on the BES.

When cognitive and affective subscales were examined separately, females scored higher on both components. For cognitive empathy, females had a mean of 22.7 (SD = 4.3) and males had a mean of 21.0 (SD = 4.9). The t-test for cognitive empathy by gender was t(198) = 2.86, p = .005, with Cohen's d = 0.36. For affective empathy, females had a mean of 22.3 (SD = 4.8) versus males' mean of 20.2 (SD = 5.2), with t(198) = 3.75, p < .001, and Cohen's d = 0.53. These results suggest that the gender difference in total BES was driven by differences in both cognitive and affective components, with a somewhat larger effect for affective empathy.

To examine whether the weak media-empathy association differed by gender, correlations were computed separately for males and females. For females, the Pearson correlation between viewing hours and BES total was r = .07, p = .50. For males, the Pearson correlation was r = .11, p = .28. Neither subgroup correlation approached statistical significance, and confidence intervals overlapped considerably, indicating no evidence of a gender-specific association in these data. An interaction term between gender and viewing hours was entered into a simple linear regression predicting BES scores to formally test for moderation. The regression model included viewing hours, gender (coded 0 = male, 1 = female), and the interaction term. The interaction coefficient was small and non-significant (beta = .04, p = .62), providing no support for a differential effect of viewing hours across genders.

Given the potential confounding effect of age, exploratory partial correlations controlling for age were calculated. The partial correlation between hours of viewing and BES total controlling for age was r_partial = .08, p = .21, which remained non-significant. Similarly, when education level and student status were entered as covariates in a multiple regression predicting BES scores from viewing hours, the coefficient for viewing hours was not significant (beta = .09, p = .16), and the overall model explained a modest proportion of variance in BES scores (adjusted R^2 = .08), largely attributable to gender.

Exploratory analyses considered genre preference as a potential moderator. Participants reported the percentage of their fictional viewing that was primarily film versus television and their preferred primary genre (drama, comedy, action, sci-fi/fantasy, or other). Correlations between viewing hours and BES within genre subgroups were underpowered and yielded no significant associations. The largest subgroup correlation observed was among participants who indicated a primary preference for drama, where r = .15, p = .10, suggesting a trend-level positive association that did not reach significance. Given the multiple comparisons and limited subgroup sizes, these exploratory findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Finally, the pre-registered analysis plan included an examination of whether the proportion of viewing that was television versus film influenced the association with empathy. A regression including viewing hours, proportion of television viewing, and their interaction did not reveal significant effects for the proportion variable or the interaction term (p-values > .30). Thus, in the present sample, aggregate hours of fictional audiovisual viewing over a fortnight showed a weak, non-significant positive association with trait empathy, whereas gender differences in empathy were significant and medium in size.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between self-reported hours of fictional film and television viewing over a fortnight and trait empathy as measured by the Basic Empathy Scale in a sample of 200 adults. The primary finding was that the number of hours participants reported spending watching fictional audiovisual media was weakly positively correlated with BES scores, but this association did not reach statistical significance. Sensitivity analyses, including nonparametric correlations, log transformations of viewing hours, and exclusion of extreme viewing values, yielded similar null results. By contrast, the study replicated a robust gender difference, with females scoring significantly higher on the BES total score and on both cognitive and affective subscales. The gender difference was of medium effect size and was not accounted for by self-reported viewing hours, age, or education in exploratory analyses.

These results contribute to the broader literature on narrative exposure and empathy in several ways. First, the absence of a significant association between aggregate audiovisual fictional viewing and trait empathy suggests that quantity of screen-based fiction alone may not be a strong determinant of dispositional empathy. This finding contrasts with some prior work documenting positive correlations between reading fiction and empathy, and it raises the possibility that the medium of narrative delivery matters. There are several plausible reasons why reading and viewing may differ in their relation to empathy. Reading typically requires active mental simulation based on linguistic cues and the reader's imagination, which may specifically exercise cognitive perspective-taking abilities. Film and television, by presenting visual and auditory cues directly, may scaffold interpretation such that viewers do not engage the same depth of inferential mentalizing. Alternatively, audiovisual media might give rise to immediate affective responses without the sustained reflective processing that contributes to trait-level cognitive empathy.

Second, the study's null finding for aggregate viewing hours highlights the need to consider qualitative aspects of media consumption beyond mere quantity. Narrative content varies widely in its emphasis on characterization, psychological complexity, and opportunities for perspective taking. Long-form serial dramas that develop characters over multiple episodes may foster identification and reflective engagement in ways that a two-hour action film may not. The exploratory trend observed among participants who favored drama suggests that genre-specific effects are possible and that future research should examine genre and narrative structure as moderators of media-empathy associations. The present study's focus on total viewing hours likely diluted such specific effects, making detection of medium- or genre-specific influences more difficult.

Third, the gender differences observed in BES scores align with a substantial body of research indicating higher average empathy among females. The present findings indicate that this gender gap is present in both cognitive and affective components of empathy and is not simply an artifact of differential media consumption. The persistence of the gender effect after controlling for basic demographic covariates supports the interpretation that gender-related socialization and dispositional factors contribute to observed differences in empathy. Nevertheless, the moderate effect size observed here suggests considerable overlap between males and females, underscoring the importance of avoiding overgeneralization.

The correlational design of the present study places limits on causal interpretation. The absence of a significant association between viewing hours and empathy does not conclusively rule out the possibility that certain types of audiovisual storytelling can influence empathic capacities under specific conditions. Experimental studies that manipulate exposure to different narrative forms and track short-term changes on state empathy measures have produced mixed evidence, and longitudinal studies examining changes in trait empathy over time as a function of habitual media engagement are scarce. It remains possible that specific stimuli or engagement patterns exert causal effects that are not captured by a cross-sectional aggregate-hours measure. For example, intense, repeated identification with complex characters over months or years of serialized viewing might produce gradual changes in perspective-taking habits that would not be apparent in a two-week snapshot.

Measurement issues are an important caveat in interpreting the findings. The BES is a well-validated self-report instrument, but self-report measures capture participants' perceptions of their empathic tendencies rather than direct behavioral demonstrations of empathy. Self-reports are subject to social desirability and introspective limitations. People might overestimate or underestimate their empathic responsiveness, and media consumption estimates can be imprecise. Although anchoring the media question to a recent two-week period likely mitigated some recall bias, self-reported hours remain an imperfect proxy for actual exposure. Passive exposure versus active, engaged viewing may differ in their psychological impact, and brief survey measures cannot easily distinguish these dimensions.

The sample composition also influences the generalizability of results. The convenience sample of predominantly young adults affiliated with a university limits extrapolation to older populations or to individuals with different educational and cultural backgrounds. Media consumption patterns vary substantially by age cohort, with younger adults often engaging in different platforms and content genres than older adults. Therefore, the absence of an association in the present sample might not hold in other demographic groups. Future studies employing stratified sampling or population-representative designs could provide a more comprehensive picture of the media-empathy relationship across the lifespan.

Another limitation concerns the aggregation of films and television into a single fictional viewing metric. While this choice was justified by the study's aim to examine audiovisual narrative exposure as a class, it necessarily obscures potential differences between formats. Films often provide a self-contained narrative experience, whereas television series allow for extended character arcs and sustained exposure. Such differences could be theoretically meaningful for empathy development. Future research should disaggregate film and television viewing and consider metrics such as episodic engagement, character identification, and narrative complexity.

The exploratory analyses touched on genre preferences but were underpowered to detect moderated effects robustly. The observed trend-level association among drama-preferring participants suggests that certain content types may be more conducive to empathic engagement. More detailed content coding, perhaps using participant-reported favorite shows and films paired with independent assessments of character complexity and moral focalization, could illuminate which narrative features are most strongly associated with empathy. Additionally, research using experience sampling or passive sensing to capture real-time engagement and affective responses during viewing could overcome limitations of retrospective self-report.

Despite the null result for viewing hours, the present study contributes to theory by clarifying boundary conditions for the narrative-empathy link. The findings suggest that simply consuming more fictional audiovisual media does not necessarily translate into higher dispositional empathy. This refinement helps temper broad claims that media exposure per se is empathically beneficial and points to the importance of content, engagement depth, and perhaps active reflection post-exposure as key mechanisms. If narrative effects on empathy depend on mental activity such as perspective taking, then interventions intended to harness media for empathy-building might need to promote reflective engagement, guided discussion, or pedagogical framing rather than relying on passive exposure.

Practical implications follow for educators and policymakers interested in media as a tool for social-emotional learning. Selecting high-quality narrative content that foregrounds internal states and moral dilemmas, and combining viewing with structured reflection or discussion, may be a more promising route to fostering empathy than simply increasing screen time. Media literacy programs that encourage critical engagement and perspective-taking might amplify the potential empathic benefits of narrative exposure. For media producers, these results suggest that works that encourage viewer identification and prolonged contemplation of characters’ inner lives could have social value beyond entertainment.

Several avenues for future research emerge from this study. Longitudinal designs that track changes in empathy over extended periods in relation to persistent viewing patterns would help clarify directionality and cumulative effects. Experimental work that contrasts different narrative modalities and manipulates engagement (for example, instructing participants to adopt a perspective-taking stance while viewing) could test mechanistic hypotheses directly. Use of behavioral and performance-based empathy measures, such as empathic accuracy tasks or physiological measures of resonance, would complement self-report assessments and provide convergent validity. Finally, research incorporating neuroimaging or psychophysiological methods could elucidate whether audiovisual and textual narratives differentially engage brain networks associated with mentalizing and affective processing.

In conclusion, this study found no significant association between the number of hours of fictional film and television viewing over a fortnight and trait empathy as measured by the Basic Empathy Scale, while confirming a medium-sized gender difference favoring higher empathy scores among females. The results suggest that the relationship between narrative media and empathy is more nuanced than a simple quantity-based effect and highlight the importance of considering modality, content, engagement, and measurement approach in future research. Understanding how different forms of storytelling contribute to social cognition remains a fertile domain for interdisciplinary inquiry, with implications for education, media production, and theories of social development.
