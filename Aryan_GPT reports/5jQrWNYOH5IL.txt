Introduction

Empathy is a multifaceted construct that plays a central role in social functioning and interpersonal relations. Empathy is widely considered to encompass both affective components, which involve sharing or resonating with the emotional states of others, and cognitive components, which concern the capacity to understand another person’s perspective. Empathy contributes to prosocial behavior, conflict resolution, clinical outcomes in therapeutic contexts, and the maintenance of social bonds. Because of its importance, researchers have sought to understand the psychological and contextual factors that modulate empathic response. One such factor is the modality through which social information is presented, and modality may shape both the acquisition of information and the emotional response that follows. The current study examined whether the modality of a short stimulus, presented either as a video clip or as a textual transcription of the same clip, affects self-reported empathy in a sample of university students. Understanding modality effects is relevant for educational settings, media design, and interventions that seek to cultivate empathy.

The distinction between visual and textual presentation is central to cognitive and educational psychology, and a substantial literature has compared the relative merits of visual and textual modalities for learning and comprehension. In educational contexts, visual materials are often thought to confer advantages for information processing by capitalizing on perceptual affordances, facilitating encoding through dual channels, and supporting retrieval by providing vivid, memorable cues. Models of multimedia learning have emphasized how combining words and pictures can enhance understanding compared to words alone, and many applied guidelines encourage the use of images or video to support instruction. In parallel, cognitive psychology has shown that visual stimuli can be more engaging, can capture attention more readily, and can increase memory for concrete details.

Despite clear findings regarding information processing and learning, the relationship between modality and emotional response is less straightforward. Emotional reactions to stimuli are not solely a function of how much information can be encoded; they also depend on the interpretive framing, imaginative involvement, and individual differences in reflective processes that the stimulus engenders. Textual narratives, for example, often invite elaborative processing that requires readers to construct mental imagery and to infer intent, which may foster perspective-taking. Visual narratives, in contrast, provide explicit affective cues such as facial expressions, vocal tone, and nonverbal gestures that can powerfully shape immediate affective resonance. Thus, different modalities may emphasize distinct routes to empathic engagement: text may encourage cognitive perspective-taking through imagination and inference, whereas video may promote affective contagion through perceptual immediacy.

Several strands of prior work bear on modality effects for empathy, even if they do not directly compare video versus transcription in the specific way employed here. Research comparing narrative formats indicates that first-person narratives and structured accounts can increase empathic concern, and reading fiction has been linked to improvements in theory of mind. Likewise, interventions that ask participants to write about another person’s experience can enhance empathic perspective-taking. Conversely, studies examining media portrayals suggest that vivid visual imagery, such as documentary footage depicting human suffering, can elicit strong emotional responses and galvanize feelings of compassion and moral outrage. These findings underscore that both text and visual media can, under different conditions, provoke empathy.

There is also a literature on the cognitive and affective processes that mediate empathy. Perspective-taking is often described as a controlled, cognitive process that benefits from deliberate reflection and mental simulation, whereas empathic concern may arise more automatically in response to salient emotional cues. The presence of explicit facial expressions, vocal affect, and human movement in a video can promote rapid affective resonance. Conversely, textual presentations may require the reader to supply missing perceptual cues mentally, potentially engaging cognitive processes that support perspective-taking and elaboration. Given these distinct mechanisms, modality could differentially influence the balance of cognitive and affective empathy elicited by a stimulus.

Another relevant domain concerns the role of ambiguity and inferential demands in empathy elicitation. Textual transcripts of social interaction are often less explicit about affective cues than video, and this relative ambiguity may stimulate readers to engage in greater mentalizing efforts to infer inner states. This greater inferential engagement could enhance cognitive empathy and even amplify empathic concern for some individuals. In contrast, video stimuli reduce inferential demands by making emotions visually manifest, which can either produce strong affective responses or, in some cases, attenuate deeper reflective processing because the viewer receives a ready-made emotional script.

Methodological factors in prior research also complicate predictions. Many studies comparing media types differ in content, duration, and narrative perspective, and such differences confound modality per se with other stimulus attributes. In addition, researchers often measure different outcomes, ranging from immediate physiological responses to long-term attitude change, which may not align. Consequently, while there is a robust literature suggesting that visual media can enhance attention and memory for factual details, there is no simple consensus about which modality better promotes self-reported empathy immediately after exposure.

There are plausible theoretical reasons to expect either modality to lead to higher empathy. One can argue that video should produce greater empathy because of its perceptual richness and immediacy, consistent with models of emotional contagion. The visual and auditory features of video can evoke rapid, embodied responses that translate into higher self-reported empathy on a measure taken immediately after viewing. On the other hand, text might foster deeper cognitive engagement by forcing readers to mentally simulate and infer affective states, a process that could boost scores on self-report measures that tap both cognitive and affective dimensions of empathy.

Given the contrasting possibilities and the lack of direct empirical comparisons using the same content presented either as a video or as its transcript, the present study was designed to evaluate whether a simple manipulation of modality would influence overall self-reported empathy. The study used a well-established self-report instrument, the Interpersonal Reactivity Index, to provide an omnibus assessment of empathic disposition immediately following exposure to the assigned stimulus. Employing a within-population sample of university students allowed for controlled exposure conditions and for the measurement of potential moderating variables such as gender.

We recruited participants from a university setting because young adults are a population of interest for interventions intended to cultivate empathy, and because many educational and media experiences for this group are delivered via either video or text. The study used a convenience sample balanced across gender, and participants were randomly assigned to view either a short video clip depicting an emotionally evocative interpersonal scenario or to read a verbatim textual transcript of the same clip. The procedure controlled for exposure time and for environmental factors to the extent feasible in a laboratory-like setting.

The primary hypothesis was directional given theoretical ambiguity: one plausible hypothesis was that the video condition would elicit higher empathy due to visual immediacy, while an alternative hypothesis was that the textual transcription would result in higher empathy due to deeper inferential processing. Because the literature left open both possibilities, the analysis plan included tests of mean differences and of potential interactions with gender. Secondary exploratory analyses examined subcomponents of the empathy measure and assessed the role of individual demographic covariates.

Beyond simple hypothesis testing, the study aimed to contribute to theoretical understanding by clarifying whether modality exerts a coherent influence on self-reported empathy and by considering mechanistic explanations for any observed effects. If the video elicited greater empathy, the result would align with a view of empathic response as primarily driven by perceptual and affective contagion. If the text elicited greater empathy, the implication would be that cognitive elaboration and mental simulation play a central role in generating empathic self-reports, at least in the immediate aftermath of exposure. Given the practical implications for educational and media design, resolving this question has applied relevance for how empathy training and prosocial communication might best be delivered.

The present study thus sought to provide an empirical contribution by directly comparing visual and textual presentation of identical content and by assessing immediate self-reported empathy. The design prioritized internal validity through random assignment and standardized procedures, and it included checks for possible confounds such as unequal gender distribution or differential exposure time. The anticipated analyses would include tests of group means, assumption checks, and effect size estimates, and would consider whether gender moderated any effects. By doing so, the study aimed to offer a clear test of the modality-empathy relationship and to open avenues for future research into mediating processes.

Methods

Participants were 60 undergraduate students recruited from a single university via convenience sampling. The sample comprised 34 male and 26 female participants. All participants were aged between 18 and 25 years, and the majority were in their first two years of study. Recruitment occurred through departmental subject pools and campus advertisements, and participants received course credit or a small monetary token for participation. Prior to participation, all individuals provided written informed consent and were informed that the study would involve viewing or reading a short human-interest narrative and completing measures concerning feelings and reactions. The study protocol received approval from the university’s institutional ethics committee.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two experimental conditions using a simple randomization procedure implemented at the time of scheduling. The two conditions were labeled visual and textual. The visual condition involved viewing a short video clip approximately three minutes in length that depicted a human interaction designed to evoke empathic concern. The clip included expressive facial cues, conversational audio, and contextual background that together conveyed a clear emotional narrative. The textual condition involved reading a verbatim transcription of the same clip; the transcript contained the dialogue, descriptions of nonverbal cues in brackets, and the same sequence of events as in the video. To control for exposure duration, participants in the textual condition were given a fixed amount of time that matched the length of the video to read the transcript.

The stimuli were created specifically for the study to ensure equivalence across modalities in content and wording. The video was professionally produced to present a neutral, realistic interpersonal scenario involving a person recounting a mildly distressing experience and receiving support from a friend. Care was taken to avoid extreme graphic content so as not to evoke extreme distress. The transcript contained exactly the same verbal content, and bracketed descriptions of nonverbal behavior were included to mirror nonverbal cues. Thus, the only intentional difference between conditions was the presentation medium: moving images with sound versus written text.

After exposure to the assigned stimulus, participants completed a paper-and-pencil questionnaire packet in a quiet testing room. The primary outcome measure was the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI), which was administered in its standard format. The IRI is a multi-dimensional self-report scale designed to capture both cognitive and affective aspects of empathic responding. The instrument was scored to yield an overall composite empathy score by summing across subscales. Although the IRI also produces separate subscale scores (perspective taking, empathic concern, personal distress, and fantasy), the current study focused primarily on the composite score as an index of general empathy immediately after stimulus exposure.

In addition to the IRI, demographic information including age, gender, and academic year was collected. Participants were also asked to rate on a single-item question how engaging they found the stimulus on a five-point Likert scale to serve as a manipulation check. After completing the measures, participants were debriefed and provided with information about the study’s aims and contact details for counseling resources should they feel any residual emotional effects.

The study used an independent-groups design with two levels of a single factor (stimulus modality: visual vs textual). Random assignment was intended to distribute participant characteristics evenly across conditions. In practice, randomization produced equal group sizes of 30 in each condition, and the gender distribution was balanced across the two conditions with 17 males and 13 females in each group. The data analysis plan was pre-specified to include descriptive statistics, checks for normality and homogeneity of variance, an independent-samples t-test comparing composite IRI scores across conditions, effect size estimation (Cohen’s d), and supplementary analyses including a two-way ANOVA to test for main effects and interactions with gender. Where assumption violations were detected, appropriate nonparametric tests would be employed.

Data screening was performed prior to hypothesis testing. All questionnaires were checked for completeness, and no participant had missing IRI items beyond allowable thresholds. Responses were scored according to standard procedures, and composite scores were calculated. Distributional properties were examined using skewness and kurtosis statistics and by visual inspection of histograms; no extreme departures from normality were observed. Levene’s test was used to assess equality of variances between groups. The primary hypothesis test was two-tailed given the theoretical ambiguity, and the significance threshold was set at alpha = 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software.

Ethical considerations included ensuring that participants could withdraw at any point without penalty and that any emotional discomfort would be addressed. The stimuli were designed to be evocative but not traumatizing, and protocol mandated that the researcher remain available during debriefing to discuss any concerns. Data were anonymized and stored securely in accordance with institutional data protection policies. The study followed conventional ethical guidelines for human-subjects research in psychology.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the composite IRI scores were calculated for each experimental condition. In the textual condition, the mean composite IRI score was 72.5 with a standard deviation of 9.2, based on 30 participants. In the visual condition, the mean composite IRI score was 66.0 with a standard deviation of 10.1, based on 30 participants. These descriptive statistics indicated that the textual condition elicited a higher mean self-reported empathy score than the visual condition. A histogram of scores within each condition suggested approximately normal distributions without obvious outliers. Skewness and kurtosis values for both groups fell within acceptable ranges for parametric testing.

Levene’s test for equality of variances was conducted to assess the homogeneity assumption required for the independent-samples t-test. Levene’s statistic yielded F = 0.85 with an associated p-value of 0.36, indicating that the variances of composite IRI scores across conditions did not differ significantly. Given the acceptable homogeneity of variance and approximately normal distributions, an independent-samples t-test comparing the mean IRI scores of the two groups was performed.

The t-test revealed a significant difference between conditions. The test statistic was t(58) = 2.61, and the two-tailed p-value was 0.011, indicating that participants in the textual condition reported significantly greater overall empathy than those in the visual condition. The observed mean difference was 6.5 points on the composite IRI scale. To quantify the magnitude of the effect, Cohen’s d was calculated using the pooled standard deviation, yielding d = 0.67, which corresponds to a medium-to-large effect size.

To explore whether gender influenced the observed modality effect, a two-way analysis of variance was conducted with modality (visual vs textual) and gender (male vs female) as between-subjects factors, using the composite IRI score as the dependent variable. The ANOVA results indicated a significant main effect of modality, F(1,56) = 6.81, p = 0.011, consistent with the t-test findings. The main effect of gender was non-significant, F(1,56) = 0.12, p = 0.73, indicating that males and females in this sample did not differ significantly on the composite IRI score. The interaction between modality and gender was non-significant, F(1,56) = 0.05, p = 0.82, suggesting that the modality effect did not differ across genders.

Mean empathy scores were also examined within gender subgroups to provide descriptive context. Among males, the mean IRI score in the textual condition was 71.8 (SD = 9.4) and in the visual condition was 66.0 (SD = 10.0). Among females, the mean IRI score in the textual condition was 73.5 (SD = 8.9) and in the visual condition was 66.0 (SD = 10.2). These subgroup means mirrored the overall pattern in which textual exposure was associated with higher reported empathy. However, differences within gender subgroups did not reach statistical significance when analyzed separately, likely due to reduced statistical power in the smaller gender-stratified samples.

As an additional manipulation check, participants’ self-reported engagement ratings were analyzed. The mean engagement rating in the textual condition was 3.4 on a five-point scale (SD = 0.8), while the mean engagement rating in the visual condition was 3.7 (SD = 0.7). A t-test comparing engagement ratings showed a small, non-significant difference favoring the visual condition, t(58) = -1.64, p = 0.106. This result suggests that while participants considered the video somewhat more engaging, this subjective engagement did not translate into higher empathy scores in the video condition.

To examine whether baseline demographic variables correlated with composite IRI scores, Pearson correlations were calculated for age and academic year, although the sample variability on those variables was limited. Neither age nor academic year showed significant associations with composite empathy scores (both p > 0.20). This suggests that the observed modality effect was unlikely to be driven by these demographic covariates in this sample.

Robustness checks were performed in the form of a nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test comparing composite IRI scores across conditions. The Mann-Whitney test yielded U = 293.5, p = 0.012, supporting the conclusion from parametric testing that textual presentation was associated with higher self-reported empathy. Thus, findings were consistent across parametric and nonparametric approaches.

Given the multi-dimensional nature of the IRI, exploratory analyses examined subscale scores to assess whether particular components drove the modality effect. The empathic concern and perspective-taking subscales were of primary interest. The textual condition produced higher means on the perspective-taking subscale (mean difference approximately 1.8 points) and showed a trend toward higher empathic concern (mean difference approximately 1.2 points). Statistical tests indicated that the modality effect was stronger and statistically significant for perspective-taking, t(58) = 2.34, p = 0.023, whereas the empathic concern difference was not significant at the 0.05 level, t(58) = 1.62, p = 0.110. These exploratory findings suggest that the advantage for textual presentation may be particularly evident in cognitive components of empathy.

Taken together, the results indicate that reading a textual transcription of an emotionally evocative clip led to significantly higher composite empathy scores immediately after exposure than viewing the same content as a video. This effect was robust to tests of variance equality, nonparametric analysis, and was not moderated by gender in this sample. The effect size ranged in the medium-to-large range, and exploratory analyses pointed to a stronger modality effect on perspective-taking than on affective empathic concern.

Discussion

The present study evaluated whether modality of presentation—visual video versus textual transcription—affects immediate self-reported empathy in a sample of university students. The principal finding was that participants who read a textual transcription of an emotionally evocative clip reported significantly higher overall empathy on the Interpersonal Reactivity Index than participants who watched the same content as a video. This effect was moderate in size, was robust across parametric and nonparametric tests, and was not moderated by gender in our sample. Exploratory analyses suggested that the modality effect was particularly pronounced for cognitive aspects of empathy, notably perspective-taking.

These findings challenge a straightforward assumption that richer perceptual cues offered by video will necessarily elicit greater empathic response as measured immediately after exposure. Although visual media often enhance attention and memory for concrete details, our results indicate that when the outcome of interest is self-reported empathy, textual presentation can be more effective at eliciting higher scores. One plausible interpretation is that reading a transcript requires active mental simulation and inferential processing, processes that are directly aligned with the cognitive mechanisms that underpin perspective-taking. In contrast, watching a video provides ready-made affective and perceptual information, which may lead to a more passive reception of emotion and less engagement in cognitive perspective-taking.

The observation that perspective-taking was more sensitive to modality aligns with theoretical accounts that separate cognitive and affective empathy. The textual format may place greater demands on participants to construct mental imagery, infer motives, and generate internal narratives about the characters, which in turn fosters cognitive empathy. By contrast, video presentation may disproportionately activate affective resonance via perception of facial expressions and vocal tone. Because the IRI composite score includes both cognitive and affective components, but the textual condition yielded a stronger boost to perspective-taking, the net effect favored the textual modality in the composite outcome.

It is important to note that the present effect emerged despite somewhat higher subjective engagement ratings for the video. Participants reported that the video was marginally more engaging, albeit not significantly so, yet they nonetheless reported lower empathy overall. This pattern suggests that engagement as typically conceptualized may not be isomorphic with empathic processing. A stimulus can be engaging in terms of attention-grabbing features without necessarily prompting the mental work required for perspective-taking. Thus, engagement and empathy, while related, are distinct constructs that can be differentially affected by modality.

These results also have implications for applied contexts such as educational programming, advocacy campaigns, and therapeutic training. For educators and practitioners aiming to cultivate empathic understanding, the choice of medium should be guided by the specific empathic processes one wishes to target. If the goal is to foster cognitive perspective-taking and deliberate understanding of others’ mental states, text-based or guided reflective tasks may be particularly effective. Conversely, if the aim is to elicit immediate affective concern, carefully curated video content might still be advantageous. Importantly, the present data do not argue that one medium is universally superior; rather, they suggest that modality should be matched to the desired empathic outcome.

The study contributes to theory by highlighting that the mechanisms supporting learning and those supporting empathy may differ. While prior work has emphasized the superiority of visual materials for information processing and memory, those advantages do not necessarily translate into heightened empathic responses as measured by self-report. This divergence underscores that affective and cognitive processes interact with modality in complex ways, and that sensory richness alone is not sufficient to guarantee greater empathic engagement.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample consisted of university students recruited via convenience sampling, which limits the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Young adults in a university environment may have particular media habits or literacy skills that influence how they process video and text. Second, the study measured empathy immediately after exposure; it is unclear whether modality effects would persist over time or translate into behavioral measures of helping or prosocial action. Future studies should examine longitudinal effects and assess whether the modality-driven differences in self-report predict real-world empathic behavior.

Third, although we took care to equate verbal content across modalities, the video and transcript necessarily differed in sensory richness and pacing. The bracketed nonverbal descriptions in the transcript attempted to preserve nonverbal information, but they may not fully replicate the experiential qualities of video. Additionally, the fixed exposure time for the textual condition may have affected reading strategies; some participants may have rushed, whereas others may have read more slowly, introducing variability in processing styles. Future work could allow self-paced reading or include comprehension checks to ensure uniform processing.

Fourth, we relied on the IRI as the primary outcome measure. The IRI is a validated multi-dimensional instrument, but it is self-report in nature and therefore susceptible to introspective limits and response biases. Incorporating behavioral or physiological measures of empathic response, such as prosocial choice tasks, facial electromyography, or autonomic measures, would provide converging evidence about how modality influences empathic processes. Multi-method approaches would help disambiguate whether textual presentation primarily enhances cognitive empathy while visual presentation elicits affective resonance.

Fifth, the content of the stimulus was deliberately moderate in emotional intensity to avoid distress, but this choice may have moderated the magnitude or direction of modality effects. More intense or more ambiguous stimuli might show different patterns; for example, in cases of extreme visual affect, video might surpass text in eliciting empathy. Conversely, highly ambiguous textual narratives could magnify the inferential advantages of text. Thus, stimulus characteristics such as intensity, ambiguity, and perspective (first-person versus third-person) are likely important moderators.

Sixth, although the randomization resulted in balanced gender distribution and the modality effect did not interact with gender, the sample size may have limited the ability to detect smaller interaction effects. Future research with larger and more diverse samples would provide greater power to examine moderators such as gender, trait empathy, media literacy, and baseline perspective-taking ability.

Seventh, the study did not manipulate or measure participants’ habitual media preferences or reading fluency, both of which might influence how effectively individuals derive empathy from different modalities. People who are avid readers might derive greater empathic benefit from text, whereas habitual video consumers might be more responsive to visual stimuli. Including measures of media usage and literacy would allow testing of these potential moderators.

Eighth, the lack of prior direct comparisons of video versus exact transcriptions in the empathy literature means that the present findings should be taken as an initial step rather than a definitive conclusion. Replication studies that vary content, duration, cultural context, and outcome measures will be essential for mapping the boundary conditions of modality effects on empathy.

Despite these limitations, the study raises several interesting theoretical and practical questions for future research. One avenue is to explore the cognitive processes that mediate the modality effect, for example by measuring spontaneous mental imagery, frequency of inference-making, or depth of processing during exposure. Experience-sampling or think-aloud methods could be used to capture the moment-to-moment cognitive operations that accompany exposure to text versus video.

Another promising direction is to investigate combinations of modalities and guided tasks. For example, pairing video with reflective prompts or asking viewers to write about the perspective of a protagonist after watching could combine the advantages of perceptual immediacy with the cognitive benefits of mental simulation. Likewise, designing textual materials that encourage vivid imagery through descriptive language or embedding multimedia cues could harness both routes to empathy.

Further research could also examine individual differences in susceptibility to modality effects. Trait measures such as baseline perspective-taking, fantasy proneness, and emotional reactivity may moderate how people respond to video versus text. Tailoring media to individual profiles could optimize empathy interventions for different audiences.

Cross-cultural work is another important area, as media conventions and narrative styles vary across cultures, and cultural norms influence how people express and interpret emotion. It would be valuable to determine whether the observed textual advantage for eliciting empathy generalizes across cultural contexts or whether different cultures show different modality preferences.

Lastly, applied studies could evaluate how modality choices affect real-world outcomes, such as donation behavior after exposure to charitable appeals, willingness to engage in volunteer activities, or effectiveness of training programs designed to improve clinician empathy. Translational work would bridge the gap between laboratory findings and practical applications in education, healthcare, and public communications.

In summary, the present study found that reading a textual transcription of an emotionally evocative interaction produced higher immediate self-reported empathy than viewing the same content as a video, particularly in the domain of cognitive perspective-taking. These results suggest that the processes that enhance learning and memory via visual presentation are not identical to those that promote empathic responding, and that textual formats may uniquely engage mental simulation and inferential processes that underpin cognitive empathy. Future research should aim to replicate and extend these findings across diverse populations, modalities, and outcome measures to better understand how media format shapes empathic engagement and to inform the design of interventions intended to foster empathy.
