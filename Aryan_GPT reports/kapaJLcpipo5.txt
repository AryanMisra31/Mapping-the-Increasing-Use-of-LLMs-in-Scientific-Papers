Introduction

Human beings constantly interact with faces, extracting information about identity, intention, and emotional state in social environments. Faces are among the most salient visual stimuli in our environment and support rapid judgments that guide approach and avoidance behaviors. One well-studied phenomenon in visual cognition and affective science is the so-called face-in-the-crowd effect, in which observers search for a target facial expression among distractor faces. This paradigm has been used to probe how emotional information in faces influences attentional selection and visual search efficiency. Within this literature, a frequently reported pattern is the anger superiority effect, whereby negatively valenced faces such as angry expressions are detected more quickly and efficiently than positively valenced faces such as happy expressions in crowds of neutral or differing emotional distractors. The anger superiority effect has been interpreted as reflecting an evolutionary adaptation whereby threat cues receive prioritized processing in order to facilitate rapid defensive responses. This interpretation aligns with broader theories of attention that emphasize the privileged status of threat-relevant stimuli.

At the same time, the empirical literature on the face-in-the-crowd effect contains noteworthy inconsistencies. Several studies have replicated the anger superiority effect, whereas other investigations have reported either no valence-based advantage or, in some cases, a happiness superiority effect in which positive faces are detected more rapidly than negative ones. These mixed findings indicate that simple, general claims about the automaticity of threat detection in visual search may be premature. Instead, the pattern of results may depend on multiple moderating factors, including task parameters, stimulus properties, participant characteristics, and contextual variables. If the face-in-the-crowd effect is sensitive to such moderators, then a more nuanced understanding is necessary to specify when and how emotional valence shapes search performance.

One important class of moderators concerns the observer’s affective state. Mood and momentary affective tone color information processing and can bias attention toward mood-congruent stimuli. Mood-congruent processing effects have been observed across a range of cognitive domains, such as memory recall, interpretation of ambiguous stimuli, and attentional allocation. If mood congruency operates in visual search for emotional faces, then participants who are experiencing a relatively negative affective state should show heightened efficiency for detecting negative target faces relative to positive ones. Conversely, participants in a more positive affective state might show an advantage for detecting positive faces. The potential role of mood congruency is theoretically important because it speaks to how flexible and top-down processes interact with low-level perceptual and survival-relevant mechanisms. In other words, even if threat detection is prioritized by hardwired systems, transient affective states might modulate the extent to which threat or positive social cues capture attention.

Previous research on the interplay of mood and the face-in-the-crowd effect has been limited and mixed. Some studies that manipulated mood experimentally reported modulations of threat detection consistent with mood-congruent attention, while others failed to find such effects. It is also the case that many search studies have not assessed participants’ actual affective states, relying instead on assumptions about baseline moods or using college samples without characterizing affective variability. Consequently, a gap remains in understanding whether naturally occurring individual differences in affective state influence the detection of positive and negative facial expressions during visual search. This gap matters for theoretical debates about whether emotional salience is primarily driven by bottom-up, stimulus-driven factors or whether top-down states can bias early attentional selection mechanisms.

Another methodological concern that could account for divergent findings in the literature is the presence of low-level perceptual confounds in stimulus sets. For example, happy faces commonly display teeth and open mouths, providing high-contrast features that may be more visually salient than the features typically associated with angry faces, such as lowered brows and compressed lips. If positive faces are more easily detected because of these low-level visual cues rather than their emotional meaning, then purported emotional effects will be misattributed. Therefore, experiments attempting to test mood congruency in the face-in-the-crowd paradigm must consider equating or at least acknowledging potential perceptual differences between expressions and ideally include control analyses or stimulus preprocessing to minimize these discrepancies.

In addition to perceptual issues, task parameters such as set size, target prevalence, and distractor composition can influence search efficiency. Classic visual search theory posits that parallel search results in flat set-size slopes, whereas serial search manifests in increasing reaction times as set size increases. The face-in-the-crowd task often yields intermediate results where search slopes vary by emotion type, suggesting that some emotional expressions may benefit from more parallel processing. Thus, any claim that mood influences detection must be situated within the context of how set size and other search parameters were configured in a given study.

Given these complexities, the present study sought to investigate whether naturally occurring affective state, measured with a validated self-report instrument, would produce mood-congruent enhancements in the detection of valenced faces during a visual search task. The overarching theoretical question was whether bottom-up salience of emotional facial cues would dominate search performance or whether top-down mood states would bias attentional deployment in a manner consistent with mood congruency. Practically, measuring participants’ current affective state allows for the examination of individual difference moderators in visual search performance rather than relying on between-study manipulations that may not reflect typical affective variability.

We operationalized mood using the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS), a well-established measure that captures the extent to which individuals endorse positive affective states and negative affective states at the time of assessment. The PANAS provides continuous scores for both positive and negative affect, allowing for analyses that consider both dimensions independently and their potential interaction. Importantly, the PANAS can capture transient mood states as participants complete the measure immediately before the search task, thereby testing the congruency hypothesis with contemporaneous affective data.

Drawing from the existing literature, we anticipated that if mood congruency shapes early attentional allocation in the face-in-the-crowd task, then participants reporting higher negative affect on the PANAS should detect negative target faces more rapidly than positive target faces. Conversely, participants reporting higher positive affect might detect positive target faces more rapidly. We designed the visual search task to include both happy and angry target faces among neutral distractors to directly compare detection efficiency for positive versus negative targets. We included multiple set sizes to assess whether potential mood effects interacted with search demand, for example by appearing only when search was more serial or resource-demanding.

We were also mindful of the possibility that positive faces might be detected more quickly than negative ones under some conditions. If such a pattern emerged, it would call into question a universal anger superiority effect and instead suggest that positive social cues can also benefit from prioritized processing. Taken together, the project aimed to adjudicate between competing accounts by testing the mood-congruency hypothesis within a controlled visual search paradigm while attending to stimulus and task considerations that have complicated prior work.

Because mood congruency remains an open empirical question in the context of face-in-the-crowd research, the current study adopted a conservative analytic strategy. We planned to analyze reaction time data after trimming outliers and controlling for accuracy to ensure that speed differences did not emerge at the expense of performance quality. We also intended to examine mood both categorically—by comparing groups split on negative affect—and continuously—by correlating individual PANAS scores with valence-specific search advantages. Including both approaches increases diagnosticity for detecting mood-congruent effects.

We further specified that if mood congruency were present, it might manifest as a Valence x Mood interaction in an omnibus mixed-model analysis, such that the valence advantage would reverse depending on participants’ affective state. We also planned to test simple effects separately within high- and low-mood groups if the interaction was present. If mood did not modulate detection, but a valence advantage nonetheless appeared, we would consider alternative explanations such as perceptual salience and evaluate supplementary analyses to the extent possible.

Overall, the study was designed to advance theoretical understanding of how affective states and emotional content interact during visual search for faces. By measuring mood, including multiple set sizes, and carefully preprocessing reaction time data, the study sought to provide a rigorous test of mood congruency in the context of emotional face detection. We hypothesized that mood-congruent patterns would appear, particularly for participants reporting relatively higher negative affect who would detect negative faces more quickly. The data collected allowed for a detailed inspection of this hypothesis and for alternative outcomes that could illuminate the constraints and mechanisms underlying the face-in-the-crowd effect more generally.

Methods

Participants

A total of 84 adults participated in the study and provided complete data for both the mood assessment and the visual search task. Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from the surrounding local community. The sample included both undergraduate students and community volunteers, with an age range of 18 to 45 years. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological disorders. Participants provided informed consent and received course credit or monetary compensation for participation. Four participants were excluded from analyses due to excessive error rates (>25% incorrect on the search task) or failure to follow task instructions, leaving 80 participants in the final dataset. The final sample consisted of 80 participants, with a roughly equal gender distribution and a mean age of 22.7 years. No formal clinical screening for mood disorders was performed, as the study aimed to assess naturally occurring variability in affect.

Materials: affect assessment

Participants’ affective state was measured using the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS). The PANAS comprises two 10-item scales assessing positive affect and negative affect. Participants rated the extent to which they felt each adjective at that moment on a five-point Likert scale from 1 (very slightly or not at all) to 5 (extremely). The PANAS was administered on a computer immediately prior to the visual search task to capture contemporaneous affective state. Scores for the positive and negative affect scales were computed by summing the item responses for each subscale, yielding two scores that ranged from 10 to 50. The PANAS was completed in approximately three to five minutes and served as the primary individual-difference predictor in analyses. Internal consistency for the positive and negative scales in the present sample was acceptable, with Cronbach’s alpha values exceeding 0.80 for each scale.

Materials: visual stimuli

Facial stimuli consisted of grayscale photographs of adult faces showing happy, angry, and neutral expressions. Images were selected to represent a balanced set of male and female faces and to include diverse facial features. To reduce low-level color cues, all images were converted to grayscale and standardized for luminance and contrast using image-processing software. Faces were cropped to include only the head and hairline and were presented on a mid-gray background. Each face subtended approximately 3.5 by 4.5 degrees of visual angle when viewed at the standard testing distance. Stimulus size and positioning were chosen to mimic typical displays in face-in-the-crowd studies.

We constructed search arrays comprising either four faces (set size 4) or eight faces (set size 8). Distractor faces were always neutral expressions, and target faces were either happy or angry expressions. Target present and target absent trials were included, and target position was randomized across trials. There were equal numbers of male and female target faces across the experiment, and target valence and set size were fully crossed within participants to allow for within-subject comparisons.

Procedure

Upon arrival, participants provided informed consent and completed a brief demographic questionnaire. Next, participants completed the PANAS on the computer to report their current positive and negative affect. After the PANAS, participants received standardized task instructions explaining the visual search procedure. They were instructed to indicate as quickly and accurately as possible whether an emotional target face (happy or angry) was present among neutral distractor faces by pressing one of two keys on the keyboard. Participants completed an initial practice block of 16 trials to familiarize themselves with the task and key mappings. The practice block included examples of both happy and angry targets and provided feedback.

The main search task consisted of 320 trials divided into 8 blocks of 40 trials each. Within the task, half of the trials were set size 4 and half were set size 8, presented in random order. Additionally, target valence (happy, angry) and target presence (present, absent) were counterbalanced across trials. On target present trials, a single face displayed the target expression, and the remaining faces were neutral. On target absent trials, all faces displayed neutral expressions. Trials began with a 500 ms fixation cross, followed by the presentation of the search array, which remained on the screen until the participant responded or until 3000 ms had elapsed. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible and were reminded to keep their gaze centered. No eye-tracking was used; response timing and accuracy were recorded by the experimental software.

Feedback indicating whether the response was correct or incorrect was provided during the practice block only. During the main task, no trial-by-trial feedback was given, although short breaks were provided between blocks. The entire testing session lasted approximately 45 to 60 minutes, including consent, questionnaires, practice, and task completion.

Design and planned analyses

The core design was a within-subjects manipulation of Target Valence (happy, angry) and Set Size (4, 8), with participant-level affective state as a between-subjects continuous predictor. For categorical analyses, participants were split into high and low negative affect groups based on a median split of PANAS negative affect scores. Similar splits were used for positive affect where relevant.

Primary dependent variables were reaction time (RT) for correct responses on target-present trials and accuracy (proportion correct) on the search task. Reaction times were recorded in milliseconds. Before analysis, RTs were trimmed to exclude trials with RTs < 200 ms or > 3000 ms. Additionally, for each participant, RTs that deviated more than 2.5 standard deviations from the participant’s mean were excluded to reduce the influence of outliers. Accuracy and RT data were analyzed separately.

The principal statistical tests were repeated-measures ANOVAs with Target Valence and Set Size as within-subjects factors, and Mood Group as a between-subjects factor in categorical analyses. For continuous analyses, multiple regression models were used to predict valence-specific RTs and RT difference scores from PANAS positive and negative scores. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta-squared for ANOVA effects and Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons. Where appropriate, supplementary Bayesian analyses were conducted to quantify evidence for null effects, but the primary conclusions were based on frequentist statistics. All analyses were conducted in standard statistical software, and alpha was set at .05 for significance testing.

Data preprocessing and quality control

Data quality checks included inspection for excessive missing responses, unusually fast or slow responses, and error patterns indicative of noncompliance. Four participants were excluded for excessive errors or noncompliance. For the remaining participants, the mean proportion of trials removed due to extreme RTs was low. Accuracy was generally high across conditions, reducing concerns about speed-accuracy trade-offs. Prior to inferential testing, assumptions of ANOVA were examined, including sphericity and normality of residuals. Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied where sphericity was violated. Regression diagnostics confirmed that no individual cases unduly influenced the continuous analyses.

Results

Overview of data and preprocessing outcomes

All reported analyses are based on the final sample of 80 participants after the exclusion of four participants for excessive errors or clear noncompliance. From the complete trial set, approximately 3.7% of trials were removed due to RTs below 200 ms or above 3000 ms. An additional 2.9% of trials were excluded because they exceeded 2.5 standard deviations from the participant’s mean RT. After trimming, the dataset contained RTs for correct target-present trials that formed the basis of primary analyses. Accuracy on the task was high overall, with a mean accuracy of 94.1% across conditions. Descriptive statistics indicated that the mean RT across all target-present trials was 724 ms, with a standard deviation of 142 ms.

PANAS scores in the sample showed variability. The mean positive affect score was 31.8 (SD = 6.1), and the mean negative affect score was 15.9 (SD = 5.2). The distribution of negative affect scores was approximately normal, justifying the use of median split grouping for categorical analyses while also supporting continuous modeling. A median split on negative affect produced groups of 40 participants each categorized as low negative affect and high negative affect for between-group comparisons.

Initial ANOVA: main effects of valence and set size

To test the primary hypotheses, we conducted a repeated-measures ANOVA with Target Valence (happy, angry) and Set Size (4, 8) as within-subject factors and Mood Group (low negative, high negative) as a between-subjects factor, using trimmed RTs for correct target-present trials as the dependent variable. The ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Target Valence, F(1,78) = 18.67, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .193. This main effect reflected faster detection of happy targets (M = 702 ms, SE = 10 ms) compared with angry targets (M = 745 ms, SE = 11 ms). The main effect of Set Size was also significant, F(1,78) = 102.54, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .568, indicating faster responses at set size 4 (M = 655 ms, SE = 9 ms) than at set size 8 (M = 792 ms, SE = 12 ms). These two main effects were robust and indicated that both valence and search load influenced reaction times.

Interaction effects and mood group

The Target Valence x Set Size interaction was not significant, F(1,78) = 1.82, p = .18, partial eta-squared = .023, suggesting that the happy advantage was consistent across set sizes and did not depend on search demand in this dataset. Critically, the between-subjects Mood Group factor did not show a significant main effect on RTs, F(1,78) = 0.92, p = .34, partial eta-squared = .012. More importantly for the mood-congruency hypothesis, the Target Valence x Mood Group interaction was not significant, F(1,78) = 0.46, p = .50, partial eta-squared = .006. The three-way interaction among Target Valence, Set Size, and Mood Group was likewise non-significant, F(1,78) = 0.13, p = .72, partial eta-squared = .002. These null interactions indicate that the observed happiness advantage was not moderated by a categorical split on negative affect.

Pairwise comparisons

Given the significant main effect of Target Valence, simple pairwise comparisons were conducted. Overall, participants detected happy faces significantly faster than angry faces, t(79) = -4.32, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.48. When examined separately within low negative affect and high negative affect groups, the happy advantage persisted in both groups: in the low negative affect group, mean RT for happy targets was 695 ms versus 737 ms for angry targets, t(39) = -2.84, p = .007, Cohen’s d = 0.45; in the high negative affect group, mean RT for happy targets was 709 ms versus 753 ms for angry targets, t(39) = -3.12, p = .003, Cohen’s d = 0.49. Thus, the within-group comparisons demonstrated consistent happiness superiority that did not differ appreciably between mood groups.

Continuous analyses with PANAS scores

To supplement the categorical analyses, we conducted regression analyses with continuous PANAS positive and negative scores predicting the valence-specific RTs and their difference scores. A multiple regression predicting the RT difference score (RT_angry – RT_happy) from negative affect and positive affect revealed no significant associations. Specifically, negative affect did not significantly predict the RT difference (beta = .09, t = 0.80, p = .43), and positive affect likewise did not predict the difference (beta = -.12, t = -1.05, p = .30). When predicting RTs to angry targets alone, negative affect showed a non-significant trend in the expected direction (greater negative affect associated with slightly faster angry RTs), but this effect was small and did not reach significance (beta = -.16, t = -1.43, p = .16). Regressions predicting happy target RTs revealed no significant predictors from PANAS scores. These continuous analyses corroborate the categorical analyses in showing a lack of evidence for mood congruency effects.

Accuracy analyses

Analyses of accuracy rates showed high performance across conditions, consistent with ceiling-level accuracy in the paradigm. A repeated-measures ANOVA on accuracy with the same factors (Target Valence and Set Size as within-subjects factors, Mood Group as between-subjects) showed no significant main effect of Target Valence on accuracy, F(1,78) = 0.41, p = .52. The main effect of Set Size was significant, F(1,78) = 12.03, p = .001, indicating slightly lower accuracy at set size 8 relative to set size 4, but mean accuracy remained above 90% in both conditions. Mood Group did not affect accuracy, F(1,78) = 0.22, p = .64, and no higher-order interactions were significant. Therefore, the RT effects are unlikely to reflect speed-accuracy trade-offs.

Supplementary analyses: low-level feature checks

To probe whether low-level visual features might explain the happiness advantage, we conducted post hoc descriptive checks using image luminance and contrast measures. Mean luminance and contrast values were standardized across images during preprocessing, and descriptive checks confirmed no systematic mean differences between happy and angry images in global luminance. However, inspection of local contrast around the mouth region suggested that happy faces tended to show greater local contrast due to teeth visibility in some stimuli. To examine whether this factor could drive the RT advantage, we recomputed RTs for a subset of trials featuring happy faces with closed-mouth smiles (a subset of stimuli without visible teeth). In this subset, the happy RT advantage was reduced but not eliminated: mean RT for closed-mouth happy targets was 718 ms compared with 742 ms for angry targets, t(79) = -1.98, p = .05. This suggests that low-level features may contribute to the happiness advantage but do not fully account for it in the present dataset.

Bayesian analysis of mood effects

Given the theoretical importance of establishing evidence for the absence of mood congruency effects, we conducted Bayesian t-tests comparing valence-specific RT differences between low and high negative affect groups. The Bayes factor comparing the null hypothesis of no difference to the alternative hypothesis of a difference was BF01 = 4.12, indicating moderate evidence in favor of the null. Bayesian regression analyses with PANAS scores as predictors of RT difference scores also yielded Bayes factors suggesting anecdotal to moderate evidence for the null effect. These Bayesian outcomes strengthen the interpretation that mood did not meaningfully modulate the valence-specific search advantage in this sample.

Exploratory analyses: gender and target gender effects

As exploratory checks, we examined whether participant gender or target gender interacted with valence effects. A mixed ANOVA including participant gender as an additional between-subjects factor did not reveal any significant interactions with Target Valence, and pairwise comparisons showed that the happiness advantage held for both male and female participants. Similarly, whether the target face was male or female did not substantially change the pattern of results; happy targets were detected faster regardless of target gender. These checks indicate that the happiness advantage was robust across these exploratory subgroupings.

Summary of results

In summary, the primary finding was a clear happiness superiority effect in which happy faces were detected faster than angry faces across set sizes. Set size exerted the expected influence on reaction times, with larger arrays producing slower responses. There was no evidence that participants’ self-reported negative affect on the PANAS moderated the valence advantage, either when operationalized categorically via median split or continuously in regression analyses. Accuracy remained high and did not account for RT differences. Supplemental analyses suggested that low-level perceptual features such as visible teeth in smiles may partially contribute to the happiness advantage but do not appear to be the sole driver. Bayesian analyses provided moderate support for the null hypothesis regarding mood modulation of valence-specific search performance.

Discussion

This study examined the role of participants’ affective state in moderating the face-in-the-crowd effect, asking whether mood congruency would shape the detection advantage for negatively valenced (angry) versus positively valenced (happy) faces. Contrary to the initial hypothesis that higher negative affect would produce relatively faster detection of negative faces, the primary behavioral outcome revealed a happiness superiority effect: participants detected happy target faces more rapidly than angry target faces across set sizes. Furthermore, there was no consistent evidence that the PANAS-measured affective state—either negative or positive—modulated this valence advantage. The absence of significant interactions between valence and mood, coupled with Bayesian analyses that moderately favored the null for mood effects, suggests that mood congruency did not manifest in the current paradigm under the tested conditions.

The finding of a happiness superiority effect contributes to a growing body of evidence challenging the universality of an anger superiority effect in visual search. The presence of population-level variability in which positive faces can be more rapidly detected raises theoretical questions about the assumptions that threat detection is always prioritized by attentional mechanisms. One plausible interpretation is that positive facial expressions, particularly those signaling affiliation or reward, carry adaptive social utility that warrants efficient detection. In many daily interactions, rapidly identifying approachability and affiliative signals can be equally important as detecting threat, for example in coordinating social bonding, cooperation, and prosocial behavior. Attentional systems may thus be tuned not only for threat vigilance but also for the efficient processing of socially relevant positive cues.

However, the happiness advantage observed here must be interpreted with caution, as low-level perceptual factors may partly explain the result. Our preprocessing standardized global luminance and contrast, but descriptive checks indicated that happy faces sometimes exhibited greater local contrast due to visible teeth. Teeth and the open mouth configuration can be highly salient visual features that enhance detectability independent of emotional meaning. The exploratory analysis of closed-mouth happy faces showed a reduced happiness advantage, which suggests that perceptual features contribute to the speed of detection. Nevertheless, even when restricting analyses to closed-mouth smiles, a marginal advantage for happy faces persisted, implying that emotion-related factors beyond local contrast may also play a role. Future work should more thoroughly equate low-level features across expressions, perhaps through image morphing, controlled photography, or computational techniques to mask teeth, to isolate the emotional component from perceptual salience.

The null result regarding mood congruency is notable given theoretical expectations that affective state should bias perceptual and attentional processes. Several possible explanations can account for the lack of mood effects. First, the PANAS measures relatively general positive and negative affect and relies on self-report; while it captures momentary affect to some degree, it may not reflect the specific affective states most relevant to attentional biases in face processing. For example, discrete emotions such as anxiety, anger, or sadness could differentially influence threat detection, whereas broad negative affect scores might average across different states and thus dilute specific effects. Second, mood congruency effects observed in other domains may be more pronounced when affect is experimentally induced to reach higher intensity levels; naturalistic variability in mood within a nonclinical sample may have been insufficient to generate detectable biases in early visual search. Third, it is possible that mood influences later stages of processing, such as interpretation and memory, rather than the rapid preattentive extraction that underlies search efficiency. If mood primarily affects post-perceptual processes, it would not be expected to modulate RTs in a speeded search task.

Another consideration is that mood congruency might interact with other task parameters not manipulated here. For instance, the presence of multiple emotional distractors, target prevalence, or more ambiguous expressions could amplify mood-dependent biases. The current design used neutral distractors and isolated emotional targets, creating a relatively clear signal that may have allowed bottom-up features to dominate. In contrast, under conditions of ambiguity or when top-down task sets are more demanding, mood-related biases might have greater opportunity to influence selection.

Methodological features of the present study also influence interpretation. Although we standardized images for global contrast and luminance, and included both set sizes 4 and 8 to sample across search loads, we did not employ eye-tracking or electrophysiological measures that could offer more fine-grained temporal or spatial evidence of attentional allocation. Eye-tracking could reveal whether mood influences initial saccade targets or dwell time on particular faces, even if RTs converge across groups. Likewise, event-related potentials could detect mood-related modulation of early sensory components that do not necessarily translate to overt behavioral differences. Thus, the absence of an RT-based mood effect does not preclude subtler attentional biases that require more sensitive methodologies to detect.

The data nonetheless have important implications. First, they underscore that positive faces can enjoy detection advantages under certain conditions, challenging simplistic evolutionary narratives that treat threat detection as uniformly dominant. The social environment values both the detection of danger and the detection of opportunity; attentional systems may therefore prioritize different signals depending on immediate contingencies and stimulus properties. Second, the findings emphasize the need to carefully consider stimulus confounds when interpreting valence effects in visual search. Because perceptual distinctiveness can produce apparent emotion effects, researchers must ensure that emotional meaning rather than visual salience drives observed differences.

The study also points to fruitful avenues for future research. Experimental mood induction could be employed to test whether more intense or discrete affective states produce congruent search biases. For example, inducing anxiety specifically might enhance detection of threat-related faces, whereas inducing contentment or social reward sensitivity might accentuate detection of positive faces. Such manipulations should be paired with objective measures such as pupilometry or electrophysiology to capture transient attentional shifts. Additionally, future work should use stimuli that control for or remove salient local features such as teeth, or implement morphing techniques to equate expressive features across emotions, to rule out perceptual confounds more definitively.

Examining clinical populations offers another promising direction. Individuals with mood or anxiety disorders often display attentional biases for certain emotional stimuli, and testing whether these individual differences translate into search advantages in the face-in-the-crowd task could shed light on the functional nature of attentional biases in psychopathology. Longitudinal designs could also explore whether fluctuations in affect over time correspond to changes in attentional priorities.

On a theoretical level, integrating models of visual attention with affective science is a growing imperative. Computational or neural models that incorporate both bottom-up feature-driven salience and top-down affective and motivational biases would provide richer predictions about when mood congruency should emerge. Such models could guide empirical tests that systematically vary stimulus salience, task demands, and affective state to map the parameter space governing emotion-based search effects.

Limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. The sample was relatively young and nonclinical, limiting generalizability. The PANAS, while reliable, is limited in diagnostic specificity, and reliance on self-report may not capture physiological or implicit affective states. Stimuli, though standardized for global luminance and contrast, could not be perfectly equated for every local feature across expressions. The visual search task used static photographs and did not incorporate dynamic or contextual cues that accompany real-world facial expressions. Finally, behavioral RTs offer a limited window into ongoing cognitive and affective processes, and combining behavioral paradigms with converging methods would strengthen causal inferences.

In conclusion, the present study provides evidence that happy faces can be detected more rapidly than angry faces in a standard visual search paradigm and that naturally occurring variability in self-reported affective state, as measured by the PANAS, did not produce mood-congruent modulations of this effect. The findings highlight the need to consider both positive social signals and low-level perceptual factors when interpreting valence-based advantages in face detection. Future work that combines rigorous stimulus control, targeted mood manipulations, and multimodal measurement approaches will be essential to determine the boundary conditions under which mood congruency shapes attentional selection for emotional faces.
