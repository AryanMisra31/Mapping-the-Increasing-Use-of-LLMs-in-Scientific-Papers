Introduction

Academic performance is a central outcome of interest in education research and policy because it is closely tied to future opportunities, self-esteem, and social mobility. Many stakeholders including students, parents, educators, and policymakers seek to understand the factors that reliably influence academic outcomes so that resources can be allocated effectively. Among the personal characteristics frequently implicated in academic success is self-regulation, which encompasses a range of behaviors and cognitive strategies that support the completion of academic tasks. Procrastination is one of the most visible and studied failures of self-regulation in academic contexts and it has been characterized as the voluntary delay of an intended course of action despite expecting to be worse off for the delay. The phenomenon of procrastination is widespread among student populations and has been associated in some studies with distress, lower well-being, and underachievement. Because of its apparent prevalence and potential negative consequences, procrastination has been proposed as a primary target for educational interventions and counseling services. At the same time, the empirical literature linking procrastination to academic performance has produced mixed results, with some studies reporting moderate negative associations and other studies finding weak or inconsistent relationships. This heterogeneity in findings has led researchers to query the conditions under which procrastination is most detrimental to grades, and whether aggregate measures of discouraging behavior correspond to objective indices of performance. One issue complicating interpretation is definitional variability; researchers have operationalized procrastination in multiple ways, ranging from general tendency scales to task-specific self-reports, and this variability may contribute to inconsistent findings. Another complication stems from measurement of academic achievement, where studies have used diverse metrics including cumulative GPA, course-specific grades, self-reported grade categories, and standardized test scores, each of which has its own advantages and limitations. The time frame over which procrastination is assessed can also influence observed associations, since procrastination measured over a short timescale may not map onto long-term academic outcomes. In addition, there is evidence suggesting that the relationship between procrastination and performance may be moderated by a host of factors such as task difficulty, intrinsic motivation, coping strategies, perceived competence, time management skills, and compensatory behaviors. For example, some students who procrastinate may employ last-minute coping strategies that effectively preserve performance, while others may engage in self-sabotaging procrastination that clearly undermines their grades. Personality traits such as conscientiousness and aspects of executive functioning have been implicated as potential confounds or mediators in the association between procrastination and academic success. Moreover, contextual and cultural factors can shape both procrastination tendencies and grading practices, which complicates cross-study comparisons and generalizations. Given this complex backdrop, targeted research that carefully measures procrastination and academic performance within specific educational contexts is needed to clarify the nature and strength of any association. Secondary school end-of-course examinations are a particularly important context in which to examine these relationships because high-stakes outcomes such as qualification for further study hinge on performance in these assessments. In many educational systems, including those that use subject-specific certification exams, students often prepare intensively for a relatively short series of terminal assessments and may manifest different procrastination behaviors compared with ongoing coursework environments. The current investigation focused on students who had recently completed a particular level of secondary certification, asking them to report their best three subject results from that examination and complete a standard measure of procrastination tendency. Using the top three subject scores as an index of academic performance allows exploration of achievement in disciplines where each student perceives themselves to be relatively successful, and it also provides variability by capturing performance across subjects. While some prior research has used aggregate or average performance across all subjects, focusing on top subject results can highlight whether procrastination relates to peak academic achievement rather than mean-level performance. If procrastination exerts a clear negative influence on academic outcomes, one would expect students with higher procrastination scores to report lower subject results even among their strongest subjects. Conversely, a lack of association in this narrower context might suggest that procrastination is less relevant to grades than other factors or that students are able to compensate for procrastinatory behavior when stakes are high. The current study also has practical implications for resource allocation within educational institutions. Counseling services and interventions aimed at reducing procrastination consume time and financial resources, and it is important to prioritize those supports that demonstrably improve student outcomes. If a robust link between procrastination and performance were present, then investment in interventions targeting procrastination might be justified as a route to improved grades. If, however, procrastination shows little or no relationship with objective measures of achievement in a given context, then resources might be better directed towards other supports such as programs to raise self-awareness, study-skills training, or structural changes to assessment practices. The present study therefore sought to provide empirical evidence regarding the relationship between procrastination and academic performance in a sample of students who had recently completed a level of secondary certification. The study recruited participants to complete an online survey in which they reported the results of their best three subjects and completed a validated procrastination scale. The choice of an online survey was intended to maximize reach and convenience while collecting both self-reported academic outcomes and standardized measures of procrastinatory tendencies. Because the literature offers mixed predictions, the current study adopted a hypothesis-testing approach while remaining open to null findings; specifically, the primary analysis examined the correlation between the composite academic performance indicator derived from the best three subject results and the total score on the procrastination scale. Secondary analyses examined whether the relationship varied by demographic covariates and whether splitting the sample into high and low procrastination groups yielded group differences in academic performance. Given prior work suggesting that the relationship between procrastination and performance can be modest and contingent on numerous moderators, the study also evaluated whether any observed effect was robust to controlling for age and gender. The rationale for the sample size was pragmatic and aimed at achieving adequate sensitivity to detect small-to-moderate effects while remaining feasible for online recruitment. The study’s social and educational relevance derives from its potential to inform counseling priorities and to contribute to theoretical debates concerning the causal role of procrastination in academic achievement. In summary, this study was designed to provide targeted evidence regarding whether self-reported procrastination tendencies are linked to self-reported peak subject performance on a recent standardized assessment, with implications for both theory and practice in educational settings.

Methods

Participants were 101 individuals who had recently completed the specified secondary-level certification and who consented to take part in an online survey. The sample size of 101 was based on a balance between practical recruitment considerations and the goal of having sufficient power to detect small-to-moderate correlations, recognizing that the study would be most sensitive to effects in the small-to-medium range. Participants were recruited using an online advertisement distributed through social media platforms and through student networks that facilitated access to those who had recently completed the examination in question. Inclusion criteria required that participants be aged 16 or older and that they had completed the examination within the prior 12 months so that recall of subject results would be reasonably accurate. Exclusion criteria included incomplete survey responses and reports that indicated implausible grade combinations, such as nonstandard grading scales that could not be reliably converted to the study’s scoring metric. All participants provided informed consent electronically prior to beginning the survey, and the survey protocol was approved by an institutional review process that ensured ethical treatment and data confidentiality. The online survey was hosted on a secure platform and participants were able to complete it in their own time; average completion time for the survey was recorded to assess engagement and to screen for excessively brief responses that might indicate inattentive responding. The survey collected demographic information including age, gender, and the time elapsed since examination completion. Participants were asked to list the three subjects in which they achieved their best results and to provide the official result for each of those three subjects. For consistency, participants were given a standardized conversion table within the survey to translate commonly used letter grades and categorical outcomes into a numeric point scale, and they were instructed to use their official certification documentation when available. The three subject scores were converted to numeric points and an academic performance composite score was calculated as the mean of the best three subject points for each participant. The performance composite was used as the primary outcome variable in subsequent analyses because it summarized peak academic attainment while accommodating differences in subjects across participants. Procrastination was measured using a widely used self-report procrastination scale that yields a total score reflecting general procrastinatory tendency. The scale consisted of multiple items rated on a Likert-type response scale, with higher total scores indicating a greater propensity to procrastinate. The scale has been widely utilized in student samples and was chosen because it captures broad tendencies rather than task-specific instances, which aligned with the study’s aim to assess general predisposition. Scale items were presented in randomized order to minimize response patterning, and internal consistency for the scale was computed for the present sample using Cronbach’s alpha. Data screening procedures included checks for missing values, checks for normality of distribution for key variables, and inspection for outliers defined as values more than three standard deviations from the mean. Cases missing any of the three subject scores or the complete procrastination scale were excluded from primary analyses; descriptive statistics were computed both for the full recruited sample and for the analytic sample after exclusions. Primary analyses focused on the bivariate association between the academic performance composite and the procrastination total score and employed Pearson’s correlation coefficient when variables met parametric assumptions. When assumptions of normality were not met, Spearman rank-order correlation was used as a nonparametric alternative to assess the robustness of observed relationships. A multiple linear regression analysis was also conducted with the academic performance composite as the dependent variable and procrastination score as the primary predictor, adjusting for covariates including age and gender to evaluate whether the relationship persisted after accounting for those factors. The regression model was inspected for multicollinearity, heteroscedasticity, and influential observations using variance inflation factors, inspection of residual plots, and Cook’s distance statistics, respectively. In addition to the continuous analyses, an exploratory categorical analysis was conducted by dichotomizing the procrastination score into high and low groups based on a median split and comparing mean academic performance using an independent-samples t-test. Effect sizes for group comparisons were reported using Cohen’s d, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for primary effect size estimates to aid interpretation of precision. Given contemporary interest in evaluating evidence in favor of null hypotheses, a Bayesian correlation analysis was conducted as a supplementary check to quantify the relative support for the null versus the alternative hypothesis using default priors. All statistical analyses were performed using standard statistical software and the level of significance was set at alpha = .05 for conventional hypothesis testing. Results are reported with both p-values and effect size metrics to provide a fuller picture of the magnitude and practical significance of observed relationships. Data and analytic code were stored securely and anonymized prior to analysis to preserve participant confidentiality.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the sample are presented first to provide an overview of the data used in subsequent analyses. The final analytic sample consisted of 101 participants after exclusion of incomplete responses and entries that could not be reliably scored. The age of participants ranged from late adolescence to early adulthood with a mean age that reflected the typical age for those completing the secondary-level certification in the studied context. Gender distribution indicated a mix of male and female participants with a minority identifying as other or preferring not to say; the breakdown permitted exploratory gender comparisons but was not balanced. The time elapsed since examination completion varied among participants but was generally within the predefined 12-month window, which reduced concerns about inaccurate recall of results. The academic performance composite, computed as the mean points across the participant’s best three subject scores, showed variability across participants but was slightly skewed toward higher values, reflecting that participants reported their top subjects. The procrastination scale produced a range of scores in the sample and showed acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha indicating satisfactory reliability for the scale items. Tests of normality revealed that both the academic composite and the procrastination scores approximated normal distributions sufficiently for parametric analyses, although the academic composite had a modest negative skew consistent with a concentration of higher scores among top-performing subjects. Bivariate Pearson correlation was conducted to test the primary hypothesis of an association between procrastination tendency and academic performance. The Pearson correlation coefficient between procrastination total score and the academic performance composite was small in magnitude and not statistically significant, indicating that higher self-reported procrastination was not associated with lower performance among participants’ best three subjects. A Spearman rank-order correlation corroborated the Pearson result, yielding a similarly small and non-significant association, which suggested that deviations from normality were not masking a monotonic relationship. The observed correlation coefficient translated to a very small effect size that would correspond to a negligible proportion of variance explained in academic performance by procrastination tendency. To examine whether the relationship persisted after accounting for demographics, a multiple linear regression model was estimated with academic performance composite as the dependent variable and procrastination score as the primary predictor, alongside age and gender as covariates. In this regression model, procrastination did not emerge as a significant predictor of academic performance, and the coefficient estimate was small and close to zero. Age and gender neither exerted significant independent effects in the model, and inclusion of these covariates did not meaningfully alter the null association between procrastination and the academic composite. Diagnostics for the regression model indicated no serious multicollinearity, residuals displayed approximate homoscedasticity, and no single observation unduly influenced model estimates based on Cook’s distance metrics. The R-squared for the regression was low, indicating that the included predictors collectively explained little variance in the academic composite. An exploratory categorical analysis compared mean academic performance between participants classified as high versus low procrastinators based on a median split of the procrastination score. The independent-samples t-test revealed no significant difference in mean academic composite between the two groups, and the group mean difference corresponded to a very small Cohen’s d effect size. Confidence intervals around the mean difference encompassed zero and were narrow enough to suggest that large effects were unlikely in the present sample. Given the null findings from standard frequentist analyses, a Bayesian correlation analysis was performed as a supplementary check to evaluate the relative evidence for the absence of a relationship. The Bayesian analysis produced a Bayes factor that modestly favored the null hypothesis over the alternative, indicating that the data provided more support for no association than for an association of the magnitude detectable in this sample. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to explore whether restricting the sample to narrower age bands or excluding participants with extreme procrastination scores would alter results; these analyses produced results consistent with the primary analyses, with no statistically significant associations emerging. Given that some prior literature has suggested the possibility of a curvilinear relationship between procrastination and performance, an exploratory quadratic term for procrastination was added to the regression model but did not yield a significant quadratic association, and the quadratic coefficient was near zero. To assess whether lack of variance in grades among top subjects might obscure associations, an analysis was conducted focusing on participants who reported greater variability across their three best subject scores; even within this subset, procrastination did not significantly correlate with the academic composite. Finally, post hoc power calculations indicated that with the obtained sample size, the study had adequate power to detect small-to-moderate correlations but limited power to detect very small effects; thus, while the data provided clear evidence against moderate or large negative associations, they could not conclusively rule out very small relationships. Overall, the pattern of analyses converged on a consistent outcome: within this sample and measurement context, there was no meaningful or statistically significant relationship between self-reported procrastination tendency and self-reported peak subject performance.

Discussion

The present study examined whether general procrastinatory tendency, as assessed by a standard self-report scale, was associated with academic performance indexed by the average of participants’ best three subject results on a recent secondary-level certification. Across multiple analytic approaches including bivariate correlations, multiple regression controlling for age and gender, categorical group comparisons, and supplementary Bayesian analysis, the study found no evidence of a meaningful relationship between procrastination scores and top-subject academic performance. These null findings were robust to sensitivity checks and supplementary analyses designed to probe nonlinearity and sample subgroups. Taken together, the results suggest that, at least in the examined context, students’ general tendency to procrastinate does not translate into lower achievement in the subjects where they perform best. This outcome has several important implications for theory, practice, and future research. Theoretically, the absence of a clear negative association challenges simplistic characterizations of procrastination as a uniformly detrimental trait with direct, sizable impacts on objective academic outcomes. The literature has long pointed to variability in how procrastination manifests and in the consequences it produces, and the present findings align with views that emphasize heterogeneity in outcomes based on coping strategies, task characteristics, and individual differences. For some students, procrastination may coexist with effective compensatory strategies such as intensive last-minute studying, targeted use of resources, or strategic allocation of effort that preserves performance in key subjects. These students may be able to marshal sufficient motivation when stakes are high and thus avoid grade penalties despite procrastinatorial tendencies. For other students, procrastination may indeed undermine performance, but such cases may not be prevalent enough or may be concentrated in contexts other than top-subject outcomes to produce detectable effects in the present sample. In addition, peak subject grades may reflect domain-specific strengths and intrinsic interest that mitigate the impacts of procrastination; when students are highly engaged or competent in a subject, they may be less vulnerable to the negative consequences of delayed task initiation. Practically, the findings bear on decisions about allocating counseling and intervention resources. If procrastination were a primary driver of poor grades across the board, then interventions aimed at reducing procrastinatory behavior might be expected to yield measurable improvements in academic outcomes. However, the lack of association observed here suggests that blanket investments in counseling aimed specifically at reducing general procrastination may not be the most efficient strategy for improving grades among students who are already performing well in their top subjects. Instead, it may be more productive to target resources toward approaches that enhance self-awareness, metacognitive skills, and context-specific study strategies that can be directly applied to areas of weakness. Programs designed to help students accurately assess their study habits, recognize when procrastination becomes problematic, and implement concrete time-management strategies in subjects where they struggle might have greater impact than generalized anti-procrastination counseling. The present results also suggest that educational stakeholders should be cautious about treating procrastination as a universal explanation for underperformance; a more nuanced assessment that considers the interplay between procrastination, task characteristics, and individual resources is warranted. The study’s findings raise several lines of inquiry for future research. First, longitudinal designs that track procrastination and objective academic outcomes over time would help clarify causal dynamics and whether changes in procrastination predict subsequent changes in performance. Second, research that includes a broader range of academic outcomes, such as average grades across all subjects, course-specific assessments, and standardized tests, could determine whether the null association observed for peak subjects generalizes to other performance indices. Third, studies that incorporate behavioral measures of procrastination—such as logged study time, timestamps of assignment submissions, or ecological momentary assessment—could complement self-report scales and help identify instances where self-perceived procrastination diverges from actual behavior. Fourth, experimental interventions that specifically target procrastination and evaluate downstream effects on objectively measured grades would provide direct evidence regarding the utility of counseling approaches aimed at reducing procrastinatory behaviors. Fifth, exploring moderators such as task aversiveness, conscientiousness, executive functioning, and coping style could illuminate for whom and under what conditions procrastination is most consequential. Sixth, qualitative work could enrich quantitative findings by revealing how students experience and manage procrastination in relation to their subject strengths and weaknesses. The study’s limitations temper the degree to which the results can be generalized and point to avenues for methodological refinement. A primary limitation is reliance on self-reported academic results, which might be subject to recall error or social desirability bias; obtaining official exam records in future research would strengthen the validity of performance measures. The sample, while adequate for detecting small-to-moderate effects, was recruited online and may not be fully representative of the broader population of students who completed the examined certification. The decision to focus on the best three subjects conflates peak performance with a narrower range of outcomes and therefore may not capture patterns relevant to overall academic success or subjects where students struggle most. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about whether procrastination affects performance or whether observed behaviors and grades are influenced by third variables. Additionally, the procrastination measure assessed general tendency rather than task- or subject-specific procrastination, and the latter may be more closely tied to outcomes in specific disciplines. Another consideration is that the educational context in which exams are taken and graded could moderate effects; for example, systems with high-stakes terminal examinations may induce compensatory behaviors that mitigate the influence of procrastination on grades. Despite these limitations, the study contributes useful evidence by demonstrating that general procrastinatory tendency was not associated with peak subject performance in this sample and by highlighting the importance of considering context and measurement choices when investigating procrastination and achievement. The practical recommendation that counseling targeted specifically at reducing general procrastination may not be the highest priority for improving grades should be interpreted carefully. It does not imply that procrastination lacks psychological costs or that interventions are never warranted; procrastination has been linked to distress, reduced well-being, and academic disengagement in other research, and those outcomes may justify supportive services even in the absence of grade effects. Rather, the present findings suggest a more strategic allocation of resources where interventions are tailored to identified needs, such as helping students who demonstrate both high procrastination and poor performance or those who report distress related to procrastination. Emphasizing student self-awareness and metacognitive training could be a productive complement to counseling because such approaches empower students to identify situations where procrastination is problematic and to apply concrete strategies in those contexts. Future programs might combine elements that increase self-monitoring, teach evidence-based study techniques, and provide scaffolding for time-limited tasks, thereby targeting both the behaviors and the self-regulatory capacities that support academic success. In conclusion, the current study found no significant relationship between general procrastination tendency and students’ best-subject academic performance within the sampled cohort. The results underscore the need for nuanced understanding of how procrastination relates to achievement and for targeted, evidence-based interventions that attend to individual profiles and context-specific demands. Continued research that leverages longitudinal designs, objective performance measures, behavioral indicators of procrastination, and tests of moderators and mediators will be crucial for refining theory and guiding practical efforts to support student success.
