Introduction

Working memory is widely recognized as a central component of human cognitive architecture, supporting the temporary storage and manipulation of information necessary for a host of complex behaviors. Early models of working memory separated the system into specialized components, recognizing a need to account for both storage and processing aspects in short-term tasks. Within this framework, one influential approach has partitioned verbal short-term maintenance into a phonological store and a rehearsal mechanism, often termed the phonological loop. The phonological loop model posits that phonological representations are held in a temporary buffer and are refreshed via subvocal articulatory rehearsal, thereby protecting them against decay and interference. Much of the empirical foundation for the phonological loop has emerged from studies using immediate serial recall tasks, where participants are asked to recall sequences of verbal items in the order presented. Immediate serial recall has been a fertile testing ground because it produces robust and replicable effects that are interpretable within the phonological loop architecture.

Two classical phenomena that have been taken as signatures of phonological coding and articulatory rehearsal are the phonological similarity effect and the word length effect. The phonological similarity effect shows that items that are phonologically similar are more likely to be confused in serial recall, which suggests a reliance on phonological representations. The word length effect demonstrates that lists composed of longer spoken words produce lower recall performance than lists of shorter words, consistent with a rehearsal process whose efficiency depends on the time required to subvocally articulate the items. Articulatory suppression studies, in which participants are required to perform a concurrent overt articulatory task during retention, provide another key line of evidence. Articulatory suppression typically reduces or eliminates the word length effect and generally lowers recall performance, findings that are readily interpretable as disruption of the subvocal rehearsal process.

At the same time, another line of research has emphasized the role of auditory input and perceptual processes in shaping short-term verbal memory. The irrelevant sound effect refers to impaired verbal serial recall performance in the presence of background sounds that are unrelated to the memoranda. This effect has been robustly demonstrated using sequences of environmental sounds, tones, or spoken syllables, and is often sensitive to the changing-state properties of the background sequence. The changing-state hypothesis holds that sequences with many acoustic changes disrupt serial order retention more than steady-state sequences, and that this disruption occurs even when the background sound is unattended. Such findings suggest that low-level auditory processing can intrude on the organization of serial order representations, potentially independent of or interacting with articulatory rehearsal.

These two sets of phenomena—those implicating articulatory rehearsal and those implicating auditory interference—have been accommodated within the phonological loop model by assuming that rehearsal acts upon a phonological store that is itself influenced by incoming auditory information. Under this view, rehearsal is primarily articulatory but may draw upon or interact with auditory traces laid down by perception. However, the precise relationship between articulatory rehearsal and auditory maintenance processes remains contested. Some researchers have argued that subvocal articulation is the core maintenance mechanism, and that auditory contributions are secondary or epiphenomenal. Others have proposed that an auditory trace, perhaps akin to echoic memory, contributes substantially to short-term recall, and that rehearsal and auditory representations can both support performance depending on task demands.

A persistent methodological observation is that many demonstrations of the phonological loop's properties have relied heavily on manipulations of articulation, most notably articulatory suppression, whereas the contributions of auditory processes have sometimes been tested with less consistency or with stimuli and conditions that vary widely across studies. For example, different studies have used different types of irrelevant sound—changing-state vs. steady-state, speech vs. tones—and different degrees of attentional load during interference presentation. These variations make it difficult to draw firm conclusions about the conditions under which auditory interference affects rehearsal-based maintenance. There is also conceptual ambiguity about what constitutes an auditory contribution to rehearsal: does auditory input merely interfere at a perceptual level, disrupting serial order encoding, or does it contribute an alternative maintenance trace that participants can use instead of articulation?

Beyond these empirical and conceptual issues, there is a complementary methodological concern about the operationalization of articulatory and auditory manipulations. Articulatory suppression is typically implemented as repeated utterance of a simple syllable or word at a fixed or approximate rate, but participants may vary in compliance and in the extent to which the suppression task actually blocks subvocal rehearsal. Likewise, irrelevant sound presentations may differ in loudness, complexity, or the presence of salient events, any of which could alter the degree of distraction. The interaction between the modality of interference and the degree of attentional engagement required by that interference also remains underexplored. Some accounts predict that auditory interference will disrupt rehearsal regardless of attention because of automatic perceptual capture mechanisms, whereas others predict that attentional engagement with the auditory stream is necessary for interference to manifest.

Given these unresolved issues, re-examining the articulatory component of rehearsal while simultaneously probing for an auditory contribution using carefully controlled interference tasks is timely. A systematic manipulation that contrasts a baseline control condition with articulatory suppression, passive auditory interference, and auditory interference requiring an active attentional response during the retention interval would allow for a direct test of multiple mechanisms in a single experimental context. Such an approach could clarify whether the articulatory mechanism is necessary for maintenance under typical serial recall conditions, and whether auditory interference exerts an effect when it either passively streams into perceptual systems or actively competes for attention.

It is also important to consider how list length, item characteristics, and serial position might interact with different forms of interference. If articulatory rehearsal primarily refreshes phonological codes, its disruption might be most evident for middle serial positions where neither primacy nor recency affords protection. Conversely, auditory interference that affects serial order through perceptual capture might produce a more uniform decrement across positions or might disproportionately affect the earliest items if the auditory trace disrupts initial encoding. Characterizing these serial position profiles is thus crucial for attributing observed performance decrements to specific cognitive mechanisms.

Another methodological consideration pertains to sample size and statistical power. Many past studies that have found robust articulatory suppression effects used modest sample sizes but large effects. Effects of auditory interference can be smaller and more variable, suggesting that adequately powered samples are necessary to detect them reliably. Therefore, any study aiming to compare articulatory and auditory contributions ideally should recruit a sample sufficient to detect medium-sized effects in within-subjects designs and to provide reasonably precise estimates of effect sizes.

Finally, contemporary theoretical discussions have emphasized that maintenance in working memory is not monolithic and may involve multiple, potentially redundant mechanisms. Models that allow for both articulatory and auditory traces, perhaps with flexible weighting depending on task demands, make distinct predictions from models that hold one mechanism to be dominant. Empirical tests that disambiguate these predictions are needed to refine our theoretical understanding and to inform computational and neurobiological implementations of verbal working memory.

The present study was designed to contribute to this debate by directly re-examining the articulatory component of rehearsal and testing for an additional auditory component under well-controlled conditions. Participants completed an immediate serial recall task under four within-subjects conditions: a silent control condition, an articulatory suppression condition, a passive auditory interference condition, and an auditory interference condition that additionally required detection of infrequent targets and a manual response to ensure attentional engagement. The articulatory suppression condition was implemented with a standardized overt articulatory rate to maximize disruption of subvocal rehearsal. The auditory interference conditions used a changing sequence of spoken syllables presented at a comfortable listening level; for the active auditory-attention condition, occasional target tones were embedded and participants responded with a keypress to ensure that attention was at least periodically engaged.

The study tested three primary hypotheses. First, consistent with the classic phonological loop account, articulatory suppression would significantly reduce verbal serial recall relative to the silent control. Second, if auditory input contributes independently to maintenance or organization of serial order, passive auditory interference would also reduce recall relative to control. Third, if attentional engagement amplifies the disruptive potential of auditory input or allows an auditory trace to be formed that competes with articulatory rehearsal, the auditory interference condition requiring active attention would produce greater disruption than the passive auditory condition.

In addition to these hypotheses, the study examined serial position effects across conditions to determine whether articulatory and auditory manipulations produce distinct profiles of impairment. The inclusion of a moderate sample size was intended to provide sufficient power to detect medium-sized within-subjects effects and to produce estimates that could inform future, larger-scale investigations. The selection of stimuli and task timings was motivated by prior work but chosen to strike a balance between ecological validity and experimental control.

The experiment reported here therefore aimed to provide a clear and direct test of articulatory and auditory components in verbal short-term maintenance within a single, well-controlled paradigm. By contrasting silent baseline, articulatory suppression, passive auditory interference, and active auditory attention interference, the study sought to isolate the contributions of subvocal rehearsal and auditory input to immediate serial recall. The results bear on theoretical debates about the locus of maintenance in verbal working memory and have implications for experimental designs used to probe this critical cognitive system.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures are described first. A total of 48 healthy adult volunteers participated in the study. Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and from the surrounding community through advertisements. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological disorder or hearing impairment. The sample included both undergraduate students and community members, aged between 18 and 35 years, with the majority being in the 18 to 25 age range. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with institutional review board guidelines. Participants received course credit or a small monetary stipend for their participation. Data collection proceeded until 48 usable datasets were obtained; participants whose data were not usable due to equipment failure or noncompliance with task instructions were replaced to reach this target sample size.

Materials and stimuli are detailed below. The memoranda consisted of sequences of seven monosyllabic English words selected to minimize semantic clustering and to balance phonological properties across trials. The words were common high-frequency items to avoid confounds related to unfamiliar vocabulary. Each word was recorded in a neutral female voice and normalized for amplitude; however, the words were presented visually on a computer screen to ensure precise timing and to limit auditory presentation of the memoranda. Presenting memoranda visually allows the retention interval to be filled with auditory interference without overlapping with the auditory presentation of the items. Visual presentation ensured that any auditory interference effects observed were not due to masking of the encoded items at presentation.

For the auditory interference conditions, a set of spoken syllables were recorded in the same voice and normalized to match the perceptual loudness used in pilot testing. The sequence of auditory syllables was a changing-state sequence, with rapid temporal variation and no semantic relation to the memoranda, thereby providing an interference stream likely to produce an irrelevant sound effect if the auditory stream impinged on maintenance. For the active auditory attention condition, infrequent target tones (a short beep distinct in pitch from the syllables) were embedded pseudo-randomly within the syllable sequence; targets occurred on approximately 20% of trials and could appear at any time during the retention interval. Participants were instructed to press the spacebar as quickly as possible when they heard a target tone, and performance on this secondary task was recorded to verify attentional engagement.

The articulatory suppression task required participants to repeat the syllable "la" aloud at approximately two syllables per second throughout the retention interval. Participants were instructed and trained to maintain a consistent rate and volume, and the experimenter monitored compliance. Audio was recorded during the experiment to permit offline verification of compliance; trials with clear noncompliance could be excluded in secondary analyses, although the primary analyses used the full dataset to preserve sample size and power. The rate of two syllables per second was chosen because it is commonly used in suppression paradigms and is sufficient to occupy articulatory processes thought to underlie subvocal rehearsal.

Apparatus and timing parameters are described next. The experiment was programmed and run on laboratory desktop computers using standard experiment presentation software. Visual stimuli were displayed on a single monitor, and auditory stimuli were delivered via circumaural headphones at a comfortable listening level. The trial timing was as follows: a fixation cross appeared for 500 ms, followed by the sequential presentation of seven words, each displayed for 500 ms with an interstimulus interval of 200 ms. Immediately after the final item, the retention interval of 3,000 ms began, during which one of the four interference conditions was in effect. After the retention interval, a recall prompt appeared indicating that participants should type the words in the order presented. Typed responses were recorded and later scored for serial order accuracy. A short intertrial interval of 1,000 ms followed each recall.

The within-subjects design is explained here. Each participant completed trials in all four interference conditions: silent control, articulatory suppression, passive auditory interference, and auditory interference with an additional attention requirement. Condition order was randomized across trials for each participant to minimize order effects. Participants completed a total of 48 trials, with 12 trials per interference condition. Trial order was randomized subject to the constraint that no more than three trials of the same condition occurred consecutively. Prior to the main experimental blocks, participants completed 8 practice trials that included examples of each condition to ensure understanding of the tasks and compliance with the articulatory suppression and auditory detection requirements.

Scoring and dependent measures are described here. The primary dependent measure was strict serial order accuracy, scored as the proportion of items recalled in the correct serial position for each trial. This conservative scoring method is standard in serial recall research because it captures both item memory and order memory. Secondary measures included item-correct irrespective of order and response latencies for the auditory detection task in the active auditory condition. The audio recordings of participants’ overt articulations during suppression were analyzed offline to compute mean syllable rate and to identify trials with noncompliance.

Statistical analysis procedures are described next. The primary analysis was a one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with interference condition (four levels: control, articulatory suppression, passive auditory, active auditory) as the within-subjects factor and mean proportion correct as the dependent variable. Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied where sphericity violations were detected. Planned pairwise comparisons between control and each interference condition were conducted using paired-samples t-tests with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta squared for ANOVAs and Cohen’s d for t-tests. To provide evidence for the absence of auditory interference effects where appropriate, Bayesian paired-samples analyses were also performed to estimate the relative support for the null hypothesis over the alternative; Bayes factors are reported when relevant.

Analyses of serial position effects were conducted using a 4 (condition) × 7 (serial position) repeated-measures ANOVA to test for condition-by-position interactions. When interactions were observed, follow-up analyses examined simple effects of condition at each serial position using paired t-tests with correction for multiple comparisons. Additional exploratory analyses correlated articulatory rate during suppression with recall performance in the suppression condition to assess whether residual rehearsal or suppression variability predicted memory outcomes. Data quality checks included inspection for outlier trials, aberrant response patterns, and verification of compliance with articulatory suppression and auditory detection tasks.

Finally, ethical and data handling procedures are noted. All data were de-identified and stored on secure laboratory servers in accordance with institutional policies. Participants were debriefed after completion of the experiment and given the opportunity to ask questions about the procedures. The experimental script and anonymized data files are available upon request from the corresponding author to facilitate transparency and replication.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary dependent variable, proportion correct in strict serial order, are provided first. Across the 48 participants, mean proportion correct in the control condition was 0.68 with a standard deviation of 0.12. In the articulatory suppression condition, the mean proportion correct was 0.51 with a standard deviation of 0.14. For the passive auditory interference condition, the mean proportion correct was 0.66 with a standard deviation of 0.13. In the active auditory condition requiring detection responses, the mean proportion correct was 0.64 with a standard deviation of 0.14. These descriptive values suggest a clear decrement in performance associated with articulatory suppression relative to the other three conditions, with relatively similar means across the control and the two auditory interference conditions.

A one-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to test for an overall effect of interference condition on proportion correct. The ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of condition, F(3, 141) = 38.72, p < .001, partial eta squared = .45. This large effect indicates that the type of interference present during the retention interval substantially influenced serial recall performance. Inspection of the means indicated that the articulatory suppression condition was primarily responsible for the overall effect.

Planned pairwise comparisons were performed to examine differences between the control condition and each interference condition. Compared to the control condition, the articulatory suppression condition showed a large and statistically significant reduction in recall, t(47) = 9.27, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.34. This result strongly supports the hypothesis that articulatory suppression disrupts the rehearsal process and reduces serial recall performance. In contrast, the passive auditory interference condition did not differ significantly from the control condition, t(47) = 1.14, p = .26, Cohen’s d = 0.16. The auditory interference condition that required active attention also did not differ significantly from control, t(47) = 1.78, p = .08, Cohen’s d = 0.26, although the mean was numerically lower than control.

To further probe the relative effects of the two auditory conditions, a paired t-test compared passive and active auditory interference performance. The difference between passive auditory (M = 0.66) and active auditory (M = 0.64) conditions was not statistically significant, t(47) = 0.93, p = .36, Cohen’s d = 0.13. This suggests that embedding an attentional detection requirement within the auditory stream did not produce a reliable additional decrement in recall under the current experimental parameters.

Given the non-significant findings for auditory interference, Bayesian paired-samples t-tests were conducted to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis. For the comparison of control versus passive auditory interference, the Bayes factor in favor of the null hypothesis over the alternative was BF01 = 4.2, providing moderate evidence for the null. For the comparison of control versus active auditory interference, the Bayes factor was BF01 = 2.1, indicating anecdotal to moderate evidence for the null. These Bayes factors suggest that, under the present design and sample size, the data favor the absence of a sizable auditory interference effect, particularly for the passive auditory condition.

An analysis of serial position curves was performed using a 4 (condition) × 7 (serial position) repeated-measures ANOVA. The analysis revealed significant main effects of condition, F(3, 141) = 38.72, p < .001, and serial position, F(6, 282) = 112.45, p < .001, as well as a significant condition-by-position interaction, F(18, 846) = 4.33, p < .001. The serial position main effect reflected the typical U-shaped curve with superior performance at early and late positions compared to middle positions. The interaction indicated that the pattern of serial position performance differed across interference conditions.

Follow-up simple effects analyses examined the effect of articulatory suppression at each serial position. Articulatory suppression produced significant reductions in recall relative to control at positions 2 through 6 (all ps < .001), with the largest absolute decrement occurring at positions 3 and 4. The suppression effect was smaller and non-significant at the final position (position 7), consistent with a recency advantage that is relatively robust to articulatory interference. In contrast, the passive and active auditory conditions did not significantly differ from control at any individual serial position after correcting for multiple comparisons (all corrected ps > .05), although there were small, non-significant numerical decrements at middle positions in the active auditory condition.

Additional analyses examined whether participants’ compliance with articulatory suppression influenced recall. Audio recordings were analyzed to compute each participant’s mean syllable rate during suppression, and trials with clear pauses exceeding 500 ms were flagged. The mean overt syllable rate across participants was 1.9 syllables per second, close to the instructed rate. A correlation between mean syllable rate and proportion correct in the suppression condition was small and non-significant, r = .08, p = .58, indicating that small variations in rate did not predict performance. Trials or participants with gross noncompliance (n = 2 participants who produced extended silence on multiple trials) were inspected; excluding these participants from the analysis did not meaningfully alter the main suppression effect, which remained large and significant.

Performance on the auditory detection task in the active auditory condition confirmed that participants were attending to the auditory stream at least intermittently. Mean detection accuracy for the embedded target tones was 89% with a standard deviation of 8%, and mean reaction time to detected tones was 412 ms (SD = 74 ms). There was no reliable correlation between detection accuracy or reaction time and recall performance in the active auditory condition (all ps > .10), suggesting that variability in attentional engagement with the auditory stream did not systematically predict memory outcomes in the present dataset.

Secondary analyses considered item-only correctness irrespective of order. When scoring was relaxed to count items recalled regardless of position, the articulatory suppression condition still produced a significant reduction relative to control, although the effect size was somewhat reduced compared to strict order scoring. This finding indicates that suppression affects both item retention and the maintenance of serial order, with a stronger impact on the latter as expected in serial recall paradigms.

To examine potential practice or fatigue effects, performance was plotted across trial blocks for each condition. There was a modest upward trend in performance across the first two blocks followed by a slight decline in later blocks, but no consistent condition-by-block interactions emerged. This suggests that practice or fatigue did not differentially affect the interference conditions in a way that would confound the main comparisons.

Finally, exploratory comparisons examined whether the magnitude of the articulatory suppression effect correlated with individual differences in baseline control performance. The reduction in proportion correct from control to suppression was negatively correlated with control performance (r = -.42, p = .003), indicating that participants with higher baseline performance tended to show larger absolute decrements under suppression. This pattern is consistent with a general ceiling effect logic in which better-performing individuals have more to lose when rehearsal is disrupted.

In summary, the key result is a robust and large inhibitory effect of articulatory suppression on immediate serial recall, accompanied by a lack of reliable detrimental effects of passive auditory interference and only weak, non-significant effects of auditory interference that required attentional detection. Serial position analyses indicate that suppression disproportionately affected middle serial positions, whereas auditory manipulations did not produce a distinguishable pattern of impairment. Compliance checks confirmed adequate adherence to suppression and engagement with the auditory detection task, and Bayesian analyses supported the absence of a sizable passive auditory interference effect.

Discussion

The present study directly compared the effects of articulatory suppression and auditory interference on immediate serial verbal recall within a single, controlled experimental paradigm. The data provide clear evidence that articulatory suppression substantially impairs serial recall performance, supporting the longstanding view that articulatory rehearsal plays a critical role in the maintenance of phonological representations. In contrast, under the present experimental conditions, auditory interference—even when it required participants to detect and respond to targets—did not produce reliable deleterious effects on recall. These outcomes have several theoretical and methodological implications for models of verbal working memory and for the design of experiments intended to probe rehearsal and auditory contributions to maintenance.

First, the robust effect of articulatory suppression observed here aligns with the phonological loop model’s claim that subvocal articulation supports the active maintenance of phonological items. The large effect size and the pattern of serial position decrements concentrated in middle positions mirror classical findings and reinforce the conclusion that articulatory processes are crucial for sustaining order information over short retention intervals. The suppression manipulation was implemented with an overt, monitored articulation rate and demonstrated good participant compliance; thus, the suppression effect is unlikely to be due to procedural laxity. Additionally, the persistence of the suppression effect when relaxing scoring criteria to count items irrespective of order suggests that articulatory rehearsal contributes both to the retention of items and to the preservation of their sequential order.

Second, the absence of a reliable passive auditory interference effect, as assessed via both classical null hypothesis testing and Bayesian analysis, raises questions about the generality of auditory disruption of serial order. The Bayes factor provided moderate support for the null in the passive auditory case, suggesting that, under our specific stimulus and timing parameters, irrelevant spoken syllables did not substantially interfere with rehearsal. Several possible explanations warrant consideration. One possibility is that the visual presentation of memoranda privileged phonological encoding that was already robust, making the auditory stream less likely to displace or interfere with the phonological representations held via subvocal rehearsal. Another possibility is that the particular auditory sequence used, although changing in content, lacked sufficient acoustic novelty or complexity to elicit the kind of perceptual capture implicated in prior demonstrations of the irrelevant sound effect. It is also possible that the relatively brief retention interval (3,000 ms) limited the opportunity for the auditory stream to exert an effect; longer retention intervals might amplify auditory interference.

Third, the active auditory condition, in which participants monitored for infrequent target tones embedded in the syllable stream, produced a small, non-significant decrease in recall compared to control. Reaction time and detection accuracy indicated that participants did engage with the auditory stream, at least intermittently, yet this engagement did not translate into a measurable impairment of serial recall in the present sample. This null finding suggests that attentional engagement with an interfering auditory stream does not necessarily produce interference with articulatory rehearsal or with the phonological store. One interpretation is that attentional resources required for occasional tone detection are intermittent and leave sufficient capacity for subvocal rehearsal to continue; another is that attentional engagement with simple auditory detection tasks does not overlap sufficiently with the cognitive processes supporting serial order retention to cause interference. It is also possible that the auditory detection task parameters—target frequency, predictability, and motor response requirements—were not sufficiently demanding to deplete the resources necessary for rehearsal.

These findings invite re-evaluation of the manner in which auditory interference is conceptualized in relation to articulatory rehearsal. The classic account that auditory sequences disrupt serial order through obligatory perceptual processing that is independent of attention has been influential, but the present data imply that such automatic auditory disruption is not immutable across tasks and stimulus configurations. The potency of auditory interference may depend critically on factors such as the acoustic properties of the interfering stream (e.g., pitch variation, spectral complexity), the temporal overlap with encoding, the modality of the memoranda, and the relative salience of the auditory input. Additionally, individual differences in auditory filtering and attentional control could moderate susceptibility to irrelevant sounds, potentially producing heterogeneity in effects that could attenuate group-level findings.

Another important consideration concerns statistical power and effect size expectations. Although the sample size of 48 participants is adequate to detect medium to large within-subjects effects, smaller auditory interference effects might have gone undetected. The Bayesian analyses provided some support for the absence of a moderate passive auditory effect, but the evidence for the active auditory condition was more equivocal. Future studies aiming to definitively test for smaller auditory contributions should adopt larger sample sizes and possibly leverage designs that increase sensitivity, such as by increasing trial counts per condition or optimizing the interfering auditory stream to maximize perceptual disruption.

Methodological factors specific to the present experiment also merit discussion. The choice to present memoranda visually was made to permit the clean introduction of auditory interference during retention, but this decision may have inadvertently emphasized articulatory encoding strategies. Visual presentation often leads participants to generate subvocal articulations to convert the visual input into a phonological code; if participants strongly relied on this articulatory route, auditory interference would need to be particularly potent to displace it. Future work could contrast visually and auditorily presented memoranda to assess whether the modality of encoding interacts with susceptibility to auditory interference.

The nature of the auditory interference itself is another variable worth exploring. Our syllable stream was designed to be changing in content, but changing-state properties can be operationalized in multiple ways. For example, sequences with rapid pitch modulations or with semantically salient words have been shown to produce larger irrelevant sound effects in some studies. Manipulating the rate, acoustic complexity, and semantic relevance of the interfering stream could reveal boundary conditions under which auditory input intrudes upon rehearsal. Similarly, the active auditory task could be modified to require continuous monitoring or to include more frequent or unpredictable targets, thereby placing a sustained attentional burden on participants.

The observed pattern of serial position effects provides further insight into mechanism. Articulatory suppression produced the largest decrements in middle positions, consistent with the hypothesis that rehearsal serves to refresh ongoing representations that are otherwise susceptible to decay and interference in the mid-list range. The recency advantage at the final position was relatively preserved, which suggests that short-term retention of the most recent items relies on residual perceptual traces or on temporal distinctiveness that is less dependent on ongoing rehearsal. Because auditory manipulations did not alter this profile, it is plausible that auditory input in the present design did not disrupt the serial order coding processes targeted by articulatory suppression.

The findings also have implications for broader theoretical accounts of working memory. Models that posit a single dominant rehearsal mechanism might interpret the present pattern as confirmation that articulatory processes are primary for maintaining serial order under typical task demands. Meanwhile, models that allow for multiple maintenance mechanisms, including sensory or auditory traces, must accommodate the observation that auditory contributions are not always impactful. One parsimonious reconciliation is to posit a flexible maintenance system in which articulatory rehearsal is the preferred and most effective strategy for serial order retention, while auditory traces can provide auxiliary support under specific circumstances, such as when the memoranda are auditorily presented, when the auditory stream is highly salient, or when articulatory mechanisms are unavailable.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the sample was composed primarily of young adults from a university context, which limits generalizability to other age groups and to clinical populations. Second, while compliance with suppression and auditory detection was monitored, the reliance on overt articulation and behavioral responses may not fully capture covert rehearsal strategies that participants might attempt to use. Third, the particular auditory stimuli and retention interval chosen here represent one specific instantiation of interference, and different parameter choices could yield different outcomes. Finally, although Bayesian analyses provided useful information about evidence for the null in some comparisons, they cannot definitively demonstrate the absence of an effect, especially for small effect sizes.

The present results suggest several fruitful directions for future research. First, systematic parametric manipulation of auditory stream properties—including changing-rate, pitch variability, semantic content, and loudness—would help delineate which acoustic features are most disruptive to serial order maintenance. Second, varying retention interval durations and presentation modalities (visual versus auditory) could clarify how encoding modality interacts with susceptibility to auditory interference. Third, employing continuous attention-demanding auditory tasks, such as a rapid serial auditory detection or a discrimination task, might better test whether sustained attentional engagement compromises rehearsal. Fourth, neuroimaging or electrophysiological methods could be used concurrently to probe whether auditory streams produce neural signatures of interference even when behavioral effects are absent, thereby revealing subthreshold processing that might be undetectable in overt recall performance.

Another promising extension would involve testing populations with known differences in auditory processing or attentional control, such as older adults or individuals with attention-deficit profiles. These groups might show enhanced sensitivity to auditory distraction or differential reliance on articulatory rehearsal, thereby illuminating individual-difference moderators. Additionally, computational modeling could be leveraged to simulate how a dual-maintenance system with weighted contributions from articulatory and auditory traces would behave under various interference regimes; such models could generate quantitative predictions for empirical testing.

In practical terms, the findings reinforce the importance of articulatory rehearsal for tasks requiring ordered recall and suggest that interventions aimed at supporting rehearsal (for example, through pacing strategies or rehearsal training) may be particularly valuable in educational and clinical contexts. At the same time, the lack of a robust auditory interference effect in this study cautions against assuming that background speech will universally impair verbal short-term memory; the conditions under which auditory distraction is disruptive are likely nuanced and contingent on both stimulus and task features.

In conclusion, the current study provides strong evidence for the centrality of articulatory rehearsal in immediate serial recall, while failing to find reliable support for an independent auditory contribution under the tested conditions. The results underscore the need for a more nuanced specification of auditory interference effects and for further work that systematically varies the properties of both interfering streams and rehearsal demands. By continuing to integrate careful experimental manipulations with theoretically motivated models, the field can better resolve how articulatory and auditory processes jointly support verbal working memory.
