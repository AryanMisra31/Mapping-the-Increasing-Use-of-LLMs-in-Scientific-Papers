Introduction

Procrastination is a behavior commonly observed among students and is widely believed to be detrimental to academic performance. People in many cultures regularly report postponing study-related tasks until later, sometimes until the last minute. The intuitive link between delaying study activities and poor grades has led educators and laypeople alike to encourage time management as a remedy for low performance. For decades, instructors and counselors have emphasized punctuality in assignments and steady study habits as bulwarks against failure. At the same time, researchers have documented that the phenomenon of procrastination is heterogeneous in its causes and manifestations. Some students appear to delay work because of poor planning or avoidance of aversive tasks, while others may delay as a strategic choice to increase arousal or to prioritize competing responsibilities. Psychological theories of self-regulation have emphasized the role of executive control, impulse management, and the regulation of affect in understanding procrastination. Temporal motivation models have linked procrastination to the interaction of task value, expectancy, time discounting, and impulsiveness. Personality traits, including conscientiousness and impulsivity, have also been implicated in the propensity to procrastinate. Measurement issues further complicate the picture: procrastination has been assessed with trait-oriented self-report scales, situational diaries, behavioral measures of task initiation, and retrospective reports of deadline misses. These differences in operationalization can produce variable findings when associations with academic outcomes are examined. Academic performance itself is also measured in multiple ways. Many studies use grade point average (GPA) as a broad indicator of achievement, while others focus on exam scores, course grades in specific subjects, standardized test outcomes, or instructor evaluations. The nature of the outcome measure can influence whether a relationship with procrastination is detected. A number of empirical investigations have reported a negative association between procrastination and performance, consistent with the dominant lay view. Some meta-analytic summaries have indicated that the average correlation between self-reported procrastination and grades is small but reliably negative. This has been interpreted as evidence that, on average, students who report greater procrastination tend to have somewhat lower grades. Nevertheless, this pattern is far from universal. Other studies have failed to find a reliable link, particularly when samples are drawn from competitive academic environments or when performance is measured in specific course contexts. There are several plausible reasons for inconsistent findings. One reason is heterogeneity in sample composition. Student populations differ in age, field of study, and cultural background, and these differences can modify the relationship between behaviors and outcomes. Another reason is task specificity. Procrastination might have a stronger impact on performance in courses that require consistent engagement throughout a term (for example, courses with continual assessment) than in courses in which final exams dominate the grade. The temporal structure of academic tasks could moderate whether delaying work carries a cost. Compensatory behaviors also matter. Some students who habitually procrastinate develop strategies to work efficiently under pressure, such as targeted cramming or delegation of certain tasks. When such strategies are effective, procrastination might not translate into poorer grades. In addition, the motivational profile of students could alter the link between procrastination and performance. Motivation encompasses a wide array of constructs, including intrinsic interest, attainment motives, mastery goals, extrinsic incentives, and extrinsic pressures. High levels of motivation, of whatever source, may enable students to make up for delays in task initiation by engaging intensely in the required work before deadlines. Conversely, low motivation could both increase procrastinatory behavior and reduce the quality of any compensatory effort. Cultural norms around education and parental expectations can also shape both motivation and study practices. In some cultural contexts, high parental expectations and strong institutional norms about achievement are associated with rigorous study habits among students. Observers sometimes point to patterns in which certain cultural groups perform well academically despite high reported rates of stress or heavy workloads; commentators sometimes label such patterns as a “success phenomenon.” Such descriptions, however, risk oversimplifying complex socio-cultural and structural influences, and they require careful empirical examination rather than casual generalization. Given this background, there is a conceptual need to disentangle the relative contributions of procrastination and motivation to academic performance. It is possible that procrastination per se is a weak predictor of grades when motivation is accounted for, or that the effect of procrastination is conditional on motivational states, personality traits, or task characteristics. Indeed, a model in which motivation mediates or moderates the association between procrastination and performance is theoretically plausible. Another issue that bears on empirical findings is measurement reliability. If instruments used to quantify procrastination or motivation have low reliability in a given sample, correlations with performance will be attenuated. Likewise, reports of academic performance that are imprecise or biased (for example, self-reported GPA prone to social desirability) may obscure real relationships. Methodological rigor in sampling, measurement, and analysis is therefore necessary to clarify the empirical picture. In the present work, we sought to revisit the association between self-reported procrastination and academic performance in a sample of undergraduate students. We also included a measure of academic motivation to examine its independent association with performance and its relationship to procrastination. Our study is correlational and cross-sectional, and it relies on self-report measures collected in an online format. Based on the extant literature and the conventional view, one might predict a modest negative correlation between procrastination and grades. However, given the mixed empirical record and theoretical reasons for expecting weak or context-dependent effects, we considered the direction and magnitude of this association to be an open empirical question. We also hypothesized that motivation would show a positive association with academic performance and that motivation might account for more variance in grades than procrastination. Finally, we aimed to explore whether the relationship between procrastination and performance might be better understood when motivation is statistically controlled. By clarifying these associations in our sample, we aimed to contribute to an ongoing debate about the relative importance of procrastination and motivational factors in academic outcomes and to provide guidance for future work that seeks to inform educational practice.

Methods

Participants were undergraduate students enrolled at a mid-sized university who participated for course credit or a modest monetary incentive. The initial sample comprised 170 respondents, of whom 8 were excluded for failing embedded attention checks or for providing incomplete data on the primary measures. The final analytic sample therefore included 162 participants. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 26 years, with a mean age of 20.3 years. The sample included 98 women, 62 men, and 2 participants who indicated a non-binary gender identity. Participants represented a range of academic majors, with the largest groups enrolled in social sciences, business, and natural sciences. Recruitment was carried out via university research participation systems and by flyers distributed on campus. All procedures were approved by the institution’s human research ethics committee, and participants provided informed consent before beginning the survey. The survey was administered online through a secure survey platform. After providing consent, participants completed demographic questions followed by the measures described below. Measures were presented in randomized order for subsamples to reduce order effects. Participants were informed that their responses were confidential and that they could discontinue at any time without penalty. Academic performance was operationalized as cumulative grade point average (GPA) reported by each participant on a 4.0 scale. Participants were asked to report the current cumulative GPA from their university records rather than an estimate; participants who indicated uncertainty about their GPA were asked to consult their academic record before responding. For the present article, GPA was retained as a continuous variable. Because self-reported GPA can be subject to some bias, we included a question asking participants whether they were confident about the accuracy of their reported GPA; only two participants indicated low confidence, and their data were retained after sensitivity checks. Procrastination was measured using a standardized self-report scale designed to assess general procrastinatory tendencies. The measure consisted of 16 items that probed behaviors such as delaying initiation of academic tasks, missing self-imposed deadlines, and putting off studying until the final hours before assessments. Participants rated items on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Item scores were summed to produce a total procrastination score, with higher scores indicating greater procrastination. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in our sample, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86. Academic motivation was assessed using a multi-dimensional measure that captures both intrinsic and extrinsic motivational orientations toward study. The motivation instrument comprised 18 items, with subscales tapping intrinsic interest, mastery orientation, and extrinsic reward orientation. Items were rated on a five-point scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much), and a total motivation composite was computed by averaging the item scores such that higher values reflect greater overall academic motivation. The motivation scale showed strong internal consistency in our sample, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89. In addition to the primary measures, the survey included brief assessments of conscientiousness and perceived stress to serve as covariates in supplementary analyses. Conscientiousness was measured with four items drawn from a short personality inventory, and perceived stress was measured with a brief four-item checklist reflecting recent stressors. Both scales showed acceptable internal consistency (alphas > 0.70). Before analysis, data were screened for missing values, outliers, and normality. Missing data were minimal; no participant had missing values on the primary variables of interest. Univariate outliers were examined via z-scores, and no scores exceeded |3.5| on the primary measures; therefore, no cases were excluded on the basis of extreme values. The distribution of procrastination scores was slightly positively skewed but within acceptable limits; GPA showed minor left skew consistent with many undergraduate samples, with most students clustered at moderate to high GPAs. Pearson correlation was selected as the primary analytic technique to examine bivariate associations among procrastination, motivation, and GPA. Where variables deviated from normality, Spearman rank correlations were computed as sensitivity checks. Multiple regression analyses were then conducted to evaluate the relative predictive contributions of procrastination and motivation to GPA. In the regression models, we entered procrastination and motivation scores simultaneously in the first model, and then added covariates (age, gender, conscientiousness, perceived stress) in a subsequent model to examine robustness. Standardized beta coefficients, R-squared values, and change-in-R-squared were reported. We also tested for potential moderation by gender by including interaction terms between gender and the primary predictors; interactions were probed when p-values fell below the conventional threshold. Finally, given the theoretical plausibility that motivation might mediate any association between procrastination and GPA, we explored a mediation model using bootstrapped indirect-effect estimation. The mediation analysis used 5,000 bootstrap samples and produced bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals for the indirect effect. For all inferential tests, an alpha level of .05 (two-tailed) was adopted. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional benchmarks for small, medium, and large effects. All analyses were performed with a standard statistical software package and checked for reproducibility by an independent analyst within the research team.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The mean GPA in the sample was 3.24 on the 4.0 scale, with a standard deviation of 0.45. The mean procrastination score was 36.8 on a possible range from 16 to 64, and the standard deviation for procrastination was 8.9. The average motivation composite score was 3.67 on a 1-to-5 scale, with a standard deviation of 0.71. These descriptive values indicate a broadly typical undergraduate sample in which most students reported moderate to high motivation and a range of procrastination tendencies. Bivariate correlations among GPA, procrastination, and motivation are shown in the correlation matrix. The correlation between procrastination and GPA was r = -0.019, two-tailed p = .81. This indicates a near-zero and non-significant relationship in this sample. Sensitivity checks using Spearman rank correlation yielded a similar result, rho = -0.021, p = .79, confirming that the near-zero association was robust to distributional assumptions. In contrast, motivation and GPA showed a moderate positive correlation, r = 0.43, p < .001, suggesting that students with higher academic motivation tended to have higher GPAs in this sample. The correlation between procrastination and motivation was moderately negative, r = -0.45, p < .001, indicating that more procrastination was associated with lower reported motivation. These bivariate results illustrate a pattern in which procrastination per se did not relate to GPA, whereas motivation did. To examine the relative contributions of procrastination and motivation to GPA, we conducted multiple regression analyses. In the first model, both procrastination and motivation were entered as simultaneous predictors of GPA. The overall model accounted for 18% of the variance in GPA (R^2 = .18), F(2,159) = 17.37, p < .001. Within this model, motivation emerged as a significant positive predictor of GPA (standardized beta = .41, t = 6.05, p < .001). Procrastination, by contrast, did not predict GPA when entered alongside motivation (standardized beta = -0.02, t = -0.24, p = .81). These results indicate that motivation explains a meaningful portion of variance in grades while procrastination does not provide unique predictive power above and beyond motivation in this sample. We expanded the regression model to include covariates to test the robustness of these findings. Age and gender were entered in the second step, followed by conscientiousness and perceived stress in the third step. The inclusion of these covariates increased the model R^2 modestly to .22, and the change in R^2 associated with covariates was significant, ΔR^2 = .04, p = .02. In this full model, motivation remained a significant predictor of GPA (standardized beta = .39, t = 5.21, p < .001). Conscientiousness also showed a small but significant positive association with GPA (standardized beta = .12, t = 2.05, p = .042). Procrastination remained non-significant (standardized beta = -0.01, t = -0.18, p = .86). Gender and age were not significant predictors in the adjusted model. We also tested whether the relationship between procrastination and GPA might be nonlinear by including a quadratic term for procrastination in a supplementary model. The quadratic term was not significant (p = .74), and the addition of the quadratic term did not improve model fit (ΔR^2 < .01). We examined whether the association between procrastination and GPA differed across gender by testing an interaction between procrastination and gender. The interaction term did not reach significance (p = .67), indicating no evidence that the relationship between procrastination and GPA varied by gender in our sample. Given the theoretical possibility of mediation, we explored whether motivation mediated any effect of procrastination on GPA. Because the total association between procrastination and GPA was effectively zero, mediation was unlikely; nevertheless, we estimated an indirect effect using bootstrapping with 5,000 replications. The indirect effect of procrastination on GPA via motivation was estimated at -0.06 (95% bias-corrected CI: -0.16 to 0.03). The confidence interval included zero, indicating that the indirect effect was not statistically significant. This result aligns with the lack of a total effect and suggests that, in this sample, motivation does not statistically mediate a relationship that was not present. We also categorized participants into tertiles of procrastination (low, medium, high) to examine group differences in GPA. An ANOVA revealed no significant differences in GPA across these groups, F(2,159) = 0.12, p = .88. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons showed trivial mean differences with small effect sizes (Cohen’s d < 0.05). In contrast, grouping participants by tertiles of motivation produced significant differences in GPA, F(2,159) = 22.5, p < .001, with the high motivation group having substantially higher mean GPA than the low motivation group (d = 0.95). We inspected the distributions and scatterplots to ensure that the null bivariate association between procrastination and GPA was not driven by a few influential cases. Influence diagnostics, including Cook’s distance and leverage statistics, did not identify any cases exerting undue influence on the regression estimates. As a further robustness check, we conducted the primary analyses restricting the sample to those who reported high confidence in their GPA report (n = 160); the pattern of results remained unchanged. Finally, we explored potential domain specificity by testing correlations between procrastination and GPA within subsamples defined by broad disciplinary grouping (social sciences, natural sciences, professional programs). None of these subgroup correlations were significant, and the direction of associations varied slightly across groups without any consistent pattern. Overall, the results indicate a near-zero relationship between self-reported procrastination and academic performance in this sample, whereas academic motivation showed a moderate and systematic positive association with GPA. Procrastination was moderately inversely correlated with motivation, but it did not exert an independent effect on grades when motivation and other covariates were taken into account.

Discussion

The present study examined the associations among self-reported procrastination, academic motivation, and undergraduate GPA in a cross-sectional sample of university students. Our principal finding is that procrastination, as measured by a standard self-report scale, was essentially unrelated to cumulative GPA in this sample. The bivariate correlation between procrastination and GPA was near zero (r = -0.019) and far from statistical significance. This null finding was robust across analytic approaches, including nonparametric sensitivity checks, regression models with covariate adjustment, and subgroup analyses. In contrast, academic motivation exhibited a moderate and statistically significant positive correlation with GPA (r = 0.43). When both procrastination and motivation were entered together in regression analyses, motivation emerged as a meaningful predictor of academic performance, while procrastination did not contribute unique variance. These results contribute to a nuanced understanding of the procrastination-performance relationship and suggest that the role of motivation deserves particular attention. At first glance, our null finding regarding procrastination may appear to conflict with common assumptions and some empirical reports linking procrastination to poorer academic outcomes. However, it is important to situate our results within the broader pattern of mixed findings in the literature. Previous work has reported small negative correlations on average, but there is notable heterogeneity across studies with respect to sample characteristics, measurement approaches, and task demands. Our findings underscore that a simple, direct relationship between self-reported procrastination and GPA cannot be assumed universally. One plausible interpretation of our results is that motivation serves as a more proximal determinant of academic performance than procrastination. In our data, procrastination and motivation were moderately inversely correlated, indicating that students who report more procrastination tend to report lower motivation. However, the absence of a direct association between procrastination and GPA suggests that procrastination may be associated with grades primarily insofar as it co-occurs with low motivation. When motivation is taken into account, procrastination itself may not exert an additional influence on performance. This pattern aligns with theoretical perspectives that emphasize the central role of goal-directed motivation and task value in academic achievement. Students with higher intrinsic interest and stronger mastery goals may allocate effort more effectively and derive greater benefit from study time, leading to higher grades regardless of occasional delays in task initiation. Another interpretation is that certain forms of procrastination are relatively benign or even functional in some contexts. For some students, postponing work until nearer a deadline may coincide with increased task focus and efficient effort allocation. This phenomenon, often labeled “active” or “strategic” procrastination, reflects a behavioral strategy in which delay is combined with effective, concentrated work that preserves performance. Our measures in this study captured general procrastinatory tendencies rather than distinguishing between passive, avoidant procrastination and more active, strategic forms. If many students who endorse procrastinatory items are engaging in compensatory strategies that preserve grade outcomes, the net association with GPA may be negligible. The structure of course assessment and instructional practices may also moderate the impact of procrastination. In courses where grades are heavily determined by end-of-term examinations, last-minute study may compensate for delays in earlier study. By contrast, courses with continuous assessment, frequent low-stakes tasks, or participation grades may penalize procrastination more directly. Our study used cumulative GPA as a global outcome; this aggregation may conceal task- and course-level variations in the consequences of procrastination. Another methodological point concerns measurement. The self-report measures used here are subject to biases and may not capture the temporal dynamics of procrastination as it unfolds day by day. Ecological momentary assessment or time-use diaries could reveal episodic patterns of delay that are not reflected in trait-level scales. Moreover, self-reported GPA, while generally correlated with objective records, may contain reporting error; however, sensitivity analyses restricting the sample to participants confident in their GPA did not alter the substantive findings. Importantly, the cross-sectional and correlational design of our study precludes causal conclusions. Although motivation correlates with GPA, we cannot determine directionality. It is plausible that higher motivation leads students to study more and perform better. Conversely, students who experience academic success may develop greater motivation due to positive feedback and self-efficacy. Longitudinal or experimental designs are necessary to elucidate causal pathways. Our findings also have potential implications for interventions. Many academic-support programs focus heavily on reducing procrastination through training in time management and planning. While such interventions can be beneficial for some students, our results suggest that enhancing motivation may be a more effective lever for improving grades at the population level. Interventions that increase intrinsic interest, clarify attainment values, or support mastery goals—through course design, feedback, or advising—could plausibly impact performance more strongly than interventions that exclusively target task initiation. That said, a multifaceted approach that fosters motivation while offering practical strategies to manage deadlines and workload may be optimal. We must also address cultural and contextual considerations with caution. The original motivations for this study included observations that motivation sometimes seems to explain patterns of high academic performance in groups or settings characterized by strong educational norms. Although some commentators use shorthand labels to describe such patterns, it is essential to avoid simplistic cultural generalizations. Our data do not speak directly to cross-cultural differences, and we did not sample across a broad range of national or cultural contexts. What can be said is that cultural and familial expectations, institutional norms, and structural factors such as access to resources likely shape both motivation and study practices. Future comparative research that includes diverse cultural samples and objective performance measures could help clarify how societal-level factors interact with individual motivation and procrastination to influence achievement. Several limitations of the present study warrant attention. First, the reliance on self-report measures for both predictors and outcomes introduces the possibility of shared-method variance and reporting biases. Although our primary pattern of results is not consistent with an inflationary effect of common method variance—because procrastination and motivation differed in their associations with GPA—objective measures would strengthen future work. Second, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, as noted. Third, the sample comprised undergraduates from a single university, which may restrict generalizability to other educational levels or institutional contexts. Fourth, our procrastination measure did not discriminate among subtypes of procrastination (e.g., decisional, avoidant, or arousal-based delay), and different subtypes may have distinct relationships to performance. Fifth, we did not directly measure task-specific behaviors such as study time, scheduling practices, or use of study strategies, which likely mediate the translation of motivational states into performance. Despite these limitations, the study contributes to the literature by highlighting the relatively weak direct association between general procrastination tendencies and cumulative academic performance in a typical undergraduate sample, alongside a robust association between motivation and grades. These findings point to several productive avenues for future research. First, longitudinal designs that measure motivation, procrastination, and performance repeatedly across terms could disentangle temporal precedence and identify trajectories of change. Second, studies that incorporate objective behavioral data—such as learning management system logs, time-stamped study activity, or ecological momentary assessments—could link real-time procrastinatory episodes to short-term performance outcomes. Third, experimental manipulations that enhance motivation (for example, through interventions that increase perceived task value or autonomy support) could test causal effects on study behaviors and grades. Fourth, finer-grained measurement that distinguishes procrastination subtypes and their situational triggers would help clarify which forms of delay are most deleterious. Fifth, cross-cultural and multi-institutional studies are needed to examine how societal and institutional contexts condition the effects of procrastination and motivation. Finally, intervention research that combines motivational enhancement with time-management training would test whether synergistic approaches produce greater improvements in performance than single-focus programs. In conclusion, the current study suggests that self-reported procrastination may not be a reliable predictor of cumulative academic performance in all contexts. Instead, academic motivation appears to be a more potent correlate of GPA in our sample. These results invite a shift from a narrow focus on procrastination reduction toward broader strategies that cultivate motivation and support effective study practices. Educators and researchers should be attentive to the complexity of procrastination and to the multiple pathways through which motivation and behavior jointly influence learning outcomes.
