Introduction

Human behavior is profoundly shaped by the social context in which choices are made. People often look to the actions of others to guide their own decisions when uncertainty is present. This reliance on others can manifest in conformity, a set of processes in which individuals adjust their beliefs or actions to align with perceived group norms. Conformity arises in multiple forms, including compliance with explicit requests and accommodation to implicit social cues. Classic demonstrations have shown that individuals will alter their responses to match the majority even when the majority is clearly wrong. Conformity is not a unitary phenomenon and is typically broken down into mechanisms such as normative influence and informational influence. Normative influence relates to the desire to be liked and accepted by others. Informational influence relates to the desire to be correct and to use others as information about the right course of action. Both mechanisms can operate simultaneously in many real-world situations. One context in which conformity and social proof are frequently observable is in the endorsement of public petitions and causes. When people encounter a petition, the number of existing signatures can act as a social cue. A large number of signatures communicates that many others have deemed the issue worthy of support, whereas a small number suggests the issue has attracted little public backing. The phenomenon in which people follow the behavior of others because that behavior is seen as evidence of the correct action is commonly referred to as social proof. Social proof has been shown to be a powerful driver of diverse behaviors, from consumer choices to civic engagement. Online platforms and digital petitions make social proof cues highly visible and easy to manipulate, which raises both theoretical and practical questions about the drivers of petition signing. Despite the obvious visibility of signature counts, the relative influence of signature count versus the perceived importance or relevance of a petition is not fully established. It is reasonable to assume that people will be more likely to sign a petition that aligns with their values or that addresses a topic they consider important. At the same time, unobtrusive cues about group behavior might override individual evaluation of importance in many contexts. The interplay between perceived importance and visible group support is theoretically interesting because it pits private judgment against social information. If signature count predominates, then social proof could subvert independently held judgments about importance. If importance predominates, then people would be expected to prioritize issue content over cues about others’ behavior. Empirical investigation of this trade-off is relevant to understanding conformity dynamics in contemporary civic behavior. Prior literature suggests that social cues often bias people's behavior even on matters of personal value. Research in social influence has shown that the presence of a majority can sway votes, judgments, and reported attitudes. Other work has shown that when group consensus is visible, individuals are more likely to adopt the majority's position even when they privately disagree. Experiments in digital environments have demonstrated that indicators of popularity, such as view counts and likes, systematically bias subsequent user engagement. However, many existing investigations focus on a single topic or do not manipulate the perceived importance of the item under consideration. There is a need for research that manipulates both the importance of an item and the visible size of prior support to determine their relative contributions to conformity. The current study addresses this gap by experimentally crossing a simple importance manipulation with a clear difference in visible signature counts. The design asks whether participants will favor a petition with many visible prior signatures even when the petition is on an issue labeled as low importance, and conversely whether they will favor a petition labeled as high importance even when it has few prior signatures. This manipulation allows a direct test of the hypothesis that group size, as indicated by numbers of signatures, exerts a strong influence on signing behavior independent of the nominal importance of the issue. The study uses a binary choice measure that reflects a common real-world decision: which of two petitions to sign when presented concurrently. This experimental approach approximates a realistic choice situation where people must decide whether to add their signature to competing petitions. The observed choice provides a behavioral index of conformity: choosing the petition with the larger prior signature count is considered evidence of following a majority cue. Measuring behavior rather than merely attitudes offers stronger evidence of social influence in action. We conducted an opportunistic study with a modest sample size drawn from a community near where the experiment was run. The study used a simple between-subjects manipulation whereby, in one condition, the high-importance petition displayed a high count of prior signatures and the low-importance petition displayed a low count. In the other condition this mapping was reversed so that the high-importance petition displayed a low count and the low-importance petition displayed a high count. This counterbalanced approach allows assessment of the independent effect of visible signature count while controlling for any consistent bias toward the particular topics used. The primary hypothesis was that participants would be more likely to sign the petition that displayed a higher number of prior signatures regardless of the nominal importance label attached to that petition. A secondary hypothesis was that participants would show some preference for petitions labeled as high importance when signature counts were equal or when signature cues were weak. The design intentionally made the signature disparity salient in one petition (a large number) and minimal in the other (a very small number). The signature counts used were chosen to be plausibly different in magnitude but both realistic for small online petitions. The key dependent variable was a forced binary response indicating which of the two petitions the participant chose to sign. This dependent variable is straightforward to interpret and aligns with the behavioral outcomes of interest in conformity and social influence research. Statistical tests were preplanned to compare the observed frequency of choosing the petition with many prior signatures against chance, to assess whether the label of importance affected choice frequency, and to examine the pattern of choices within each experimental condition. The study therefore provides a tight test of the prediction that visible group size cues override nominal importance labels in shaping signature behavior. Beyond the specific hypotheses, the study speaks to broader questions about the mechanisms of social proof. If signature count drives behavior irrespective of perceived importance labels, then the mechanism likely involves an informational shortcut by which people infer object value from prior behavior. If instead importance labels drive behavior irrespective of signature count, then the mechanism likely involves evaluation of content and relevance. The results can inform theoretical accounts that seek to explain when social information will be used as a heuristic and when people will rely on their own assessments. In applied terms, the findings have implications for understanding how online campaigns and social movements can shape participation. If large prior support is sufficient to attract additional signatures even on low-importance topics, then visibility algorithms and display design become powerful levers to increase engagement. Conversely, if perceived importance maintains primacy, then content and framing may be more consequential than simple popularity cues. The present investigation thus illuminates both theoretical and applied concerns. The remainder of the paper reports the methods, the statistical outcomes, and an interpretation of the results in the context of social influence theory. The reporting follows conventional standards for transparent description of sample, procedure, and analysis. The results will be summarized with appropriate effect sizes and confidence intervals. The final discussion will consider theoretical implications, limitations, and directions for future research.

Methods

Participants were recruited opportunistically in a public area near a university campus. A total of sixty individuals participated in the study. Recruitment was done by approaching passersby and inviting them to take part in a brief study about public petitions. Participation was voluntary and no compensation was provided. The sample included adults of varying ages, genders, and backgrounds as encountered in the recruitment setting. The recruitment approach yielded a convenience sample rather than a representative sample of any population. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two experimental conditions. Random assignment was implemented by alternating the condition on successive participant recruitments. The alternation procedure ensured balance across the two conditions given the opportunistic recruitment method. Each condition included thirty participants. The experimental session for each participant lasted only a few minutes. Prior to participation, individuals were provided with a brief verbal description of the study and were asked for their consent to participate. The consent procedure was simple and designed to be proportionate to the minimal risk nature of the task. Participants were informed that their decisions were anonymous and that the petitions were mock items created for research purposes. The experimental materials consisted of two mock petition pages presented to the participant on a tablet device. Each petition had a short descriptive headline indicating the issue area and an explicit label indicating the nominal importance of the petition as either "High importance" or "Low importance." The wording of these labels was identical across participants and served as the manipulation of perceived importance. For each petition, a visible signature count was displayed beneath the headline. In the high-signature condition for a given petition, the count displayed "40 signatures." In the low-signature condition, the count displayed "3 signatures." These two values were chosen to be clearly different and easily interpretable by participants. The content of the two petitions was intentionally neutral and was not intended to strongly elicit particular political or moral commitments. The headlines were brief descriptions of plausible community issues and were chosen to be comparable in perceived relevance. The exact topics were held constant across conditions; only the combination of importance label and signature count was manipulated. Each participant saw both petition items on the screen side-by-side. The order of left-right presentation was randomized across participants to control for any spatial bias. Participants were asked to look at the two petition displays and to indicate which petition they would prefer to sign by tapping the corresponding "Sign" button on the tablet. This provided a forced binary choice between the two petitions. Participants were told that their response would be anonymous and that no actual petition would be filed. The key dependent measure was therefore a behavioral choice indicated by the participant's tap. The experimental manipulation consisted of two between-subjects configurations. In Condition A (n = 30), the petition labeled "High importance" displayed the high signature count (40) and the petition labeled "Low importance" displayed the low signature count (3). In Condition B (n = 30), the mapping was reversed so that the petition labeled "High importance" displayed the low signature count (3) and the petition labeled "Low importance" displayed the high signature count (40). Random assignment of participants to these two configurations allowed us to examine whether choices were driven by visible signature count or by the nominal importance label. After the participant made their choice, a brief debriefing statement was shown on the tablet that reiterated the mock nature of the petitions and thanked them for participation. No personally identifying data were retained. The analysis plan was specified prior to viewing the data. The principal analysis was a test of whether participants were more likely than chance to choose the petition that displayed 40 signatures. A simple binomial test (or equivalently a chi-square goodness-of-fit test) was planned to compare the observed frequency of choosing the high-signature petition against the null expectation of 50% if signatures had no effect. Secondary analyses were planned to test whether the nominal importance label predicted choice. These analyses included a chi-square test of whether the frequency of selecting the petition labeled "High importance" differed from chance and a set of within-condition tests to see whether the preference for the high-signature petition held when that petition was labeled high importance and when it was labeled low importance. Effect sizes were to be reported for significant effects. Confidence intervals for observed proportions were also to be provided. All tests were two-tailed and conventional alpha thresholds were used. Data cleaning consisted only of ensuring that the binary choice variable was recorded for all participants. No participants were excluded from analysis. Because the dependent variable was categorical, no assumptions of normality were required for the primary tests. The analysis was conducted using standard statistical software capable of computing chi-square statistics, binomial probabilities, confidence intervals for proportions, and measures of effect size for contingency tables.

Results

The first analysis examined the overall tendency to choose the petition that displayed 40 prior signatures. Across the full sample of sixty participants, forty-two participants chose the petition that displayed 40 signatures. Eighteen participants chose the petition that displayed 3 signatures. Expressed as a proportion, 42 out of 60 corresponds to 70% choosing the high-signature petition. The 95% confidence interval for this proportion is approximately 58.4% to 81.6%. A chi-square goodness-of-fit test comparing the observed counts (42 vs. 18) to the null expectation of equal choice (30 vs. 30) yielded chi-square(1) = 9.60. The associated p-value for this test is approximately p = 0.002. This result indicates a statistically significant tendency to choose the petition with many prior signatures across the sample. The effect size for this overall preference can be indexed by phi, which equals the square root of chi-square divided by sample size. Here phi = sqrt(9.60/60) ≈ 0.40, which represents a medium-sized effect. To examine whether the nominal importance label had an independent effect on choice, we tabulated the number of participants who chose the petition labeled "High importance" regardless of signature count. Across both conditions, thirty-two participants chose the petition labeled "High importance" and twenty-eight chose the petition labeled "Low importance." A chi-square goodness-of-fit test comparing these counts to the null expectation of equal choice gave chi-square(1) = 0.27. The p-value for this test is approximately p = 0.61, indicating no reliable overall preference for the petition labeled as high importance. This pattern suggests that the nominal importance label by itself did not drive choice. Next we examined choices within each condition separately. In Condition A, in which the high-importance petition displayed 40 signatures and the low-importance petition displayed 3 signatures, twenty-two of thirty participants chose the high-signature petition. Eight participants chose the low-signature petition in that condition. A chi-square goodness-of-fit test for Condition A against equal choice produced chi-square(1) = 6.53, p ≈ 0.011. This indicates a significant preference for the high-signature petition when it was also labeled high importance. In Condition B, in which the high-importance petition displayed 3 signatures and the low-importance petition displayed 40 signatures, twenty of thirty participants chose the high-signature petition (the one labeled low importance). Ten participants chose the low-signature petition in that condition. A chi-square goodness-of-fit test for Condition B against equal choice yielded chi-square(1) = 3.33, p ≈ 0.068. This result is marginally non-significant by conventional thresholds, though the direction of effect remains consistent with the hypothesis. The pattern within conditions therefore shows a clear majority choosing the high-signature petition in Condition A and a majority also choosing the high-signature petition in Condition B, though the latter did not reach conventional significance in isolation. To directly test whether choices were contingent on the mapping of signature count to importance labels, we constructed a two-by-two contingency table crossing experimental condition with the choice of petition labeled as high importance versus low importance. In Condition A (high-importance = 40 signatures), twenty-two participants chose the petition labeled high importance and eight chose the petition labeled low importance. In Condition B (high-importance = 3 signatures), ten participants chose the petition labeled high importance and twenty chose the petition labeled low importance. A chi-square test of independence on this contingency table yielded chi-square(1) = 9.64, p ≈ 0.002. This indicates that the distribution of choices was dependent on the experimental condition and, by implication, on which petition carried the larger visible signature count. To further quantify the relative likelihood of choosing the high-signature petition depending on whether it was labeled high or low importance, we computed the odds of selecting the 40-signature petition in each condition. When the 40-signature petition was labeled high importance (Condition A), the odds were 22 to 8, or approximately 2.75. When the 40-signature petition was labeled low importance (Condition B), the odds were 20 to 10, or 2.0. The odds ratio comparing these two odds is approximately 1.38, indicating only a modest difference in odds of choosing the high-signature petition depending on the importance label. The overall preference for the high-signature petition was robust when data from both conditions were combined. To assess the practical magnitude of the effect, the observed proportion choosing the 40-signature petition (0.70) implies that participants were 40 percentage points more likely than chance to choose the high-signature petition. This difference is substantial in the context of a brief, opportunistic behavioral choice. We also computed the binomial probability of observing 42 or more successes out of 60 trials under a null hypothesis of p = 0.5. The resulting binomial p-value is consistent with the chi-square result and is below conventional thresholds, confirming the statistical significance of the observed preference for the high-signature petition. The secondary analyses supported the conclusion that the nominal importance label did not independently predict choice. The marginal non-significance in Condition B suggests that when the high-signature petition was labeled low importance, the preference for the petition with more signatures was slightly attenuated relative to when it was labeled high importance. However, the direction of preference remained consistent across both conditions, and the aggregated data show a clear effect of signature count. No data exclusions were necessary and no missing choice data were present. All planned analyses were carried out and reported. The pattern of results across tests converges on the same conclusion: visible group size, as indexed by the number of prior signatures displayed, biased participants toward selecting the petition with more prior support. The nominal importance label by itself did not produce a reliable net effect across participants. These findings provide empirical support for the hypothesis that group size cues promote conformity in the domain of public petition signing.

Discussion

The present study examined whether visible group size, operationalized as the number of prior signatures on a petition, would influence individuals’ choices to sign a petition independent of a nominal importance label. The principal finding was that participants were significantly more likely to choose a petition that displayed a larger number of prior signatures. Across the full sample, seventy percent of participants selected the petition displaying forty prior signatures. This preference for the petition with more signatures emerged even when that petition was labeled as low importance. The nominal importance label, in contrast, did not produce a reliable effect on choice when considered across the entire sample. These results support the hypothesis that visible group size cues strongly shape signing behavior and can override a simple importance cue. The observed effect is consistent with accounts of social proof and informational social influence in which people use the behavior of others as a heuristic for appropriate action. When individuals encounter a petition with many prior endorsements, they may infer that the issue is worthy of support or that signing is a socially validated course of action. In the operational environment of the present study, the signature count provided an easily processed cue that could be used to shortcut a more deliberative evaluation of issue content. The finding that the high-signature petition attracted a majority of choices in both experimental configurations suggests that group size plays a substantial role, at least under the relatively low-investment, brief decision conditions employed here. The insignificance of the nominal importance label when examined across all participants indicates that a superficial label of importance was not sufficient to counter the influence of visible group support. This suggests that, in situations where people have only a brief exposure and make a quick choice, social cues may have greater sway than labels about importance. The condition-level analyses provide further nuance. When the petition with high signatures was also labeled high importance, the preference for the high-signature petition was clear and statistically significant. When the high-signature petition was labeled low importance, the preference for the high-signature petition remained but was somewhat weaker and only marginally significant when considered in isolation. This attenuation implies that contextual framing or perceived relevance can interact with social proof cues to modulate their strength. In practical terms, a label that suggests low importance may reduce but not eliminate the tendency to follow a visible majority. In theoretical terms, the result is consistent with a model in which social proof is a dominant heuristic that is nevertheless subject to modulation by other cues. The present findings relate to prior work on conformity and social influence in a number of ways. They extend laboratory findings about majority influence to a task that more closely resembles routine civic behavior. They also complement studies showing that popularity cues in online systems—such as view counts, likes, and prior endorsements—shape subsequent engagement. The results underscore how the visibility of collective behavior can create a self-reinforcing dynamic: early signs of support attract additional support, which in turn amplifies the social signal. The study also contributes to understanding the boundary conditions of influence by showing that a nominal importance label does not override a strong popularity cue in brief decision contexts. Several limitations of the study warrant careful consideration. First, the sample was a convenience sample recruited opportunistically in a public area. The demographic composition of the sample is unknown in detail and may not represent broader populations. Hence the generalizability of the observed effect to different demographic groups or to populations with different cultural norms regarding conformity remains open. Second, the importance manipulation consisted of a straightforward label reading "High importance" or "Low importance." This manipulation was intentionally brief and superficial in order to isolate its effect relative to signature count. It is possible that more elaborate or personally relevant importance manipulations—such as a persuasive paragraph about why the issue matters—would produce stronger effects and perhaps interact differently with signature counts. Third, the experimental setting involved a simulated petition choice on a tablet, and participants were informed that no actual petition would be filed. Although the paradigm was designed to mimic real-world choices, the absence of real-world consequences could have affected participants' motivations. In real petition signing contexts, additional factors such as affective engagement, social identity, and perceived impact may alter the balance of influence between importance and signature counts. Fourth, the signature counts used in the study were specific numeric values (3 and 40). Different magnitudes, ratios, or absolute levels of prior support may produce different effects. For instance, when signature counts scale to hundreds or thousands, the social proof effect might be stronger, whereas very small differences might not be noticed. Future research could systematically vary signature magnitudes to map dose-response relationships. Fifth, the study used only two petition items with neutral content. There is reason to believe that content valence and personal relevance moderate conformity effects. For highly salient or moralized topics, individuals may be less susceptible to social proof and more guided by intrinsic convictions. Conversely, for topics perceived as trivial, social proof cues may dominate. Investigating a broader set of topics with varying personal relevance would help clarify these moderating influences. Notwithstanding these limitations, the study has several strengths. The experimental manipulation was simple, transparent, and directly probes the theoretical question at hand. The forced-choice behavioral measure reduces reliance on self-report and provides a concrete index of conformity. Random assignment ensured that individual differences were unlikely to account for the observed pattern. The convergence of multiple analytic approaches—chi-square tests, binomial probabilities, and effect size estimates—strengthens confidence in the central finding. The pattern of results points to several promising directions for future work. One avenue would be to examine the temporal dynamics of social proof effects on petition signatures. Longitudinal designs that monitor how signature counts evolve over time and how visibility of counts influences subsequent accrual would illuminate feedback loops in mobilization. Another direction is to investigate the psychological mechanisms underlying the preference for high-signature petitions. Process measures, such as asking participants to explain their choice or measuring response times, could reveal whether decisions are driven by perceived normative endorsement, assumptions about impact, or simple heuristic reliance. Neurocognitive or physiological markers during choice could offer additional insight into the cognitive processes at play. It would also be valuable to explore interventions that either bolster resistance to misleading popularity cues or harness social proof responsibly for prosocial goals. For instance, providing contextual information about the significance of signature counts or highlighting substantive content alongside signature numbers might encourage more deliberative decision-making. From a policy and design perspective, the results suggest that platform designers should be mindful of how the display of popularity metrics can shape public engagement. Transparent presentation of signature counts can promote participation but may also bias attention and create cascades that do not reflect deliberative assessment of issues. Designers concerned with civic quality might consider design choices that balance visibility of social cues with prompts to attend to content and evidence. In conclusion, the current study demonstrates that visible group size, in the form of prior signature counts, significantly influences petition-signing choices in a brief decision context. The effect holds across configurations in which the high-signature petition is labeled as high importance or low importance, indicating that social proof can override a simple importance label. These findings contribute to the literature on conformity by showing how observable collective behavior operates as a powerful guide for individual action in civic contexts. Future research that addresses the identified limitations and expands the empirical scope will be important for developing a fuller understanding of when and why people follow the crowd in civic and digital settings.
