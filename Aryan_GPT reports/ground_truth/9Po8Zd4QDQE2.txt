Introduction

The relationship between media exposure and individual psychological outcomes has been a subject of sustained interest across multiple disciplines including psychology, sociology, and media studies. Over recent decades there has been a steadily growing body of empirical work exploring how the content and quantity of media consumption relate to perceptions of the self and to social beliefs. One of the most robust and frequently investigated associations in this literature is between media exposure and body image, particularly in the context of visual media that emphasize physical attractiveness. A parallel stream of research has examined the connections between body image and romantic relationships, exploring how self-perception around appearance affects dating behaviors, relationship satisfaction, and mate selection. A third area of inquiry examines how media exposure shapes beliefs about romance and about ideal relationships, with some scholars arguing that media portrayals of love, partnership, and sexual relationships contribute to cultural norms and individual expectations. Despite the thematic overlap among these three areas, relatively few investigations have placed media exposure, body image, and romantic beliefs into a single empirical model to examine how they covary in the same sample. This absence of integrated investigation means that questions about how the three constructs interrelate in everyday experience remain incompletely answered.

There are conceptual reasons to expect links among media exposure, body image, and romantic beliefs. Social comparison theory proposes that individuals evaluate themselves by comparing their attributes to those of others, and mediated images of highly idealized bodies provide a potent social comparison target. Cultivation theory similarly suggests that long-term exposure to media content can shape perceptions of social reality, including normative beliefs about attractiveness and relationship roles. Objectification frameworks further hypothesize that exposure to sexualized imagery encourages external valuation of physical appearance, which can promote body dissatisfaction. At the same time, research on romantic beliefs has highlighted the extent to which cultural portrayals of idealized romance can influence expectations about intimacy, permanence, and exclusivity in relationships. These theoretical perspectives collectively yield plausible mechanisms by which media exposure might influence both body image and romantic beliefs, and by which body image could in turn relate to romantic expectations and behaviors.

Empirical work on media exposure and body image has consistently reported negative outcomes, particularly for women, although there is variability in effect sizes and some findings suggest moderating roles for personal vulnerability factors. Meta-analyses and large-scale reviews have documented a reliable association between consumption of appearance-focused media and increases in body dissatisfaction, drive for thinness, and disordered eating behaviors. Grabe, Ward, and Hyde, among others, synthesised results demonstrating modest but consistent negative effects of media exposure on body image in women. These reviews also highlight that the portrayal of thinness as an ideal is a particularly potent feature of many mainstream media environments. At the same time, some scholars have emphasized that media effects are not uniform; individual differences in pre-existing body esteem, critical media literacy, and social support can attenuate the impact of media images.

Research examining gender differences in media effects has been particularly informative. Women, by virtue of cultural norms and targeted marketing, are often more frequently exposed to and socialized around appearance standards, and this differential exposure has been proposed as a key reason why women more consistently show negative body-image effects. Men are not immune to these effects; increasing attention has been paid to the media promotion of lean and muscular male ideals, which can be associated with muscularity-oriented dissatisfaction. Nonetheless, many empirical reports still find stronger and more consistent relationships between media exposure and body dissatisfaction among women. These gendered patterns point to the need to evaluate media exposure effects separately by gender or to test gender as a moderator of media–body image associations.

The literature on body image and romantic relationships emphasizes several pathways linking self-perceptions of appearance to experiences and expectations within romantic contexts. Individuals with greater body dissatisfaction often express lower romantic confidence, may be less likely to initiate romantic contact, may interpret partner behavior through a lens of suspicion regarding physical desirability, and may report lower relationship satisfaction. Body image concerns can also affect sexual intimacy and communication within relationships. In short, body image is embedded within the psychosocial mechanisms that govern romantic relationship formation and maintenance. Given this linkage, it is reasonable to posit that body image could mediate or moderate the association between media exposure and romantic beliefs.

Media exposure has also been connected directly to romantic beliefs. Popular narratives in film, television, and social media often promote particular scripts about love, such as the notions of soulmates, destiny, and perfect romantic reciprocity. These portrayals can shape expectations about what relationships should look like and what behaviors are normative in romantic contexts. For instance, romantic comedies often instantiate the idea that love conquers obstacles and that partners will tolerate or even celebrate behaviors that reinforce an idealized romance. Researchers have identified that strong endorsement of romantic myths, such as the belief in eternal love or the expectation of unconditional compatibility, can influence relationship satisfaction and coping behaviors. Consequently, media exposure might be expected to strengthen certain romantic beliefs by repeatedly presenting idealized images of partnership.

Despite theoretical links and partial empirical connections across these domains, the three constructs of media exposure, body image, and romantic beliefs have rarely been examined together in a single study. Many prior studies have focused on pairwise associations, such as media exposure and body image or body image and relationship outcomes, but few have tested whether media exposure is associated with both body image and romantic beliefs in the same sample, and whether body image and romantic beliefs are interrelated once media exposure is accounted for. Integrating all three variables is important for both theoretical and practical reasons. Theoretically, it enables examination of whether media exposure operates through appearance concerns to shape romantic expectations, or whether media influence on romance is independent of body image. Practically, understanding these relationships could inform interventions that aim to reduce harmful media effects by clarifying whether campaigns should target body image, romantic ideals, or both.

A further gap in prior work concerns the role of gender in these interrelations. Given documented gender differences in media exposure effects on body image, it is possible that gender moderates associations among media exposure, body image, and romantic beliefs. For example, women may be more susceptible to media-induced body dissatisfaction, and this dissatisfaction might more strongly influence romantic beliefs and behaviors among women relative to men. Conversely, men may be influenced by different media genres or by ideals emphasizing muscularity rather than thinness, producing different empirical patterns. Thus, a complete account of how media exposure relates to body image and romantic beliefs should evaluate potential gender differences.

The current study was designed to address these empirical and theoretical gaps by investigating the correlations among media exposure, body image, and romantic beliefs in a single, cross-sectional dataset. The study assessed media exposure, body image attitudes, and endorsement of romantic beliefs using brief standardized questionnaires administered via an online survey to a volunteer sample of 100 adult participants. The primary aims were threefold. First, to investigate whether higher media exposure is associated with more negative body image in the overall sample. Second, to evaluate whether media exposure is associated with stronger endorsement of idealized romantic beliefs. Third, to examine whether body image and romantic beliefs are related to each other. In addition to these primary aims, the study explored gender differences in these associations and tested whether gender moderates the relation between media exposure and body image.

On the basis of prior findings that increased media exposure relates to poorer body image particularly for women, the study hypothesized that greater media exposure would be associated with more negative body image and that this association would be stronger in female participants. The study also hypothesized that media exposure would be associated with stronger romantic beliefs in the form of idealized notions of love, although the direction and strength of this association was more exploratory given mixed findings in previous literature. Finally, the study examined whether romantic beliefs and body image would be associated; given the connections between self-perception and romantic behavior in prior work, a modest association was expected, although this prediction remained tentative.

Beyond these hypotheses, the study incorporated methods to ensure internal consistency and measurement reliability and to consider potential confounds. The online survey collected basic demographic information including age and gender, and included standard psychometric checks such as reliability analyses for each scale. Statistical analyses focused on Pearson correlations to estimate bivariate associations and linear regression to test moderation by gender. The study’s cross-sectional and correlational design precluded causal inference, but the integrative approach aimed to provide a clearer descriptive map of how these three constructs relate in the same sample. By bringing media exposure, body image, and romantic beliefs into a single analytic frame and by considering gender moderation, the study sought to contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the psychosocial consequences of media consumption in modern social contexts.

Methods

Participants

One hundred volunteers participated in the present study. Participants were recruited through online advertisements distributed via university mailing lists and social media platforms that targeted young adults and community members. The inclusion criteria were minimal: participants were required to be at least 18 years of age and to provide informed consent for participation. Of the 100 participants, 64 identified as female, 36 identified as male, and none selected non-binary or other categories in the demographic questions for this sample. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 46 years with a mean age of 24.5 years and a standard deviation of 6.7 years. Participants also reported their height and weight, from which body mass index (BMI) was calculated; the mean BMI for the sample was 23.1 with a standard deviation of 4.2. All participants completed the survey online and received no monetary compensation but were entered into a drawing for one of five five-dollar electronic gift cards as a small incentive for participation.

Ethical considerations

The study procedures were reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of the hosting university. Participants were presented with an online consent form describing the nature of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, and the procedures for data confidentiality and storage. Participants were informed that they could discontinue the survey at any time without penalty. Data were collected anonymously; identifying information was not linked to the survey responses. After completing the measures, participants were provided with debriefing information and referrals to resources relevant to body image concerns and mental health.

Procedure

After providing informed consent, participants completed an online battery of questionnaires hosted on a survey platform. The survey began with demographic questions including age, gender, height, and weight. Participants then completed three primary measures in counterbalanced order to reduce potential order effects. The three measures were a media exposure scale, a body image questionnaire, and a romantic beliefs questionnaire. Finally, participants answered a brief set of attention-check items to ensure data quality and were debriefed at the end of the survey. The entire survey took approximately 15 to 20 minutes to complete for most participants.

Measures

Media exposure

Media exposure was assessed using a brief multi-item scale developed for the purposes of this study to capture both quantity and content focus of media consumption. Participants reported average time spent per day on several media channels (television, social media, streaming video, magazines, and online articles) and rated the extent to which their media use was focused on appearance-related content (e.g., fashion, fitness, celebrity imagery) on a seven-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree/not at all) to 7 (strongly agree/a great deal). The scale combined frequency items (converted to a standardized per-day metric) and content-focus items into a composite score reflecting overall exposure to appearance-oriented media. The final media exposure composite was the standardized sum of the frequency and content focus components; higher scores indicated greater exposure to appearance-focused media. In the current sample the media exposure scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency with Cronbach’s alpha equal to .84.

Body image

Body image was measured using a widely used 16-item scale designed to capture global dissatisfaction and preoccupation with body shape and weight. Items reflected cognitive and affective dimensions of body image (e.g., “I feel uncomfortable when I look at my body in the mirror,” “I worry about gaining weight even when others say I don’t look heavy”), and were rated on a seven-point scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Items were summed and averaged to create a composite body image score in which higher values indicated more negative body image and greater dissatisfaction. The body image measure demonstrated strong internal consistency in the current sample with a Cronbach’s alpha of .91.

Romantic beliefs

Romantic beliefs were assessed using a 12-item questionnaire that asked participants to rate the extent to which they endorsed idealized notions of romantic relationships, such as belief in soulmates, belief that true love overcomes all obstacles, and belief in a forever-type relationship. Each item was rated on a seven-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Items were averaged to create an overall romantic beliefs score where higher scores reflected stronger endorsement of idealized romantic scripts. The romantic beliefs scale showed acceptable reliability in this sample with Cronbach’s alpha equal to .79.

Attention checks and data quality

Two attention-check items were embedded in the questionnaire to detect random or inattentive responding (e.g., “For quality control, please select ‘Agree’ for this item”). Participants who failed both attention check items would have been excluded; however, in the current dataset all participants passed at least one attention-check item, and no participant failed both checks. Missing data were minimal (<1% across items) and were handled with mean imputation for scale-level scoring when appropriate.

Analytic strategy

The data analytic plan was designed to test the primary hypotheses regarding the relationships among media exposure, body image, and romantic beliefs and to examine gender differences in these associations. Data screening procedures included inspection of univariate and bivariate distributions and tests of normality. The primary analyses consisted of Pearson correlation coefficients to estimate bivariate associations among the three primary variables across the full sample. Given theoretical expectations for gender differences, separate correlation analyses were then conducted within female and male subsamples to determine whether associations differed by gender. To formally test gender as a moderator of the relationship between media exposure and body image, a hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted in which body image was regressed on mean-centered media exposure, gender (coded 0 = male, 1 = female), and the interaction term between media exposure and gender. Age and BMI were included as covariates in secondary analyses to examine whether associations remained after accounting for these potential confounds. All statistical tests were two-tailed with an alpha threshold of .05 for statistical significance. Effect sizes are reported alongside p-values to facilitate interpretation of practical significance.

Results

Data screening and descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables. The mean media exposure composite score (standardized metric) for the full sample was 0.02 with a standard deviation of 0.98, reflecting the z-score transformation used to combine frequency and content focus measures. The mean body image score for the sample was 4.12 on the seven-point scale (SD = 1.06), indicating a moderate average level of body-related dissatisfaction. The mean romantic beliefs score was 4.31 (SD = 0.90), suggesting modest endorsement of idealized romantic scripts. Skewness and kurtosis statistics for the three main variables were within acceptable limits for parametric analyses (absolute skewness < 1.0 and absolute kurtosis < 2.0), and inspection of histograms did not reveal extreme departures from normality. No univariate outliers were flagged using a z-score threshold of ±3.29, and no bivariate outliers were detected using Mahalanobis distance criteria.

Internal consistency for the study measures was acceptable to strong. Cronbach’s alpha for the media exposure composite was .84, for the body image scale was .91, and for the romantic beliefs scale was .79. These values indicate that the measures functioned reliably in the current sample.

Bivariate correlations in the full sample

Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to examine pairwise associations among media exposure, body image, and romantic beliefs. In the full sample of 100 participants, media exposure showed a significant positive correlation with negative body image (r = .31, p = .002), indicating that higher exposure to appearance-focused media was associated with more negative body image. The correlation magnitude corresponds to a small-to-moderate effect size. Media exposure had a non-significant association with romantic beliefs (r = .08, p = .423), suggesting little to no relationship between time and focus on appearance-related media and endorsement of idealized romantic scripts in this sample. Romantic beliefs and body image were weakly and non-significantly correlated (r = -.06, p = .546), such that higher endorsement of romantic beliefs was not associated with more negative or more positive body image in this dataset.

Gender-specific correlations

Given theoretical expectations and prior empirical observations of gender differences in media effects, correlations were computed separately for female and male participants. Among female participants (n = 64), the correlation between media exposure and negative body image was r = .38 and was statistically significant (p = .002), indicating a moderate association among women such that greater exposure to appearance-focused media was associated with greater body dissatisfaction. In contrast, among male participants (n = 36), the correlation between media exposure and negative body image was r = .05 and was not significant (p = .743), indicating no reliable association for men in this sample.

The association between media exposure and romantic beliefs was non-significant in both gender groups. For female participants, r = .10 (p = .414), and for male participants, r = .01 (p = .948). Similarly, the correlation between romantic beliefs and body image was small and non-significant in both groups (females: r = -.08, p = .531; males: r = -.01, p = .957). These gender-specific analyses reveal that the significant bivariate relationship between media exposure and negative body image in the full sample was driven primarily by the female subsample.

Regression analysis and gender moderation

To formally evaluate whether gender moderated the association between media exposure and body image, a hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted. In the first step the main effects of mean-centered media exposure and gender (0 = male, 1 = female) were entered to predict body image. In the second step the interaction term between media exposure and gender was added. Age and BMI were included as covariates in an additional model to test the robustness of effects. In the model without covariates, the main effect of media exposure was significant (β = .30, t(97) = 3.10, p = .002), indicating that media exposure predicted more negative body image overall. Gender also exhibited a significant main effect (β = .20, t(97) = 2.04, p = .044), with female participants reporting higher levels of body dissatisfaction compared to male participants. The addition of the interaction term in Step 2 produced a significant increment in explained variance (ΔR2 = .045, p = .021), and the media exposure × gender interaction term was significant (β = .22, t(96) = 2.35, p = .021). Examination of simple slopes indicated that media exposure was a significant predictor of negative body image for female participants (simple slope β = .41, p < .001) but not for male participants (simple slope β = .09, p = .57).

When age and BMI were included as covariates in the regression model, the pattern of results remained substantively similar. Media exposure continued to predict negative body image (β = .28, t(95) = 2.70, p = .008), gender remained significant (β = .18, t(95) = 1.85, p = .067; marginal), and the media exposure × gender interaction remained significant (β = .20, t(94) = 2.13, p = .036). BMI was a significant predictor of body image in the expected direction (β = .26, t(95) = 2.51, p = .014), indicating higher BMI associated with more negative body image, while age was not a significant predictor (β = -.05, t(95) = -.49, p = .626). These additional analyses suggest that the observed gender-moderated association between media exposure and body image is not fully explained by variations in BMI or age.

Exploratory analyses

Given the absence of relations between media exposure and romantic beliefs in primary analyses, exploratory analyses were conducted to examine whether specific media channels or the content-focus component of the media exposure composite was differentially related to romantic beliefs. Correlations between romantic beliefs and time spent on different media channels (television, social media, streaming video, magazines, online articles) ranged from r = -.02 to r = .11 and were non-significant. The content-focus subscale (appearance-focus of media use) was weakly and non-significantly related to romantic beliefs (r = .12, p = .23). These exploratory findings suggest no reliable evidence that either the amount of media consumption or the degree of appearance focus in that consumption is associated with endorsement of idealized romantic beliefs in this sample.

Effect sizes and practical significance

Across the results, the association between media exposure and negative body image represents the most consistent and practically meaningful effect observed. The correlation of r = .31 in the full sample, and r = .38 among women, indicates that media exposure explains approximately 9.6% of the variance in body image among women (r2 for females ≈ .14), which is a modest but substantively meaningful effect in psychological research on media effects. The moderation analyses further clarified that the effect is concentrated among female participants. The lack of significant associations involving romantic beliefs suggests either that media exposure does not exert a strong cross-sectional association with these beliefs in this sample, or that more nuanced measures or longitudinal designs are required to detect such effects.

Statistical power and limitations regarding sample size

Although the sample size of 100 provided adequate power to detect small-to-moderate correlations (e.g., r ≈ .27 at α = .05, two-tailed), it had limited power to detect very small effects, particularly within gender-specific subsamples, and to detect interactions of small magnitude. The observed non-significant associations with romantic beliefs, therefore, may reflect absence of a true effect or simply limited sensitivity to detect small effects in the present sample. Nonetheless, the statistically significant and robust association between media exposure and body image among women suggests that the study had sufficient power to detect the hypothesized gendered media effect on body dissatisfaction.

Summary of key findings

In summary, the principal findings were that greater exposure to appearance-focused media was associated with more negative body image in the sample as a whole, that this association was significant and of moderate magnitude among female participants but absent among male participants, and that no significant associations were observed between media exposure and romantic beliefs or between romantic beliefs and body image. Regression analyses confirmed that gender moderated the media exposure–body image association even after controlling for age and BMI. Exploratory analyses did not identify specific media channels or content features that related to romantic beliefs. Taken together, the results provide converging evidence for a gendered relation between media exposure and body image and no evidence in this sample for cross-sectional relationships linking media exposure to romantic beliefs or linking romantic beliefs to body image.

Discussion

The present study investigated the interrelationships among media exposure, body image, and romantic beliefs in a volunteer sample of adults who completed online questionnaires assessing each construct. The overarching aim was to bring these three lines of inquiry together and to test whether media exposure was associated with negative body image and with endorsement of idealized romantic beliefs, and whether body image and romantic beliefs were related to one another. The findings provide clear support for a relationship between media exposure and negative body image in the overall sample, but they show that this relationship is driven primarily by female participants. In contrast, the data provided no evidence for associations between media exposure and romantic beliefs, or between romantic beliefs and body image, in either the full sample or the gender-specific subsamples. Broadly construed, these results align with prior work documenting that media exposure—particularly exposure to appearance-focused content—relates to poorer body image, and they highlight the importance of gender as a moderator of this association. At the same time, the findings indicate that media exposure may not be strongly or directly linked to general romantic beliefs, at least as assessed by the measures used in this cross-sectional design.

The finding that higher media exposure is associated with more negative body image is consistent with a substantial body of literature that has demonstrated the deleterious effects of appearance-focused media on body satisfaction. Reviews and meta-analyses have shown that repeated exposure to idealized and often unattainable body types in media can increase social comparison processes, internalization of cultural appearance standards, and self-objectification, all of which contribute to body dissatisfaction. The current study extends this literature by demonstrating that when media exposure, body image, and romantic beliefs are assessed within the same sample, the clearest empirical link is between media exposure and body image. The magnitude of the association observed here—small to moderate in the full sample and moderate among women—is in keeping with prior meta-analytic estimates of media effects on body dissatisfaction, which often report small-to-moderate effect sizes that accumulate meaningfully across populations and repeated exposure.

The clear gender difference observed in this study is also consonant with extant research. Women in many cultures receive disproportionate social messages about the importance of physical appearance and are more frequent targets of media content that idealizes thinness and beauty. Consequently, women may be more likely than men to internalize appearance standards, engage in appearance-focused social comparison, and experience negative body image following exposure to appearance-centric media. The current study’s regression analysis found that gender moderated the media exposure–body image relationship even after accounting for BMI and age, suggesting that gendered socialization and media targeting rather than simple body size differences drive this pattern. Practically, this underscores the importance of gender-sensitive prevention and media literacy interventions that specifically address how media content impacts women’s body image. That said, it remains important to recognize that men are also subject to media pressures—often focused on muscularity rather than thinness—and that male body dissatisfaction may manifest in different ways not captured fully by the body image measure used here.

The absence of a significant association between media exposure and romantic beliefs was somewhat unexpected in light of theoretical frameworks suggesting that media shape cultural norms around relationships. Cultivation theory and narrative persuasion perspectives argue that prolonged exposure to narratives in film, television, and other media can influence beliefs about what relationships should look like. However, the romantic beliefs measure in this study assessed fairly stable, abstract endorsement of idealized relationship scripts (e.g., belief in soulmates), and it may be that such broad beliefs are more resistant to short-term media influence or that they are shaped more strongly by familial, peer, and cultural socialization than by media consumption alone. Alternatively, media influence on romantic beliefs might be more nuanced and channel-specific than our composite media exposure metric could detect. For example, exposure to romantic films might influence romantic beliefs differently from exposure to celebrity gossip or dating-related social media content. Our exploratory analyses examined separate media channels and found no reliable associations, but these analyses were underpowered for detection of small effects and were limited by the relatively coarse measurement of channel-specific exposure.

Another possibility for the null findings concerning romantic beliefs is that the relationship between media exposure and romantic beliefs is mediated or moderated by other psychological constructs not assessed in this study. For instance, individual differences in attachment style, prior relationship experience, or critical media literacy could influence whether media exposure translates into endorsement of romantic myths. It is also plausible that media exposure affects relationship-related attitudes more strongly in certain developmental periods, such as adolescence or early emerging adulthood, while our sample included a broader age range centered in the mid-twenties. In short, while theory supports a potential media–romantic beliefs connection, our data suggest that this relation may be weaker, more context-specific, or more contingent on other variables than the media–body image relation.

The lack of correlation between body image and romantic beliefs was also notable. Prior literature suggests that negative body image can influence romantic and sexual behaviors, self-perceived desirability, and relationship confidence. Nevertheless, the link between body dissatisfaction and endorsement of idealized romantic scripts is not as direct as the link to behaviors and emotional functioning within relationships. It may be that body image primarily affects interpersonal behaviors and relationship satisfaction rather than abstract beliefs about love. Alternatively, there may be bidirectional processes or complex interactions by which body image and romantic beliefs coevolve in the context of relationship experiences, which a cross-sectional design is poorly equipped to detect. Longitudinal or experimental designs are likely needed to unpack these potential dynamics.

Methodological considerations are important when interpreting the present findings. The study used self-report questionnaires administered online to a convenience sample of volunteers who were predominantly young adults. Such a sample provides efficiency and ecological validity for examining social-cognitive associations, but it also limits generalizability to wider populations in terms of age, cultural background, and socioeconomic status. The sample size of 100 allowed reasonable power to detect modest associations in the full sample, but subgroup analyses (e.g., within gender categories) had reduced power, particularly for detecting small effects. This limitation should temper conclusions concerning the null findings for men and for associations involving romantic beliefs; replication with larger and more diverse samples is recommended.

Another methodological issue concerns the measurement approach. The media exposure composite combined frequency and content-focus components into a standardized score to capture the overall exposure to appearance-focused media, but this approach necessarily aggregates across heterogeneous media formats and narrative genres. As noted earlier, it is plausible that specific types of media have different relationships with romantic beliefs, and that different aspects of body image (e.g., muscularity concerns versus thinness concerns) may vary by gender and by media content. Future research would benefit from more granular measures of media content, including coding of specific media messages, and from multi-dimensional measures of body image that capture varied aspects of appearance concern.

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although media exposure was associated with negative body image in this study, the directionality of this association cannot be established. It is possible that individuals with pre-existing body dissatisfaction seek out more appearance-focused media, or that some third variable (e.g., peer norms, personality traits) influences both media choices and body image. Experimental studies that manipulate media exposure and longitudinal designs that assess changes over time are necessary to establish causal pathways. Similarly, the absence of significant associations with romantic beliefs might reflect timing or measurement issues rather than definitive evidence of absence of effect.

Despite these limitations, the study makes several theoretical and practical contributions. Theoretically, it demonstrates that when media exposure, body image, and romantic beliefs are measured in the same sample, the strongest and most consistent association is between media exposure and body image, and that this association is gender-specific. This finding underscores the centrality of appearance-focused media in shaping self-perceptions around body image—particularly among women—and suggests that media effects on romantic beliefs are either weaker, more nuanced, or contingent on unmeasured moderators. Practically, the results reinforce the need for media literacy and public health interventions aimed at reducing the negative impact of appearance-centric media on body satisfaction. Such interventions might emphasize critical engagement with media images, promote diverse and realistic body representations, and support individual coping strategies for resisting harmful social comparison processes.

Several avenues for future research follow from the current findings. First, longitudinal studies could track media exposure and changes in body image and romantic beliefs over time to clarify directionality and potential mediation processes. For example, future studies might test whether exposure to appearance-focused media predicts increases in body dissatisfaction, which in turn predict changes in dating behaviors or relationship satisfaction. Second, experimental paradigms can manipulate exposure to different media content types—romantic narratives, idealized appearance images, or neutral content—to test causal effects on body image and romantic beliefs and to examine moderators such as gender, trait self-objectification, and critical media literacy. Third, more nuanced measurement of media exposure should be employed, including ecological momentary assessment of media use, content analysis of consumed media, and objective tracking of screen time across platforms. Fourth, research should expand demographic diversity to test whether cultural context and age moderate the observed associations, as media portrayals and body ideals vary across cultures and developmental stages. Fifth, interventions that aim to reduce negative body image should be evaluated for their downstream effects on romantic beliefs and relationships to determine whether improving body satisfaction translates into healthier romantic expectations and behavior.

In conclusion, the present study contributes to the literature by jointly examining media exposure, body image, and romantic beliefs in a single sample and by identifying a robust, gender-moderated association between media exposure and negative body image. The findings reinforce the need for ongoing efforts to address the potentially harmful influence of appearance-focused media on body satisfaction, particularly among women, and suggest that the impact of media on romantic beliefs may be more limited or more complex than some theoretical accounts propose. Future research that employs longitudinal, experimental, and more granular measurement designs will be critical for advancing understanding of how media consumption shapes self-perception and social beliefs in contemporary media-saturated environments.
