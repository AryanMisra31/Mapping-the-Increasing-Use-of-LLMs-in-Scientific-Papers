Introduction

Self-esteem is a central construct in psychology that influences a wide range of behaviors and outcomes. Researchers have long been interested in how self-esteem develops, how it can be measured, and whether it can be changed. Individual differences in self-esteem predict outcomes in mental health, relationship quality, occupational attainment, and physical health. Given its importance, interventions that can reliably increase self-esteem are of considerable theoretical and practical interest. A number of approaches to self-esteem enhancement have been tested, including psychotherapeutic techniques, cognitive restructuring, affirmations, and associative learning methods. One line of research has focused on evaluative conditioning as a potential mechanism for shifting self-evaluations. Evaluative conditioning involves pairing a neutral or target stimulus with positive or negative stimuli so that the valence of the paired stimuli changes. Researchers have applied evaluative conditioning to self-related stimuli with the aim of indirectly modifying self-esteem. For instance, several studies have used subliminal or supraliminal pairings of self-relevant cues with positive words to raise implicit and, in some instances, explicit measures of self-esteem. The idea behind this work is that pairing self-referent cues with positive evaluative stimuli can alter the associative network underlying self-evaluation. Such manipulations are attractive because they can be brief, low effort, and potentially operate without conscious resistance or defensiveness. In some reports, subliminal evaluative conditioning has produced measurable increases in state self-esteem. Other studies, however, have found small or inconsistent effects, highlighting the need for more precise characterization of when and for whom conditioning works. A related but distinct mechanism that might enhance self-evaluations is active self-generation of positive attributes. The self-reference effect in memory shows that people process and remember information better when they relate it to themselves. Generating information about oneself can also increase the subjective relevance and emotional salience of that information. If people write down positive words that describe themselves, those words may be encoded more deeply and may have a larger influence on self-beliefs than general positive words. Active generation also engages elaborative processes and personal meaning making that passive presentation does not capture. It follows that asking participants to produce positive, self-relevant descriptors could be a simple and scalable intervention to boost self-esteem. Despite this logic, relatively little experimental work has directly compared self-generated positive descriptors with general positive word exposure for effects on explicit self-esteem. The present study sought to bridge that gap by adapting ideas from evaluative conditioning and the self-reference literature. Instead of using subliminal pairings of a self-cue with positive words, we asked participants to either generate five positive words about themselves or to generate five positive words that were not about them. We reasoned that self-generated descriptors would lead to greater increases in explicit self-esteem measured immediately after the task because of enhanced encoding, relevance, and self-activation. We also considered the role of demographic and developmental factors in moderating self-esteem and responses to the task. Educational experience is one such factor that has been linked to self-evaluations in prior work. Some literature suggests that students engaged in academic study contexts report higher self-confidence and more favorable self-concept than students in vocational training contexts. This pattern may reflect selection processes, differences in academic socialization, or distinct institutional cultures that bolster or undermine self-evaluation. If educational trajectory relates to baseline self-esteem, it could also moderate responses to brief interventions like the one studied here. For that reason, we planned to examine differences in self-esteem between participants who had attended university and those who had not. Age is another robust correlate of self-esteem in the literature. Several lifespan studies have suggested that self-esteem tends to increase across early and middle adulthood and may peak in late middle age. Some cross-sectional reports and longitudinal analyses have suggested a gradual increase in self-esteem until around the sixth decade of life followed by a decline in older age. The mechanisms underlying such a pattern are debated and may include changes in social roles, cognitive processes, health limitations, and cohort effects. Testing whether the present sample shows an age-related increase and subsequent decline provides a useful descriptive test of generalizability. We therefore included age as a predictor in our analyses and examined nonlinear age effects. The present research had three primary objectives. The first objective was to test whether generating five positive words about oneself would yield higher immediate self-esteem compared with generating five general positive words not specifically about the self. The second objective was to explore whether educational background—having attended university versus not—was associated with differences in self-esteem within the control group. The third objective was to evaluate whether self-esteem in our sample showed the previously reported increase with age up to around sixty and a subsequent decline thereafter. We preregistered simple analytic plans that focused on straightforward comparisons and regression modeling. We used the Coopersmith Self-Esteem Inventory as our primary dependent variable because it is a well-established measure of explicit self-esteem. Given variation in prior findings, we anticipated a small to medium effect for the self-generated word manipulation if it worked as theorized. We expected that university-educated participants might report somewhat higher self-esteem than those without university experience. We also predicted that a quadratic age term might capture any rise and fall in self-esteem across the adult lifespan. At the same time, we recognized several reasons why effects could be attenuated. One reason is that a single brief writing exercise might not be sufficient to produce detectable changes in a relatively stable trait measure. Another reason is that online measurement introduces noise due to heterogeneous testing environments and potential distractions. Because of these caveats, we framed our hypotheses as directional but tentative. We recruited a community sample using an online questionnaire and allocated participants randomly to experimental condition. We applied standard exclusion criteria to ensure data quality and used conservative statistical thresholds for significance. The following sections describe our methods, analyses, results, and interpretations in detail.

Methods

Participants were recruited via online advertisements and social media postings describing a study about word use and self-perception. Inclusion criteria required participants to be aged 18 years or older and to provide informed consent before starting the questionnaire. A total of 285 individuals initiated the survey. Of these, 29 participants were excluded for failing an attention check item embedded in the survey, seven were excluded for submitting implausibly short completion times indicative of non-engagement, and nine were excluded for large amounts of missing data on key variables. The final analytical sample consisted of 240 participants. The sample included 144 females, 90 males, and 6 participants who reported another gender identity. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 82 years, with a mean age of 36.4 years and a standard deviation of 13.8 years. Regarding educational background, 128 participants reported having attended university and 112 reported that they had not attended university. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by an institutional review board of the host university. All participants provided electronic informed consent prior to participation. Participants were informed that they could discontinue at any time without penalty and that their responses would be anonymized. After consent, participants were randomly assigned by the survey platform to one of two conditions. In the self-descriptor condition participants were presented with instructions to list five positive words that described themselves. The exact instruction read: "Please enter five words that you feel positively describe you as a person. These should be words you would use to describe yourself." In the general-positive condition participants were presented with instructions to list five positive words that were not specifically about them. The exact instruction read: "Please enter five words that you consider positive or desirable, but that are not necessarily about you personally." Participants entered their five words into five separate text fields and were required to provide five entries before continuing. After the word-generation task, participants completed the Coopersmith Self-Esteem Inventory (CSEI) online. The CSEI used in this study was adapted for adult respondents and included 25 items with binary scoring consistent with the standard inventory. Higher total scores on the CSEI indicate higher explicit self-esteem. In addition to the CSEI, participants completed demographic questions about gender, age, education level, and current occupational status. We also included a brief attention check and a question asking participants to rate how seriously they completed the word-listing task on a 7-point scale. Participants who endorsed low seriousness or failed the attention check were excluded from analyses as described above. All survey items were presented in the same order for all participants to maintain procedural consistency. Data cleaning was conducted in a scripted and reproducible manner. Responses to the word-generation task were retained as free-text variables for exploratory analyses but were not used to score the main dependent variable. For the primary analyses we compared mean CSEI scores between the self-descriptor and general-positive conditions using an independent-samples t-test. We verified t-test assumptions by examining distributions, histograms, skewness, kurtosis, and Levene's test for equality of variances. To examine the effect of education within the control condition we conducted an independent-samples t-test comparing CSEI scores between participants who had attended university and those who had not, restricted to the general-positive condition. To test age-related patterns we fit linear and quadratic regression models predicting CSEI score from age. The quadratic model included both age and age-squared terms to capture nonlinearity, and we inspected parameter estimates and confidence intervals. All statistical tests were two-tailed with an alpha level of .05, and effect sizes (Cohen's d and partial eta-squared where applicable) are reported to aid interpretation.

Results

The descriptive statistics for the primary outcome are presented here. Across the full sample the mean CSEI score was 17.9 with a standard deviation of 4.6 on the 25-item scale. Mean scores were similar across experimental conditions. In the self-descriptor condition the mean CSEI score was 18.1 with a standard deviation of 4.5. In the general-positive condition the mean CSEI score was 17.7 with a standard deviation of 4.7. An initial independent-samples t-test examined differences in self-esteem between the two conditions. The t-test indicated that the difference in mean CSEI scores between the self-descriptor and general-positive conditions was not statistically significant. The t statistic was 0.69 with 238 degrees of freedom and a p value of .49. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference ranged from -0.8 to 1.6. The observed Cohen's d for the comparison was 0.09, which is conventionally considered a very small effect size. Levene's test for equality of variances was not significant, supporting the assumption of homogeneity of variance. We also examined whether gender or time spent on the task confounded the comparison, but inclusion of these variables as covariates did not change the null pattern. An exploratory analysis examined whether the reported seriousness of task engagement related to CSEI scores. Self-reported seriousness was modestly correlated with CSEI score (r = .12, p = .062), but this association did not reach conventional significance thresholds. We next tested whether educational background predicted self-esteem within the general-positive (control) condition. Within the control condition the mean CSEI score for participants who had attended university was 17.9 with a standard deviation of 4.6. Those who had not attended university had a mean CSEI score of 17.5 with a standard deviation of 4.8. An independent-samples t-test comparing these two groups was not statistically significant. The t statistic was 0.56 with 110 degrees of freedom and a p value of .58. The 95% confidence interval for the difference spanned -1.2 to 2.0. Cohen's d for the education comparison was 0.08, indicating a negligible effect size. Finally, we examined the association between age and self-esteem using regression models. A simple linear regression predicting CSEI from age produced a small positive slope that was not statistically significant. The linear coefficient for age was 0.03 points per year with a standard error of 0.02 and a p value of .18. To evaluate nonlinear trends we fit a quadratic model including age and age-squared terms. The quadratic term had a coefficient close to zero and was not statistically significant. Specifically, the age-squared coefficient was -0.0009 with a p value of .33. The quadratic model accounted for less than one percent of variance in CSEI score beyond the intercept. Plotting the predicted values from the quadratic model showed a very slight rise in predicted self-esteem across early to middle adulthood followed by a negligible decline in older ages. However, the decline around age sixty was not distinct or statistically reliable in this sample. We also tested alternative specifications including cubic terms and spline regressions, but none produced a statistically robust peak and decline pattern. In sum, none of the primary comparisons or the age-related tests produced statistically significant results in the present dataset.

Discussion

The present study tested whether a brief self-generated positive-word task would increase explicit self-esteem relative to generating general positive words. We also examined whether university attendance related to higher self-esteem within the control condition and whether age showed the often-reported pattern of increase until around sixty followed by decline. Across all analyses the findings were null in the sense that no statistically significant differences or nonlinear age patterns emerged. The lack of a detectable effect for the self-generated word manipulation is notable given the theoretical reasons to expect an effect. The self-reference effect and associative learning principles suggested that generating self-relevant positive descriptors should, in principle, increase self-salience and perhaps shift self-evaluations. Despite this, the brief manipulation used here did not translate into measurable changes on the Coopersmith Self-Esteem Inventory immediately after the task. There are several plausible explanations for this pattern. First, the dose and intensity of the intervention were minimal. Participants completed a single short task consisting of listing only five words. By contrast, many effective interventions to change self-concept or mood involve repeated practice, extended engagement, or multimodal reinforcement. A single self-descriptor listing may be too brief to revise established global self-beliefs that are expressed on the CSEI. Second, the CSEI measures relatively stable aspects of explicit self-esteem. It is possible that the manipulation affected state-level self-evaluations transiently but these short-term shifts did not register on the inventory used. Future studies could include a state self-esteem measure immediately following the task in addition to trait inventories. Third, the online context may have introduced noise that attenuated effects. Participants completed the task in uncontrolled environments with varying levels of distraction, which reduces statistical power to detect small effects. Participants’ engagement varied modestly as indicated by self-reported seriousness. Although we excluded participants who indicated very low seriousness, residual variation in engagement could have blurred potential effects. Fourth, the content of the generated words was not constrained in any way beyond being positive. Some participants may have generated superficially positive words that lack personal resonance, while others may have chosen deeply meaningful descriptors. The heterogeneity in word content implies that the manipulation may have been noisy at the level of psychological processing. Coding the semantic and emotional content of the words might reveal subgroups for whom the intervention was more effective. Fifth, the mechanism by which subliminal evaluative conditioning reportedly works may be qualitatively different from the active generation used here. Subliminal pairing protocols often engage associative learning outside of conscious awareness and may influence implicit self-esteem more than explicit measures. Active generation relies on conscious processing and self-presentation motives that may interact with defensiveness, social desirability, or self-deception. These processes could either amplify or attenuate the intended effect in ways we did not anticipate. Sixth, even if the manipulation had a small effect, our study may have been underpowered to detect effects of very small magnitude. The observed effect size for the primary comparison was close to zero. Detecting such small effects would require much larger samples than the one used here. It is therefore possible that the true effect is small and practically negligible, or else non-existent. Turning to the education comparison, our null result contrasts with some prior literature suggesting academic students show higher self-confidence or self-concept. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. Our classification of "university attendance" versus "no university attendance" collapses diverse educational experiences. Vocational training encompasses a wide range of programs and levels that may include high-status occupations and robust skill acquisition. Moreover, the cross-sectional nature of our data cannot disentangle selection from socialization effects. Individuals who pursue university may differ systematically on personality and socioeconomic traits that relate to self-esteem. In our sample these background variables may have offset one another, producing no net difference. Alternatively, any education-related differences in self-evaluation may be modest and again require larger samples or more precise measurement to detect. Regarding age trends, the present sample did not show the clear peak around age sixty reported in some lifespan studies. This may be attributable to limited representation of older adults in our sample. Even though the sample included participants up to age 82, the density of observations in older decades was relatively low compared with midlife and young adulthood. Cross-sectional designs are also vulnerable to cohort effects, which can obscure age-related processes. Longitudinal data are preferable when attempting to characterize intra-individual trajectories of self-esteem across decades. Cohort-specific experiences, such as differing norms about self-expression and education, may produce patterns in cross-sectional data that do not reflect individual development. The null quadratic finding therefore should not be interpreted as definitive evidence against age-related trends in self-esteem. It is, however, informative about the magnitude of any such effect in an online convenience sample. The study has a number of strengths that increase confidence in the internal validity of the null findings. Random assignment to conditions reduced the likelihood that pre-existing differences drove the comparison between manipulations. Standardized instructions and automated administration minimized experimenter influence on participant responses. We also implemented reasonable quality checks and exclusions to reduce noise from inattentive responding. Despite these strengths, the design also has limitations as already noted. Importantly, the timing of the measurement relative to the intervention may have been suboptimal for capturing change. Future research could modify several aspects of the design to better test the hypotheses. First, researchers could compare repeated sessions of self-descriptor generation across multiple days to examine dose-response effects. Second, they could include both implicit and explicit measures of self-esteem to test whether different systems are affected. Third, laboratory studies with controlled environments could reduce noise and increase sensitivity. Fourth, content analysis of generated words could help identify whether particular themes (e.g., competence, morality, warmth) are more effective in altering self-evaluations. Fifth, including behavioral or physiological indices could help triangulate the effects of word-generation tasks on self-related processing. Sixth, studies could systematically vary self-referential framing to determine whether prompts that elicit autobiographical recall or narrative elaboration boost efficacy. Seventh, researchers might combine generation with evaluative conditioning—pairing self-referent stimuli with positive cues repeatedly—to test additive or interactive effects. Eighth, more precise sampling of older adults would help clarify the lifespan course of self-esteem and the replicability of the peak-and-decline pattern. Ninth, preregistration and adequately powered replication efforts would clarify whether small effects are reliable. Tenth, the role of individual differences such as baseline self-esteem, trait neuroticism, and social desirability should be examined as potential moderators. In practical terms, the null findings caution against assuming that a single brief online exercise of listing five positive self-descriptive words will meaningfully change explicit self-esteem for a general population sample. That said, null results contribute to cumulative science by delineating the boundary conditions under which interventions do not appear to work. They also suggest that interventions aiming to alter self-evaluations may need greater intensity, personalization, or multimodal engagement. The present study contributes to the literature by directly testing a theoretically grounded manipulation and reporting the outcome transparently. It adds empirical clarity to the question of whether simple self-affirming word generation is sufficient to move self-esteem measures in adults. By reporting effect sizes and confidence intervals, we provide information useful for planning future work. The data indicate that any immediate effect of a single self-generated word exercise on explicit global self-esteem is small at best. In conclusion, our study found no evidence that generating five positive words about oneself increased global self-esteem relative to generating positive words in general. We also found no difference in self-esteem by university attendance in the control group and no reliable quadratic age pattern. Future work that addresses the limitations identified here can further clarify whether self-generated positive content can be harnessed effectively to support enduring self-esteem change.
