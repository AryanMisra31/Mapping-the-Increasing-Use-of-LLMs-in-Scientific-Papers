Introduction

Participation in organized and informal sport is a common experience for many individuals across the lifespan. Engagement in athletic activity has been linked to a range of psychological outcomes. These outcomes include improvements in mood, self-concept, and perceived competence. Researchers have repeatedly found that regular involvement in sport can be associated with higher levels of self-esteem. Work in developmental and social psychology has emphasized the role of mastery experiences and social feedback within sport contexts in shaping personal beliefs about ability. Coaches, teammates, and competition structure provide opportunities for skill development and social recognition. These experiences can foster a sense of efficacy that generalizes beyond the sporting domain. Confidence, defined here as a belief in one’s capabilities to achieve desired outcomes, is a construct that has been frequently examined in sport psychology and general psychological research. Confidence is often treated as adaptive. It supports goal persistence, task engagement, and adaptive coping with challenges. In sport-specific contexts, confidence has been associated with better performance, greater enjoyment, and longer-term participation. Beyond performance outcomes, confidence is implicated in broader well-being and social functioning. It can influence academic achievement, interpersonal relationships, and occupational success. Given these broad links, studying the relationship between sports engagement and confidence remains a theoretically and practically important endeavor.

Less attention has been paid to potential negative psychological sequelae of sport involvement. One such area that has been comparatively neglected is appearance-related attitudes that may become intensified through athletic participation. Sport often places a premium on body function, physique, and appearance. Uniforms and performance ideals create contexts in which the body is both instrument and object. In some sports, the aesthetic presentation of the body is directly judged. In others, body shape and size are indirectly associated with performance expectations. These contextual features can place selective pressure on athletes to attend closely to appearance and to evaluate themselves based on body-related standards. This heightened attention to appearance can in some cases promote healthy body care and investment. In other instances, it can contribute to excessive preoccupation with looks and self-presentation.

Vanity, operationalized in this study as an excessively high valuation of one’s appearance and abilities that is primarily self-focused and often tied to social comparison, is a construct that could be potentiated by intensive sport involvement. Vanity differs from adaptive confidence in important ways. Confidence primarily concerns realistic appraisals of ability and a stable belief in capability that supports functioning across contexts. Vanity is more concerned with outward appearance, social valuation, and an inflated sense of the importance of looks and external validation. While vanity may co-occur with confidence, it can also involve maladaptive social comparison and an overemphasis on superficial attributes. The psychological literature has explored related constructs such as narcissism, body image preoccupation, and social vanity, but the specific interplay of sport commitment, confidence, and vanity remains under-specified.

Some prior studies have suggested that athletes report higher body satisfaction than non-athletes, possibly due to functional appreciation for what their bodies can do. Other research has suggested that athletes in certain sports may be at greater risk for body dissatisfaction and unhealthy appearance-related behaviors. The mixed pattern in these studies points to nuance. The type of sport, the level of competitiveness, cultural norms within a sport, and individual differences may moderate how sport involvement relates to appearance concerns. For example, athletes in aesthetic sports such as gymnastics or figure skating often report greater body scrutiny than athletes in team sports that emphasize power and strategy. Similarly, sports that require specific weight classes or body sizes, such as wrestling or distance running, can produce pressures that increase both body monitoring and potentially maladaptive vanity.

Another body of work has examined the relationship between confidence and traits related to vanity. Some findings suggest that modest levels of self-enhancement and self-promotion can be associated with confidence and positive functioning. However, excessive self-focused attention and inflated self-views can be associated with interpersonal difficulties and fragile self-concept. The distinction between healthy confidence and problematic vanity has theoretical importance because interventions that aim to boost confidence via sport participation might have unintended consequences if they simultaneously encourage excessive focus on appearance or external validation. This possible trade-off has been underexplored.

The present study responds to several gaps in the literature. First, much of the existing research has focused on binary comparisons between athletes and non-athletes, or between sport types, rather than examining individual differences in commitment to sport within a mixed sample. Commitment, as a continuous measure, reflects the degree to which an individual invests time, identity, and resources into sport participation. Examining commitment allows for more granular analysis of how varying levels of involvement are related to psychological outcomes. Second, prior research has often measured confidence and appearance-related attitudes without explicitly assessing vanity as a distinct construct. By including a measure of vanity alongside confidence, the present study aims to identify whether sport commitment relates to both positive self-beliefs and potentially problematic appearance valuation. Third, the majority of prior studies have used cross-sectional designs without carefully mapping the pattern of interrelationships among commitment, confidence, and vanity. A correlational approach that examines pairwise associations provides a necessary first step to define the pattern of relationships that future longitudinal and experimental work can probe.

The current project therefore uses a correlational design to investigate three specific associations. The first association of interest is between commitment to sport and confidence. Based on prior literature that links sport involvement to perceived competence and self-efficacy, it is hypothesized that higher sport commitment will be positively associated with greater confidence. The second association of interest is between commitment to sport and vanity. Given the dual pathways by which sport can both enhance functional body appreciation and increase appearance scrutiny, it is hypothesized that greater sport commitment may be associated with higher levels of vanity, although the effect size may be modest. The third association of interest is between confidence and vanity. Because these constructs can co-occur but are conceptually distinct, it is hypothesized that confidence and vanity will be positively correlated, reflecting overlapping self-evaluative processes, but not so highly correlated as to be redundant.

To address these hypotheses, the present study recruited a sample of university students and administered an original self-report instrument designed to assess sport commitment, confidence, and vanity. The choice of a university sample reflects the accessibility of a population with a range of sport involvement experiences, from casual recreational participation to varsity-level training. While student samples have limitations in generalizability, they provide a practical first step to explore the pattern of relationships among variables. The instrument was constructed drawing on established conceptualizations of commitment, confidence, and vanity, and items were pilot tested for clarity. Analyses included descriptive statistics, reliability estimates for the scales, and Pearson product-moment correlations among the scale scores. Additional exploratory analyses examined potential moderating variables such as gender, given prior work showing gender differences in body-related attitudes and sport participation patterns.

This study offers several potential contributions. By simultaneously assessing commitment, confidence, and vanity, it can clarify whether sport involvement is uniformly associated with adaptive self-beliefs or whether there are concurrent increases in appearance-focused valuation. By measuring commitment continuously, the study can detect incremental associations across levels of involvement. This approach complements existing sport psychology literature that tends to dichotomize participants. By reporting reliability estimates and basic distributional characteristics, the study also provides methodological groundwork for subsequent research that may use similar measures. Finally, the study’s correlational findings can inform both theoretical refinement—by clarifying how confidence and vanity relate—and practical guidance for coaches, educators, and mental health professionals working with athletes. If higher commitment is related to both increased confidence and increased vanity, practitioners may wish to maximize the former while mitigating the latter through targeted education or interventions.

In sum, the present investigation tests three correlated hypotheses using a cross-sectional survey approach with a sample of 100 university students. The first hypothesis predicts a positive correlation between sport commitment and confidence. The second hypothesis predicts a positive correlation between sport commitment and vanity. The third hypothesis predicts a positive correlation between confidence and vanity. Given the exploratory nature of the vanity analyses and the mixed prior findings on appearance-related outcomes, the study emphasizes cautious interpretation and the need for future longitudinal work to establish developmental trajectories. The next section details the methodology employed, including participant recruitment, instrument construction, data collection procedures, and planned analytical strategies. The subsequent Results section reports scale properties, descriptive statistics, and the correlation matrix. The Discussion interprets these findings, discusses limitations, and outlines directions for future research and practical applications.

Methods

Participants were 100 undergraduate students recruited from a mid-sized university. Recruitment occurred via campus email lists and flyers posted around student common areas. Participation was voluntary and not for course credit. The final sample comprised 100 individuals after excluding incomplete questionnaires that had more than 20 percent missing data. The sample included 55 females and 45 males. Ages ranged from 18 to 26 years. The mean age was 20.6 years with a standard deviation of 2.7 years. Participants represented a variety of academic majors and reported varying levels of sport participation, including recreational, intramural, and varsity involvement.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the university’s institutional review board. All participants provided informed consent prior to completing the survey. The informed consent document explained the study aims at a general level, described the voluntary nature of participation, and informed participants that their responses would be anonymized. No personally identifying data were collected beyond demographic information such as age and gender. Participants were free to withdraw at any time without penalty.

The survey instrument was developed specifically for this project to assess three primary constructs: commitment to sport, confidence, and vanity. The questionnaire contained three sections: a demographic section, a sport commitment section, and a confidence-and-vanity section. The demographic section asked about age, gender, year in school, and basic sport involvement (type of sport, years of participation, whether participation was recreational or competitive). The sport commitment section consisted of 10 items designed to assess intensity of involvement, personal identification with sport, time investment, and emotional attachment to sport activities. Items included statements such as “I organize my schedule around my sport activities,” “Playing sport is a central part of who I am,” and “I regularly train or practice for my sport.” Respondents indicated agreement on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Items were summed to create a sport commitment score, with higher scores indicating greater commitment.

The confidence-and-vanity section contained two subscales. The confidence subscale included eight items assessing perceived ability, certainty in performance, and general self-efficacy in both sport and non-sport domains. Example items included “I am confident in my ability to learn new physical skills,” and “I feel certain about my capacity to perform under pressure.” Responses were recorded on the same five-point Likert scale and summed to yield a confidence score. The vanity subscale consisted of eight items designed to tap excessive valuation of appearance and abilities, preoccupation with how one looks, and the tendency to seek external validation for appearance. Example items included “I often think about how good I look,” “My appearance is one of the most important aspects of my life,” and “I feel upset if others do not recognize my abilities.” The vanity items used the same Likert scoring, and higher summed scores indicated higher reported vanity.

Items for the three scales were generated based on conceptual definitions in the literature and iteratively refined following pilot testing with a separate group of 20 students for clarity. Pilot participants provided feedback on item wording and interpretability, which led to minor revisions to improve comprehension. No items were removed following the pilot, but phrasing adjustments were made to reduce ambiguity. The final battery had 26 substantive items across the three scales, plus demographic questions.

Data collection was conducted in a quiet campus room and online using a secure survey platform. Approximately 60 percent of participants completed the survey on paper during drop-in sessions scheduled in a campus study room. The remaining 40 percent completed the survey online during the same data collection period. The procedures for in-person and online administration were standardized in terms of instructions and time allocation. The average completion time was approximately 12 minutes. To minimize response bias, the presentation of items alternated between positively and negatively worded statements where appropriate, and reverse-scored items were included in the confidence and vanity scales to reduce acquiescence bias.

Prior to conducting correlation analyses, data were screened for missingness, normality, and outliers. Cases with more than 20 percent missing responses were excluded. For the retained sample, missing values on individual items were imputed by mean substitution for that item within the sample if fewer than two items were missing for a participant in a given scale. Scale scores were computed by summing item responses after reverse coding where applicable. Internal consistency for each scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Descriptive statistics including means, standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis were computed to evaluate distributional characteristics.

The primary analysis plan involved Pearson product-moment correlations among sport commitment, confidence, and vanity scale scores. Pearson correlation was chosen due to the continuous nature of the summed scale scores and the sample size, which supported parametric estimation. Prior to running correlations, scatterplots were inspected for linearity and homoscedasticity, and Shapiro-Wilk tests were performed to assess univariate normality. Given that Pearson correlation is robust to moderate departures from normality in samples of the present size, the analysis proceeded as planned with reporting of parametric correlation coefficients. As a sensitivity check, Spearman rank-order correlations were also computed to ensure that results were consistent when using a nonparametric method.

In addition to zero-order correlations, exploratory multiple regression analyses were conducted. Two regressions were run. The first used confidence as the dependent variable and sport commitment and vanity as independent variables, to explore the combined predictive value of sport engagement and vanity on reported confidence. The second used vanity as the dependent variable and sport commitment and confidence as predictors. Regression assumptions including multicollinearity, linearity, normality of residuals, and homoscedasticity were evaluated. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were computed to check for multicollinearity. These regressions were treated as exploratory and interpreted cautiously given the correlational design.

Given documented gender differences in body-related attitudes, supplementary analyses tested whether gender moderated the associations among the primary variables. This involved computing correlations separately for males and females and testing for differences using Fisher r-to-z transformations. Age and years of sport participation were examined as potential covariates by computing partial correlations controlling for these variables. All statistical tests used a two-tailed alpha of .05. Analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and effect sizes were reported alongside significance tests. The overall analytic approach aimed to be transparent and replicable.

Results

Data screening and preliminary analyses were conducted first. No participant had more than 20 percent missing data after initial data collection and therefore all 100 participants were retained. For participants with one or two missing items on a given scale, mean substitution for that item was applied as described. Descriptive statistics for the three scales were computed. The sport commitment scale had a mean score of 36.4 and a standard deviation of 7.8 on a potential range of 10 to 50. The confidence scale had a mean of 28.1 and a standard deviation of 5.9 on a potential range of 8 to 40. The vanity scale had a mean of 22.5 and a standard deviation of 6.2 on a potential range of 8 to 40.

Examination of skewness and kurtosis values indicated acceptable distributional characteristics for the sport commitment and confidence scales. The vanity scale showed mild positive skew but remained within acceptable bounds for parametric testing. Shapiro-Wilk tests identified no severe departures from normality for sport commitment or confidence, while the vanity scale produced a modest deviation; however, given the sample size, Pearson correlations were judged to be robust. Scatterplots for each pair of variables indicated linear relationships and no clear nonlinearity. Inspection for univariate outliers using z-scores found two potential outliers on the sport commitment scale, but these did not substantially influence the correlations when removed and thus the full sample was retained for reporting.

Internal consistency reliability estimates were computed for each scale. Cronbach’s alpha for the sport commitment scale was .87, indicating high internal consistency. The confidence scale had an alpha of .82, consistent with acceptable reliability. The vanity scale produced an alpha of .79, which is acceptable for exploratory research. Item-total correlations were generally positive and indicated that all items contributed meaningfully to their respective scales.

The primary Pearson correlation analyses among the three scales were conducted next. Sport commitment was positively correlated with confidence. The Pearson correlation coefficient for sport commitment and confidence was r = .45. This correlation was statistically significant at p < .001. The effect size is a medium-sized positive association, suggesting that greater commitment to sport was associated with higher self-reported confidence among participants in the sample.

Sport commitment was also positively correlated with vanity. The Pearson correlation coefficient for sport commitment and vanity was r = .36. This correlation was statistically significant at p = .0003. This medium-small positive association indicates that higher levels of sport commitment were associated with higher reported vanity. The association was somewhat smaller in magnitude than that between sport commitment and confidence.

Confidence and vanity were positively correlated as well. The Pearson correlation coefficient for confidence and vanity was r = .41. This correlation was statistically significant at p < .001. This medium-sized positive association suggests that higher confidence scores tended to co-occur with higher vanity scores in the sample.

As a robustness check, Spearman rank-order correlations were computed. Spearman correlations were directionally consistent with the Pearson results and produced similar magnitudes: sport commitment and confidence rho = .43 (p < .001); sport commitment and vanity rho = .34 (p = .0007); confidence and vanity rho = .39 (p < .001). These nonparametric findings suggest that the observed relationships were not an artifact of distributional assumptions.

Supplementary analyses examined the potential role of gender. Correlations computed separately for males and females indicated that the associations were present within both groups but varied somewhat in magnitude. For males, sport commitment and confidence correlated at r = .47 (p < .001), sport commitment and vanity at r = .33 (p = .03), and confidence and vanity at r = .38 (p = .01). For females, sport commitment and confidence correlated at r = .42 (p = .002), sport commitment and vanity correlated at r = .38 (p = .004), and confidence and vanity correlated at r = .44 (p < .001). Fisher r-to-z tests did not indicate statistically significant differences in these correlations across gender at the .05 level, suggesting that the associations are present in both males and females within the sample.

Partial correlations controlling for years of sport participation and age were conducted to assess the possibility that the observed associations were driven by more extensive experience rather than commitment per se. When controlling for years of sport participation, the partial correlation between sport commitment and confidence remained significant at r_partial = .40 (p < .001). The partial correlation between sport commitment and vanity controlling for years of participation remained significant at r_partial = .31 (p = .002). The partial correlation between confidence and vanity controlling for years of participation remained significant at r_partial = .38 (p < .001). Controlling for age produced similar patterns, with small attenuation of coefficients but all remaining statistically significant. These partial correlation results suggest that the relationships among the primary variables are not solely accounted for by age or cumulative years of participation.

Exploratory multiple regression analyses were conducted as described in the Methods. In the first model, confidence was the dependent variable and sport commitment and vanity were entered as predictors. The overall model was significant, F(2,97) = 21.6, p < .001, and accounted for approximately 30 percent of the variance in confidence (adjusted R^2 = .30). Within this model, sport commitment was a significant positive predictor of confidence (standardized beta = .35, p < .001). Vanity also contributed positively to the prediction of confidence, though to a lesser extent (standardized beta = .26, p = .007). Variance inflation factors were below 1.5, indicating no problematic multicollinearity.

In the second regression model, vanity served as the dependent variable and sport commitment and confidence were predictors. This model was also significant, F(2,97) = 14.5, p < .001, and accounted for roughly 23 percent of the variance in vanity (adjusted R^2 = .23). Sport commitment emerged as a significant positive predictor of vanity (standardized beta = .28, p = .002). Confidence was also a significant positive predictor in this model (standardized beta = .31, p = .001). These results indicate that both sport commitment and confidence uniquely contributed to variance in vanity scores.

Residual diagnostics for the regression models indicated approximate normality of residuals and acceptable homoscedasticity. No influential cases with high Cook’s distance values were identified. Given the cross-sectional design, these regression results should not be interpreted as causal but rather as an indication of overlapping associations among the constructs.

Taken together, the results support the hypotheses that sport commitment is positively associated with both confidence and vanity, and that confidence and vanity themselves are positively related. Effect sizes were in the small-to-medium range and robust to several sensitivity checks, including nonparametric correlations, partial correlations controlling for age and years of participation, and gender-stratified analyses. The internal consistency of the scales was acceptable, supporting the reliability of the measures in the current sample.

Discussion

The present study examined associations among commitment to sport, confidence, and vanity in a sample of 100 university students. The primary findings were threefold. First, sport commitment was positively correlated with confidence. Second, sport commitment was positively correlated with vanity. Third, confidence and vanity were positively correlated with one another. These associations were statistically significant and of small-to-medium magnitude, and they held across a range of supplementary analyses including nonparametric checks, partial correlations controlling for age and years of sport participation, and gender-specific evaluations. The internal consistencies of the measures were acceptable, which supports the reliability of the inferences drawn from the observed patterns.

The positive correlation between sport commitment and confidence aligns with a substantial body of literature suggesting that engagement in sport and physical activity can foster perceived competence and self-efficacy. The present work adds to this literature by conceptualizing sport involvement as commitment rather than a simple athlete-versus-non-athlete dichotomy. This approach highlights that gradations of investment in sport—reflected in time spent, emotional attachment, and identity—are meaningfully associated with confidence. From a theoretical standpoint, these findings are consistent with social-cognitive models that posit mastery experiences as crucial sources of self-efficacy. Individuals who commit more to sport are likely to accrue more practice, receive more feedback, and experience more opportunities for achievement and social reinforcement, all of which can bolster confidence.

The observed positive relationship between sport commitment and vanity is more complex in its interpretation. On one hand, the association is consistent with the idea that greater involvement in sport increases attention to the body and appearance. Uniforms, public displays, and social comparisons that are common in many sport contexts may heighten valuation of appearance-related attributes. The positive association suggests that commitment to sport can coincide with greater preoccupation with looks and external validation related to appearance. This pattern is not necessarily surprising, given that part of sport culture often revolves around visible bodies and performance aesthetics.

On the other hand, it is important to contextualize the vanity construct used here. The measure assessed self-reported emphasis on appearance and perceived importance of looks, which may encompass both adaptive and maladaptive elements. Some aspects of vanity as measured could reflect legitimate pride in physical condition or functional appreciation for what the body can do. For instance, an athlete who values their physique because it reflects training effort may score higher on vanity items without necessarily reflecting psychological maladjustment. Nevertheless, the positive link between sport commitment and vanity raises the possibility that increased focus on appearance could accompany otherwise beneficial psychological gains. This duality suggests that sport participation may confer adaptive confidence while simultaneously increasing susceptibility to appearance-focused valuation.

The positive correlation between confidence and vanity further informs this duality. The two constructs were related, but not so highly correlated as to be indistinguishable. This indicates that confidence and vanity share some common variance—possibly related to general self-evaluation—while retaining distinct aspects. Confidence likely reflects a stable sense of competence that supports functioning across multiple domains. Vanity, in contrast, taps into the degree to which one prioritizes appearance and external affirmation. The co-occurrence of higher confidence and higher vanity might reflect a broader self-enhancement orientation in some individuals. Alternatively, it could indicate that confidence gained through sport participation makes individuals more comfortable expressing pride in appearance, which might register on vanity scales.

These findings have both theoretical and practical implications. Theoretically, the pattern reinforces the notion that psychosocial outcomes of sport participation are multifaceted. Sport can foster positive intrapersonal resources such as confidence, but it may also shape social-comparative and appearance-focused attitudes. The results encourage theoretical models of sport psychology to incorporate both adaptive and potentially maladaptive self-related attitudes when considering outcomes of sport engagement. Practically, coaches, athletic trainers, and university sport programs might consider interventions that retain the confidence-building benefits of sport while explicitly addressing appearance-related pressures. Educational programming that emphasizes body functionality, health, and intrinsic motivation, rather than solely aesthetic outcomes or external validation, may help mitigate the emergence of excessive vanity among highly committed athletes.

Several limitations of the study warrant careful consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although sport commitment was associated with higher confidence and vanity, it is equally plausible that individuals higher in confidence or vanity are more likely to commit to sport. For instance, confident individuals may self-select into athletic pursuits where their perceived competence is affirmed, and individuals who place high value on appearance may be drawn to sports that showcase or enhance their physical attributes. Longitudinal studies are necessary to disentangle directionality and potential reciprocal relationships among these constructs.

Second, the use of a university student sample limits generalizability. University students may have distinct sport participation patterns, developmental contexts, and cultural norms compared to older adults, adolescents, or elite athletes. The sample included a range of sport involvement levels but did not recruit specifically from elite or professional athlete populations. Future research should examine whether the observed associations hold in other populations and across cultural contexts where body ideals and sport cultures differ.

Third, the measures in the present study were self-report and included scales that were developed for the purposes of this research. Although internal consistency estimates were acceptable and pilot testing was conducted, the psychometric properties of the measures require further validation. Future research should aim to use or develop psychometrically validated instruments to measure sport commitment, confidence, and vanity, and to test convergent and discriminant validity with related constructs such as self-esteem, narcissism, and body image dissatisfaction.

Fourth, the vanity construct is complex and can encompass normative pride, healthy body appreciation, and maladaptive preoccupation. The present measure did not explicitly distinguish these facets. Future work could parse vanity into subcomponents, such as pride in performance-related appearance, social vanity related to impression management, and pathological preoccupation that aligns with disordered eating or body dysmorphic tendencies. Differentiating these elements would clarify whether the observed associations reflect healthy identity and pride or problematic appearance concerns.

Fifth, the study did not deeply examine sport-specific moderators. Prior research suggests that sport type (e.g., aesthetic versus non-aesthetic), level of competitiveness, and coach climate can affect body-related outcomes. Although years of participation were considered as a covariate, the current sample size limited the ability to conduct well-powered subgroup analyses by sport type. Larger-scale studies could test whether associations differ by sport domain or by the presence of weight- or appearance-focused coaching messages.

Sixth, cultural and social context likely play major roles in shaping both vanity and confidence. The present study conducted in a single university and likely within a particular cultural setting may not capture broader societal influences such as social media use, which can intensify appearance valuation and social comparison. Future research should assess media exposure, peer norms, and cultural values as potential moderators.

Seventh, the study design did not include behavioral or objective measures. Incorporating behavioral indicators such as frequency of gym use, time spent on appearance-related activities, or performance metrics could enrich the understanding of how commitment, confidence, and vanity manifest in action. Qualitative research might also illuminate the subjective meanings athletes attach to appearance and confidence.

Despite these limitations, the present study contributes meaningfully to the literature by demonstrating that higher commitment to sport is associated with both greater confidence and greater vanity, and that confidence and vanity are positively related. These findings prompt a nuanced perspective: sport participation appears to enhance adaptive self-beliefs while also potentially elevating appearance-related valuation. This dual outcome suggests that practitioners should not assume that sport involvement is unambiguously protective for all aspects of self-concept.

Future research directions include longitudinal studies that track changes in commitment, confidence, and vanity over time; intervention studies that attempt to boost confidence while reducing maladaptive vanity; and psychometric work to refine measures. Studies that examine potential mediators—such as coach feedback style, peer comparison frequency, or social media engagement—could clarify mechanisms linking sport commitment to vanity and confidence. Experimental designs that manipulate feedback or focus on body function versus appearance during sport training could provide causal evidence regarding the malleability of vanity and confidence.

In applied settings, sport programs could incorporate psychoeducation about healthy body image, emphasize functional goals, and train coaches to provide feedback that supports mastery and intrinsic motivation rather than appearance-based praise. Mental health professionals working with athletes might assess for appearance preoccupation even when athletes present with robust confidence and apparent well-being, recognizing that confidence and vanity can coexist.

In conclusion, the current correlational study identifies meaningful associations among sport commitment, confidence, and vanity in a university sample. The results underscore the multifaceted psychological consequences of sport engagement and highlight the importance of attending both to the benefits and potential risks associated with intensive involvement in athletics. Future research that addresses the limitations noted here and tests targeted interventions will be critical to ensuring that sport participation promotes holistic well-being.
