Introduction

Intelligence and memory are two central constructs in cognitive psychology that have long been studied both separately and in relation to one another. Research has consistently demonstrated links between various measures of intelligence and different aspects of memory across the lifespan. Studies in childhood have provided particularly robust evidence that higher intelligence quotient (IQ) scores are associated with better memory performance, including both working memory and long-term memory tasks. The developmental literature indicates that as basic cognitive skills develop in childhood, memory capacity and efficiency often improve in parallel. This co-development has been used to argue that underlying neurocognitive processes support both problem solving and memory encoding, consolidation, and retrieval. The relationship between intelligence and memory has often been examined using objective tests of cognitive performance, but less is known about how individuals’ own perceptions of their memory ability relate to cognitive skills such as verbal, numerical, and spatial reasoning.

Perceived memory ability is an important construct in its own right because subjective beliefs about memory can influence study behavior, strategy use, and motivation in educational contexts. If students believe their memory is poor, they may be less likely to engage in effective study techniques or may rely on compensatory strategies that are less efficient. Conversely, individuals who perceive themselves as having strong memory skills may approach learning tasks with greater confidence and persistence. Perceived memory ability can also affect help-seeking behavior and the use of external memory aids. Thus, understanding the factors that relate to perceived memory ability is important for both theoretical and practical reasons.

Most previous work linking intelligence and memory has focused on objective measures of memory performance rather than on subjective perceptions of memory ability. This is a notable gap because subjective memory judgments are only modestly correlated with objective memory performance in many studies, and they can be influenced by a range of dispositional and contextual factors. For example, mood, fatigue, and self-efficacy beliefs can bias people’s perceptions of their memory independent of actual ability. Therefore, examining how basic cognitive skills relate to perceived memory ability can shed light on whether individuals’ metacognitive beliefs are calibrated to their underlying cognitive capacities.

The developmental literature suggests that basic cognitive skills such as verbal, numerical, and spatial reasoning are foundational building blocks for more complex cognitive operations. Verbal skills support the encoding and retrieval of verbal material, numerical skills are linked with the application of strategies for problem solving and calculation, and spatial skills underpin the mental organization of information and the use of visuospatial strategies for memory. Together, these domains reflect a breadth of cognitive abilities that are often assessed in IQ tests and related standardized measures. When considered jointly, verbal, numerical, and spatial skills provide a broad indicator of cognitive functioning that may relate to how individuals perceive their memory capabilities.

Although the relationship between intelligence and memory has been demonstrated in children and older adults, less attention has been paid to late adolescence and emerging adulthood. The age range from late teens through the twenties is a period characterized by continued cognitive maturation and by educational transitions that place new demands on memory and reasoning. Students in this age range are frequently required to learn and retain complex information, apply reasoning across domains, and adapt study strategies. Because of these circumstances, perceptions of memory ability during this period may be particularly consequential for academic outcomes. Yet the extent to which basic cognitive skills are associated with perceived prospective memory (i.e., remembering to carry out future intentions) and perceived retrospective memory (i.e., remembering past events or information) in this age group remains underexplored.

Prospective and retrospective memory are distinct yet related facets of mnemonic functioning. Prospective memory involves planning, cue detection, and execution of intended actions at the appropriate time or in response to an event. Retrospective memory involves the encoding, storage, and retrieval of information from the past. Individuals may hold differing beliefs about their prospective versus retrospective memory strengths. For example, someone might feel confident in remembering appointments but less confident in recalling factual information, or vice versa. Therefore, a nuanced investigation that separates prospective and retrospective perceived memory is important for understanding the specific associations between basic cognitive skills and subjective memory judgments.

Self-perceptions of memory ability are influenced by multiple factors, and cognitive skills are one plausible source of variation. It is reasonable to hypothesize that individuals who perform better on tasks that tap verbal, numerical, and spatial skills may also perceive themselves as having better memory. This could occur because better cognitive skills facilitate more successful memory experiences, which in turn inform self-assessed memory ability. Alternatively, strong performance in reasoning tasks may bolster general self-efficacy or academic confidence, and such confidence may generalize to beliefs about memory. It is therefore important to examine not only whether there is an association between cognitive skills and perceived memory but also whether this relationship holds when controlling for demographic factors such as age, gender, and educational attainment.

The existing literature on self-perceived memory and cognitive skills in young adults is limited. Some studies have examined subjective memory complaints in older adults and linked them to cognitive test scores and mood, while others have focused on clinical populations. In contrast, studies targeting normative samples of adolescents and emerging adults often emphasize objective performance and cognitive development rather than subjective evaluations of memory. This gap in the literature means that we lack clear evidence about how basic cognitive abilities relate to perceived mnemonic competence in adolescents and emerging adults who are navigating important educational transitions.

Bridging this gap could have meaningful implications for educational practice. If basic cognitive skills predict perceived memory ability, educators and counselors could consider tailoring interventions that both improve foundational cognitive skills and enhance students’ metacognitive awareness. For example, training programs that improve spatial or verbal reasoning might indirectly influence students’ confidence in their memory, which could then affect study behavior and academic outcomes. Conversely, if perceived memory ability is only weakly related to cognitive skills, interventions might need to target metacognitive beliefs and strategy instruction more directly.

The aim of the present study was to investigate whether adolescents aged between 18 and 28 with higher verbal, numerical, and spatial skill ability would perceive themselves to have higher prospective and retrospective memory compared to others. To operationalize verbal, numerical, and spatial skills, we used a brief online battery composed of items modeled after common question types found in standardized reasoning tests, adapted for an online administration context. Perceived memory ability was measured with a self-report questionnaire that separately assessed prospective and retrospective memory perceptions. The study focused on an emerging adult sample because this age group faces heightened academic and life-management demands that make perceptions of memory particularly salient.

We hypothesized that higher composite scores on verbal, numerical, and spatial skill measures (VNSS) would be positively associated with higher perceived memory ability (PMA) overall. We also hypothesized that the association would be present for both prospective and retrospective perceived memory, although the strength of the association might differ across these two domains. Specifically, we anticipated that VNSS would show a somewhat stronger association with perceived prospective memory because prospective memory requires planning and cue-driven execution, processes that rely heavily on higher-order reasoning. We further explored whether individual VNSS subcomponents (verbal, numerical, spatial) would differentially relate to prospective versus retrospective perceived memory.

The study was designed as a correlational investigation using an online questionnaire format, which allowed for efficient data collection from a geographically diverse sample of emerging adults. We included demographic questions to permit control for potential confounds such as age, gender, and level of education or training. Reliability analyses were planned to ensure that the VNSS composite and the PMA scales demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in our online sample. Statistical analyses included bivariate correlations, multiple regression to control for covariates, and exploratory comparisons of subscale associations.

By focusing on perceived memory rather than objective mnemonic performance, the present study contributes to a growing interest in metacognitive outcomes and their antecedents. Understanding whether basic cognitive skills relate to how young people perceive their memory capacity can inform both theoretical models of metacognition and applied efforts to support academic success. If VNSS is meaningfully associated with PMA, interventions may aim to strengthen reasoning skills and metacognitive monitoring to improve both objective and subjective memory outcomes. Conversely, if the relationship is modest, it may point to the need for targeted metacognitive training independent of underlying cognitive skill development.

In summary, the present study investigates the relationship between basic cognitive skills assessed via a brief VNSS battery and perceived prospective and retrospective memory in adolescents aged 18 to 28. The study builds on developmental and cognitive literature linking intelligence and memory but extends this work by focusing on subjective memory assessments in an emerging adult sample. The primary prediction was that higher VNSS scores would be associated with higher PMA scores overall and within each prospective and retrospective domain. Secondary analyses examined the contributions of verbal, numerical, and spatial subskills and tested whether associations persisted after statistically controlling for demographic covariates. The implications of the findings are relevant to theories of metacognition and to educational practice, particularly in designing interventions to support students’ memory-related beliefs and behaviors.

Methods

Participants were recruited through university participant pools, social media advertisements, and community mailing lists. A total of 320 individuals consented and completed the online questionnaire. Inclusion criteria required participants to be aged between 18 and 28 years and to have sufficient English proficiency to complete the tasks. Data from 12 participants were excluded due to incomplete responses on the primary outcome measures, leaving a final sample of 308 participants for analysis. The final sample included 308 participants, of whom 168 identified as female and 140 identified as male; 4 participants identified as non-binary, and these participants were included in analyses but gender was coded using two dummy variables to preserve analytic interpretability. The mean age of participants was 21.4 years with a standard deviation of 2.6 years. The majority of participants (approximately 72%) were current undergraduates, while the remainder were recent graduates or community members enrolled in vocational training.

All procedures were approved by the institutional research ethics committee prior to recruitment. Participants provided informed consent electronically and were told that they could discontinue at any time without penalty. Participation took approximately 25 to 35 minutes and was compensated with either course participation credit or a small electronic gift card. Data were collected anonymously and stored on secure servers accessible only to the research team.

Measures included a VNSS battery to assess verbal, numerical, and spatial skills, a perceived memory ability (PMA) questionnaire that measured prospective and retrospective perceived memory, and a brief demographic form. The VNSS battery consisted of 30 items in total with 10 items per subdomain. Items were adapted from common item formats used in standardized reasoning tests, modified to be appropriate for online administration and to avoid copyrighted materials. Verbal items tapped vocabulary, analogies, and comprehension of sentence relationships. Numerical items involved basic arithmetic, number series completion, and simple quantitative reasoning. Spatial items required mental rotation judgments, pattern completion, and spatial visualization. Items were multiple-choice with four response options. Each correct response was scored as one point and incorrect responses were scored zero. Scores for each subdomain therefore ranged from 0 to 10. A composite VNSS score was computed as the sum of the three subdomain scores, yielding a potential range from 0 to 30.

The perceived memory ability (PMA) questionnaire consisted of 16 items divided into two subscales: prospective perceived memory (8 items) and retrospective perceived memory (8 items). Items were phrased to elicit participants’ self-evaluations of their likelihood of remembering future tasks, appointments, and intentions (prospective items), and their confidence in recalling previously learned information and past events (retrospective items). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater perceived memory ability. Subscale scores were computed as sums of their items and could range from 8 to 40; a total PMA score was also computed by summing across all 16 items for a possible range of 16 to 80.

To assess the internal consistency of the VNSS subscales and the PMA scales, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated. Pilot testing with a small convenience sample prior to the main study indicated that the VNSS items produced acceptable variability and that the PMA items were well understood online. In the final sample, initial item-level descriptive statistics were inspected to identify items with extremely low variance or problematic distributions. No items were removed from the VNSS battery or the PMA questionnaire in the primary analyses.

The procedure involved presenting participants with the informed consent page followed by demographic questions. The VNSS battery was presented first to minimize potential priming from the PMA questionnaire. Items were presented one at a time and participants were advised to respond as quickly and accurately as possible, although there was no time limit to prevent undue pressure. After completion of the VNSS items, participants completed the PMA questionnaire. Finally, participants were debriefed and provided with contact information for the research team should they have questions.

Scoring was completed automatically by the online survey platform, and data were exported for analysis in a statistical software package. For VNSS, subscale scores and the composite score were used in primary analyses. For PMA, total and subscale scores were computed. Demographic variables were coded for use as covariates in multivariate models. Age was treated as a continuous variable. Gender was coded categorically with male and female dummy variables; education level was coded as current undergraduate, graduate/advanced training, or other.

The main analytic strategy involved first reporting descriptive statistics and reliability estimates for all scales. Next, bivariate Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to examine associations between the VNSS composite and PMA total score, as well as between VNSS subscales and PMA subscales. Given the skew and kurtosis statistics in initial diagnostic checks, transformations were considered if any variable violated normality assumptions substantially; however, all primary variables demonstrated acceptable approximation to normal distribution for correlation analyses.

To test the primary hypothesis, multiple linear regression models were used with PMA total score as the dependent variable and VNSS composite score as the primary predictor. Age, gender, and education level were included as covariates to assess whether the association between VNSS and PMA persisted after controlling for demographic factors. Standardized beta coefficients, t statistics, p values, and 95% confidence intervals for coefficients were reported. Model R-squared values and adjusted R-squared values were included to quantify explained variance.

Secondary analyses included separate regressions predicting PMA prospective and PMA retrospective subscale scores from VNSS composite and subscale scores. These analyses aimed to determine whether patterns of association differed between prospective and retrospective perceived memory. Interaction effects were explored to test whether the relationship between VNSS and PMA varied by gender; interaction terms were computed by mean-centering VNSS scores and multiplying by gender dummy variables.

To ensure the robustness of findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted excluding participants at the extreme ends of the VNSS distribution and using nonparametric Spearman correlations to compare results with Pearson correlations. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a conventional alpha level of 0.05 was used to assess significance. Effect sizes were interpreted according to conventional benchmarks, with correlation coefficients of 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively.

Data cleaning steps and analytic choices were pre-registered in an internal analysis plan prior to inspecting bivariate outcomes. Missing data were minimal and were handled using pairwise deletion for correlation analyses and listwise deletion for regression models. The final regression models included 300 to 308 participants, depending on complete case availability for covariates. All analyses were reproducible via a documented code script and summary output files archived with the research team.

Results

Descriptive statistics for key variables are presented first. The VNSS composite score had a mean of 19.8 with a standard deviation of 4.6, with scores ranging from 6 to 29. The verbal subscale mean was 6.8 (SD = 1.9), the numerical subscale mean was 6.5 (SD = 2.1), and the spatial subscale mean was 6.5 (SD = 2.0). The perceived memory ability (PMA) total score had a mean of 56.7 and a standard deviation of 8.3, with observed values ranging from 22 to 78. The prospective PMA subscale mean was 28.4 (SD = 4.5), and the retrospective PMA subscale mean was 28.3 (SD = 4.6). Skewness and kurtosis for the primary variables were within acceptable limits (absolute skew < 1.0 for VNSS composite and PMA total), suggesting that parametric tests were appropriate.

Internal consistency estimates were satisfactory for the scales used. Cronbach’s alpha for the VNSS composite was not applicable directly to the composite of discrete subtests, but internal consistency for each subscale was computed. The verbal subscale showed an alpha of 0.78, the numerical subscale alpha was 0.74, and the spatial subscale alpha was 0.77. The PMA total scale demonstrated high internal consistency with Cronbach’s alpha equal to 0.89. The prospective PMA subscale alpha was 0.86 and the retrospective PMA subscale alpha was 0.84. These reliability estimates indicated acceptable to good internal consistency for the study measures.

Bivariate Pearson correlations were computed between VNSS composite and PMA total and subscale scores. The VNSS composite was positively correlated with PMA total score, r(306) = .32, p < .001. This correlation represented a medium-sized effect according to conventional benchmarks. The composite score was also correlated with prospective PMA, r(306) = .35, p < .001, and with retrospective PMA, r(306) = .28, p < .001. These results supported the primary hypothesis that greater verbal, numerical, and spatial skills relate to higher perceived memory ability.

Correlations between the VNSS subscales and the PMA subscales were examined to assess domain-specific associations. The verbal subscale correlated with PMA total at r = .28, p < .001, with prospective PMA at r = .30, p < .001, and with retrospective PMA at r = .25, p < .001. The numerical subscale showed smaller but significant correlations: with PMA total r = .15, p = .007; with prospective PMA r = .17, p = .003; and with retrospective PMA r = .12, p = .032. The spatial subscale correlated with PMA total r = .24, p < .001, with prospective PMA r = .27, p < .001, and with retrospective PMA r = .20, p < .001. These patterns indicated that verbal and spatial skills had stronger relationships with perceived memory than numerical skills did, although numerical skills still showed a modest positive association.

Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted with PMA total score as the dependent variable. In the primary model, VNSS composite score was entered as the predictor while controlling for age, gender, and education level. Results indicated that VNSS composite remained a significant predictor of PMA total after accounting for covariates. Specifically, the standardized beta for VNSS composite was β = .31, t(300) = 6.01, p < .001, 95% CI for β = [.22, .40]. The overall model explained 12.4% of variance in PMA total, R2 = .124, adjusted R2 = .114. Among covariates, age did not significantly predict PMA total (β = −.03, p = .62), and gender dummy variables were not significant predictors when included together (female β = .05, p = .14; non-binary β = .02, p = .81), although sample sizes for non-binary respondents were small. Education level showed a weak but non-significant relationship with PMA total after accounting for VNSS composite.

Separate regression models were run to predict prospective and retrospective PMA subscales. When predicting prospective PMA, VNSS composite was a significant predictor controlling for covariates, β = .34, t(300) = 6.68, p < .001, 95% CI = [.25, .43]. The model explained 14.7% of variance in prospective PMA, R2 = .147, adjusted R2 = .138. For retrospective PMA, VNSS composite remained a significant predictor but with a slightly lower standardized beta, β = .27, t(300) = 5.05, p < .001, 95% CI = [.18, .36], and the model accounted for 9.1% of variance, R2 = .091, adjusted R2 = .081. These results suggested that the association between VNSS and perceived prospective memory was somewhat stronger than the association with perceived retrospective memory.

To examine the unique contributions of verbal, numerical, and spatial subscales, a multiple regression with the three VNSS subscales entered simultaneously as predictors of PMA total was conducted. Verbal ability had the largest independent contribution (β = .20, t = 3.86, p < .001), spatial ability made a moderate independent contribution (β = .17, t = 3.29, p = .001), and numerical ability showed a small and marginal contribution when controlling for the other two (β = .09, t = 1.79, p = .074). The combined subscales explained 13.6% of variance in PMA total, R2 = .136.

Interaction analyses were performed to explore whether gender moderated the relationship between VNSS composite and PMA total. Interaction terms were computed by mean-centering VNSS composite and multiplying by gender dummy variables. The VNSS × female interaction was not significant, β = −.02, p = .67, indicating that the relationship between VNSS and PMA did not differ meaningfully for female participants. Similarly, the VNSS × non-binary interaction was non-significant, β = .04, p = .58, though power to detect interactions involving small groups was limited.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of findings. Excluding the top and bottom 5% of VNSS composite scores did not materially change the pattern of results; the correlation between VNSS composite and PMA total in the truncated sample remained significant at r = .30, p < .001. Spearman rank-order correlations produced similar outcomes to Pearson correlations, indicating that results were not driven by outliers or non-normality. Additionally, when using a hierarchical regression approach that entered demographic covariates at Step 1 and VNSS composite at Step 2, the inclusion of VNSS composite produced a significant incremental increase in explained variance, ΔR2 = .105, p < .001.

Exploratory analyses examined whether any single VNSS subscale more strongly predicted prospective versus retrospective PMA by running separate subscale regressions for each PMA domain. Verbal ability was a stronger predictor of retrospective PMA (β = .22, p < .001) than numerical ability (β = .11, p = .028), while spatial ability was marginally more predictive of prospective PMA (β = .20, p < .001) than retrospective PMA (β = .14, p = .007). These results suggested some domain-specific patterns where spatial skill related particularly to prospective memory perceptions and verbal skill related more to retrospective memory perceptions.

Checks on regression assumptions, including inspection of residual plots and tests for heteroscedasticity, indicated that model assumptions were adequately met. Variance inflation factors were below 2.0 for all predictors, suggesting no problematic multicollinearity. Cook’s distance values revealed no cases exerting undue influence on the regression estimates. Given these diagnostics, the reported regression coefficients were considered reliable estimators of the population associations in this sample.

Taken together, the results supported the primary prediction that higher verbal, numerical, and spatial skills are associated with higher perceived memory ability among adolescents and emerging adults. The association was evident for both prospective and retrospective perceived memory domains, with somewhat stronger links for prospective memory. Verbal and spatial skills appeared to contribute most strongly to perceived memory, whereas numerical skills showed a smaller association.

Discussion

The current study investigated whether basic cognitive skills encompassing verbal, numerical, and spatial reasoning (VNSS) are associated with perceived memory ability (PMA) among adolescents and emerging adults aged 18 to 28. Using an online battery of VNSS items and a self-report measure of prospective and retrospective perceived memory, we found that higher VNSS composite scores were significantly associated with higher overall PMA. This relationship remained significant after controlling for age, gender, and education level. The association was evident for both prospective and retrospective memory perceptions, with a somewhat stronger relationship for prospective PMA. Analyses of VNSS subcomponents indicated that verbal and spatial skills contributed more to perceived memory ability than numerical skills, although numerical skills were modestly related to PMA as well. These findings extend prior developmental work linking cognitive skill and memory by showing that even in late adolescence and emerging adulthood, basic reasoning abilities relate to individuals’ beliefs about their memory strengths and weaknesses.

The observed positive association between VNSS and PMA aligns with theoretical perspectives that propose a shared cognitive foundation for reasoning and memory processes. One interpretation of the findings is that stronger verbal, numerical, and spatial abilities lead to more successful memory experiences, which then inform individuals’ subjective evaluations of their memory capacity. For example, someone with superior verbal reasoning may encode verbal material more effectively and retrieve it more readily, leading to a growing sense of competence in remembering facts and verbal content. Similarly, spatial reasoning could facilitate organization and visualization strategies that improve remembering intentions and tasks, thereby enhancing perceived prospective memory. These mechanisms are plausible and consistent with the domain-specific correlations observed in our data.

The stronger link between VNSS and prospective PMA compared to retrospective PMA is an intriguing aspect of the findings. Prospective memory tasks often require planning, monitoring, and cue detection—processes that demand executive control and higher-order reasoning. Spatial skills, in particular, may support the mental representation of plans and the contextual encoding of cues that trigger intended actions. The somewhat greater association with prospective PMA suggests that individuals’ reasoning skills play a crucial role in how they assess their ability to remember to perform future actions. Educationally, this implies that students who possess stronger reasoning abilities may feel more confident in their ability to manage schedules, remember deadlines, and carry out planned tasks—capacities that are essential for academic success.

Our finding that verbal skills were a robust predictor of retrospective PMA also has theoretical plausibility. Retrospective memory often involves the encoding and retrieval of verbal information such as lecture content, textbook material, and lecture notes, so verbal reasoning ability may directly support such processes. When individuals experience successful retrieval of verbal material, they may develop a stronger belief in their retrospective memory. Conversely, weaknesses in verbal reasoning may manifest as frequent retrieval failures or misunderstandings, undermining confidence. The domain-specific patterns observed reinforce the notion that different cognitive skills contribute differentially to how people judge their memory capacities across domains.

The modest relationship between numerical skills and PMA indicates that mathematical reasoning contributes to perceived memory to a lesser extent than verbal or spatial abilities. This pattern could reflect the fact that many everyday memory demands in educational contexts are verbal and spatial in nature rather than numerical. However, numerical skills may still support memory through logical structuring, sequencing, and the use of mnemonic strategies that rely on ordered relationships. The weaker association might also reflect variability in participants’ exposure to numerically demanding tasks; some students may rarely engage with numerical materials in their studies, which could attenuate the relationship between numerical reasoning and perceived memory.

It is important to situate these findings within the broader literature on subjective memory and metacognition. Previous studies have often reported only modest correlations between subjective memory judgments and objective memory performance, suggesting that people’s beliefs about their memory are influenced by multiple factors including affect, personality, and contextual experience. Our results contribute to this literature by demonstrating that basic cognitive skills account for a meaningful portion of variance in perceived memory ability, albeit not the majority. In our sample, VNSS composite explained approximately 10–15% of variability in PMA, indicating that while cognitive ability matters, a large proportion of subjective memory beliefs is shaped by other influences.

The practical implications for education merit attention. If students’ perceived memory abilities are linked to their underlying reasoning skills, then interventions that aim to strengthen verbal and spatial reasoning might have downstream benefits for students’ confidence in managing learning tasks. Enhancing perceived prospective memory confidence, for example, may improve adherence to study schedules and completion of assignments. However, given that VNSS accounts for a modest share of PMA variance, educators should also consider direct metacognitive training that targets beliefs about memory and teaches effective mnemonic and organizational strategies. Combining cognitive skills training with metacognitive instruction could be particularly effective in promoting both objective learning outcomes and students’ confidence.

Several limitations of the study should be acknowledged. First, the correlational design precludes causal inferences. It is possible that higher perceived memory ability leads individuals to engage more in cognitive tasks and thereby strengthen VNSS over time, or that a third variable influences both. Longitudinal and experimental approaches would be necessary to disentangle directionality. Second, the VNSS battery was composed of brief, adapted items modeled after common IQ test formats rather than full standardized assessments. While the subscales demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, comprehensive standardized tests might yield different effect sizes. Third, the PMA measure relied on self-report and is therefore subject to biases such as social desirability and mood effects. Including objective memory tasks alongside subjective measures in future research would clarify how perceived and actual memory performance relate to VNSS.

Another limitation pertains to the sample composition. Although the sample size was adequate for the analyses performed and included a range of educational statuses, the sample was relatively homogenous with respect to being predominantly undergraduate and likely to be higher education seekers. As a result, generalization to non-student populations or to those with different educational backgrounds should be made cautiously. Future studies should recruit more diverse samples with respect to socioeconomic status, academic discipline, and cultural background to assess the robustness of the observed relationships.

The present study also did not measure potential psychological mediators such as general self-efficacy, academic self-concept, or mood states, all of which may influence perceived memory. It remains possible that the relationship between VNSS and PMA is partly mediated by confidence or self-concept; for instance, individuals with strong reasoning skills might possess higher general cognitive self-efficacy, which then elevates their perceived memory ability. Future research should incorporate measures of self-efficacy, metacognitive knowledge, and affect to test mediation hypotheses and to build a more comprehensive model of subjective memory beliefs.

Future research directions are numerous. Longitudinal designs could clarify developmental trajectories and causal pathways between basic cognitive skills and perceived memory. Intervention studies could test whether training in verbal or spatial reasoning leads to changes in perceived memory and whether such changes translate into improved academic behaviors and outcomes. Experimental manipulations of perceived memory (e.g., through feedback or strategy training) could also reveal whether changes in PMA alter objective memory performance or study habits. Additionally, neurocognitive investigations might examine whether individual differences in neural networks associated with reasoning and memory correspond to variations in perceived memory ability.

Exploration of domain-specificity offers another promising avenue. Our results hinted at differential relations between VNSS subcomponents and prospective versus retrospective PMA. Future research could test these domain-specific hypotheses more directly by using domain-aligned memory tasks (e.g., verbal academic recall vs. visuospatial prospective tasks) alongside subjective measures. This approach would help clarify whether people’s subjective beliefs are calibrated more closely to specific types of cognitive demand.

The educational implications extend beyond intervention design. Understanding that students’ perceptions of their memory are partially grounded in basic cognitive skills suggests that assessments and counseling should consider both objective abilities and subjective beliefs. Educators could incorporate brief screening tools for VNSS and PMA to identify students who may benefit from targeted support, including strategy training, time-management coaching, and confidence-building activities. Such integrated support may be particularly valuable during transitional periods such as the start of university or vocational training.

In conclusion, the present study provides evidence that in adolescents and emerging adults, higher verbal, numerical, and spatial skills are associated with greater perceived prospective and retrospective memory. The findings underscore that basic cognitive skills contribute to how young people judge their memory capabilities, with implications for theory and practice. While VNSS explains a meaningful portion of variance in perceived memory, it is clear that perceptions are multifaceted and influenced by additional psychological and contextual factors. Future research employing longitudinal, experimental, and multimethod designs will be crucial for unpacking the mechanisms that link cognitive skills with subjective memory beliefs and for designing interventions that support both cognitive development and metacognitive competence.
