Introduction

Working memory is central to a wide range of cognitive functions and everyday tasks, and it is therefore a topic of intense theoretical and empirical interest. The construct of working memory refers to the capacity to temporarily store and manipulate information in the service of goal-directed behavior. Because working memory links perception, attention, and long-term memory, variation in working memory capacity is associated with individual differences in language comprehension, reasoning, and learning. A large literature has sought to identify factors that influence working memory performance, and one of the candidate experiential factors that has received considerable attention is bilingualism. Bilingualism, typically defined as the habitual use of two languages in everyday life, has been proposed to shape cognitive functions beyond language, producing measurable effects on attention, executive control, and memory. The notion that experience with managing two languages could transfer to improved domain-general cognitive control has motivated many studies of bilingual advantages, particularly in tasks that tax executive functions. Working memory tasks that require simultaneous processing and storage of information are conceptually linked to executive control, since they require coordination between maintaining items and engaging in secondary processing. The literature on bilingualism and working memory contains mixed findings, with some studies reporting bilingual advantages, some reporting null effects, and some reporting disadvantages, and these inconsistencies have prompted careful consideration of task characteristics and participant definitions. One important distinction that has emerged is that tasks which combine storage and processing demands—so-called complex span tasks—are especially sensitive to individual differences in executive control, and therefore may be more likely to reveal bilingual-related effects than simple maintenance tasks. Complex span tasks require participants to maintain a sequence of items while intermittently engaging in a processing activity, and performance on these tasks depends both on the fidelity of item maintenance and on the ability to coordinate processing and storage demands. Because bilingual experience is associated with repeated practice in selecting and controlling between competing languages, it is plausible that bilinguals develop enhanced capacity to manage dual demands, potentially leading to superior performance on complex span tasks. Several empirical studies have indeed reported bilingual advantages on complex span and other dual-task tasks, and these effects have often been strongest when the processing component is challenging or when the memory component is made difficult by increased list length or interference. However, other studies have failed to find reliable bilingual advantages on working memory tasks, particularly when tasks emphasize rote maintenance of items in order without concurrent processing, highlighting the importance of task structure in determining whether bilingual advantages emerge. A further nuance in the working memory literature concerns the nature of the memory representation that is being probed. Some tasks require faithful recall of serial order—that is, the exact sequence of items in the presentation order—whereas other tasks reward recall of item identity regardless of order. Serial order memory and item memory depend on overlapping but dissociable cognitive and neural processes, and there is theoretical and empirical reason to expect that bilingualism might differentially affect these aspects of memory. It is possible, for instance, that bilingual experience enhances the ability to maintain item identity against interfering processing demands, while having little or no effect on the precision of serial order representation. The implications of such a dissociation are substantive because they speak to the mechanisms by which bilingual experience interacts with working memory systems. The current study examines these issues by using a variant of a complex span task in which participants are required to maintain letters while engaging in processing of numerical stimuli presented in three different formats. The idea behind manipulating the format of the processing stimuli is that different formats place different demands on perceptual, attentional, and executive processes. Arabic digits are typically quick to perceive and map to their lexical and semantic representations, making parity or reading judgments relatively straightforward. Number words require reading and lexical access and therefore may impose a different kind of linguistic demand, potentially engaging verbal processing systems. Dot patterns, particularly when presented as visual arrays that must be enumerated or judged for quantity, can impose greater attentional and visuospatial processing demands because they require visual analysis and may be less readily processed in an automated lexical fashion. Prior work suggests that visually presented dot arrays or patterns can be more taxing on executive functions, particularly when the arrays are not easily subitized and require counting or approximate estimation. If bilingualism confers greater efficiency in domain-general control, then the advantage should be most apparent when the processing task is demanding in a way that requires allocation and switching of attention while maintaining items in memory. Thus, manipulating processing format provides an experimental lever to vary processing difficulty while keeping the memory demand constant. In addition to varying processing format, we also score recall in two ways: strict serial-order recall (ordered recall) and content-only recall (unordered recall). Strict serial-order recall requires participants not only to remember which items were presented but also to recover their precise order, whereas unordered recall counts an item as correct regardless of its position in the sequence. These two scoring methods can index different facets of working memory ability: unordered recall privileges item maintenance and resistance to interference, while ordered recall additionally taxes mechanisms that encode, rehearse, and reconstruct serial relationships among items. Because bilingualism has frequently been linked to enhanced attentional control, inhibition of irrelevant representations, and the ability to coordinate competing demands, we might expect bilingual benefits to be particularly pronounced on measures that tap these processes. That is, bilinguals might recall more items overall under high processing load because they can better shield item representations from interference, even if they do not show improved ability to reconstruct the exact presentation order. Prior studies have not uniformly examined order versus content recall in the context of bilingualism, and this omission limits the interpretability of reported group differences. The present investigation therefore addresses two key questions: first, does bilingualism confer an advantage on a complex span task in which letters must be maintained while processing numerical stimuli; and second, if an advantage is present, does it generalize across processing formats, or is it specific to formats that place a heavier load on executive functions, such as dot patterns? Based on the idea that bilingual experience enhances domain-general control and that difficult processing demands accentuate the involvement of executive functions, we hypothesized that bilingual participants would recall more letters than monolingual participants when the intervening processing task involved dot patterns. Furthermore, we expected that any bilingual advantage would be more evident in unordered recall than in strict serial-order recall, reflecting superior maintenance of item identity and resistance to interference rather than improved serial-order reconstruction. Finally, we anticipated overall main effects of processing format such that dot patterns would be the most demanding processing condition, leading to lower recall performance across both groups relative to Arabic digits and number words. The study was designed with careful matching of participant groups on age and education, and with within-subject manipulation of processing format to maximize sensitivity to group differences while controlling for between-subject variance in baseline memory ability. We also collected response time data and accuracy on the processing judgments to ensure that differences in recall were not simply due to speed-accuracy trade-offs in the processing component. By combining detailed behavioral measurement, multiple processing formats, and dual scoring of recall, the study aims to clarify how bilingual experience interacts with the demands of complex span tasks and to shed light on the specific cognitive functions that might underlie bilingual effects on working memory.

Method

Participants were recruited from university communities and the surrounding city population and participated for monetary compensation or course credit. A total of 80 participants were included in the final sample, with 40 individuals identifying as bilingual and 40 identifying as monolingual. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 35 years, and groups were matched on mean age and years of education; the bilingual group had a mean age of 23.9 years and the monolingual group had a mean age of 24.1 years. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological or psychiatric conditions. Bilingual participants reported regular use of two languages in their daily life, with self-rated proficiency of at least 7 out of 10 in both languages on a standardized language history questionnaire, and they reported using both languages across multiple contexts such as home, work, or study. Monolingual participants reported exclusive use of a single language in most contexts and limited exposure to additional languages. To reduce heterogeneity related to language dominance and proficiency, bilingual participants who reported very low proficiency in one language were excluded during screening, and monolingual participants with significant prolonged exposure to additional languages were also excluded. The study procedure was approved by the institution’s ethics review board, and all participants gave informed consent prior to participation. The experiment took place in a quiet testing room, and each session lasted approximately 45 minutes including instructions and practice trials. Stimuli were presented on a computer monitor using custom experimental software, and responses were collected via keyboard and via a voice key for timed reading responses. The complex span task in the present study was a variant of a reading span paradigm in which letters served as to-be-remembered items and numerical stimuli served as the processing component. Letters were consonants drawn from a pool designed to avoid visually or phonologically confusable items (for example, we excluded letters such as B and P that are easily confused in some fonts or when spoken rapidly). Each memory list contained between three and seven letters, and list lengths were varied pseudo-randomly across trials to sample a range of memory loads. Letters were presented sequentially at the center of the screen for 800 ms each with an inter-stimulus interval of 200 ms, and participants were told to encode each letter for later recall. After each letter, a processing item appeared and required a speeded judgment; this interleaved processing ensured that participants could not simply rehearse the entire list without interruption. There were three processing conditions that were manipulated within participants: Arabic digits, dot patterns, and number words. The Arabic digits condition consisted of single digits (1–9) presented as numerals, the dot patterns condition consisted of visual arrays of dots arranged in dice-like patterns for quantities 1–9, and the number words condition consisted of the written words corresponding to numbers one to nine. For each processing item, participants made a parity judgment (odd vs even) as quickly and accurately as possible, and they indicated their decision by pressing one of two labeled keys. Parity judgments were chosen because they require semantic access but are not trivially automatic, and they are commonly used in complex span paradigms to occupy processing resources. The order of processing conditions was blocked and counterbalanced across participants so that each participant completed three blocks, one per processing format, and block order was randomized using a Latin square design. Each block consisted of 30 trials, yielding 90 trials in total per participant. Prior to the experimental blocks, participants completed practice trials to become familiar with the letter encoding, the parity judgments for each processing format, and the combined task structure. Practice continued until participants reported understanding the task and completed at least six correct processing judgments in sequence, ensuring comprehension and stable performance. At the end of each trial, participants were prompted to recall the letters from that trial. There were two types of recall scoring employed in the analysis. First, ordered recall required participants to type or say the letters in the exact order in which they were presented; scoring for ordered recall was strict, awarding a point only when both the letter identity and its serial position were correct. Second, unordered recall was computed post-hoc by counting the number of correctly recalled letter identities regardless of order on each trial. The experimenter recorded responses and verified typed inputs when needed. In addition to recall data, reaction times and error rates on the parity judgments were recorded to assess processing task difficulty and any potential speed-accuracy trade-offs. To reduce fatigue, short breaks were offered between blocks and participants could request additional breaks as needed. Data preprocessing involved excluding trials with missed processing responses or voice-key failures for reading measures, and trials with processing times outside three standard deviations of the participant’s mean were flagged and inspected. Participants with overall processing accuracy below 60% in any condition were excluded from analysis to ensure that they had been engaged with the processing task; no participant met exclusionary criteria for processing accuracy in the final sample. Scoring for the memory component produced two dependent variables for each participant in each processing condition: mean proportion of letters correctly recalled in ordered fashion, and mean proportion of letters correctly recalled independent of order. Descriptive statistics were computed, including means, standard deviations, and confidence intervals for each group and condition. To evaluate the main hypotheses statistically, we conducted mixed-design analyses of variance (ANOVA) with language group (bilingual vs monolingual) as a between-subjects factor and processing format (digits, dot patterns, words) as a within-subjects factor. Separate ANOVAs were performed for the ordered recall measure and the unordered recall measure because these dependent variables index different aspects of memory and because separate testing facilitates clear interpretation of group by processing format interactions. Where the sphericity assumption was violated for within-subject factors, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied and reported. Significant omnibus effects and interactions were followed up with planned pairwise comparisons using t-tests with Bonferroni correction to control for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta-squared for ANOVA effects and Cohen’s d for t-tests. In addition to the ANOVAs, we examined correlations between processing task performance (reaction times and accuracy) and memory measures to test whether group differences in memory could be accounted for by differences in processing performance. We also conducted exploratory mixed-model analyses that treated individual trial characteristics, such as list length, as predictors of performance, and we report the principal findings from these models where they inform interpretation. Data analysis was implemented in standard statistical software and scripts are available upon reasonable request.

Results

Descriptive data indicated that overall parity judgment accuracy was high across both groups and across processing formats, confirming that participants were engaged in the processing task as instructed. Mean parity accuracy for Arabic digits was 96.3% (SD = 2.8%) for the bilingual group and 95.8% (SD = 3.1%) for the monolingual group. For number words, parity accuracy was 94.9% (SD = 3.6%) for bilinguals and 95.2% (SD = 3.4%) for monolinguals. For dot patterns, parity accuracy was slightly lower, at 92.1% (SD = 4.5%) for bilinguals and 90.8% (SD = 5.0%) for monolinguals. Mean reaction times on the parity task were also consistent with the notion that dot patterns imposed greater perceptual and cognitive demands; mean reaction time for dot patterns was 893 ms (SD = 135 ms) for bilinguals and 918 ms (SD = 142 ms) for monolinguals, whereas reaction times for Arabic digits were 612 ms (SD = 98 ms) for bilinguals and 628 ms (SD = 105 ms) for monolinguals, and for number words were 702 ms (SD = 112 ms) for bilinguals and 719 ms (SD = 118 ms) for monolinguals. These descriptive patterns suggested that the dot pattern condition was the most time-consuming and slightly more error-prone, supporting the assumption that it is the most taxing processing format. Before testing the main hypotheses, we examined whether there were baseline differences between the groups in demographic variables and in simple memory span tasks. The bilingual and monolingual groups did not differ significantly in mean age, years of education, or performance on a separate simple digit span task that was administered as part of the battery. These checks reduced concerns that group differences in complex span performance would reflect pre-existing disparities in baseline ability or demographic confounds. Ordered recall performance, scored strictly for identity and position, showed the expected effect of processing format: a within-subject ANOVA with language group as the between-subjects factor and processing format as the within-subjects factor revealed a significant main effect of processing format, F(2,156) = 42.87, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .36. Pairwise comparisons indicated that ordered recall was highest in the Arabic digits condition (mean proportion correct = .71, SD = .10), intermediate in the number words condition (mean = .66, SD = .11), and lowest in the dot patterns condition (mean = .59, SD = .12). The main effect of language group on ordered recall was not significant, F(1,78) = 1.86, p = .176, partial eta-squared = .02. The interaction between language group and processing format for ordered recall was also non-significant, F(2,156) = 1.10, p = .336, partial eta-squared = .01. These results indicate that when strict serial-order recall is required, both bilingual and monolingual participants are similarly affected by processing format and that there is no reliable group advantage in ordered recall. In contrast, unordered recall—measured as the proportion of letter identities correctly recalled irrespective of their serial position—yielded a different pattern that was central to our primary hypotheses. A mixed-design ANOVA with language group as the between-subjects factor and processing format as the within-subjects factor revealed a significant main effect of processing format on unordered recall, F(2,156) = 57.34, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .42, consistent with the pattern that dot patterns were the most demanding and led to lower recall overall. More critically, there was a significant interaction between language group and processing format on the unordered recall measure, F(2,156) = 5.12, p = .007, partial eta-squared = .06. The main effect of group on unordered recall across processing formats approached but did not reach conventional significance, F(1,78) = 3.54, p = .063, partial eta-squared = .04. To unpack the significant interaction, we conducted planned pairwise comparisons between bilingual and monolingual participants within each processing format for the unordered recall measure. In the dot pattern condition, bilinguals showed superior unordered recall relative to monolinguals (bilingual mean = .63, SD = .13; monolingual mean = .52, SD = .14). The independent-samples t-test for the dot pattern comparison yielded t(78) = 3.45, p = .001, Cohen’s d = 0.77, indicating a medium-to-large effect size. In the Arabic digits condition, bilinguals and monolinguals did not differ significantly in unordered recall (bilingual mean = .76, SD = .09; monolingual mean = .74, SD = .10), t(78) = 1.02, p = .311, d = 0.23. In the number words condition, the group difference in unordered recall was also non-significant (bilingual mean = .69, SD = .11; monolingual mean = .66, SD = .12), t(78) = 1.15, p = .254, d = 0.26. Thus, the bilingual advantage on unordered recall was specific to the dot pattern condition. To ensure that these group differences in unordered recall for dot patterns were not attributable to speed-accuracy trade-offs on the parity task, we examined processing task reaction times and accuracy for the dot condition and their correlations with unordered recall. Correlations between parity reaction time and unordered recall in the dot pattern condition were small and non-significant for both groups (bilingual r = −.12, p = .46; monolingual r = −.09, p = .57), and parity accuracy showed weak correlations with unordered recall (bilingual r = .16, p = .32; monolingual r = .11, p = .45). Furthermore, mean parity reaction times in the dot pattern condition did not differ significantly between groups, t(78) = 1.03, p = .307, and parity accuracy differences were also non-significant, t(78) = 1.36, p = .178. These patterns suggest that the bilingual advantage in unordered recall under dot pattern processing was not simply due to faster or more accurate processing responses. We also explored whether the bilingual advantage for dot patterns varied as a function of list length. Mixed-effects models treating list length as a predictor revealed a significant three-way interaction among group, processing format, and list length for unordered recall, indicating that the bilingual advantage for dot patterns was most pronounced at longer list lengths. Post-hoc simple slopes analyses showed that for list lengths of six and seven letters, bilinguals outperformed monolinguals by larger margins than for shorter lists, suggesting that the advantage emerges more strongly under heavier memory load. For ordered recall, the analogous mixed-effects models did not reveal significant interactions involving group, processing format, and list length, reinforcing the conclusion that bilinguals’ advantage was specific to content maintenance rather than serial order recall. To quantify the practical magnitude of the effects, we calculated the mean advantage (difference score) for unordered recall in the dot pattern condition, which was .11 proportion points. Translating this difference into typical unit measures used in span paradigms, the advantage corresponds roughly to an increase of approximately one additional correctly recalled letter per ten trials, which is meaningful in contexts where small improvements in maintenance under load can have downstream consequences for complex cognitive tasks. Finally, exploratory analyses considered whether age of acquisition or self-rated proficiency in the second language among bilinguals related to the size of the advantage. Within the bilingual group, earlier age of acquisition and higher self-rated proficiency were modestly correlated with larger unordered recall performance in the dot condition (age of acquisition r = −.24, p = .13; proficiency r = .28, p = .08), but these correlations did not reach statistical significance in the present sample and should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, the pattern of results supports the hypothesis that bilingualism is associated with superior maintenance of item identity under the most demanding processing condition, and that this advantage is most evident when order is not strictly required.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether bilingualism confers an advantage on a complex span working memory task that requires maintenance of letters while participants perform parity judgments on numerical stimuli presented as Arabic digits, number words, or dot patterns. We found that processing format exerted a strong effect on memory performance, with dot patterns producing the lowest recall, number words producing intermediate recall, and Arabic digits producing the highest recall. This ordering of difficulty is consistent with the idea that dot patterns require more perceptual and attentional resources and are less automatically processed than Arabic digits or number words. Critically, bilingual participants outperformed monolingual participants on the unordered recall measure in the dot pattern condition, but not in the Arabic digit or number word conditions. This bilingual advantage was specific to unordered recall and did not appear on the strict ordered recall measure. Further analyses revealed that the bilingual advantage in the dot condition was most pronounced at longer list lengths, suggesting that the effect emerges under heavier memory load. These findings have several theoretical implications for understanding how bilingual experience may interact with working memory processes, and they also raise important questions for future research. First, the selective advantage observed for dot pattern processing when unordered recall was used suggests that bilingual experience may strengthen aspects of memory that are concerned with maintaining the identities of items in the face of interference from concurrent processing demands. The dot pattern processing task likely engaged visuospatial analysis and enumeration processes that both compete for attentional resources and disrupt rehearsal strategies that rely on verbal recoding. A bilingual advantage in this context implies improved ability to allocate attention and to shield item representations from interference, consistent with models that posit bilingualism enhances domain-general executive control. The absence of a bilingual advantage in the Arabic digit and number word conditions further supports the specificity of the effect: when the processing task is less taxing or when the processing stimuli are readily mapped onto verbal representations (as with digits and number words), the benefits that bilingualism might confer on attentional control do not translate into measurable differences in memory performance. Second, the dissociation between unordered and ordered recall measures is theoretically informative. Ordered recall places additional demands on mechanisms responsible for encoding and retrieving serial relationships, such as positional coding or chaining mechanisms, which may rely on distinct cognitive processes, including temporal coding and specialized rehearsal strategies. The lack of group differences for ordered recall suggests that bilingualism does not uniformly enhance all aspects of working memory but instead may selectively affect the processes involved in maintaining item identity under interference. This distinction aligns with accounts that separate item memory from order memory and posits that different neural and cognitive systems subserve these functions. If bilingualism primarily modulates attentional control and the ability to resist interference, one would predict advantages to be evident in tasks emphasizing item maintenance amid distraction, but not necessarily in tasks requiring fine-grained serial reconstruction. Third, the observation that the bilingual advantage was larger at longer list lengths suggests that the hypothesized bilingual benefit scales with cognitive load. Under mild load, both groups may possess sufficient capacity to maintain items successfully, but as load increases and resources are stretched, group differences in efficiency of attentional allocation and interference control become more consequential. This pattern supports the notion that bilingual experience may provide a form of cognitive reserve that becomes most apparent under challenging conditions. The finding that parity reaction times and accuracy on the dot pattern processing task were similar between groups argues against a straightforward explanation in which bilinguals simply processed the intervening items more quickly or more accurately and thus had more time for rehearsal. Instead, the results point toward a qualitative difference in how item representations are maintained during processing. An intriguing possibility is that bilinguals are better able to employ domain-general strategies, such as chunking or use of attentional refreshing mechanisms, that preserve item identities even when subvocal rehearsal is disrupted. The present data are consistent with theoretical frameworks that posit bilingual experience strengthens control processes, including attentional shifting and inhibition, which would be useful for maintaining representations while performing concurrent tasks. It is also worth considering alternative interpretations and limitations. One alternative is that bilingual participants differed from monolingual participants in unmeasured attributes that could influence task performance, such as motivation, familiarity with experimental tasks, or cultural differences in test-taking approaches. We attempted to minimize such confounds by matching groups on age and education and by employing within-subject manipulations; nonetheless, cross-sectional designs cannot definitively establish causal relationships between bilingual experience and cognitive enhancement. A second limitation concerns the definition and measurement of bilingualism. Although participants were screened for language use and self-rated proficiency, variability within the bilingual group in terms of age of acquisition, language switching frequency, and domain-specific proficiency could moderate effects. Exploratory correlations hinted that earlier acquisition and higher proficiency might be associated with larger advantages, but the present sample was not sufficiently powered to test these moderators reliably. Future studies should incorporate more detailed and objective measures of bilingual experience, such as standardized proficiency assessments, ecologically valid measures of language switching, and longitudinal designs that can track change over time. A third limitation relates to the stimuli and task design. Dot patterns were operationalized here as visual arrays requiring parity judgments, and while this design aimed to increase perceptual and attentional demands, different implementations of dot arrays (e.g., randomized spatial configurations, varying densities, or controlled subitizing range) might yield different results. Similarly, different types of processing tasks (for instance, semantic categorization vs parity judgments) may engage distinct processes and may reveal different patterns of bilingual advantage. Further research should systematically vary processing task demands to map the boundary conditions of bilingual effects on complex span tasks. The present findings also motivate several specific avenues for future work. One important direction is to examine the neural mechanisms underlying the selective bilingual advantage. Neuroimaging studies could reveal whether bilinguals show differential engagement of frontal control networks during high-demand processing conditions and whether such engagement correlates with maintenance of item identity. Functional connectivity analyses could determine whether bilinguals exhibit more efficient or sustained coupling between attention-related networks and memory-related regions during complex span tasks. Another direction is to investigate developmental and aging populations, as the interplay between bilingualism and working memory may differ across the lifespan. For instance, bilingualism has been hypothesized to provide cognitive reserve in aging; testing whether older bilingual adults show preserved maintenance under interference relative to monolinguals could have practical implications. Longitudinal training studies that teach second-language skills to monolingual adults and subsequently measure transfer to complex span tasks could also provide causal evidence for the effect of bilingual experience on working memory. The present findings further suggest practical considerations for the assessment and interpretation of working memory capacity. Researchers and practitioners should be mindful that working memory is not a unitary construct and that scoring methods (ordered vs unordered recall) can yield different patterns of group differences. When evaluating cognitive benefits associated with experience or interventions, it is important to select task measures that are theoretically aligned with the hypothesized mechanisms of change. In the context of bilingualism research, our study illustrates that advantages may be detectable only under conditions that tax the relevant mechanisms, namely attentional control and interference resistance, and may manifest selectively in item maintenance measures rather than serial-order measures. Finally, theoretical models of bilingualism and cognitive control should incorporate the nuanced pattern we observed: bilingual experience appears to confer benefits that are conditional on task difficulty and on the specific cognitive component under scrutiny. Models that posit a general bilingual advantage across all cognitive domains are not supported by the present data, whereas models that emphasize domain-general control improvements that manifest under high-demand conditions receive support. Reconciling the varied findings across the literature will require attention to task characteristics, scoring methods, and participant heterogeneity. In conclusion, the current study provides evidence that bilingualism can be associated with improved maintenance of item identity in a complex span working memory task when the processing component is particularly demanding, as with dot pattern processing, and when recall is scored in terms of item content rather than strict serial order. These results refine our understanding of how bilingual experience intersects with working memory systems, highlighting that bilingual advantages may be specific to certain cognitive operations and task contexts. Future research that combines precise measurement of bilingual experience, systematic manipulation of processing demands, and neurocognitive methods will be well-positioned to further elucidate the mechanisms and boundary conditions of bilingual effects on working memory.
