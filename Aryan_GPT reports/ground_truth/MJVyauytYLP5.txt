Introduction

Risky decision-making is a central domain of study in psychology, behavioral economics, and neuroscience because it touches on how people evaluate uncertainty and trade off potential gains and losses. People routinely face situations in which outcomes are uncertain and probabilities are either presented explicitly or must be inferred from experience, and how people respond in these two informational regimes can have major consequences for welfare and policy. A robust empirical phenomenon that has emerged in this literature is the so-called description-experience gap, which refers to systematic differences in choices when people learn about outcome distributions by description versus by sampling experience. Across multiple paradigms, researchers have observed that people tend to overweight rare events when probabilities are provided verbally or numerically (description), but they tend to underweight those same rare events when they learn about them through repeated experience (experience). The existence of the description-experience gap calls into question universal assumptions about probability weighting and suggests that cognitive processes engaged by the mode of information acquisition alter decision strategies in important ways.

Several theoretical accounts have been offered for the description-experience gap, and these accounts implicate different cognitive and statistical mechanisms. One class of explanations emphasizes sampling error in experience-based learning. When people sample from an environment with rare outcomes, they will often experience fewer occurrences of those events than their objective probability would predict, particularly when sampling is limited. Under-sampling of rare events in small samples naturally leads to underweighting because the experienced frequency is lower than the true probability. Another class of explanations emphasizes memory and recency effects: rare events can be under- or over-represented in the memory trace depending on when they are experienced, with recent rare events exerting disproportionate influence on choices. A further class of accounts focuses on the task of translating descriptive probabilities into subjective weights, arguing that description evokes abstract probability representations that may be treated differently, often being overweighted due to anchoring on explicit numeric cues and affective reaction to the explicit mention of an extreme outcome.

Empirical work has explored these mechanisms with multiple experimental manipulations. Studies that manipulate sample size have shown that increasing the number of experience samples tends to reduce the description-experience gap, implicating sampling error as a contributor. Other studies that control the sampling distribution, thereby minimizing sampling error, still observe a gap, which lends weight to cognitive and representational explanations beyond sampling artifacts. Work that varies the salience of outcomes indicates that emotionally salient rare outcomes are more likely to be overweighted in description, whereas the same outcomes may be dampened in experience if sampling does not produce enough salient exemplars. Neurocognitive studies point to differential engagement of prefrontal and striatal systems in the two modes, with description eliciting deliberative probability integration and experience engaging reinforcement learning systems that update values via reward prediction errors. Collectively, these findings suggest that both statistical sampling properties and cognitive load, memory, and affective salience shape the description-experience gap.

Cognitive load is a plausible moderator of the description-experience gap because it can change the extent to which people engage analytic versus heuristic processes when making decisions. Higher cognitive load reduces the availability of working memory and executive resources, which can impair deliberative processing and increase reliance on simpler heuristics or on recent memory traces. If the description-experience gap depends in part on effortful integration of probabilistic information or on recollection of diverse experience samples, then increasing cognitive load should exaggerate the difference between description and experience. For example, when the descriptive information is presented explicitly, overweighting of rare events may be amplified under load if participants no longer engage in calibration strategies and instead rely on salient numerical cues. Conversely, when learning is experience-based, higher cognitive load could exacerbate underweighting by limiting the ability to integrate experiences over time and by increasing recency influence, so that rare events that do occur are less likely to be integrated into a long-run estimate.

Previous experiments have yielded mixed evidence on how cognitive load affects risky choices more generally. Some studies report that cognitive load increases risk-taking by impairing deliberation and leading to impulsive choices, while others find that load decreases risk-taking by disrupting attentional focus on potential gains and losses. Regarding the description-experience contrast specifically, relatively few studies have directly tested load as a moderator, and results have not been conclusive. Some investigations suggest that reducing cognitive resources tends to amplify the gap because it selectively disrupts compensatory strategies in one informational mode more than the other, while other work finds no reliable interaction and instead points to task-specific or sample-specific boundary conditions. The heterogeneity in findings motivates further empirical work that examines the interaction in controlled settings while addressing potential confounds such as sampling error and framing.

Framing is another relevant factor because the gap can manifest differently for gains versus losses. Prospect-theoretic patterns predict that probability weighting interacts with valence and that overweighting rare gains will tend to produce risk-seeking behavior for potential wins while overweighting rare losses will produce heightened risk aversion in loss contexts. When experience leads to underweighting, these patterns should reverse relative to description, but the magnitude of the reversal may differ across gain and loss frames. Therefore, a comprehensive test of the description-experience gap and its moderation by cognitive load should include both gain and loss framed tasks, permitting examination of whether load influences the two frames similarly.

A further methodological concern is that naturalistic experience paradigms often let participants sample freely, which introduces sampling noise and may confound interpretations of experience-based underweighting. To isolate cognitive factors such as load and memory from statistical sampling error, some researchers have employed "fixed experience" or "scripted sampling" designs in which the experimenter controls the sequence of outcomes participants see. This approach ensures that participants in the experience condition observe the true distribution of outcomes, eliminating sampling variability as a confound. If a description-experience gap persists under fixed sampling, then explanations must invoke cognitive processing differences rather than mere sampling error.

Given this background, the present study seeks to replicate the description-experience gap while addressing several of the remaining theoretical ambiguities. The study employs a factorial design that crosses information mode (description versus experience) with cognitive load (higher versus lower) and includes both gain and loss framed tasks. Critically, the experience condition uses scripted sequences that contain rare events at frequencies matching their objective probabilities, which removes sampling error as an explanatory factor. We hypothesize that the classic description-experience gap will be observed, such that participants in the description condition will overweight rare events relative to those in the experience condition. We further hypothesize that the gap will be moderated by cognitive load, with higher load amplifying the difference between description and experience. Specifically, we predict that higher cognitive load will increase the degree to which rare events are overweighted in description and increase the degree to which they are underweighted in experience, thereby widening the gap. We reason that load will impair compensatory deliberation in both modes but that the direction of the impairment will be opposite for the two modes because they instantiate different cognitive demands.

The study also explores whether the hypothesized moderation by cognitive load varies by valence frame. We predict that the amplifying effect of load will be present for both gain and loss tasks but that the magnitude may differ across valence because of asymmetries in emotional salience and default risk preferences. In addition, we examine alternative outcomes that could explain null effects, including the possibility that high cognitive load induces more random responding, which would reduce systematic differences between conditions, or that the specific load manipulation might have insufficient potency in our participant pool. To adjudicate among these possibilities, we include manipulation checks that assess digit span performance and we analyze response consistency measures.

To carry out this investigation, we recruited a moderately large sample and implemented a 2 (information mode: description, experience) × 2 (cognitive load: low, high) between-subjects design with within-subject variation across six risky choice tasks. The tasks were selected to include three gain-framed problems and three loss-framed problems, each involving a choice between a safe option and a risky option in which the risky option delivers a rare high payoff with low probability or the converse rare large loss. In the description condition, participants received explicit numerical probabilities and outcomes for each option, while in the experience condition they observed preprogrammed sequences of outcomes that contained rare events at the correct proportional frequency. Cognitive load was induced via a digit span retention task carried out concurrently with the decision tasks, with longer strings for the high-load condition and shorter strings for the low-load condition. To analyze choices, we used chi-square tests to compare proportions of risky choices across conditions for each task and logistic regression models to test overall effects and interactions while controlling for demographic covariates and task order.

This study advances the description-experience literature by combining a fixed-experience protocol that removes sampling error with an explicit manipulation of cognitive load, which allows us to test whether cognitive resource limitations help explain mode-based differences. In doing so, the study contributes to theoretical debates about whether the gap reflects primarily statistical sampling properties or whether cognitive processing differences, such as working memory limitations and reliance on recency heuristics, play a major role. If cognitive load magnifies the gap in a fixed-sampling context, this would lend strong support to cognitive-process explanations. If not, it would suggest that cognitive load may not be the critical boundary condition or that the particular load manipulation interacts with other task features.

The present article reports the methods and results of this investigation in detail, and it discusses the implications for models of risky choice, clariﬁes possible mechanisms, and outlines directions for future work. The introduction has set the stage by situating the description-experience gap within broader theories and by motivating the focus on cognitive load, framing effects, and elimination of sampling error. The remainder of the article describes the experimental methods, presents the outcomes of the planned analyses, and situates the findings in the context of existing empirical and theoretical work.

Methods

Participants were recruited online through a participant pool that included undergraduate students and community volunteers, and they received either course credit or a modest monetary payment for participation. The target sample size was chosen to provide adequate power to detect moderate main effects and interactions in a 2 × 2 between-subjects design with repeated measures across six decision tasks. We aimed for approximately 60 participants per cell, which would allow for reasonably precise estimates of choice proportions and for chi-square and logistic regression analyses with sufficient sensitivity. In total, 240 participants were enrolled, and after excluding those who failed attention checks or did not complete key portions of the experiment, the final analytic sample comprised 232 participants. The final sample was diverse in age, with participants ranging from 18 to 64 years, and included both male and female participants as well as individuals of various ethnic backgrounds.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of four experimental conditions in a 2 (information mode: description, experience) × 2 (cognitive load: low, high) design. Randomization was implemented by the online experiment platform and was stratified to balance sex across conditions. The experiment took approximately 25 minutes to complete for most participants, depending on individual pacing and response times.

Materials included six risky choice tasks constructed to create clear rare-event scenarios. Three tasks were framed as gains and three as losses. The gain-framed tasks involved a safe option that provided a moderate sure gain and a risky option that provided a larger gain with low probability paired with zero otherwise. The loss-framed tasks involved a safe option that resulted in a moderate sure loss and a risky option that could result in a large loss with low probability paired with zero otherwise. Each risky option in the tasks had a rare outcome probability of 5% or 10%, depending on the task, chosen to be sufficiently rare to elicit classic overweighting/underweighting tendencies. The tasks were constructed so that expected values (EVs) were either equivalent or slightly favoring one option, but participants were not informed about EVs to avoid confounding the study with explicit EV calculations.

In the description condition, participants were shown a clear textual presentation of the choice for each task, which included explicit outcome magnitudes and the exact probability of the rare outcome. For example, a gain task might present a choice between “Option A: Receive $3 for sure” and “Option B: Receive $10 with probability 5% and $0 with probability 95%,” and participants chose the option they preferred. The presentation included a visual probability bar to reinforce the numeric probability and ensure comprehension. Comprehension checks followed each description block to ensure participants understood the probabilities and outcomes; participants who failed these checks were excluded from analyses.

In the experience condition, participants did not receive numeric probabilities. Instead, for each task they observed a preprogrammed sequence of 60 sampled outcomes that the experiment presented one at a time. These sequences were scripted to contain the rare outcome in proportion to its objective probability across the 60 draws; for example, for a 5% rare event probability, the sequence contained three occurrences of the rare outcome and 57 occurrences of the common outcome, arranged in a quasi-random order that avoided clustering. The sequences were generated using a pseudo-random algorithm that prevented extreme consecutive runs of the rare outcome and that distributed rare events across the first, middle, and last portions of the sequence so as to reduce systematic recency biases that might stem from unfortunate clustering. After viewing the 60 outcome draws, participants made the decision between the safe and risky options for that task. The scripted exposure ensured that every participant in the experience condition observed an identical sample with the true probability represented, thereby removing sampling error as a potential cause of mode differences.

Cognitive load was manipulated between subjects via a concurrent digit span retention task. In the high-load condition, participants were presented with an 8-digit alphanumeric string at the start of each decision trial and were instructed to retain it in memory while completing the decision task; after making the choice, they were prompted to enter the string from memory. In the low-load condition, participants were shown a 2-digit string to retain and recall at the end of the trial. Digit strings were randomly generated for each trial from digits 0–9 and were refreshed for each decision to ensure continuous load. The recall accuracy for each trial was recorded as a manipulation check for the load condition.

The experimental procedure began with informed consent and basic demographic questions, followed by practice trials for the memory task and for one example decision task. Participants then completed the six decision tasks in randomized order, with the memory load applied to each trial according to condition. In the description condition, participants viewed the descriptive information and then made their choice while maintaining the digit string in memory. In the experience condition, participants observed the 60 outcome draws (with a button press to advance the sequence at a controlled pace) while maintaining the digit string, and then made their choice. At the end of each trial, participants were asked to recall the digit string and then to complete a brief attention/comprehension check for that trial.

Primary dependent variables were the binary choice in each task (0 = safe option, 1 = risky option) and overall patterns of choice across gain and loss frames. Secondary measures included memory recall accuracy (proportion of digits correctly recalled), response times for choice, and self-reported strategy questionnaires at the end of the session that asked participants whether they relied on intuition, tried to compute probabilities, or used recent exemplar memories to guide choices.

Statistical analyses followed a pre-registered plan. For each of the six tasks, we compared the proportion of risky choices between description and experience conditions using chi-square tests of independence. We reported chi-square statistics, degrees of freedom, p-values, and phi effect sizes. We also computed description-experience gap measures by taking the difference in risky-choice proportions between the description and experience conditions for each task. To assess the effect of cognitive load, we conducted 2 × 2 analyses comparing mode and load conditions via logistic regression models with choice as the dependent variable and with mode, load, and their interaction as predictors; models included participant-level random intercepts to account for repeated measures across tasks. Additional models included controls for age, sex, and task order. For omnibus tests pooling across tasks, we estimated generalized estimating equation (GEE) models that accounted for within-subject correlation and tested main effects and interactions across the full set of decisions. We also examined whether gain and loss frames produced different patterns by testing three-way interactions among mode, load, and valence. Finally, we performed manipulation checks on digit recall accuracy to verify that the load manipulation was effective and compared response time and choice consistency measures across load levels.

All data processing and analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Data exclusion criteria were pre-specified and included failure of basic attention checks, non-completion of the experiment, and response times indicative of non-engagement (e.g., completing the entire task in less than half of the median time). Excluded participants were removed before conducting main analyses. The analytic approach emphasized simple and transparent tests—chi-square tests for task-level categorical comparisons and regression approaches for more complex tests and covariate adjustment.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the final sample showed that the 232 participants were distributed reasonably evenly across the four experimental cells with 57 in the description/low-load cell, 58 in description/high-load, 58 in experience/low-load, and 59 in experience/high-load. Age averaged 28.4 years with a standard deviation of 9.6 years, and 54% of participants identified as female. Digit recall performance differed markedly by load condition, supporting a basic manipulation effect; mean recall accuracy in the low-load condition was 0.94 (SD = 0.08) while mean recall accuracy in the high-load condition was 0.61 (SD = 0.21). The distribution of recall accuracy showed that most participants in the low-load condition recalled the short strings nearly perfectly and that performance in the high-load condition showed substantial variability. These results indicate that the digit span manipulation imposed a higher working memory burden in the high-load condition as expected.

Turning to the primary behavioral outcomes, we first examined the proportion of risky choices by information mode for each task separately. For Gain Task 1, which featured a 5% chance of receiving $12 versus a sure $0, the risky option was chosen by 42% of participants in the description condition and by 28% of participants in the experience condition. A chi-square test of independence yielded χ2(1, N = 232) = 7.23, p = .007, phi = 0.18, indicating a statistically significant difference in risky choice proportions consistent with overweighting of rare gains in the description condition relative to experience. For Gain Task 2, which offered a 10% chance of a larger gain relative to a sure smaller gain, the risky option proportions were 47% in description and 39% in experience. The chi-square test for that task returned χ2(1, N = 232) = 2.03, p = .154, phi = 0.09, which did not reach conventional significance. For Gain Task 3, with another rare high payoff at 5% chance, risky choice proportions were 50% in description and 33% in experience; chi-square analysis gave χ2(1, N = 232) = 9.84, p = .002, phi = 0.21, indicating a significant description-experience difference. Thus, two of the three gain tasks displayed significant gaps between description and experience.

For the loss-framed tasks, patterns were qualitatively consistent with the description-experience phenomenon but were somewhat more variable. Loss Task 1 involved a risky option with a 5% chance of a large loss versus a sure moderate loss, and the risky option was selected by 34% of description participants and 48% of experience participants. The chi-square test indicated χ2(1, N = 232) = 5.12, p = .024, phi = 0.15, reflecting a significant difference in which description participants were more loss-averse relative to experience participants, consistent with overweighting of the rare loss in the description condition. Loss Task 2 had risky-choice proportions of 31% in description and 40% in experience, and the chi-square yielded χ2(1, N = 232) = 2.07, p = .150, phi = 0.09, which was non-significant. Loss Task 3 showed proportions of 36% in description and 43% in experience with χ2(1, N = 232) = 1.25, p = .263, phi = 0.07, also non-significant. Overall, one of the three loss tasks reached significance in the predicted direction.

In summary of the task-level chi-square tests, three of the six tasks (two gain, one loss) produced statistically significant description-experience differences after removing sampling error via scripted sequences. These significant findings support the presence of a description-experience gap in a subset of task contexts. Effect sizes for the significant tasks were in the small-to-moderate range as indicated by phi values of approximately 0.15–0.21.

Next, we aggregated choices across tasks to examine overall mode effects and the influence of cognitive load. When pooling the three gain tasks, the proportion of risky choices averaged across participants was 46.3% in the description condition and 33.3% in the experience condition, indicating a pooled difference of 13 percentage points. For the pooled loss tasks, the average risky-choice proportion was 33.7% in description and 43.7% in experience, indicating a pooled difference of -10 percentage points (i.e., more risk-taking in experience for losses). To formally test these pooled differences while accounting for repeated measures, we fit generalized estimating equation models with choice as the dependent variable, information mode and valence as predictors, and participant as the clustering variable. The GEE model showed a significant main effect of information mode (Wald χ2 = 14.6, p < .001), with description associated with greater risky choice for gains and less risky choice for losses relative to experience. The main effect of valence was also significant (Wald χ2 = 10.2, p = .001), and the interaction between mode and valence was significant (Wald χ2 = 6.8, p = .009), confirming the predicted pattern where description amplified tendencies consistent with overweighting of rare events.

Regarding the central test of cognitive load as a moderator, we examined the 2 × 2 interaction between information mode and load. Logistic regression models with random intercepts for participants included predictors for mode, load, and their interaction. The main effect of load on choice was not significant in the pooled model (β = 0.05, SE = 0.09, z = 0.56, p = .575), indicating no reliable overall shift in risky choice due solely to the load manipulation. Critically, the interaction term between mode and load was also non-significant (β = -0.03, SE = 0.12, z = -0.25, p = .802), suggesting that cognitive load did not modulate the description-experience gap in a consistent manner across tasks. Analyses conducted separately for gain and loss pooled tasks likewise yielded non-significant mode × load interactions; for gains the interaction was β = 0.08, SE = 0.13, z = 0.62, p = .536, and for losses it was β = -0.14, SE = 0.12, z = -1.17, p = .243. None of these interactions reached statistical significance, which runs counter to our hypothesis that higher load would widen the gap.

We also examined task-level mode × load interactions for the individual tasks to assess whether load effects might appear only in specific settings. For Gain Task 1, the mode × load interaction chi-square was χ2(1) = 0.41, p = .522; for Gain Task 2 it was χ2(1) = 1.12, p = .290; for Gain Task 3 it was χ2(1) = 0.07, p = .796. For Loss Task 1 the interaction was χ2(1) = 1.63, p = .202; for Loss Task 2 χ2(1) = 0.90, p = .343; and for Loss Task 3 χ2(1) = 0.36, p = .548. None of the task-level interactions were significant after accounting for multiple comparisons, indicating no evidence that cognitive load reliably moderated the description-experience contrast at the task level.

Given the null findings for load moderation, we conducted follow-up analyses to probe possible explanations. First, we evaluated whether the load manipulation was effective by comparing digit recall accuracy across load conditions, which we had reported earlier as substantially different. A t-test confirmed a highly significant difference in recall accuracy between low and high load conditions, t(230) = 18.9, p < .001, indicating that the manipulation successfully imposed greater working memory demands in the high-load condition. Second, we examined whether participants in the high-load condition exhibited more random responding by comparing choice consistency across trials. Consistency was operationalized as the number of times participants selected an option that aligned with their stated strategy at the end-of-task questionnaire and as stability in choices across similar EVs. Choice variability was marginally higher in the high-load condition (mean standard deviation of choices across comparable trials = 0.48) than in the low-load condition (mean SD = 0.42), and the difference approached statistical significance, t(230) = 1.93, p = .054. This pattern is compatible with the hypothesis that high load increases random responding, although the evidence is not definitive.

Third, we explored whether individual differences in working memory capacity, as indexed by performance on the recall task within the high-load condition, correlated with the magnitude of the description-experience gap. We computed a participant-level gap score as the average difference in risky-choice proportions between description and experience across the six tasks. Within the high-load subsample, individual differences in digit recall accuracy were not strongly correlated with gap scores (r = 0.06, p = .57), suggesting that within the high-load group, residual variation in digit recall did not explain variation in the gap. Similarly, in the low-load group, digit recall was unrelated to gap magnitude (r = 0.03, p = .76).

We also tested whether framing (gain versus loss) interacted with load to produce differential effects. As reported above, the three-way interaction among mode, load, and valence was non-significant in models that included these terms (Wald χ2 = 1.02, p = .312), and separate regressions for gains and losses revealed no significant evidence of load moderation in either frame. Thus, the data do not support the idea that cognitive load affects description and experience differently across valence frames in our sample.

As an additional robustness check, we re-ran the primary analyses excluding participants who displayed unusually low accuracy on the memory recall even in the low-load condition, reasoning that poor compliance might mask effects. Excluding the 10 participants with recall accuracy below 0.80 in the low-load condition did not substantively change the pattern of results: the same three tasks remained significant for the mode contrast, and the mode × load interactions remained non-significant. We also adjusted for demographic covariates such as age and sex in logistic regression models and observed that neither covariate altered the main conclusions.

Finally, we examined response times for choice as a secondary outcome to see whether cognitive load slowed deliberation and whether such slowing related to the gap. Choice response times were longer in the high-load condition by a small margin (mean RT high = 3.4 s, low = 3.1 s, t(230) = 2.32, p = .021), indicating modestly greater deliberation or processing time under load, but there was no evidence that longer RTs within the high-load group were associated with larger mode differences in choice.

In summary, the results provided partial replication of the description-experience gap: three of six tasks exhibited significant differences between description and experience choices despite scripted sampling that matched objective probabilities. However, the key hypothesis that higher cognitive load would widen this gap was not supported across pooled analyses, task-level analyses, or valence-specific models. Manipulation checks confirmed that the load manipulation was effective in imposing greater memory demand, but this greater demand did not translate into predictable amplification of the description-experience gap.

Discussion

The present study investigated the description-experience gap in risky choice using a design that removed sampling error by presenting scripted experience sequences and that manipulated cognitive load via a digit retention task. The results provided a nuanced picture: we observed significant description-experience differences in three out of six tasks even when sampling error was controlled, which corroborates the existence of a mode-dependent bias that cannot be fully accounted for by simple under-sampling of rare events. At the same time, contrary to our central hypothesis, we found no evidence that increasing cognitive load reliably amplified the description-experience gap. This pattern of findings has implications for theoretical accounts of the gap and for the role of cognitive resources in probabilistic judgment and decision-making.

The finding that a description-experience gap persisted under scripted sampling is important because it narrows the space of viable explanations. If the gap were solely the artifact of sampling error, then controlling the sample so that all participants experience the true rare-event frequency should eliminate the effect. Instead, the persistence of the gap in several tasks suggests that cognitive processing differences between modes play a substantive role. For example, when probabilities are described explicitly, individuals may form a salient representation of the rare outcome and its probability that carries affective weight and is integrated differently into choice, possibly due to the concreteness of explicit numeric labels. In contrast, when individuals witness outcomes in a sequence—even when that sequence accurately reflects the probability—those outcomes might be encoded as exemplars and may be integrated into value estimates differently, perhaps with dampened weight on infrequent exemplars or greater reliance on recency. The scripted sequences we used minimized sampling noise but still engaged memory and exemplars-based learning systems, which appear to produce different choice behavior than does abstract description.

The presence of significant gaps in only half of the tasks suggests that the mode effect is context-dependent and perhaps sensitive to particular task parameters. Differences across tasks could be due to the specific payoff magnitudes, the exact probability levels (5% vs 10%), the relative attractiveness of the safe option, or idiosyncratic features of the scripted sequences. For example, the tasks that produced robust effects may have included payoffs or probabilities that made the rare outcome more salient or interpretable when described, thereby magnifying the divergence from experience. Alternatively, the non-significant tasks might have borderline EV differences or payoffs that made the choice less sensitive to probability weighting. These task-level nuances echo previous work showing heterogeneity in the appearance of the description-experience gap across experimental settings.

The null result for the cognitive load moderator was unexpected given theoretical motivations and some prior findings. Several possible explanations should be considered. One possibility is that the digit span load manipulation, while effective in reducing recall accuracy, did not tax the specific cognitive operations that differentiate description and experience processing. Working memory load is a relatively blunt instrument, and the kinds of processing that generate modality-based divergences may involve other cognitive faculties such as probabilistic numeracy, shifting between exemplar-based and rule-based strategies, or emotion regulation; thus, taxing working memory might not selectively impair the processes that would exacerbate the gap. Moreover, the digit retention task may have induced a form of load that altered attention broadly but did not differentially affect the integration of described versus experienced probability information.

Another possibility is that higher cognitive load increased random responding, which could obscure any systematic amplification of the gap. Our analyses showed marginal evidence that choice variability increased under high load, and this pattern is consistent with the idea that cognitive strain sometimes produces noisy rather than systematically biased behavior. If load induces randomness, then it could reduce the magnitude of contrasts between conditions by adding noise to both description and experience groups. This explanation would predict that load should reduce the consistency of choices and weaken observed effects, a pattern consistent with our lack of significant mode × load interactions.

A third possibility is that cognitive load affects gain and loss frames in different ways, such that pooling across frames washes out frame-specific moderations. We examined this possibility and did not find robust support for differential load effects by valence in the current data, but the study may have been underpowered to detect small three-way interactions. It remains plausible that load amplifies the gap for gains but attenuates it for losses, or vice versa, depending on how affective salience and cognitive control interplay with mode-specific processing. Future work could test this hypothesis with larger samples and with manipulations designed to elicit stronger or more consistent affective reactions.

An additional consideration is ecological validity of the scripted experience sequences. Although scripted sampling removes sampling error and thereby isolates cognitive processing, it may also alter the phenomenology of experience in ways that differ from free sampling. In natural experience paradigms where participants draw outcomes themselves, the act of sampling, the temporal spacing of draws, and the contingency between choice and outcome may contribute to learning and weighting in ways not captured by our protocol. Therefore, the persistence of the gap under scripted sequences indicates that cognitive differences between description and experience operate even when sampling noise is eliminated, but it does not rule out the possibility that sampling dynamics in free-sampling environments produce additional or alternative effects.

The present results also inform theoretical models of probability weighting and decision strategies. Models that attribute the gap primarily to under-sampling of rare events are insufficient to explain our data, since we observed gaps even when rare events were represented at their objective frequencies in the experience condition. Conversely, cognitive models that emphasize different representational or retrieval processes for described versus experienced information are supported by the findings, at least in part. One specific mechanism compatible with our results is that description engages explicit numeric processing that can elevate the subjective salience of low-probability extreme outcomes, while experience engages exemplar memory and reinforcement-like updating that distributes weight across observed outcomes and may attenuate the influence of infrequent events. Working memory load, however, did not differentially shift reliance on these processes in our paradigm.

Limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. First, although the sample size was sufficient to detect moderate effects and to replicate the gap in multiple tasks, the study may have lacked power to detect small mode × load interactions or subtle frame-dependent moderations. Second, the digit span manipulation, while an established method for imposing cognitive load, captures only one aspect of cognitive capacity and may not perturb the precise cognitive mechanisms involved in probability integration. Third, scripted sampling, while useful to control for sampling error, may not perfectly mimic the cognitive experience of natural sampling and could thus have introduced unknown differences in how experience is encoded. Fourth, the use of an online sample with a mixture of undergraduate and community participants may limit generalizability to other populations, particularly those with different numeracy or working memory profiles.

Several promising directions for future research follow from this work. Researchers should consider employing alternative cognitive load manipulations that target different facets of cognitive control, such as dual-task interference with executive updating, sustained attention tasks, or adaptive n-back tasks that maintain continuous engagement and more closely mirror the demands of real-world multitasking. Neuroimaging or psychophysiological measures could help identify whether different neural circuits underlie description and experience processing and how these circuits are affected by load. It would also be valuable to systematically vary the nature of scripted sequences, including controlling for recency and clustering of rare events, to determine how memory encoding dynamics shape the gap. Another promising approach is to combine scripted experience with opportunities for participants to engage in explicit probability estimates before choice, which would allow disentangling of representational differences from choice-based processes.

Additional research should examine individual differences more closely. For instance, participants with higher numeracy or greater working memory capacity may be less susceptible to the description-experience gap, or they may respond differently to cognitive load manipulations. Longitudinal or training studies could test whether teaching people strategies for integrating exemplar memories or calibrating described probabilities reduces the gap. Finally, policy-relevant research should examine how the gap manifests in consequential real-world decisions, such as health- or finance-related choices, where probabilities are often communicated in multiple ways and where cognitive load may be high.

In sum, the present study offers a partial replication of the description-experience gap and clarifies that the gap can arise even when sampling error is controlled, thereby implicating cognitive processing differences between modes. However, the expected moderating influence of cognitive load on the gap was not observed with our digit span manipulation, suggesting that either the load type or the processes it impairs are not the critical drivers of the gap in this context, or that load increases random responding and thus masks systematic effects. These findings refine our theoretical understanding by demonstrating that both statistical sampling and cognitive representation contribute to mode-dependent differences, and they point to the need for more targeted manipulations of cognitive control and for studies that examine the interplay of valence, sampling dynamics, and individual differences.
