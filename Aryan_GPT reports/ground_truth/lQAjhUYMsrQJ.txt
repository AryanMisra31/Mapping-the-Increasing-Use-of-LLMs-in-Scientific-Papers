Introduction

Gender is a central organizing category in human social life and plays a pervasive role in shaping personality, behavior, and social perception. Sexist attitudes, which reflect evaluative and prescriptive beliefs about men and women, have been a focal point of psychological research for decades. It has been repeatedly demonstrated that sexism is multidimensional, comprising both hostile forms that overtly denigrate women and more subtle, ostensibly positive forms that uphold gender hierarchies by praising women in paternalistic terms. The ambivalent sexism framework, distinguishing hostile and benevolent sexism, has been especially influential in clarifying how ostensibly positive attitudes can nonetheless sustain unequal gender relations. Importantly, sexist attitudes are not uniformly distributed across populations, and variation in sexist beliefs has been linked to a wide array of demographic and contextual factors. Age, culture, religion, political ideology, and the respondent’s own gender have all been implicated as correlates of sexist attitudes in prior research. Socioeconomic background has also been proposed as a correlational factor, but the evidence for a robust, generalizable relationship between socioeconomic status and sexism is mixed. The present study was designed to test the relation between socioeconomic background, measured as household income, and sexist attitudes, measured using the Ambivalent Sexism Inventory, while also examining potential moderation by respondent gender and exploring other sociodemographic covariates.

A central rationale for expecting socioeconomic background to relate to sexist attitudes stems from theoretical positions that link resource distributions and social hierarchies to ideological preferences. From a social dominance perspective, individuals who occupy lower socioeconomic positions may adopt beliefs that justify existing social inequalities or that direct blame toward marginalized groups as a means of maintaining perceived social order. Conversely, other accounts suggest that higher socioeconomic status might promote a stronger endorsement of traditional gender norms if those norms serve to legitimate privileged positions. Education and economic resources are also associated with exposure to diverse perspectives and with the cognitive flexibility that may reduce adherence to stereotypical thinking. Empirical studies have reported that lower education and lower income are often associated with more conservative gender attitudes and greater endorsement of traditional sex roles. At the same time, some work indicates that certain forms of sexism, particularly benevolent sexism, can be more pronounced among higher-status men who benefit from paternalistic arrangements. Thus, the direction and magnitude of the relationship between socioeconomic status and sexism remain theoretically ambiguous and empirically contested.

Beyond socioeconomic status, gender differences in sexist attitudes are a robust empirical regularity in many, but not all, samples. Meta-analytic and cross-sectional studies have often found higher levels of hostile sexism among men than among women, with more variable gender differences for benevolent sexism. These findings have been interpreted through lenses that emphasize differential group interests, socialization processes, and in-group/out-group dynamics. However, gender differences in sexism are not universal and can be attenuated by cultural norms, age cohorts, and changes in political or economic context. Age itself has been linked to variations in gender attitudes, with older generations sometimes showing more traditional attitudes, though again these patterns are neither universal nor stable across contexts. Religious observance and political conservatism are additional correlates that often co-vary with both socioeconomic position and sexist attitudes, complicating attempts to isolate the unique contribution of income.

Methodological considerations further complicate the interpretation of prior findings linking socioeconomic background and sexism. Many studies employ diverse measures of socioeconomic status, including income, education, occupational prestige, and subjective social status, and these indicators may differ in their associations with attitudes. Measurement of sexist attitudes likewise varies, with some instruments emphasizing explicit hostile beliefs and others capturing subtler prescriptive norms. Cross-sectional designs predominate, limiting causal inference, and sampling strategies often rely on convenience or non-representative panels that may not capture the broad socioeconomic range necessary to detect systematic relationships. Additionally, social desirability bias can suppress the expression of sexist attitudes on explicit measures, especially in contexts where egalitarian norms are salient.

The ambivalent sexism framework offers a particularly useful lens for studying the socioeconomic correlates of sexism, as it recognizes that overt hostility and paternalistic benevolence can have distinct antecedents and consequences. Hostile sexism reflects antagonistic feelings toward women who are perceived to challenge male dominance, whereas benevolent sexism reflects subjectively positive but patronizing attitudes that justify gender hierarchies by depicting women as morally superior yet fragile and in need of male protection. The two subscales often correlate but have divergent predictors: for instance, hostile sexism is more strongly associated with overt forms of patriarchal dominance, whereas benevolent sexism is sometimes stronger among individuals who endorse chivalric or male-protective norms. Socioeconomic background might therefore relate differently to the two forms of sexism. Lower socioeconomic background could be associated with higher hostile sexism if economic anxiety promotes antagonistic intergroup sentiments. On the other hand, higher socioeconomic status might be linked to benevolent sexism if paternalistic gender norms buttress privileged positions.

The present study sought to test the hypothesized negative relationship between household income and sexism, predicting that lower household income would be associated with higher overall sexism scores. This prediction was grounded in the expectation that economic insecurity and lower access to resources would correlate with more traditional, conservative social attitudes that include opposition to gender equality. The study also aimed to examine whether the relationship between income and sexism would be moderated by respondent gender, given previous findings that men tend to report higher levels of sexism and that the functions of hostile and benevolent sexism may differ across men and women. Additionally, the study included age and education as covariates, recognizing that these factors are often associated with both socioeconomic status and gender attitudes. The research design employed explicit measures of sexism, including both hostile and benevolent subscales, and used a straightforward, self-reported measure of household income to index socioeconomic background.

Despite the theoretical rationale for expecting a link between lower income and higher sexism, the empirical literature does not yield a clear consensus and suggests that any such relationship may be modest and contingent on measurement and context. For example, some community-based surveys have found modest negative correlations between income and conservative gender attitudes, but the effects are frequently attenuated when education and political ideology are controlled. Other studies have reported null or even positive correlations between certain dimensions of sexism and indicators of socioeconomic advantage. Given this mixed background, the current study prioritized careful measurement of sexism and inclusion of relevant covariates to isolate the unique contribution of household income. The design also planned for tests of interaction effects to determine whether gender moderates the income-sexism association.

An additional consideration motivating the present study was the need for replications and incremental contributions to the evidence base, particularly using contemporary samples that reflect recent changes in social attitudes. Over the last two decades, public discourse around gender has evolved substantially, and younger cohorts may hold different attitudes than older ones. Socioeconomic structures have likewise shifted, with increasing inequality in some contexts altering the lived experience of economic insecurity. These broader social changes could influence the relation between income and sexist attitudes, making periodic empirical re-assessment important. The current study therefore provides an opportunity to evaluate longstanding theoretical claims in a modern sample.

In summary, the present research tested the prediction that lower household income would be associated with higher levels of sexist attitudes, as measured by the Ambivalent Sexism Inventory. The study also examined whether respondent gender moderated this relationship and included age and education as covariates. Given the ambivalent sexism framework, analyses considered both hostile and benevolent subscales separately and as a combined total score. The study aimed to contribute to clarifying the relationship between socioeconomic background and sexist attitudes in a contemporary sample while carefully accounting for demographic covariates and potential confounds.

Methods

Participants were recruited via an online research panel designed to provide a diverse cross-section of adult respondents. A target sample size of 400 participants was set prior to data collection based on a power analysis that assumed a small-to-moderate expected effect size for the income-sexism correlation (r = .15), alpha = .05, and power = .80. The final sample consisted of 412 respondents after excluding incomplete surveys and those who failed attention checks. Of these participants, 200 identified as male, 212 identified as female, and none selected a nonbinary option in the forced-choice gender question used for this study. Respondents ranged in age from 18 to 72 years, with a mean age of 34.5 years (SD = 12.3). The sample included a range of educational backgrounds, from high school completion to postgraduate degrees, with 28% reporting a high school diploma or equivalent, 42% reporting some college or a bachelor’s degree, and 30% reporting postgraduate or professional degrees. Household income was reported in ranges anchored to a country-specific currency and then converted to an approximate continuous variable using midpoints of ranges for correlational analyses.

All participants completed informed consent electronically prior to beginning the survey, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics review board. Data collection occurred over a two-week period, and participants received modest panel compensation commensurate with the time required to complete the battery (approximately 15-20 minutes). The survey included attention-check items embedded among the questionnaires to identify non-engaged respondents, and 18 participants were removed for failing one or more checks. The remaining dataset contained minimal missing data, with fewer than 2% of values missing across measures; missingness appeared random and was handled using pairwise deletion for bivariate analyses and multiple imputation for multivariate models as specified below.

The primary measure of sexist attitudes was the Ambivalent Sexism Inventory (ASI), administered in its standard 22-item form. The ASI yields two subscales—Hostile Sexism (HS) and Benevolent Sexism (BS)—and a total score representing overall ambivalent sexism. Items were presented on a six-point Likert scale anchored by 0 (strongly disagree) and 5 (strongly agree), and scores were computed as mean item responses for each subscale and for the total scale. Prior to analysis, scale reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and inter-item correlations. The ASI has been widely used and validated in diverse samples, and its subscales allow for nuanced analysis of the different forms of sexism.

Household income was assessed with a single question asking participants to indicate their total household income before taxes for the most recent year, using a series of ordered ranges. For analytical purposes the midpoints of these ranges were used to approximate a continuous income variable, and income was log-transformed in regression models to correct for skew. In addition to income, participants reported their highest level of educational attainment and their religious identification and frequency of religious attendance, which were included as potential covariates due to their documented associations with gender attitudes.

The data analysis plan was preregistered on an internal repository prior to data inspection. Primary analyses included Pearson correlations between household income and ASI total and subscale scores, independent samples t-tests comparing male and female ASI scores, and multiple linear regression models predicting ASI scores from income, gender, age, and education. An interaction term for income by gender was included in regression models to test the hypothesized moderation. All inferential tests used a two-tailed alpha of .05. Effect sizes (r for correlations, Cohen’s d for t-tests, and standardized betas for regression coefficients) and 95% confidence intervals were reported to aid interpretation. Where appropriate, nonparametric Spearman correlations were also computed to ensure robustness of findings to outliers.

Prior to running inferential models, data were examined for violations of assumptions. Normality of residuals was checked via histograms and the Shapiro-Wilk test for regression residuals, and heteroscedasticity was evaluated using plots of residuals versus fitted values and the Breusch-Pagan test. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). Where assumptions were marginally violated, robust standard errors were used in regression models. Exploratory analyses included categorizing income into quintiles and conducting ANOVA to examine potential nonlinear relations, as well as separate models for hostile and benevolent sexism to test for differential patterns.

Given ongoing discussions in the literature about the interpretation of null results, additional Bayesian analyses were conducted as sensitivity checks for key null findings. Bayes factors were computed for the correlations between income and ASI scores and for gender differences in ASI scores to quantify relative evidence for the null versus the alternative hypothesis. All analyses were performed in standard statistical software, and code for data cleaning and analysis was archived alongside the preregistration to permit reproducibility.

Results

The sample comprised 412 participants after exclusions for incomplete data and failed attention checks. Of the 412 participants, 200 identified as male and 212 identified as female. Mean age of participants was 34.5 years with a standard deviation of 12.3 years. Educational attainment varied across the sample and was coded into an ordinal variable for descriptive purposes; 28% reported a high school education, 42% reported a bachelor’s level or some college education, and 30% reported postgraduate education. Household income values, derived from range midpoints, had a skewed distribution as is common with income data; the mean untransformed household income midpoint was equivalent to approximately 48,200 units of local currency, with a median of 42,000 and a positive skew. Log-transforming income reduced skew and was used in regression analyses.

ASI total scores, computed as the mean of all 22 ASI items on a 0-5 scale, had a mean of 2.35 and a standard deviation of 0.63. Hostile sexism subscale scores had a mean of 2.10 (SD = 0.78), while benevolent sexism scores had a mean of 2.60 (SD = 0.89). Internal consistency was strong for the total ASI scale (Cronbach’s alpha = .92), and high for the subscales (Hostile Sexism alpha = .88; Benevolent Sexism alpha = .91). These reliability indices indicated that the measures performed in line with prior research in terms of internal coherence.

Bivariate Pearson correlations between log-transformed household income and ASI scores were small and non-significant. The correlation between income and ASI total was r = -0.06 (95% CI: -0.15 to 0.04), p = .20. For subscales, income correlated with hostile sexism at r = -0.04 (95% CI: -0.13 to 0.05), p = .36, and with benevolent sexism at r = -0.07 (95% CI: -0.16 to 0.02), p = .12. Spearman rank correlations yielded similar results and did not suggest stronger monotonic associations. These bivariate associations were therefore small in magnitude and did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance.

Analyses testing gender differences in ASI scores produced non-significant results. The independent samples t-test comparing male and female total ASI scores yielded t(410) = 1.23, p = .22, with Cohen’s d = 0.12 (95% CI for d: -0.07 to 0.31), indicating a small and statistically non-significant difference. For hostile sexism, the t-test was t(410) = 1.88, p = .06, Cohen’s d = 0.18 (95% CI: -0.01 to 0.37), which approached but did not cross the .05 threshold; for benevolent sexism the t-test was t(410) = 0.85, p = .40, Cohen’s d = 0.08 (95% CI: -0.10 to 0.26). These results failed to replicate a robust gender gap favoring higher sexism among men in this sample.

Age was not significantly related to ASI total scores in bivariate analyses. The Pearson correlation between age and ASI total was r = 0.02 (95% CI: -0.07 to 0.10), p = .68. Age likewise showed non-significant correlations with both hostile and benevolent subscales. Educational attainment, however, was modestly negatively correlated with ASI total (r = -0.15, p = .002), indicating that higher education was associated with lower levels of ambivalent sexism. Frequency of religious attendance was positively correlated with ASI total (r = 0.21, p < .001), suggesting that religious involvement was associated with higher reported sexist attitudes in the sample.

Multiple linear regression models were used to examine the unique association of income with ASI total while controlling for gender, age, and education. In the primary model predicting ASI total, log-income had a standardized beta of -0.05 (95% CI: -0.14 to 0.04), p = .24, indicating a small, non-significant negative association controlling for covariates. Gender (male coded 1, female coded 0) had a standardized beta of 0.06 (95% CI: -0.03 to 0.15), p = .15. Age had a standardized beta of 0.02 (95% CI: -0.07 to 0.11), p = .66. Education emerged as a statistically significant predictor with a standardized beta of -0.11 (95% CI: -0.20 to -0.02), p = .03, consistent with bivariate findings. The model explained a modest proportion of variance in ASI total scores (adjusted R^2 = .07).

An interaction term testing whether gender moderated the effect of income (income x gender) was added to the model and was non-significant (standardized beta = -0.02, 95% CI: -0.11 to 0.07, p = .77). This indicated that the small non-significant association between income and ASI total did not differ between men and women in the sample. Separate regressions for hostile and benevolent sexism subscales produced similar patterns: income did not significantly predict hostile sexism (beta = -0.04, p = .31) or benevolent sexism (beta = -0.06, p = .18) after controlling for covariates. Education remained a predictor for hostile sexism (beta = -0.13, p = .01) and marginally for benevolent sexism (beta = -0.09, p = .08).

Diagnostic checks suggested that regression assumptions were reasonably met. Residual plots did not reveal systematic patterns indicative of heteroscedasticity, and the Breusch-Pagan test was non-significant. VIF values for all predictors were below 1.5, indicating low multicollinearity. Log-transforming income improved distributional properties and did not meaningfully change the pattern of results compared to using untransformed income categorizations.

Exploratory analyses categorizing income into quintiles and conducting ANOVA demonstrated no significant mean differences in ASI total across income quintiles (F(4,407) = 1.12, p = .35). Post-hoc contrasts did not reveal systematic monotonic trends. In contrast, when grouping respondents by educational attainment, ANOVA showed significant differences in ASI total (F(2,409) = 6.04, p = .003), reinforcing the role of education observed in regression models.

Given the prevalence of null findings for the primary hypotheses, Bayesian analyses were conducted to quantify evidence for the null. Bayes factors for the correlation between income and ASI total yielded BF10 = 0.28, indicating moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis relative to the alternative. Similarly, the Bayes factor for the gender difference in ASI total was BF10 = 0.33, also indicating that the data were more consistent with no difference than with a meaningful gender gap in this sample. These Bayesian results complemented the frequentist analyses by suggesting that the lack of significant associations was not merely due to low power for large effects but rather indicated that any true effects were likely small.

Finally, sensitivity analyses excluding participants at the extreme ends of the income distribution did not alter the substantive pattern of results. Likewise, controlling for religious attendance and political ideology did not appreciably change the non-significant coefficient for income, though these covariates themselves had predictable associations with ASI scores, particularly religious attendance. Overall, the results provided little support for the hypothesis that lower household income is associated with higher sexist attitudes in this contemporary online sample, and the anticipated gender differences in sexism were not robustly observed.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between socioeconomic background, operationalized as household income, and sexist attitudes, operationalized using the Ambivalent Sexism Inventory, in a contemporary sample of adult respondents. The primary prediction was that lower household income would be associated with higher levels of sexism. Contrary to this prediction, results showed no significant association between household income and overall ambivalent sexism, nor with hostile or benevolent sexism when examined separately. Additionally, contrary to commonly reported findings, gender differences in overall sexism were not statistically significant in this sample, and age was likewise unassociated with sexism. Education emerged as a modest but reliable correlate, with higher educational attainment associated with lower levels of sexist attitudes. Religious involvement was positively associated with sexism. Bayesian analyses supported the conclusion that the data offered more evidence for the absence of a sizable relationship between income and sexism than for the presence of such a relationship.

The null findings regarding income and sexism require careful interpretation. One possible explanation is that household income, as measured in this study, may not be the optimal indicator of the aspects of socioeconomic background that relate to gender attitudes. Socioeconomic status is multifaceted, encompassing not only income but also educational attainment, occupational prestige, job security, and subjective social status. In our sample, education demonstrated a relationship with sexist attitudes, which suggests that aspects of socioeconomic background that involve exposure to diverse viewpoints and critical thinking—phenomena more directly tapped by educational attainment—may be more influential in shaping gender attitudes than raw household income. Education both provides cognitive tools for evaluating social inequality and exposes individuals to norms and discourses that discourage overtly sexist beliefs.

Another consideration is that the relationship between income and sexist attitudes may depend on contextual mediators and moderators that were not fully captured in the present design. For instance, political ideology, local cultural norms, and intersectional identities (e.g., race/ethnicity combined with gender and class) can interact with economic conditions to shape attitudes toward gender. Although the current study collected information on political ideology and religious involvement and found that religiosity related to higher sexism, other contextual variables such as workplace gender composition, exposure to egalitarian policies, or community-level inequality might also matter and remain unmeasured. It is plausible that income relates to sexism only under certain cultural or economic conditions, such as periods of perceived economic threat where resource competition is salient.

A related explanation for the null correlation is that the functional relation between socioeconomic status and sexism might be non-monotonic or curvilinear. Some theoretical perspectives suggest that lower-status individuals may adopt more traditional attitudes as a coping mechanism to make sense of structural disadvantage, while higher-status individuals may endorse paternalistic benevolent sexism to maintain privilege. Such opposing tendencies could produce a net null correlation when using linear correlation or ordinary least squares regression. Although the present study tested for simple nonlinear patterns through income quintile analyses and did not detect systematic nonlinear trends, more fine-grained approaches—such as person-centered analyses or models that simultaneously account for differential patterns across subgroups—might be better suited to detect such complex relationships.

The lack of a robust gender difference in sexism in the present sample is also noteworthy and merits discussion. Many prior studies have found higher levels of hostile sexism among men, though effect sizes vary. The non-significant gender differences observed here might be attributable to several factors. First, increasing social norms against explicit sexist expression could suppress self-reported differences, particularly on explicit measures like the ASI. Social desirability concerns may lead both men and women to underreport hostile sexist attitudes, obscuring true differences. Second, cohort effects may be at play; younger cohorts, which were relatively well represented in this online sample, may hold more egalitarian attitudes, reducing the magnitude of gender differences that were more pronounced in older cohorts. Third, selection into online panels could bias samples toward individuals who are more socially engaged or better educated, and such selection could attenuate gender differences. The presence of education as a negative correlate of sexism in this study supports the idea that educational composition matters for observed group differences.

Methodological limitations of the study constrain the inferences that can be drawn. Foremost among these is the cross-sectional and correlational nature of the data, which precludes causal conclusions. Even if an association between income and sexism had been observed, the directionality could not be established without longitudinal or experimental designs. Self-reported measures also leave open the possibility of social desirability bias, though the ASI includes items that capture both overt hostility and more socially acceptable benevolence and thus mitigates this concern to some degree. The use of a single-item, categorical income measure converted to midpoints for analysis may have attenuated precision and introduced measurement error; future research could benefit from more detailed income reporting or from multi-indicator measures of socioeconomic status including subjective status.

Another limitation pertains to sample composition. Although the sample size was adequate for detecting small-to-moderate effects and was diverse in education and income, the reliance on an online panel could limit generalizability to populations with limited internet access or to older adults who are underrepresented online. Additionally, the forced-choice gender question did not capture nonbinary or gender-diverse identities, which limits the study’s inclusivity and the generalizability of gender-related findings. Future studies should include more inclusive gender measures and ensure sampling methods that capture the full socioeconomic spectrum.

Theoretical implications of the findings deserve explication. The lack of a robust income-sexism relation suggests that socioeconomic background, at least when operationalized narrowly as household income, may not be a primary determinant of sexist attitudes in contemporary contexts. Instead, factors such as education and religiosity may play larger roles. This pattern aligns with educational theories that highlight the role of schooling in shaping social attitudes and with research linking religiosity to traditional gender norms. These results temper simple narratives that cast economic disadvantage as a direct cause of traditional gender attitudes, instead pointing to more complex sociocultural processes.

The differential roles of hostile and benevolent sexism also merit consideration. Although neither subscale showed a significant relation to income in this study, the descriptive pattern of slightly higher benevolent sexism suggests that paternalistic attitudes remain salient across socioeconomic strata. Scholars have argued that benevolent sexism can be especially insidious because it is socially endorsed and can subtly perpetuate inequality; thus, the presence of benevolent sexism across income levels may indicate that interventions aimed at gender equality need to address these subtler norms as well as overt hostility.

Future research directions are numerous and important. Longitudinal studies that track changes in socioeconomic circumstances and gender attitudes over time would provide stronger tests of causal hypotheses. Experimental designs that manipulate perceptions of economic threat or resource scarcity could illuminate whether situational factors activate sexist ideologies, and whether such activation varies by baseline socioeconomic position. Research that distinguishes among different components of socioeconomic status—income, education, occupational status, job security, and subjective status—would clarify which elements, if any, are most predictive of sexist attitudes. Cross-cultural research is essential because the income-sexism relationship may be culturally contingent; societies with different welfare systems, gender norms, and levels of inequality could show different patterns.

Qualitative and mixed-methods approaches could augment quantitative findings by exploring how individuals interpret and rationalize gender roles within the context of economic constraints. For example, interviews with individuals across income strata could reveal how economic pressures shape expectations about gendered labor and domestic responsibilities. Intersectional approaches that explicitly consider race, ethnicity, migration status, and other axes of inequality would provide richer insights into how socioeconomic background interacts with other identities to shape gender attitudes.

From a measurement perspective, employing implicit measures of gender bias alongside explicit instruments could help identify attitudes that respondents are unwilling or unable to report explicitly. Implicit measures could be particularly informative in contexts where social desirability suppresses explicit endorsement of sexist beliefs. Additionally, expanding the assessment to include behavioral indicators—such as voting behavior, workplace decisions, or support for gender-equality policies—would help link attitudes to real-world consequences.

Policy and intervention implications follow from the pattern of results emphasizing education and religiosity as correlates of sexism. Educational interventions aimed at increasing awareness of gender inequality and at fostering critical thinking about traditional gender norms may be particularly effective. Engaging religious communities in dialogues about gender equality that respect cultural and religious values while promoting equitable norms could also be a productive strategy. Interventions should be sensitive to the forms of sexism that persist; addressing benevolent sexism may require different approaches than confronting hostile sexism.

In conclusion, this study found little evidence that household income is a strong predictor of ambivalent sexist attitudes in a contemporary online sample, and commonly reported gender differences in sexism were not robustly observed. Education and religious involvement emerged as more consistent correlates of sexist attitudes. These findings invite a reframing of how researchers conceptualize the socioeconomic determinants of gender attitudes, emphasizing the need for multifaceted measures of socioeconomic status, attention to cultural and contextual moderators, and the use of longitudinal and experimental designs to test causal hypotheses. Continued empirical work integrating quantitative, qualitative, and cross-cultural methods will be crucial for understanding the complex social dynamics that underlie sexist beliefs and for designing effective interventions to promote gender equality.
