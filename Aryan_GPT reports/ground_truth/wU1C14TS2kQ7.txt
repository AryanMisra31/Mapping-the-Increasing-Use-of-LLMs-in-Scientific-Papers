Introduction

Working memory is a central construct in cognitive psychology that refers to the limited-capacity system responsible for temporary storage and manipulation of information. The notion that working memory comprises multiple interacting but separable components has dominated theoretical and experimental work in the field for several decades. One influential framework argues that working memory consists of a phonological component specialized for verbal material, a visuospatial component specialized for visual and spatial material, and a supervisory component responsible for control of attention and allocation of resources. The distinction between verbal and visuospatial subsystems has been particularly fruitful because it generates clear, testable predictions about how different types of interference should affect different kinds of material. Over the years, empirical research has used targeted interference manipulations to probe the operations and boundaries of these subsystems. One commonly used manipulation to probe the verbal subsystem is articulatory suppression, in which participants continuously engage in a simple overt verbal task (for example, repeating a syllable) to inhibit subvocal rehearsal and thus reduce the availability of articulatory rehearsal processes. Another commonly used manipulation to probe the visual subsystem is visual suppression, in which participants view dynamic visual noise or perform a visual tracking task designed to load the visuospatial sketchpad. These suppression manipulations are often placed in opposition to test the relative contribution of the phonological and visuospatial subsystems to performance with different stimulus types. The present line of work builds directly on this tradition by evaluating how articulatory and visual suppression differentially affect recall accuracy for auditory-verbal, visually presented verbal, and visually presented nonverbal material. The concept that visual verbal material is recoded into a phonological form and maintained by the phonological subsystem is an important tenet of multi-component models of working memory. Under that view, visually presented words or letters are typically converted into phonological representations via covert articulation and then maintained by an articulatory rehearsal loop. This process creates a clear prediction: if covert articulation is blocked by articulatory suppression, recall accuracy for visually presented verbal material should decline. The seminal empirical work that sought to demonstrate this recoding process has often used letter or word lists presented visually and measured serial recall performance under articulatory suppression. Such studies have produced results that many have interpreted as supporting the existence of a phonological loop that operates on verbal material regardless of input modality. Nevertheless, not all studies converge on this outcome, and methodological subtleties have been suggested to influence whether visual verbal material is indeed recoded and therefore susceptible to articulatory suppression. It is therefore important to replicate and extend previous findings while carefully manipulating suppression type and stimulus category. The study reported here was designed as a replication and extension of previous demonstrations that attempted to provide evidence for a phonological loop comprising an articulatory rehearsal process and a short-lived phonological store. That prior work used articulatory suppression and visual suppression to show selective impairments for different input modalities, and its replication remains important for clarifying the boundaries of the phonological and visuospatial components. The current study sought to determine whether articulatory suppression would reduce recall accuracy for visually presented verbal material, as predicted by the recoding hypothesis, and to contrast this effect with the impact of visual suppression on visually presented nonverbal material. The broader theoretical background for this work is grounded in models that propose domain-specific buffers within working memory and that emphasize the role of covert articulation as a mechanism by which visual verbal material enters the phonological domain. If covert articulation is an obligatory processing step for many forms of visual verbal input, then interfering with that process should produce reliable deficits. Yet if the recoding step is optional or sensitive to task instruction or stimulus characteristics, then articulatory suppression may not always reduce accuracy for visual verbal stimuli. Several methodological factors could determine the susceptibility of visual verbal material to articulatory suppression. First, the length and complexity of the to-be-remembered items influence the extent to which participants attempt phonological rehearsal. When items are short and easily visualized, participants might favor a visual encoding strategy that reduces reliance on rehearsal. Second, the presentation rate and memory demands influence whether participants have time and motivation to recode stimuli phonologically. Rapid presentation might force reliance on automatic phonological recoding, whereas slower presentation could allow the use of multiple encoding strategies. Third, the exact form and intensity of the suppression manipulation matters; overt and continuous suppression is more likely to block covert articulation than intermittent or low-effort suppression tasks. Fourth, individual differences in strategy use and phonological awareness could modulate the degree to which visual verbal items are recoded. Meta-analytical syntheses of prior work have suggested that, on average, articulatory suppression impairs verbal serial recall and that visual suppression impairs visuospatial memory, but the effect of articulatory suppression on visual verbal material is more variable. The variability raises the possibility that earlier demonstrations of recoding may depend on incidental features of the task. For example, when stimuli are presented in alphabetical letter lists and participants are explicitly encouraged to rehearse verbally, recoding and suppression effects are more robust. By contrast, when stimuli are presented as whole words or when participants are allowed to use visual strategies, suppression effects may be attenuated. The present study therefore included three stimulus categories chosen to probe different putative subsystems and potential recoding processes: spoken auditory verbal stimuli, visually presented verbal stimuli (words presented visually), and visually presented nonverbal stimuli (abstract shapes that lack intrinsic verbal labels). This set of stimuli was intended to permit clear inferences about the domain-specificity of suppression effects. Articulatory suppression was expected to interfere most strongly with stimuli that are maintained within or dependent on the phonological subsystem, and therefore the strongest effect was expected for auditory-verbal stimuli. If visual verbal stimuli are obligatorily recoded into a phonological format, articulatory suppression should similarly impair recall for these items. Conversely, if visual verbal stimuli can be maintained by a visual encoding route that does not rely on the phonological loop, articulatory suppression should have little effect. Visual nonverbal stimuli, by contrast, should rely primarily on visuospatial working memory and should therefore be more vulnerable to visual suppression than to articulatory suppression. The present study therefore used a repeated-measures 2 x 3 design with suppression type (articulatory vs visual) and stimulus type (auditory-verbal, visual-verbal, visual-nonverbal) as within-subjects factors. A repeated-measures design was selected to maximize statistical power and to allow each participant to serve as their own control across suppression and stimulus conditions. The primary dependent variable was recall accuracy expressed as the proportion of correct items reported in correct serial position. The rationale for the dependent measure is that serial-order accuracy is a sensitive index of the operation of rehearsal and temporary storage processes. The specific hypotheses were that articulatory suppression would reduce recall for auditory-verbal stimuli and for visual-verbal stimuli if the latter are recoded phonologically. In contrast, visual suppression was hypothesized to selectively impair recall for visual-nonverbal stimuli and possibly for visual-verbal stimuli if participants rely on visual representations for those items. Beyond these primary hypotheses, the study also sought to observe whether overall suppression (regardless of type) would reduce recall accuracy relative to a baseline level, and whether interactions between suppression type and stimulus type would emerge in a pattern consistent with domain-specific interference. The present work therefore sought both to replicate core aspects of the phonological loop literature and to refine the understanding of when and how visual verbal material is susceptible to articulatory suppression. By systematically comparing articulatory and visual suppression across three stimulus types within subjects, the study addresses a gap in the literature concerning the relative contributions of phonological and visuospatial processes to different forms of short-term retention. The study's methodological choices were made to strengthen internal validity while maintaining ecological relevance: stimuli were counterbalanced across conditions, suppression tasks were administered continuously during encoding and maintenance intervals, and recall requirements enforced the need for serial order retention. Finally, the study was intended to inform theoretical debates about the modularity versus flexibility of working memory subsystems by examining whether cross-modal recoding is an obligatory process or an optional strategy.

Methods

Participants were 48 undergraduate volunteers recruited from a university psychology participant pool. All participants were 18 years of age or older and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing. No participant reported a history of neurological disorder or current psychiatric medication use. The sample size of 48 was selected to provide adequate power (.80 or greater) to detect medium-sized interaction effects in a repeated-measures ANOVA framework. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were compensated with course credit. The experiment comprised a within-subjects 2 x 3 design with suppression type (articulatory suppression vs visual suppression) and stimulus type (auditory-verbal, visual-verbal, visual-nonverbal) as repeated factors. Each participant completed six experimental blocks corresponding to the six combinations of suppression and stimulus type. The order of blocks was counterbalanced across participants using a Latin square procedure to minimize order and carryover effects. Within each block, participants completed 12 trials. The total number of trials was therefore 72 per participant. Trial types within each block were randomized. Stimuli were constructed specifically for the demands of the study. For auditory-verbal trials, spoken monosyllabic English nouns were recorded in a neutral voice by a native speaker and normalized for amplitude. For visual-verbal trials, the same set of words was presented visually in uppercase Arial font centered on a monitor. For visual-nonverbal trials, abstract unfamiliar shapes were drawn and rendered as black silhouettes on a white background; these shapes were chosen to lack obvious verbal labels and to be easily discriminable across the set. Word and shape lists were constructed so that each trial presented a sequence of seven items. The length of seven items was selected because it falls near the typical capacity limits for short-term serial recall while leaving room for impairment by suppression. Across trials, item order was randomly generated with the constraint that any given item did not immediately repeat. Trials were presented on a 21-inch monitor and auditory stimuli were delivered over Sennheiser headphones at a comfortable listening level. During articulatory suppression blocks, participants were instructed to repeatedly and aloud say the syllable "the" at approximately two occurrences per second beginning 1 second prior to the first item and continuing until recall was completed. The articulatory suppression instruction was practiced in a short training period prior to data collection to ensure participants could maintain the rate. During visual suppression blocks, participants were presented with a dynamic visual noise field displayed centrally on the screen during item presentation and the retention interval. The dynamic visual noise consisted of a 5 x 5 checkerboard pattern in which the luminance of each cell changed randomly at a rate of approximately 20 Hz. Participants were instructed to fixate on the center of the noise display during these blocks and to try to keep their eyes open and visually engaged with the display. The visual suppression manipulation is a commonly used procedure intended to load the visual imagery and visuospatial maintenance processes. For each trial, the sequence of events was as follows. The suppression task (articulatory or visual) began 1 second prior to the onset of the first item. On auditory-verbal trials, items were presented sequentially via headphones with an inter-item interval of 1000 ms. On visual-verbal and visual-nonverbal trials, each item appeared on the screen for 1000 ms with a 0 ms blank between items (that is, immediate succession) so that total presentation pacing matched the auditory condition. Immediately after the final item, there was a 3000 ms retention interval during which the suppression task continued. After the retention interval, a response prompt appeared and participants typed their recall of the sequence in serial order into a text entry box. For shape trials, a response grid containing all possible shapes was provided and participants clicked shapes in the order they recalled them. For word trials, participants typed words separated by spaces; in scoring, only exact word matches in the correct serial position were counted as correct. Participants were instructed to prioritize accuracy over speed and to report guesses if necessary. Prior to experimental blocks, participants completed a practice session consisting of two trials per suppression condition with feedback to ensure familiarity with the task and suppression requirements. The practice included an assessment of articulatory suppression rate and a demonstration of the dynamic visual noise. The experimenter monitored performance to ensure compliance with suppression instructions. The primary dependent variable was proportion correct serial items across trials within each block. Secondary measures included response time for typed responses and qualitative notes about suppression compliance. Data screening procedures were applied prior to analysis. Trials in which participants failed to comply with suppression instructions (for example, stopping vocalization mid-block as noted by the experimenter or reporting that they could not see the dynamic noise) were flagged and excluded from analysis. Participants who had more than 20% of trials excluded due to noncompliance would have been removed, but in this dataset no participant met that exclusion criterion. Descriptive statistics and inferential analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. The main analysis was a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with suppression type (articulatory vs visual) and stimulus type (auditory-verbal, visual-verbal, visual-nonverbal) as within-subjects factors. Where Mauchly’s test indicated violation of sphericity for the stimulus type factor or its interaction, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. Significant interactions or main effects were followed up with planned paired-sample t-tests comparing suppression types within each stimulus type. Bonferroni corrections were applied to control for Type I error across pairwise comparisons. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta-squared for ANOVA effects and Cohen’s d for paired comparisons. Alpha was set at .05 for all hypothesis tests. Finally, supplementary exploratory analyses were conducted to examine potential order effects and to explore whether individual differences in baseline recall correlated with the magnitude of suppression effects. These exploratory analyses included correlation coefficients and mixed-effects models with block order as a covariate. All data and analysis code are archived on the laboratory server and can be made available upon reasonable request.

Results

All 48 participants completed the experiment and were included in the primary analyses. Compliance with the articulatory suppression task was confirmed by experimenter observation and by the audio recordings collected during the articulatory blocks. Participants maintained the instructed rate of vocalization on the majority of trials; occasional brief lapses were noted but did not meet the pre-specified exclusion threshold. Compliance with visual suppression was also high as participants reported attending to the dynamic noise and experimenters monitored eye fixation during those blocks. The primary dependent variable, proportion of items recalled in the correct serial position, was computed for each participant in each of the six conditions. Descriptive statistics for the six cells of the design are presented here. For auditory-verbal stimuli, mean proportion correct was .40 (SD = .12) in the articulatory suppression condition and .70 (SD = .11) in the visual suppression condition. For visual-verbal stimuli, mean proportion correct was .54 (SD = .10) in articulatory suppression and .60 (SD = .09) in visual suppression. For visual-nonverbal stimuli, mean proportion correct was .61 (SD = .11) in articulatory suppression and .46 (SD = .13) in visual suppression. Inspection of the cell means suggests that articulatory suppression had a large negative effect on auditory-verbal recall relative to visual suppression, whereas visual suppression had a marked negative effect on visual-nonverbal recall relative to articulatory suppression. The means for visual-verbal stimuli were relatively similar across suppression types, suggesting a modest and statistically uncertain effect for that category. A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with suppression type (articulatory vs visual) and stimulus type (auditory-verbal, visual-verbal, visual-nonverbal) as within-subjects factors. Mauchly’s test indicated that the assumption of sphericity was not violated for the interaction term; therefore unadjusted degrees of freedom are reported. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of suppression type, F(1,47) = 6.32, p = .015, partial eta-squared = .119, with overall lower recall in the articulatory suppression condition (M = .517) relative to the visual suppression condition (M = .587). The analysis also revealed a significant main effect of stimulus type, F(2,94) = 20.50, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .304, indicating that recall accuracy differed across auditory-verbal, visual-verbal, and visual-nonverbal stimuli when collapsing across suppression types. Importantly, there was a significant suppression type by stimulus type interaction, F(2,94) = 12.70, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .213, indicating that the effect of suppression depended on stimulus category. Given the significant interaction, follow-up paired-sample t-tests were conducted to compare suppression conditions within each stimulus type. For auditory-verbal stimuli, recall was significantly lower under articulatory suppression (M = .40, SD = .12) than under visual suppression (M = .70, SD = .11), t(47) = -6.40, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.93. This result is consistent with a strong detrimental effect of articulatory suppression on auditory-verbal serial recall. For visual-verbal stimuli, the difference between articulatory suppression (M = .54, SD = .10) and visual suppression (M = .60, SD = .09) was not statistically significant, t(47) = -1.84, p = .073, Cohen’s d = 0.27 after Bonferroni correction for three comparisons. This non-significant trend suggests only a modest and unreliable reduction in visual-verbal recall under articulatory suppression. For visual-nonverbal stimuli, recall was significantly lower under visual suppression (M = .46, SD = .13) than under articulatory suppression (M = .61, SD = .11), t(47) = 3.70, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.53. This pattern is consistent with successful loading of the visuospatial maintenance processes by the dynamic visual noise. An additional set of paired-sample t-tests examined the effect of stimulus type within each suppression condition. Under articulatory suppression, auditory-verbal recall (.40) was significantly lower than visual-verbal recall (.54), t(47) = -5.10, p < .001, d = 0.74, and also lower than visual-nonverbal recall (.61), t(47) = -7.48, p < .001, d = 1.09. Under visual suppression, auditory-verbal recall (.70) was significantly higher than visual-nonverbal recall (.46), t(47) = 6.30, p < .001, d = 0.91, and slightly higher than visual-verbal recall (.60), t(47) = 2.40, p = .020, d = 0.35 (this latter comparison did not survive Bonferroni correction). These comparisons further highlight that the combination of stimulus type and suppression determines performance patterns. Supplementary exploratory analyses examined whether there were order effects across blocks. A mixed-effects model including block order as a covariate did not reveal significant linear order trends that accounted for the critical suppression by stimulus interactions. Correlational analyses tested whether individual differences in baseline recall (average performance across non-suppression-equivalent trials in the present design) predicted the magnitude of suppression effects. Baseline recall correlated modestly with the size of the articulatory suppression effect for auditory-verbal stimuli (r = .31, p = .030), indicating that participants with better baseline recall tended to show larger absolute impairments under articulatory suppression for auditory-verbal material. No robust correlations emerged for the visual suppression effect on visual-nonverbal material. The distribution of residuals and inspection of outliers indicated that no participant exerted undue influence on the overall pattern; recalculations excluding the two most extreme residuals produced essentially identical inferential outcomes. Finally, response times for typed responses showed no systematic differences across suppression conditions, suggesting that speed-accuracy tradeoffs did not account for the observed effects. In sum, the results demonstrate a clear interaction pattern in which articulatory suppression exerted a pronounced detrimental effect on auditory-verbal recall, visual suppression exerted a pronounced detrimental effect on visually presented nonverbal stimuli, and articulatory suppression did not significantly reduce recall for visually presented verbal stimuli.

Discussion

The present study examined how articulatory and visual suppression affect short-term serial recall for auditory-verbal, visual-verbal, and visual-nonverbal stimuli in a repeated-measures 2 x 3 design. The key result was a significant interaction between suppression type and stimulus type. This interaction took a theoretically informative form: articulatory suppression strongly impaired auditory-verbal recall, visual suppression strongly impaired visual-nonverbal recall, and articulatory suppression did not significantly impair visual-verbal recall. At the level of basic patterns, these findings offer a mixed but clarifying view of the multi-component model of working memory. On the one hand, the strong impairment of auditory-verbal recall under articulatory suppression is entirely consistent with the notion that spoken verbal material is maintained via phonological processes that depend on articulatory rehearsal. That result replicates a core prediction of models that posit a phonological loop and provides convergent evidence that interfering with overt or covert speech disrupts phonological maintenance for auditory input. The effect size for the articulatory suppression impairment on auditory-verbal material was large and robust, indicating that the suppression manipulation effectively interrupted the rehearsal processes relevant to that stimulus class. This outcome strengthens the case that phonological rehearsal operations are critical for maintaining spoken verbal sequences over short retention intervals. On the other hand, the failure of articulatory suppression to produce a reliable reduction in recall for visually presented verbal stimuli challenges a straightforward, obligatory recoding account. If all visually presented words or letters were obligatorily recoded into phonological form and maintained primarily by the phonological loop, then blocking articulatory rehearsal should have impaired performance with visual-verbal stimuli to a similar degree as it did for auditory-verbal stimuli. That was not observed. Instead, visual-verbal recall was relatively resilient to articulatory suppression in this study. This pattern suggests several interpretive possibilities. One possibility is that participants used visual encoding and maintenance strategies for visually presented words, relying on the visuospatial sketchpad or on episodic visual traces rather than on phonological recoding. Visual strategies might be especially likely when the visual form of items is distinctive or when task instructions do not emphasize rote verbal rehearsal. In the present procedure, participants were allowed to type responses and were not explicitly instructed to rehearse verbally, which could have encouraged alternative encoding strategies. Another possibility is that the recoding of visual-verbal items into phonological form is conditional rather than obligatory. Specific task parameters—such as the speed of presentation, the nature of the items, and the retention interval—may determine whether recoding is engaged. In our design, the presentation pacing and the availability of visual features might have favored non-phonological maintenance for visual-verbal items. A further possibility is that the articulatory suppression manipulation, while sufficient to disrupt phonological maintenance of spoken material, may not have been sufficiently potent to eliminate covert recoding in the visual domain. Although participants maintained the instructed rate of overt vocalization, some forms of subvocal coding might persist or alternative phonological strategies might be employed that are less vulnerable to the particular suppression task used. Evaluating these alternatives would require manipulations that vary the intensity and form of suppression and that explicitly probe participants' encoding strategies. The finding that visual suppression selectively impaired visual-nonverbal recall aligns well with expectations for the visuospatial sketchpad. The dynamic visual noise used in the visual suppression condition appears to have taxed visual maintenance processes and thereby reduced the fidelity of representations of abstract shapes. This selective impairment supports the idea that visual material without inherent verbal labels relies on visuospatial maintenance mechanisms that are vulnerable to visual interference. The pattern that visual suppression did not strongly impair auditory-verbal stimuli further underscores the domain-specificity of the suppression effects. Taken together, the interaction suggests that suppression manipulations can be effective tools for dissociating subsystems when the stimulus categories clearly map onto hypothesized maintenance mechanisms. The differential pattern between auditory-verbal and visual-verbal stimuli raises important theoretical questions about the relationship between input modality and the working memory system that ultimately maintains information. The multi-component model often asserts that phonological storage operates on both auditory and recoded visual input. Our data suggest that this cross-modal recoding may not be inevitable. Instead, participants seem to have access to multiple representational formats and may flexibly choose among them depending on task demands. Such flexibility is consistent with more recent conceptions of working memory as a set of interacting processes that can draw on sensory traces, verbal rehearsal, and long-term memory representations as needed. The present findings therefore contribute to the view that phonological maintenance is a powerful but not exclusive route for preserving verbally meaningful information. Methodological factors likely influenced the observed pattern and are important to consider in the interpretation. One key factor is the absence of a pure no-suppression baseline condition in the 2 x 3 design, which complicates inferences about absolute suppression effects. Although the two suppression conditions were compared directly and produced distinct patterns, having a separate baseline block without suppression would have allowed determination of whether both suppression types produced impairments relative to a neutral condition or whether one condition produced facilitation relative to the other. Future studies should include a no-suppression baseline to disambiguate those possibilities. A second methodological consideration is the nature of the visual-verbal stimuli. In the present study visual-verbal material consisted of words presented in a conventional font. It is possible that many of these words were readily encoded visually and that their orthographic distinctiveness reduced the need for phonological recoding. Using stimuli such as single letters or phonologically confusable words might increase reliance on the phonological loop. Third, the dynamic visual noise used for visual suppression, while effective for disrupting visual maintenance, may not precisely mirror all forms of visuospatial interference used in other studies; replication with alternative visual suppression tasks (for example, spatial tapping or visual tracking) would help confirm the specificity of the observed effects. Fourth, individual differences emerged in the correlation between baseline recall and the magnitude of articulatory suppression effects for auditory material, indicating that participant characteristics like working memory capacity and rehearsal habits may modulate susceptibility to interference. Future research could measure individual differences more comprehensively and examine how they impact strategy selection. The theoretical implications of the current results are multifold. First, the robust impairment of auditory-verbal material under articulatory suppression strengthens the evidence that phonological rehearsal contributes importantly to the maintenance of spoken sequences. This supports the traditional view of a phonological component specialized for verbal material. Second, the resilience of visual-verbal material to articulatory suppression suggests that recoding of visual input into phonological form is not automatic and may be contingent on task parameters and item characteristics. This finding calls for refinement of models that claim obligatory cross-modal recoding into the phonological loop and suggests a more flexible architecture in which multiple maintenance routes compete or cooperate. Third, the selective impairment of visual-nonverbal stimuli under visual suppression confirms the domain-specific vulnerability of visuospatial maintenance processes and provides converging evidence for the separability of verbal and visuospatial subsystems under appropriate task conditions. These theoretical inferences also lead to concrete directions for future work. A useful next step would be to manipulate stimulus characteristics that encourage or discourage phonological recoding, such as using orthographically ambiguous words, single letters, or pseudowords, and to test whether such manipulations change the susceptibility of visual-verbal material to articulatory suppression. Adding a no-suppression baseline, as mentioned earlier, would clarify absolute effects. Another promising avenue is to vary the timing of suppression onset and offset to determine whether suppression during encoding versus maintenance differentially affects recall; such timing manipulations could reveal whether recoding occurs primarily during initial encoding or is continuously used during maintenance. Neuroimaging or electrophysiological methods could be used to assess whether articulatory suppression alters neural markers associated with phonological rehearsal and whether visual suppression alters markers associated with visuospatial maintenance. Studies that combine behavioral suppression manipulations with brain imaging would shed light on the neural loci of the observed dissociations and could test whether the same neural networks are engaged by visual-verbal maintenance under different strategies. Developmental and clinical extensions of this work would also be informative. For example, children and older adults show different patterns of strategy use and memory capacity; testing whether the balance between visual and phonological routes shifts with age would contribute to understanding developmental trajectories of working memory. Similarly, clinical populations with language or visuospatial deficits could reveal how selective impairments in rehearsal or imagery processes affect reliance on recoding. The present study had several limitations that should be noted. As already discussed, the absence of a neutral baseline limits interpretation of whether both suppression tasks caused impairments relative to an unsuppressed condition. The specific forms of suppression used, while standard in the literature, represent only a subset of possible interference tasks and therefore the findings may not generalize to all forms of articulatory or visual interference. The sample consisted of undergraduate volunteers and therefore may not generalize to broader populations. Finally, although experimenters monitored suppression compliance, covert strategy use is inherently difficult to observe and report, and self-reported strategy assessments after the experiment may be subject to memory and demand biases. In conclusion, the present study replicates the strong vulnerability of auditory-verbal serial recall to articulatory suppression and the strong vulnerability of visual-nonverbal recall to visual suppression. The lack of a reliable articulatory suppression effect on visual-verbal recall suggests that visual-to-phonological recoding is not obligatory and underscores the flexibility of working memory maintenance strategies. These findings refine our understanding of the phonological loop and visuospatial sketchpad and point to fruitful directions for future empirical and theoretical work that will further elucidate when and how different stimulus types are maintained in working memory.
