Introduction

Individual differences in language comprehension have been a central concern in psycholinguistics and cognitive psychology for many decades. Many theorists have proposed that variability in comprehension arises in part from differences in short-term or working memory capacity. Working memory is often invoked as a limited-capacity system that temporarily stores and manipulates information relevant to ongoing cognitive tasks. The notion that greater working memory capacity supports superior language processing has been influential in accounts of sentence parsing, ambiguity resolution, and discourse comprehension. Across a range of studies, working memory capacity has been associated with the ability to maintain information across intervening material, to integrate referents with antecedents, and to retain propositions for subsequent inference. Despite this, the precise relationship between working memory capacity and memory for specific aspects of discourse, such as semantic content versus syntactic form, remains unsettled. A related and important issue concerns how the position of information within a discourse affects later recognition and whether working memory constraints modulate position effects. Memory for discourse may be shaped both by how information is encoded at first hearing and by how it is maintained or consolidated in the moments after encoding. Empirically, many studies have documented that the gist or meaning of linguistic material is often better retained than the exact lexical or syntactic form. This pattern has been interpreted as reflecting a preference of the cognitive system for semantic abstraction over surface details when forming discursive representations. At the same time, other research has shown that surface form can be preserved under certain conditions, such as when the material is rehearsed, highly novel, or explicitly attended for form. The tension between these findings highlights the need to examine conditions under which meaning-based retention predominates and when form-based retention is preserved. One factor that may contribute to this pattern is working memory capacity. Individuals with greater capacity may be more able to maintain surface form information alongside semantic content, if they allocate resources to phonological or syntactic representation. Conversely, individuals with lower working memory capacity may preferentially encode and retain gist because it is more economical and readily integrated with existing knowledge. The question of whether working memory supports retention of form versus meaning is therefore theoretically important and empirically tractable. Another factor that could interact with working memory capacity is the serial position of the target sentence within a passage. Classic serial position effects such as primacy and recency are well established in short-term memory tasks, with early and late items generally being better recalled than middle items. In discourse contexts, however, serial position effects can be more nuanced because the flow of meaning and the establishment of discourse structure influence encoding. For example, sentences that occur at the beginning of a passage often serve to introduce topics, establish themes, or set the conceptual frame, and these features may make their meaning particularly salient and memorable. By contrast, sentences in the middle of a passage may be more likely to be processed for integration rather than for isolated retention, which could reduce their recognizability when repeated later. Sentences at the end of a passage may benefit from recency but may also be more susceptible to decay if intervening processes occupy working memory. Understanding how serial position within discourse interacts with the type of information retained (meaning versus form) can help clarify the mechanisms of encoding and maintenance in naturalistic language processing. Several methodological approaches have been used to address these issues, including recall, recognition, paraphrase, and change detection paradigms. Change detection tasks, in which participants must detect whether a repeated sentence has been altered in meaning or form relative to an initial presentation, allow researchers to probe what aspects of a sentence are stored and accessible during later recognition. Such tasks can distinguish between detection based on semantic equivalence and detection based on formal identity, thereby isolating memory for meaning versus memory for form. Change detection paradigms also permit manipulations of delay, intervening material, and serial position, making them suitable for examining the joint roles of working memory and discourse position. In the extant literature, studies using change detection and recognition tasks have often reported better recognition for semantic changes than for surface changes, particularly when participants are not instructed to attend to form. This tendency suggests that listeners naturally encode meaning more robustly, a pattern consistent with theories of gist-based encoding and with the prioritization of semantic processing in comprehension. However, when explicit instructions encourage attention to form, or when the task inherently emphasizes verbatim repetition, participants can and do retain surface details. These task-dependent outcomes indicate that attentional allocation and strategic encoding play roles alongside capacity constraints. The operationalization of working memory capacity in this literature has varied considerably. Simple measures such as digit span forward have a long history as indices of short-term memory, whereas complex span tasks that interleave storage and processing demands are argued to better index the active, multi-component nature of working memory relevant to language comprehension. There is ongoing debate about which measures are most predictive of language processing and which aspects of working memory they capture. Some studies have found robust correlations between complex span measures and comprehension outcomes such as inference generation, syntactic ambiguity resolution, and discourse updating. Other studies have found more modest or inconsistent correlations when using simple span measures, particularly for tasks that tap into semantic integration or long-term knowledge. These mixed findings motivate empirical comparisons that include multiple measures or that critically assess the sensitivity of digit span as an index of the relevant capacities. Given these considerations, there is a clear rationale for examining how recognition memory for semantic and syntactic aspects of sentences varies across serial position in discourse and how individual differences in working memory capacity relate to these patterns. The present study was designed to address three interrelated questions. First, does recognition memory for the meaning of a sentence differ from memory for its surface form when listeners are asked to detect changes following later repetitions of sentences? Second, does the serial position of a target sentence within a passage modulate any difference between memory for meaning and memory for form? Third, do individual differences in working memory capacity, operationalized via a digit span task, predict recognition performance for meaning versus form across positions? We approached these questions with an experimental paradigm in which participants listened to short passages and later heard sentences that either matched or differed from earlier sentences in meaning or form. Target sentences were placed at the beginning, middle, or end of passages to test for serial position effects. Working memory capacity was assessed with a standard digit span task administered in the same session. Based on prior evidence and theoretical considerations, we formulated competing expectations. On the one hand, we expected an overall advantage for memory of meaning over memory of form, reflecting gist-based encoding tendencies during natural listening. On the other hand, we anticipated that this advantage might be modulated by serial position, with a stronger meaning advantage for sentences that occurred early in a passage because of their role in establishing thematic structure. Regarding the role of working memory, we predicted that participants with higher digit span scores would be better able to detect form changes, because maintaining surface representations requires more storage of phonological and syntactic information, whereas memory for meaning might be less dependent on capacity. Alternatively, if digit span is an insensitive measure of the working memory processes required for discourse memory, we might observe no reliable association between digit span and recognition performance. These competing hypotheses make clear predictions that can be evaluated with a within-subjects experimental design and appropriate analyses. The study thus aims to clarify how type of information (semantic versus syntactic), serial position, and individual differences in working memory capacity interact to determine recognition memory for sentences within discourse. By doing so, it contributes to broader debates about the nature of representations formed during comprehension and the cognitive resources that support their maintenance and retrieval.

Methods

Participants were 74 adults recruited from a university participant pool and surrounding community. All participants were native speakers of English, reported normal hearing, and had no history of language impairment. The age range of participants was 18 to 35 years, and the sample included both undergraduate students and community volunteers. Participation was voluntary and participants provided informed consent prior to the experiment. They received course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. The experimental session lasted approximately 60 minutes for each participant. Materials consisted of 48 short passages constructed for the purpose of the experiment. Each passage comprised six sentences and was designed to convey a coherent mini-discourse on a familiar everyday topic. Topics included everyday scenarios such as going to a café, preparing for a trip, describing a routine, and similar contexts. For each passage, one target sentence was designated to appear in one of three serial positions: the beginning (sentence 1), the middle (sentence 3 or 4), or the end (sentence 6). Across the full set of materials, target sentences were balanced so that each serial position contained an equal number of targets across the experiment. For each target sentence we created two alternate versions: a semantic-change version in which a key content word or phrase was altered so that the meaning of the sentence differed from the original, and a form-change version in which surface wording or syntactic structure was altered without changing the core meaning. The semantic changes involved substitutions that altered propositional content (for example, changing "He took the bus" to "He drove the car"), whereas the form changes involved paraphrases or syntactic alternations that preserved the meaning (for example, changing "The manager approved the request" to "The request was approved by the manager"). In addition to the changed versions, unchanged control versions identical to the originals were prepared for filler trials. The stimuli were recorded by a trained voice actor in a quiet studio to ensure consistent prosody and intelligibility. Recordings were normalized for amplitude and presented via high-quality headphones at a comfortable listening level. The experiment employed a within-subjects design in which each participant heard all three serial positions and both types of change across trials, but with counterbalancing to avoid repetition of the same target in more than one form for a given participant. Each participant completed 48 trials in the main task, of which two-thirds contained a change (half semantic and half form, evenly distributed across positions) and one-third were unchanged fillers. The order of trials was randomized for each participant. Working memory capacity was assessed using a digit span task administered after the listening task. We used a standard forward digit span task in which sequences of digits were read aloud at a steady pace and participants were asked to repeat the sequence in order. The digit span task started with two-digit sequences and increased in length until the participant failed two sequences of the same length. The digit span score was computed as the maximum length at which the participant successfully repeated two sequences or, alternatively, the number of sequences correct out of a specified set. For the purposes of analysis we used the conventional maximum span length as the working memory index. The experiment was conducted in a quiet laboratory room. Participants were instructed that they would hear a series of short passages and that, occasionally, sentences from these passages would be repeated later in the session. They were told that the repeated sentences could be exactly the same or could have changed in meaning or wording, and their task was to indicate whether the repeated sentence was identical to the earlier one or changed. When they judged that a sentence had changed, they were further asked to indicate whether the change affected the meaning or only the form of the sentence. Participants indicated their responses on a computer keyboard by pressing labeled keys and were encouraged to respond as accurately as possible rather than as quickly as possible. Before the experimental trials, participants completed six practice trials that included examples of semantic change, form change, and no change, with feedback provided to ensure that they understood the distinction between changes in meaning and changes in form. No feedback was provided during the experimental trials. Trials proceeded as follows. Participants listened to an initial passage containing the target sentence in the designated position. After the passage was presented, a short filler task involving a simple distractor (a one-minute arithmetic task) was administered to limit immediate rehearsal of surface details. Following the distractor, participants heard single sentences presented in isolation; these were either repeats of the earlier target sentences (in identical, semantic-change, or form-change forms) or unrelated filler sentences. For each repeat, participants made a two-stage judgment: first, whether the sentence was the same or different; and second, if different, whether the difference was semantic or formal. The dependent variables of interest were accuracy of change detection (same vs different) and accuracy in classifying the type of change when a change was present. For the majority of analyses we focused on correct identification of semantic changes and correct identification of form changes when changes had occurred. Reaction times for each judgment were recorded but were treated as secondary measures because response speed was not emphasized in the instructions. Data preprocessing involved exclusion of trials with missed responses or extremely long response latencies indicative of distraction. Trials in which participants failed to respond on either the same/different or the change-type judgment were excluded from analyses, and participants with more than 20% missing or invalid trials would have been excluded; in practice, no participant exceeded this threshold and therefore all participants were retained. Descriptive statistics were computed for accuracy rates for semantic-change detection and form-change detection separately for each serial position. Prior to inferential analysis, we inspected distributions and assessed sphericity where appropriate. The primary inferential approach was a two-way repeated measures analysis of variance with factors of Change Type (semantic, form) and Position (beginning, middle, end) on change identification accuracy. Post-hoc paired comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. To address the role of individual differences in working memory capacity, we conducted two complementary analyses. First, we computed Pearson correlations between digit span scores and overall accuracy for semantic and form changes. Second, we entered digit span as a covariate in repeated measures analyses (ANCOVA) to test whether the main effects and interactions involving Change Type and Position persisted when controlling for span. In addition, we performed a median split to create high-span and low-span groups and conducted mixed-model ANOVAs to examine potential interactions between group and the within-subjects factors. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta squared for ANOVAs and Cohen's d for t-tests. All statistical tests used an alpha level of .05, two-tailed, unless otherwise noted. Statistical analyses were conducted with standard statistical software and checked for consistency with nonparametric alternatives where assumptions appeared questionable.

Results

Descriptive statistics for change detection accuracy are presented first. Across participants and conditions, mean accuracy for detecting semantic changes was higher than for detecting form changes. Specifically, the grand mean accuracy for semantic-change detection was 0.78 (SD = 0.12) and for form-change detection was 0.63 (SD = 0.15). When broken down by serial position, semantic detection accuracy at the beginning position averaged 0.84 (SD = 0.10), in the middle position averaged 0.76 (SD = 0.12), and at the end averaged 0.74 (SD = 0.14). For form detection, accuracy at the beginning averaged 0.69 (SD = 0.14), in the middle 0.62 (SD = 0.15), and at the end 0.59 (SD = 0.16). Inspection of these descriptive values suggested an overall advantage for semantic over form detection and a possible interaction with position in which the semantic advantage appeared largest at the beginning of passages. To test these observations statistically, we conducted a 2 (Change Type: semantic, form) x 3 (Position: beginning, middle, end) repeated measures ANOVA on detection accuracy. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of Change Type, F(1,73) = 68.50, p < .001, partial eta squared = .48, indicating that semantic changes were detected more accurately than form changes overall. The main effect of Position was also significant, F(2,146) = 9.21, p < .001, partial eta squared = .11, reflecting differences in detection accuracy across beginning, middle, and end positions. The Change Type by Position interaction was significant as well, F(2,146) = 6.37, p = .002, partial eta squared = .08, confirming that the semantic advantage varied by serial position. Given the significant interaction, we performed follow-up paired comparisons to clarify the pattern. At the beginning position, semantic detection accuracy (M = .84, SD = .10) was significantly greater than form detection accuracy (M = .69, SD = .14), t(73) = 8.12, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.94. At the middle position, the difference between semantic (M = .76, SD = .12) and form (M = .62, SD = .15) detection was smaller but still present, t(73) = 6.03, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.70. At the end position, semantic detection (M = .74, SD = .14) exceeded form detection (M = .59, SD = .16), t(73) = 5.22, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.61. Although all pairwise comparisons were significant, the effect sizes indicate that the magnitude of the semantic advantage was largest at the beginning position. Additional comparisons across positions within each change type revealed that for semantic changes, detection at the beginning was higher than at the middle, t(73) = 3.42, p = .001, and higher than at the end, t(73) = 3.96, p < .001, whereas detection at the middle and end did not differ reliably, t(73) = 1.10, p = .275. For form changes, detection differed marginally across positions, with beginning detection higher than end detection, t(73) = 2.44, p = .017, though this difference did not survive conservative correction for multiple comparisons. These position-related analyses reinforce the idea that beginning sentences are particularly well-encoded for meaning. We next examined whether individual differences in working memory, as measured by digit span, were associated with detection performance. Digit span scores in the sample ranged from 4 to 8 with a mean of 6.1 (SD = 1.1). Pearson correlations between digit span and overall semantic-change detection accuracy were r = .11, p = .33, and between digit span and overall form-change detection accuracy were r = .14, p = .21. These correlations did not reach statistical significance, suggesting minimal linear association between digit span and detection accuracy for either change type. To further test the possible role of span, we entered digit span as a covariate in a 2 x 3 repeated measures ANCOVA. The main effect of Change Type remained significant when controlling for span, F(1,72) = 61.04, p < .001, partial eta squared = .46. The main effect of Position also remained significant, F(2,144) = 8.74, p < .001, partial eta squared = .11, and the Change Type by Position interaction remained significant, F(2,144) = 5.98, p = .003, partial eta squared = .08. Critically, the covariate of digit span did not significantly predict overall detection performance in this model, F(1,72) = 2.10, p = .15. In addition, there were no significant interactions between digit span and the within-subjects factors, indicating that span did not moderate the difference between semantic and form detection across positions. As a complementary approach, we performed a median split on digit span to form high-span (n = 37) and low-span (n = 37) groups and conducted a mixed ANOVA with Change Type and Position as within-subjects factors and Span Group as a between-subjects factor. This analysis revealed no significant main effect of Span Group, F(1,72) = 1.87, p = .18, and no significant interactions involving Span Group, including the three-way interaction, F(2,144) = 0.54, p = .58. Both groups exhibited the same qualitative pattern of higher semantic than form detection and greater semantic advantage at the beginning position. To assess the robustness of these null effects, we computed Bayes factors for the correlation between digit span and form-change detection. The Bayes factor for the null versus the alternative hypothesis was approximately 3.2, indicating anecdotal to moderate evidence favoring the null hypothesis of no relationship in our sample. We also examined reaction time data to provide supplementary information about potential speed-accuracy trade-offs. Mean response times for correct change-type classifications did not differ substantially between semantic and form changes (semantic mean RT = 1,420 ms, SD = 320 ms; form mean RT = 1,460 ms, SD = 350 ms), and there was no reliable correlation between digit span and mean RT. These secondary analyses suggest that the absence of span effects was not due to a shift in speed-accuracy strategy across span levels. Finally, we conducted item-level analyses to ensure that the observed patterns were not driven by a small number of particularly salient items. Accuracy distributions across items were reasonably uniform, and removal of the three highest and three lowest performing items did not alter the main effects or the lack of span-related effects. Taken together, the results indicate a clear advantage for memory of meaning over memory of form when sentences are later repeated, a modulation of this advantage by serial position that is strongest at the beginning of passages, and no reliable relationship between digit span and detection of either type of change.

Discussion

The present study examined recognition memory for semantic and syntactic aspects of sentences embedded within short passages and assessed whether individual differences in digit span working memory capacity related to performance. The patterns observed provide several insights into how listeners encode and retain different types of sentence information in discourse contexts. Most notably, memory for meaning was consistently superior to memory for form across the experiment. This semantic advantage was robust, as indicated by a large main effect of Change Type and substantial effect sizes in paired comparisons. The finding aligns with theoretical perspectives that emphasize the primacy of semantic processing during natural language comprehension and supports the idea that listeners preferentially form gist-based representations that capture propositional content rather than verbatim surface details. The semantic advantage was not uniform across serial positions however; it was most pronounced when target sentences occurred at the beginning of passages. The Change Type by Position interaction and follow-up contrasts showed that beginning sentences yielded especially high accuracy for semantic-change detection relative to form-change detection. This position-dependent pattern suggests that the introductory role of opening sentences may lead to encoding that prioritizes conceptual framing and thematic establishment, making their meanings both salient and durable in memory. Beginning sentences often serve as topic setters or organizers of subsequent material, and listeners may devote processing resources to extracting and encoding the central meaning to scaffold comprehension of the passage. The primacy of beginnings for semantic retention is consistent with models of discourse processing that posit the early construction of a mental representation or situation model, anchored by the opening material. In contrast to the pronounced semantic advantage, memory for form was relatively weaker and more susceptible to decline across positions. Form detection accuracy was lower overall and less variable by position, although beginning position did show somewhat higher form detection than the end position. This pattern indicates that surface form information may not be as reliably preserved in naturalistic listening unless listeners are explicitly instructed to attend to verbatim aspects or unless the material has unusual or distinctive surface properties that encourage retention. These results speak to a broader principle that comprehension processes tend to favor semantic cohesion and integration over verbatim preservation, especially in tasks resembling everyday listening. Perhaps the most theoretically consequential finding concerns the role of working memory capacity as indexed by digit span. In this study, digit span did not significantly predict detection accuracy for semantic or form changes, and controlling for digit span did not alter the main effects or their interaction with position. Correlational analyses, ANCOVA, and group comparisons based on median split all converged on the conclusion that digit span was not associated with individual differences in recognition memory for these types of discourse changes. There are several plausible interpretations of this null result. One straightforward possibility is that simple digit span forward may not tap the specific working memory processes that support retention of sentence-level semantic and syntactic information in discourse contexts. Digit span predominantly measures short-term storage of serial phonological information, whereas the maintenance of sentence meaning likely relies more on integrative processes that draw on long-term semantic networks and on complex working memory operations. Other measures, such as complex span tasks that require simultaneous storage and processing or tasks that directly engage verbal manipulation and updating, may be more sensitive to the cognitive resources used during discourse comprehension. A second interpretation is that the task used in the present study fostered encoding strategies that obviated the influence of digit-span-limited storage. For example, listeners may have automatically integrated target sentence meanings into an evolving situation model, transferring memory traces into more durable long-term or semantically organized representations. If so, individual differences in pure short-term phonological storage would play a smaller role in subsequent recognition, and variance in performance would be determined more by factors such as semantic knowledge, attentional allocation, or comprehension skill. A third possibility is that the digit span range in our sample, though adequate, did not include extremes of low and high capacity that would provide power to detect small associations. Our sample of seventy-four adults clustered around typical college-age digit span levels, and it remains possible that a more heterogeneous sample or larger sample size might reveal subtler effects. Nonetheless, Bayes factor analyses offered some support for the absence of a meaningful correlation in the present data. The absence of digit span effects in the current study is consistent with prior research that has found mixed associations between simple span measures and discourse-level comprehension. Many studies reporting strong relationships have used complex span measures, which incorporate processing components hypothesized to reflect the active maintenance and manipulation demands of language comprehension. The distinction between simple and complex span measures thus emerges as crucial for understanding which cognitive capacities are implicated in different aspects of language processing. In considering the implications of these results, it is important to address the ecological validity and generalizability of the paradigm. The passages used in this study were designed to be naturalistic but necessarily simplified relative to real-world discourse. Moreover, the change detection task, though useful for isolating semantic versus form retention, may not capture all dimensions of comprehension that engage working memory, such as integrating multiple references over long intervals, resolving ambiguity under noise, or generating inferences under time pressure. Future studies could employ longer and more complex discourses, introduce manipulations of processing load, or vary the delay between initial presentation and repetition to probe the dynamics of maintenance and decay more fully. Several specific avenues for future research emerge from the current findings. First, researchers should examine whether complex span measures, such as reading span or operation span, show stronger associations with recognition of form and meaning than digit span. Including multiple measures in the same study would clarify the extent to which different operationalizations of working memory predict distinct components of discourse memory. Second, experimental manipulations that force attention to surface form, such as instructions to monitor for verbatim repetition or the presence of rhymes or unusual surface features, could test whether enhanced encoding of form interacts with working memory capacity. If individuals with higher complex-span capacity show greater benefit from form-focused instructions, that would support the idea that capacity matters when tasks require simultaneous storage and controlled processing. Third, neurocognitive measures such as EEG or fMRI could be used to examine the neural correlates of encoding and retrieval for semantic and form information and how these processes vary with individual differences in capacity. Such work could reveal whether distinct neural systems support gist-based versus verbatim representations and whether capacity differences modulate these systems. Fourth, developmental and aging studies could address whether the pattern of semantic advantage and position effects hold across the lifespan and whether working memory measures differentially predict performance at different ages. For example, older adults may show different reliance on gist versus form due to changes in cognitive resources and knowledge. The current study has limitations that should be acknowledged. The operationalization of working memory was limited to a forward digit span measure administered in a single session. While digit span is a valid and simple index of short-term storage, it is not a comprehensive measure of working memory. Additionally, although the passages were intended to be natural, the relatively brief lengths and constrained topics may limit the generalizability of the findings to longer or more complex real-world discourse. The sample consisted primarily of young adults with relatively homogeneous educational backgrounds, which could reduce variability in both working memory and listening strategies and thereby limit detection of individual differences. Finally, while the study controlled for some potential confounds via randomized trial order and counterbalancing, uncontrolled factors such as participants' prior familiarity with topics or motivation may have contributed to variability in encoding and retrieval. Despite these limitations, the study contributes to theoretical debates about the nature of representations formed during comprehension and the cognitive resources that support their maintenance. The robust finding that meaning is better retained than form, particularly for sentences occurring at the beginning of passages, underscores the primacy of semantic encoding in natural listening. The lack of relationship between digit span and recognition performance cautions against over-reliance on simple span measures as indicators of the cognitive processes underlying discourse memory. Instead, the data suggest that retention of sentence meaning may be supported by integration into semantic memory systems and by strategic allocation of attention independent of short-term phonological storage. In conclusion, when listeners hear a short passage they appear to preferentially encode and retain the meaning of sentences, especially those that establish the discourse framework, and this pattern is relatively insensitive to individual differences in digit-span working memory capacity. Future work using broader and more sensitive assessments of working memory, varied discourse materials, and neurocognitive measures will be valuable for delineating the boundary conditions under which capacity influences the retention of form and meaning in language comprehension.
