Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental biological process that supports cognitive, emotional, and physiological functioning. Adequate sleep is essential for attention, memory consolidation, mood regulation, and immune function. Conversely, poor sleep has been associated with a range of adverse outcomes including impaired academic performance, mood disturbances, and increased risk for psychiatric disorders. Anxiety disorders are among the most common mental health concerns in young adults and student populations. Anxiety can manifest as persistent worry, physiological arousal, and difficulties with concentration and daily functioning. Students frequently report elevated anxiety during their academic years due to examinations, deadlines, social transitions, and financial pressures. Sleep difficulties are also prevalent in student populations due to irregular schedules, late-night studying, social activities, and technology use. The co-occurrence of poor sleep and elevated anxiety in students can have compounding effects on well-being and academic success. Understanding the relationship between sleep quality and anxiety in student samples is therefore important for both clinical and educational interventions. There is a substantial body of research that has examined links between sleep disturbances and various forms of anxiety. Some studies have reported moderate associations between insomnia symptoms and generalized anxiety symptoms in non-clinical samples. Other work suggests that poor sleep predicts increases in anxiety symptoms over time, while elevated anxiety also predicts subsequent sleep disturbance in longitudinal designs. These findings have led many researchers to conceptualize sleep and anxiety as having a reciprocal or bidirectional relationship. Theoretical models propose multiple mechanisms that could underlie the association between poor sleep and heightened anxiety. One proposed mechanism involves heightened physiological arousal associated with anxiety that interferes with the ability to initiate and maintain sleep. Another mechanism highlights the role of maladaptive cognitive processes, such as worry and rumination, which can prolong sleep latency and reduce restorative sleep. Dysregulation of stress-related neurobiological systems, including the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, has also been implicated as a shared pathway linking poor sleep and anxiety. Circadian misalignment and irregular sleep-wake patterns can further exacerbate mood instability and anxiety symptoms. Lifestyle factors that are common among students, such as caffeine consumption, irregular exercise, and late-night electronic device use, may mediate or moderate the link between sleep and anxiety. Notably, research addressing sleep and anxiety often reports substantial individual differences, and demographic factors such as age and gender sometimes modify observed relationships. For example, some studies report that female students experience higher anxiety and poorer sleep compared to male students, although this pattern is not universal. Age-related differences within young adult samples are less pronounced, but transitions between late adolescence and early adulthood can involve sleep pattern changes that could influence anxiety levels. The existing literature is also marked by variability in measurement approaches, with some studies relying on single-item sleep measures and others using multi-item validated questionnaires. Similarly, anxiety has been operationalized in diverse ways, ranging from screening measures for clinical anxiety to general self-report symptom scales. Self-report measures are convenient and widely used, but they are susceptible to reporting biases and may not capture objective sleep parameters. Objective measures such as actigraphy or polysomnography provide detailed information on sleep architecture and timing, but these methods are more resource intensive and less feasible for large-scale student samples. Despite these methodological differences, a consistent pattern of moderate associations between poorer subjective sleep and higher anxiety has emerged in the literature. This pattern has raised important questions about causality, the influence of comorbid conditions such as depression, and the generalizability of findings across diverse student groups. While some longitudinal and experimental studies have provided evidence for sleep disturbance contributing to subsequent anxiety, the directionality of effects remains under active investigation. Moreover, there are gaps in the literature regarding whether age and gender consistently influence the sleep-anxiety relationship within student populations. Clarifying whether demographic variables moderate the association can inform targeted prevention and intervention strategies on campuses. There are also practical implications for student health services, which may need to consider integrating sleep-focused interventions within counseling for anxiety. Interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia and sleep hygiene education have been shown to reduce sleep problems and, in some cases, concomitantly reduce anxiety symptoms. However, the applicability and uptake of such interventions among students remain variable, and it is important to establish robust evidence of the sleep-anxiety link within specific student contexts. In addition to clinical implications, research examining sleep and anxiety in students contributes to theoretical understanding of stress regulation and resilience during a vulnerable developmental period. Given the prevalence of both sleep irregularity and anxiety symptoms in student populations, there is a pressing need for research that characterizes their association using reliable measures. The present study was designed to contribute to this literature by examining the association between sleep quality and anxiety levels in a sample of students. The study utilized two self-report questionnaires to assess subjective sleep quality and anxiety symptom severity. By focusing on a specific student population during their academic years, the study aimed to reflect a context in which irregular sleep and anxiety are likely to co-occur. The primary research question was whether poorer sleep quality would be associated with higher levels of anxiety symptoms in this sample. Based on prior empirical findings and theoretical models suggesting a positive association, the study hypothesized that there would be a positive correlation between indices of poor sleep and anxiety severity. Secondary questions concerned whether demographic factors such as age and gender would meaningfully influence the strength or direction of the association. It was plausible, based on mixed prior findings, that gender or age could moderate the relationship, but this was treated as exploratory in the current study. The study also aimed to examine whether the observed association would remain after statistically controlling for age and gender. An important limitation of correlative work is the inability to determine causation, and this study was explicit in retaining a correlational design to examine relationships rather than test causal mechanisms. Nevertheless, documenting robust associations in student samples can help to motivate longitudinal or experimental follow-up work. The current investigation sought to address several methodological needs by employing validated multi-item self-report measures, reporting internal consistency estimates, and conducting appropriate statistical controls. Data were collected from a convenience sample of students, and analyses included descriptive statistics, bivariate correlations, partial correlations, and regression modeling. The analytic approach was deliberately chosen to provide both an estimate of the bivariate association and to evaluate the association while adjusting for demographic covariates. Overall, the study intended to provide clear empirical evidence regarding the magnitude and robustness of the association between sleep quality and anxiety in a student sample. Findings were expected to have implications for campus mental health services, student well-being initiatives, and research directions focused on sleep and affective regulation in young adults. The work contributes to a growing recognition that sleep health is a foundational component of mental health and academic functioning. By situating the study within the broader literature, the present investigation aimed to bolster understanding of the interplay between sleep and anxiety at a critical developmental life stage. The results are reported below with attention to transparency in measurement and statistical procedures. The discussion section that follows will interpret findings in light of theoretical mechanisms, practical implications, limitations, and directions for future research.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university student population through email announcements, classroom invitations, and flyer postings. A total of 113 students completed the study procedures and provided usable data. Participants included both female and male students and represented a range of academic years within the university. Inclusion criteria required participants to be currently enrolled as students and to be between the ages of 18 and 30 years. Exclusion criteria were minimal and included inability to read English or inability to provide informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the institution's research ethics committee prior to data collection. All participants provided informed consent electronically before beginning the questionnaires. Participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire that included items on age, gender, year of study, and self-reported health conditions. The primary measures for the study were two self-report questionnaires assessing sleep quality and anxiety symptom severity. Sleep quality was assessed using a standardized multi-item sleep quality index designed to capture subjective sleep disturbances and overall sleep quality over the prior month. The sleep questionnaire yielded a global score in which higher values indicated poorer sleep quality. Anxiety symptoms were assessed using a validated self-report anxiety symptom scale that quantified severity of generalized anxiety features over the prior two weeks. The anxiety questionnaire produced a continuous total score with higher values reflecting greater symptom severity. Both questionnaires have been widely used in research and clinical settings to screen and quantify symptom burden in nonclinical samples. Participants completed the questionnaires online through a secure survey platform during a single assessment session. The survey was structured so that the demographic questions appeared first, followed by the anxiety questionnaire, and then the sleep questionnaire to reduce potential priming effects. Completion time for the entire assessment typically ranged from 10 to 20 minutes. To enhance data quality, attention checks were embedded in the survey and surveys that failed attention checks were excluded from final analyses. All responses were de-identified prior to analysis to protect participant confidentiality. Scoring for both instruments followed standard scoring procedures, with items reverse-scored as appropriate and component scores summed to create global totals. Internal consistency for both measures was calculated using Cronbach's alpha to confirm adequate reliability in the present sample. Descriptive statistics were computed for demographic variables and for the primary measures, including means, standard deviations, ranges, skewness, and kurtosis. The primary analysis examined the bivariate association between sleep quality and anxiety total scores. Pearson product-moment correlation was used as the primary correlation metric given the continuous nature of both instrument totals and acceptable distributional properties for the scores. Where distributional assumptions were violated, non-parametric Spearman correlations were computed as robustness checks. To evaluate the influence of demographic covariates, partial correlations were computed controlling for age and gender. In addition, hierarchical linear regression analyses were conducted with anxiety score as the dependent variable and with sleep quality entered as the primary predictor. Age and gender were entered as covariates in the regression model to examine whether sleep quality accounted for unique variance in anxiety after accounting for these demographic factors. Interaction terms between sleep quality and gender, and between sleep quality and age, were tested in separate regression models to explore potential moderation. Standard diagnostic checks for regression were performed, including assessment of multicollinearity, inspection of residuals, and identification of influential cases. Cases identified as extreme outliers or as highly influential were examined and sensitivity analyses were performed to determine their impact on key findings. All statistical analyses were performed using standard statistical software and two-tailed tests were used with an alpha threshold of .05 for statistical significance.

Results

The final analytic sample consisted of 113 participants who met inclusion criteria and passed data quality checks. The sample included 72 female participants and 41 male participants. Participant ages ranged from 18 to 29 years, with a mean age of 20.8 years and a standard deviation of 2.4 years. Descriptive statistics for the primary measures are presented below. The mean sleep quality score in the sample was 7.8 with a standard deviation of 3.4, indicating a range of sleep experience from good to poor across participants. The sleep quality scores ranged from 1 to 16 in the sample, with higher scores reflecting poorer subjective sleep quality. The internal consistency for the sleep questionnaire in this sample was acceptable, with a Cronbach's alpha of .78. The mean anxiety score for participants was 9.6 with a standard deviation of 5.1. Anxiety scores ranged from 0 to 24 in the sample, reflecting variability from minimal to elevated symptom levels. The internal consistency for the anxiety instrument in this sample was also acceptable, with a Cronbach's alpha of .85. Skewness and kurtosis values for both measures were within acceptable limits, suggesting no severe departures from normality. Visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots supported the use of parametric analyses for the primary variables. The primary bivariate analysis examined the association between sleep quality and anxiety scores. A Pearson correlation revealed a moderate positive association between poorer sleep quality and higher anxiety scores. The observed correlation coefficient was r = .45, and this association was statistically significant with p < .001. A scatterplot of sleep quality and anxiety scores showed an upward trend with some dispersion, consistent with a moderate effect size. To ensure robustness, a Spearman rank-order correlation was also computed and produced a comparable coefficient of rho = .43 with p < .001. These results indicate a reliable moderate positive association between subjective sleep problems and anxiety symptom severity in the present student sample. A partial correlation controlling for age and gender was conducted to examine whether the bivariate association persisted after adjustment for demographic covariates. The partial correlation between sleep quality and anxiety, controlling for age and gender, remained statistically significant with pr = .42 and p < .001. This finding suggests that the association between poor sleep and higher anxiety is not solely attributable to participants' age or gender differences. To further quantify the unique contribution of sleep quality to anxiety, hierarchical linear regression analyses were conducted. In the first step of the regression, age and gender were entered as predictors of anxiety severity. This initial model accounted for a small and nonsignificant proportion of variance in anxiety scores, with R^2 = .03 and p = .18. In the second step, sleep quality score was entered and produced a significant increase in explained variance. The full model including age, gender, and sleep quality accounted for R^2 = .21 of the variance in anxiety, and the change in R^2 attributable to sleep quality was .18, p < .001. In the full model, the standardized regression coefficient for sleep quality was beta = .43, t(109) = 5.24, p < .001, indicating a moderate unique association. Neither age nor gender was a significant predictor in the full model, with p-values above .10 for both covariates. Interaction terms between sleep quality and gender, and between sleep quality and age, were tested to assess moderation. The interaction between sleep quality and gender was not statistically significant, suggesting no evidence that the sleep-anxiety association differed by gender in this sample. The interaction between sleep quality and age was also nonsignificant, indicating the magnitude of the association did not vary systematically with age within the restricted age range of the sample. Diagnostic checks on the regression indicated acceptable levels of multicollinearity, with variance inflation factors below 1.5 for all predictors. Examination of residual plots did not reveal major violations of homoscedasticity or linearity assumptions. A small number of individual cases had studentized residuals beyond ±2.5, and sensitivity analyses removing these cases produced virtually identical parameter estimates and significance levels. Post hoc power calculations based on the observed effect size indicated that the sample provided adequate power to detect a correlation of the observed magnitude.

Discussion

The present study examined the association between subjective sleep quality and anxiety symptom severity in a sample of university students. The primary finding was a moderate positive correlation between poorer sleep quality and higher anxiety scores. This result aligns with prior work that has consistently observed associations between sleep disturbance and anxiety in young adult samples. Importantly, the association remained robust after controlling for age and gender, suggesting that the link between sleep quality and anxiety in this sample was not confounded by these basic demographic variables. The regression analysis demonstrated that sleep quality accounted for a meaningful portion of variance in anxiety scores beyond demographic characteristics. Taken together, these findings provide empirical support for the hypothesis that poorer sleep is associated with elevated anxiety among students. While the observed association was moderate rather than large, its magnitude is clinically and practically relevant given the high prevalence of both sleep and anxiety issues in student populations. Even moderate associations at the individual level can translate into substantial public health impact when considered across entire student bodies. The findings are consistent with theoretical models that posit reciprocal relationships between sleep problems and anxiety symptoms. Cognitive and physiological mechanisms likely contribute to the observed association. For example, cognitive hyperarousal in the form of worry and rumination may prolong sleep onset and reduce subjective sleep quality. Physiological arousal associated with anxiety, such as increased sympathetic nervous system activity, may also interfere with the initiation and maintenance of restorative sleep. Moreover, sleep deprivation or fragmented sleep may increase emotional reactivity and reduce the ability to regulate negative affect, thereby exacerbating anxiety symptoms. These bidirectional pathways are not mutually exclusive and may operate concurrently to maintain a cycle of poor sleep and increasing anxiety. The present correlational design does not permit causal inference, and it is therefore not possible to determine whether sleep problems precipitated anxiety, anxiety caused sleep problems, or whether both were driven by shared vulnerabilities or third variables. Nevertheless, the observed robust association underscores the need for longitudinal and experimental studies that can more directly test causal mechanisms. The lack of significant moderation by gender or age in the present sample suggests that the sleep-anxiety relationship may be relatively consistent across these demographic subgroups within young adults. This observation is notable given that some prior studies have reported gender differences in anxiety prevalence and sleep disturbance. It is possible that gender differences are more apparent in clinical samples or in broader age ranges than were sampled here. Alternatively, the absence of gender moderation in the present study may reflect insufficient power to detect small interaction effects or cultural factors specific to the sampled population. The absence of an age moderation effect likely reflects the relatively narrow age range inherent to a university student sample. Within this restricted developmental window, individual differences in sleep and anxiety may be driven more by situational and lifestyle factors than by age per se. Lifestyle factors common among students, including irregular schedules, late-night studying, caffeine and alcohol use, and technology use before bedtime, may be important proximal contributors to both sleep quality and anxiety. These behaviors are potentially modifiable and thus present promising targets for interventions aimed at improving both sleep and mental health in student populations. The current findings have practical implications for campus mental health services and student support programs. Screening for sleep problems may be a useful complement to anxiety screening initiatives, as identifying and addressing sleep difficulties could have downstream benefits for anxiety symptoms. Likewise, interventions such as behavioral sleep treatments and sleep hygiene education could be integrated into student counseling services or wellness programs. Evidence-based approaches that target sleep, including brief behavioral interventions, may be feasible and acceptable in student populations and could be evaluated as adjuncts to traditional anxiety treatments. Given the reciprocal potential of sleep and anxiety, combined interventions that address both domains concurrently may yield particularly strong effects. On the research front, the results support the continued integration of sleep measures in studies of anxiety and stress in young adults. Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs that can elucidate temporal sequences and causal pathways between sleep and anxiety. Experimental sleep-manipulation studies, such as controlled sleep restriction or extension paradigms, can provide critical tests of causal effects on anxiety-relevant outcomes. Similarly, randomized trials of sleep-focused interventions could evaluate whether improving sleep quality leads to reductions in anxiety symptoms within student populations. Incorporating objective sleep measures, such as actigraphy or polysomnography, would strengthen future work by allowing examination of sleep timing and architecture in relation to anxiety. Objective measures could reveal associations that are not captured by subjective questionnaires, such as fragmentation of sleep stages or circadian phase shifts. Additional research could explore potential mediators of the sleep-anxiety relationship, including stress reactivity, cognitive rumination, substance use, and physical activity. Understanding mediators could inform mechanistic models and guide the development of targeted intervention components. Moderators of the sleep-anxiety link also warrant further investigation, including sex hormones, chronotype, socioeconomic status, and cultural influences on sleep and help-seeking behavior. Some subgroups of students may be particularly vulnerable, such as those with pre-existing mental health diagnoses, shift work responsibilities, or caregiving roles. Identifying high-risk subgroups could enable more tailored prevention and early intervention efforts on campuses. The present study has several limitations that merit consideration when interpreting the results. First, the cross-sectional and correlational nature of the study precludes causal inference and leaves open the possibility of bidirectional or shared-cause explanations. Second, reliance on self-report questionnaires introduces the potential for reporting bias and common-method variance. Participants' subjective perceptions of sleep and anxiety may be influenced by transient mood states or by expectations about their own functioning. Third, the use of a convenience sample of university students limits generalizability to other populations, including non-student young adults and clinical samples. Fourth, although standard and validated instruments were employed, the study did not include objective sleep assessment or clinical diagnostic interviews, which would add depth to measurement. Fifth, the sample size, while adequate for detecting the observed moderate association, may have been underpowered for detecting smaller interaction effects or subgroup differences. Sixth, potential confounding variables such as depressive symptoms, substance use, medication use, and chronic health conditions were not comprehensively measured and could account for part of the observed association. Seventh, the timing of data collection relative to academic stressors such as exams was not systematically controlled and could have influenced both sleep and anxiety levels. Despite these limitations, the study contributes useful empirical evidence demonstrating a moderate association between poorer sleep quality and higher anxiety severity in a student sample. The findings highlight the relevance of considering sleep health within broader student mental health strategies and suggest promising avenues for intervention development. Practical next steps for campus health programs include routine sleep screening, dissemination of sleep hygiene education, and implementation of brief behavioral sleep interventions alongside existing mental health services. Research that evaluates combined sleep and anxiety interventions in randomized controlled trials could provide critical evidence for integrated care models on campus. Moreover, longitudinal cohort studies following students across their academic careers could illuminate the developmental interplay of sleep, anxiety, and academic outcomes. Investigations that include physiological measures of stress and sleep, as well as ecological momentary assessment of daily variability, would enhance understanding of dynamic processes linking sleep and anxiety in real-world settings. In conclusion, this study found a moderate and statistically robust positive association between subjective sleep problems and anxiety symptom severity in a university student sample, with no evidence that age or gender meaningfully altered this relationship. The results underscore the importance of addressing sleep as part of mental health promotion and intervention strategies for students. Future research that employs longitudinal, experimental, and objective measurement approaches will be essential to unpack causal mechanisms and to optimize interventions targeting sleep and anxiety in young adults. Addressing sleep problems early in students' academic careers may offer an opportunity to mitigate anxiety symptoms and support academic and psychosocial functioning. Overall, integrating sleep-focused strategies into student mental health initiatives represents a promising direction for both research and practice.
