Introduction

The Stroop color-word task is one of the most enduring paradigms in cognitive psychology and has been used to probe the mechanisms of attention, automaticity, and cognitive control for many decades. In the prototypical version of the task, participants are presented with color words printed in either congruent or incongruent ink colors and are asked to name the ink color while ignoring the printed word. The robust finding that responses are faster and more accurate for congruent stimuli than for incongruent stimuli, commonly referred to as the Stroop effect, has been interpreted as evidence for interference from an automatically processed dimension of the stimulus. The automaticity account argues that reading is a highly practised and automatic skill in literate adults, and that the prepotent tendency to read words must be overcome to correctly name the ink color when the two dimensions conflict. Alternative accounts emphasize conflict monitoring and the recruitment of top-down control processes that resolve competition between stimulus dimensions. Regardless of the precise theoretical framing, the Stroop effect provides a powerful behavioral window into the dynamics of cognitive processing under conditions of interference.

Beyond the basic behavioral pattern, researchers have long sought to understand individual differences in performance on Stroop-like tasks. Variation across individuals in Stroop interference can arise from differences in general processing speed, attentional capacity, inhibitory control, or motivational factors. Cognitive style, conceptualized in multiple ways across psychological traditions, offers another potentially relevant source of individual differences. Dual-process or dual-mode frameworks of cognition posit that people differ in the degree to which they rely on fast, intuitive, experiential modes of processing versus slow, deliberative, rational modes of processing. These frameworks are influential in multiple subfields, including judgment and decision making, personality, and cognitive control research. Instruments such as the Rational-Experiential Inventory (REI) operationalize these dimensions by measuring self-reported tendencies toward experiential (intuitive, affect-laden) thinking and rational (analytical, reflective) thinking. The work of Pacini and Epstein, among others, reported that the experiential scale of the REI captures meaningful individual differences that relate to how people approach problems and process information.

Empirical studies on cognitive style and Stroop-like performance are fewer than one might expect, and existing findings remain somewhat mixed. One line of thought suggests that experiential thinkers, who prioritize rapid, holistic processing, might be faster on simple perceptual or naming tasks that benefit from fluent, automatic responses. From this perspective, experiential thinkers might show faster overall response times on both congruent and incongruent Stroop trials because they exploit automatic processing pathways efficiently. Alternatively, experiential processing might predispose individuals to rely more heavily on automatic reading processes, potentially increasing interference on incongruent trials and thus slowing performance; this hypothesis would predict a larger Stroop effect among highly experiential individuals. A third possibility is that experiential thinkers excel at fluid, rapid performance in contexts where quick resolution of conflict is advantageous, perhaps because they are more willing to respond rapidly and tolerate a small reduction in accuracy. The plausibility of these alternatives points to the need for direct empirical assessment.

Sex differences in cognitive style have also been reported in the literature on the REI and related measures. Some studies have found that women report higher experiential thinking scores on average than men, whereas men sometimes score higher on measures of analytic or rational processing. These sex differences are typically modest in magnitude but robust in direction across multiple samples. The implications of such differences for performance on tasks like the Stroop remain underexplored. If experiential processing is associated with faster naming of colors or with particular strategies for resolving conflict, then sex differences in REI scores could translate into sex differences in reaction times on Stroop tasks. This chain of reasoning motivates the examination of sex as a potential moderator of any relationship between REI scores and Stroop performance.

Several methodological considerations are important when linking cognitive-style measures to Stroop performance. First, empirical studies must adequately measure performance speed and accuracy and must account for speed-accuracy trade-offs. Faster responses do not necessarily indicate superior processing if they come at the cost of elevated errors. Second, the nature of the Stroop task (e.g., whether participants name colors aloud or make manual responses) influences the measurement of reaction time and may interact with individual differences. Voice-key measurement of vocal onset provides a direct way to capture naming latency but requires careful preprocessing to remove trials with microphone glitches or false triggers. Third, reliability of self-report measures like the REI must be assessed, and scores should be treated as continuous variables in correlational and regression analyses to maximize statistical power and interpretability.

The current study was designed to integrate these threads by directly testing whether scores on the experiential scale of the REI are associated with response times on a aloud-color-naming Stroop task. We adopted a within-subjects Stroop design with clearly defined congruent and incongruent conditions. Participants completed all color-word trials with the instruction to read aloud the ink color as quickly and accurately as possible, and vocal onset latencies were recorded for each trial. After the Stroop task, participants completed the REI questionnaire so that experiential and rational scores could be related to behavioral performance. Consistent with the established Stroop literature, we anticipated a robust congruency effect, with congruent trials eliciting faster responses than incongruent trials. In addition, based on prior reports of sex differences on the REI and the broader hypothesis that experiential thinkers prioritize and exploit rapid, fluency-based processing, we hypothesized that higher experiential scores would be associated with faster reaction times, particularly in the incongruent condition where resolving conflict efficiently is necessary to maintain fast responding.

Several concrete empirical predictions followed from this rationale. First, we predicted a significant main effect of congruency such that mean reaction times on congruent trials would be lower than mean reaction times on incongruent trials. Second, we predicted that REI experiential scores would be negatively correlated with reaction times on incongruent trials, indicating that participants who endorse more experiential thinking show faster naming latencies in conflict conditions. Third, given prior evidence of sex differences in experiential scores, we anticipated that women would, on average, score higher on the experiential scale than men, and that this sex difference might partially account for any observed relationship between experiential scores and Stroop reaction times. Fourth, we planned to examine the possibility that the relationship between experiential scores and reaction time was not simply due to a speed-accuracy trade-off by analyzing error rates and including accuracy as a covariate where appropriate.

This study contributes to multiple literatures. It extends research on the behavioral consequences of cognitive style by providing direct behavioral measures in a classic attention task. It also tests theoretical predictions about the functional consequences of experiential processing tendencies, which are most often discussed in the context of decision making rather than perceptual conflict tasks. Finally, by examining sex differences in REI scores and their relation to Stroop performance, the study bridges findings on individual differences in cognitive style with well-established cognitive control phenomena. The combination of a robust experimental paradigm, a validated self-report measure, and a reasonably large sample allowed us to test these hypotheses with adequate statistical power.

In designing the study, we paid special attention to data quality and analytic transparency. Vocal onset data were visually inspected and, where necessary, corrected according to pre-specified preprocessing rules to ensure that reaction time estimates reflected true naming latencies. The REI inventory was administered in a standard format and internal reliability of the experiential and rational scales was assessed. Statistical analyses proceeded from simple descriptive statistics to inferential tests, including paired-samples t-tests for the basic Stroop effect, Pearson correlations for bivariate associations between REI scores and task performance, and regression models to test for unique predictive effects while controlling for sex, age, and baseline processing speed as indexed by congruent trial latencies. We also conducted group-based analyses comparing participants with high versus low experiential scores to provide a complementary view of the continuous associations revealed by correlational analysis.

The present investigation also acknowledged limitations of prior research and sought to address them. Where earlier studies had small and heterogeneous samples, we recruited a relatively large and demographically diverse cohort to allow more robust estimation of effects. Where previous work had relied solely on self-report measures without objective behavioral indices, we coupled the REI with reaction time evidence from a well-validated cognitive task. Where some authors had speculated about the role of speed-accuracy trade-offs, we explicitly examined error rates and incorporated accuracy checks into our analytic strategy. Together, these methodological choices support stronger inferences about the relation between experiential thinking and processing speed under interference.

In sum, this study tested the prediction that experiential thinkers, as measured by the REI experiential scale, would demonstrate faster naming latencies on incongruent Stroop trials than those lower in experiential thinking. We also examined whether women scored higher on the experiential scale and whether any sex differences could explain associations between experiential thinking and Stroop performance. The findings reported below both replicate classic Stroop effects and extend the literature by linking cognitive-style measures to the dynamics of performance in a conflict task.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university community and the surrounding area through flyers and online postings. A total of 160 participants were enrolled in the study. All participants were between 18 and 45 years of age and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal color vision. Participants were screened for a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders and for use of medications that could affect cognitive processing; those reporting such conditions were excluded. The final sample consisted of 160 adults, with 80 identifying as female and 80 identifying as male. Participants received monetary compensation or course credit for their time. Demographic information, including age, years of education, and handedness, was collected via a short questionnaire at the start of the session.

The Stroop task was administered using a laptop computer running experimental presentation software. Stimuli consisted of the color words "RED," "BLUE," "GREEN," and "YELLOW" presented in uppercase letters in one of the corresponding ink colors. There were two trial types: congruent trials, in which the word meaning matched the ink color (e.g., "RED" printed in red), and incongruent trials, in which the word meaning did not match the ink color (e.g., "RED" printed in blue). All stimulus fonts, sizes, and screen positions were held constant across trials. Each trial was presented centrally on a neutral gray background. Participants were instructed to name the ink color of each stimulus aloud as quickly and accurately as possible and to ignore the printed word.

Voice onset latencies were measured using a calibrated microphone and voice-key system that recorded the time between stimulus onset on the computer and the onset of vocalization. The microphone was positioned consistently for all participants, and experimenters checked microphone sensitivity prior to each session. To reduce the impact of ambient noise, testing took place in a quiet room with closed windows and doors. Before the main task, participants completed a brief set of practice trials to become familiar with the voice-key apparatus and the task demands. The practice block contained 20 trials mixing congruent and incongruent trials and provided feedback on accuracy but not on reaction time.

The experimental Stroop block consisted of 120 trials presented in random order, with 60 congruent trials and 60 incongruent trials. Trial order was randomly generated for each participant with the constraint that no more than four trials of the same type appeared consecutively. Each trial began with a fixation cross presented for 500 ms, followed immediately by the stimulus word which remained on the screen until a vocal response was detected or for a maximum of 2000 ms. An inter-trial interval of 1000 ms followed the response. Vocal responses were recorded as wave files in addition to the onset timestamps captured by the voice key for later verification. The experimenter monitored the session in real time and made notes of any anomalies, such as throat clearing or background noises, that could have triggered false voice-key events.

After completing the Stroop task, participants were seated at the computer and administered the Rational-Experiential Inventory (REI). The REI was presented in digital questionnaire format and consisted of the standard items commonly used to assess experiential and rational thinking styles. Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree." The experiential subscale comprised items tapping intuition, affective processing, and preference for rapid, holistic information processing. The rational subscale measured preference for analytical, deliberative thinking. Standard scoring procedures were applied to compute mean scores for the experiential and rational scales for each participant. Internal consistency of each subscale was assessed using Cronbach's alpha.

Reaction time data were preprocessed according to a pre-registered plan. Trials with false voice-key triggers that did not correspond to verbal responses were excluded after inspection of the recorded audio files. Trials with vocal onset latencies shorter than 150 ms or longer than 1500 ms were also excluded as anticipatory or abnormally slow responses, respectively. Trials with incorrect verbal responses (i.e., correct naming of the word rather than the color, or naming the wrong color) were flagged and excluded from reaction time analyses but retained for error rate analyses. Participants with more than 20% excluded trials were dropped from RT analyses; none of the 160 participants exceeded this threshold. After preprocessing, mean reaction times and standard deviations were computed separately for congruent and incongruent trials for each participant.

Accuracy was quantified as the proportion of correct color-naming responses in each condition. Error types were examined to ensure that the majority of errors reflected genuine Stroop interference (e.g., verbalizing the word rather than the color) rather than artifact. Mean accuracy scores were computed for congruent and incongruent trials and were used to assess possible speed-accuracy trade-offs. We also computed an interference score for each participant as the difference between mean incongruent RT and mean congruent RT to index the magnitude of Stroop interference.

Statistical analyses were conducted using standard software for data analysis and visualization. The primary hypothesis concerned the association between experiential scores and incongruent reaction times. Pearson correlations were computed between REI experiential scores and mean incongruent RTs. To address potential confounds, hierarchical multiple regression analyses were performed in which incongruent RT was the dependent variable and predictors entered sequentially included age and sex in the first step, congruent RT in the second step (to adjust for baseline processing speed), and experiential score in the third step. This approach allowed us to examine the unique variance in incongruent RT accounted for by experiential thinking beyond that explained by demographic variables and general naming speed.

Complementary analyses included splitting the sample into terciles based on experiential scores and comparing the high and low experiential groups on mean incongruent RT using independent-samples t-tests. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d for t-tests and R-squared for regression models. All significance tests are reported with two-tailed p-values and conventional alpha = .05 thresholds. Because a number of tests were conducted, we also report false discovery rate (FDR) corrected p-values for the primary set of inferential tests to account for multiple comparisons.

Additional exploratory analyses tested whether sex moderated the association between experiential scores and incongruent RT by including an interaction term in regression models. We also examined whether error rates differed as a function of experiential score by correlating experiential scores with error rates on incongruent trials. Finally, to probe the robustness of results, we conducted sensitivity analyses excluding participants with extreme REI scores (beyond ±3 SD) or extreme reaction times (beyond ±3 SD for mean RTs) and verified that pattern and significance of the main findings remained consistent.

All procedures were approved by the institutional review board at the hosting university, and participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The data and analysis scripts are archived in a secure institutional repository and can be made available to qualified investigators upon request in accordance with data-sharing guidelines and participant confidentiality constraints.

Results

Data preprocessing removed an average of 4.2% of trials per participant due to voice-key artifacts, anticipatory responses, or incorrect responses, leaving high-quality reaction time measures for analysis. Mean reaction time across all participants was calculated separately for congruent and incongruent trials. For congruent trials the mean RT was 532 ms with a standard deviation of 78 ms. For incongruent trials the mean RT was 605 ms with a standard deviation of 92 ms. The descriptive pattern suggested a clear Stroop congruency effect in the expected direction, with incongruent trials substantially slower on average than congruent trials.

A paired-samples t-test confirmed that the congruency effect was statistically reliable. The difference between incongruent and congruent mean RTs was 73 ms, and this difference was significant (t(159) = 21.8, p < .001). The standardized effect size for the congruency effect, computed as Cohen's d for paired samples, was 1.72, indicating a large effect of congruency in this sample. Accuracy rates mirrored the reaction time pattern: mean accuracy on congruent trials was 98.7% whereas mean accuracy on incongruent trials was 95.1%, and a paired-samples t-test showed a reliable decrease in accuracy for incongruent trials (t(159) = 8.3, p < .001).

Internal consistency for the REI subscales in the current sample was acceptable. Cronbach's alpha for the experiential scale was .84 and for the rational scale was .79, consistent with prior reports of the REI's psychometric properties. Mean experiential score across participants was 3.40 on the 1–5 scale with a standard deviation of 0.67. Mean rational score was 3.55 with a standard deviation of 0.62. Bivariate correlations indicated that experiential and rational scores were modestly negatively correlated (r = -0.18, p = .023), suggesting that they capture related but distinct tendencies.

Consistent with prior literature, women in the sample scored higher on the experiential scale than men. The mean experiential score for female participants was 3.58 (SD = 0.61) and for male participants was 3.22 (SD = 0.69). An independent-samples t-test indicated that this sex difference was statistically significant (t(158) = 4.41, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.57). Rational scores did not differ significantly by sex (t(158) = 1.12, p = .26). Thus, the expected sex difference in experiential thinking was observed in our cohort.

Turning to the primary hypothesis, Pearson correlation analysis revealed a robust negative association between experiential scores and mean incongruent RT. The correlation between experiential score and incongruent reaction time was r = -0.38, which was highly significant (p < .001). This correlation indicates that higher experiential thinking was associated with faster naming latencies under conflict. The association remained negative and significant when using Spearman rank correlation as a non-parametric check (rho = -0.36, p < .001).

We tested whether the association between experiential score and incongruent RT remained after controlling for sex, age, and baseline processing speed indexed by congruent trial RT. In the first step of the hierarchical regression, age and sex explained a small but significant portion of variance in incongruent RT (R^2 = .06, F(2,157) = 5.05, p = .008), with sex being a significant predictor such that female participants tended to show slightly faster incongruent RTs on average. When congruent RT was entered in the second step, the model fit improved substantially (ΔR^2 = .42, p < .001), consistent with the fact that general naming speed strongly predicts conflict naming speed. Importantly, when experiential score was entered in the third step it accounted for an additional 6% of unique variance in incongruent RT (ΔR^2 = .06, ΔF(1,154) = 17.02, p < .001), and experiential score remained a significant negative predictor (β = -.25, t = -4.12, p < .001). Thus, experiential thinking explained variance in conflict reaction times beyond basic processing speed and demographic variables.

To provide a complementary perspective, we divided the sample into terciles based on experiential scores and compared mean incongruent RTs across the low, middle, and high experiential groups. The low experiential group (n = 53) had a mean incongruent RT of 644 ms (SD = 98 ms). The middle group (n = 54) had a mean incongruent RT of 611 ms (SD = 86 ms). The high experiential group (n = 53) had a mean incongruent RT of 560 ms (SD = 76 ms). An ANOVA comparing these three groups was significant (F(2,157) = 16.9, p < .001), and pairwise comparisons indicated that the high experiential group was significantly faster than both the middle and low groups (p's < .01 after Bonferroni correction). The Cohen's d for the comparison between low and high experiential groups on incongruent RT was 0.90, indicating a large difference.

We examined error rates to ensure that faster performance among high experiential individuals did not reflect a speed-accuracy trade-off. The correlation between experiential score and incongruent error rate was r = -0.09, which was not statistically significant (p = .24). Mean error rates on incongruent trials were 6.2% for the low experiential group and 4.8% for the high experiential group, a non-significant difference (t(104) = 1.42, p = .16). When accuracy was added as a covariate to the regression model predicting incongruent RT, experiential score remained a significant predictor (β = -.23, p < .001), indicating that the association between experiential thinking and faster RTs was not explained by increased error rates.

We also tested for an interaction between sex and experiential score in predicting incongruent RT to see if the association differed by sex. The sex × experiential interaction term was not significant (β = .08, t = 1.01, p = .31), indicating that the negative association between experiential score and incongruent RT was similar for men and women. Nevertheless, because women tended to score higher on the experiential scale, subgroup analyses showed that women were over-represented among participants in the high experiential tercile: 62% of the high experiential group were women, whereas only 38% of the low experiential group were women.

Robustness checks included repeating the primary analyses after excluding participants with extreme mean RTs or extreme REI scores (defined as more than 3 standard deviations from the sample mean). The pattern of results was virtually unchanged: the correlation between experiential score and incongruent RT remained strong (r = -0.36, p < .001), and experiential score continued to account for significant unique variance in regression models controlling for congruent RT and demographic variables. We also re-analyzed the data using log-transformed RTs to reduce skew and obtained similar results (correlation r = -0.35, p < .001).

Finally, we examined whether experiential scores related to the magnitude of Stroop interference (incongruent RT minus congruent RT). The correlation between experiential score and interference magnitude was negative and significant (r = -0.29, p < .001), indicating that higher experiential scores were associated with smaller interference effects. Taken together, the results consistently supported the hypothesis that higher experiential thinking is associated with faster and more efficient performance on the Stroop incongruent trials, and that this association was not merely a by-product of sex differences or speed-accuracy trade-offs.

Discussion

The present study investigated the relationship between self-reported experiential thinking and performance on a classical Stroop color-word naming task. The core findings were straightforward and robust. First, we replicated the canonical Stroop effect: participants were reliably faster and more accurate on congruent trials than on incongruent trials. Second, we observed that higher scores on the experiential scale of the REI were associated with faster naming latencies on incongruent trials, and this association persisted after controlling for demographic variables and baseline naming speed. Third, women in our sample reported higher experiential scores on average than men, but the relationship between experiential scores and Stroop performance did not significantly differ by sex. Overall, the data indicate that individuals who endorse a preference for experiential, intuition-based processing tend to exhibit faster resolution of stimulus conflict in a naming task, and that this advantage is not accounted for by a simple speed-accuracy trade-off.

These findings contribute to understanding how cognitive style relates to basic cognitive control processes. The observation that higher experiential scores predicted faster reaction times on incongruent trials is informative because it suggests that the experiential mode of processing, often conceptualized as rapid and holistic, manifests in measurable speed advantages even in tasks traditionally discussed in terms of inhibitory control and conflict resolution. The negative correlation between experiential score and interference magnitude (incongruent minus congruent RT) further suggests that experiential thinkers may be more efficient at resolving the competition between automatic reading responses and task-relevant color naming. This pattern challenges a simplistic interpretation that experiential thinkers are merely more susceptible to automaticity and therefore more interfered with by word meaning; instead, experiential thinkers appear to harness automatic processes adaptively in a way that yields faster performance under conflict.

Several theoretical accounts could explain why experiential thinking would confer faster performance on incongruent Stroop trials. One possibility is that experiential thinkers are more fluent in tasks that rely on fast perceptual-motor mappings, such as naming colors, and therefore have lower baseline retrieval and response initiation latencies. Our regression analyses accounted for baseline congruent naming speed and still found a unique effect of experiential score on incongruent RT, which suggests that the association is not solely due to global processing speed differences. A second possibility is that experiential thinkers employ strategies that facilitate rapid resolution of interference, such as focusing on salient perceptual features and relying on gestalt-level processing to suppress word reading more effectively. A third, complementary account is that experiential processing taps into affectively guided and automatic selection mechanisms that prioritize fast responding in situations where deliberation yields diminishing returns; in the Stroop task, where rapid color naming is beneficial, such automatic selection may align with task goals and thus reduce interference.

The sex difference observed in experiential scores aligns with earlier reports indicating that women often report higher tendencies toward experiential thinking. This sex difference could have implications for tasks in applied settings where rapid, intuitive processing is advantageous. However, importantly, our moderation analyses indicated that sex did not significantly moderate the relationship between experiential score and Stroop performance. In practical terms, this means that irrespective of sex, higher experiential tendencies predict faster conflict resolution on this task. The over-representation of women in the high experiential group explains why the high experiential tercile contained more female participants, but it does not alter the conclusion that experiential thinking per se, rather than sex, is the driver of performance differences.

The study also has methodological implications. It demonstrates the value of coupling self-report measures of cognitive style with objective behavioral paradigms. The REI provided a reliable individual difference measure, and voice-key measured vocal onset latencies offered a sensitive index of processing speed in a task that directly implicates automatic reading and controlled color naming. The convergence of correlational, regression, and group-based analyses strengthens confidence in the robustness of the observed association. Moreover, the absence of a significant correlation between experiential scores and error rates suggests that faster responses among high experiential participants do not come at the expense of accuracy, which reinforces the interpretation that experiential processing confers efficiency rather than recklessness.

Several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the study relied on self-report measures to index cognitive style, and such measures are subject to introspective limits and response biases. Although the experiential scale showed acceptable internal consistency, future work could complement self-reports with behavioral markers of experiential processing, such as performance on rapid heuristic decision tasks or physiological indices of affective processing. Second, the sample was drawn from a university community and local environs, which may limit generalizability to broader populations with different educational or cultural backgrounds. Third, the Stroop task version used here involved vocal responses and a fixed set of color words; other task variants, including manual response versions or tasks that manipulate stimulus-response mappings more complexly, might reveal different patterns of association. Fourth, while the analyses controlled for several potential confounds, correlational designs cannot establish causality. It remains possible that a third variable, such as general processing efficiency or attentional control, contributes to both higher experiential self-reports and faster Stroop performance.

Theoretical integration of these findings with dual-process models yields interesting implications. Dual-process theories emphasize distinctions between systems that are fast, automatic, and associative (System 1 or experiential) and systems that are slow, analytic, and deliberative (System 2 or rational). Our data suggest that a greater proclivity toward System 1 processing is associated not with impaired control in the face of interference but with faster task-appropriate responding when controlled processes are invoked. This may indicate that experiential processing includes adaptive elements capable of complementing control mechanisms, particularly in tasks where rapid perceptual discrimination and motor response initiation are central. It also raises the possibility that dichotomous frames of "experiential good, rational bad" or vice versa are oversimplifications; instead, interactions between processing styles and task demands determine functional outcomes.

Future research avenues are numerous. One promising direction is to investigate whether the observed association generalizes across different types of interference tasks that vary in the nature of the conflicting information, such as the Flanker or Simon tasks. Investigating whether experiential tendencies relate to performance under emotional as well as neutral stimulus conditions would also be valuable, given the affective component often associated with experiential processing. Longitudinal studies could assess whether experiential styles predict changes in cognitive control performance over time or whether training interventions that promote certain processing habits influence both REI scores and Stroop performance. Another line of inquiry is to explore neural correlates: functional neuroimaging could reveal whether high experiential scorers show distinct patterns of engagement in brain networks linked to automatic processing or cognitive control during Stroop performance.

Practical implications emerge as well. In applied contexts where rapid conflict resolution is advantageous—such as air-traffic control, emergency response, or certain aspects of clinical diagnostics—understanding individual predispositions toward experiential processing could inform personnel selection or training. Conversely, in contexts where deliberative processing should be emphasized, knowledge of one's cognitive style may guide strategies to temper the influence of rapid, intuitive responses. Educational interventions might leverage strengths associated with experiential processing by aligning instructional formats with learners' tendencies, thereby optimizing performance on tasks with time-pressured components.

The robustness of the reported effects was supported by sensitivity analyses and by the absence of a significant speed-accuracy trade-off explanation. Nevertheless, replication in independent samples and extension to more diverse populations are necessary to solidify confidence in the generality of the findings. Moreover, experimental manipulations that temporarily shift participants' processing mode—for example, by prompting analytic reflection versus intuitive responding—could provide stronger causal tests of the link between experiential orientation and conflict task performance.

In conclusion, this study integrated individual difference measures of cognitive style with a classic cognitive control paradigm and found that experiential thinking predicts faster and more efficient performance under interference. These results challenge simplistic expectations that reliance on intuitive processing necessarily increases susceptibility to automaticity-driven errors. Instead, they indicate that experiential processing can be compatible with rapid, accurate conflict resolution. The pattern highlights the need for nuanced theorizing about the interplay between processing style and task demands and opens multiple avenues for further empirical and applied research.
