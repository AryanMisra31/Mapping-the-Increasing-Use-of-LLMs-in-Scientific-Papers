Introduction

Human beings are fundamentally social creatures, and social connections have long been understood to play an essential role in subjective well-being and life satisfaction. Over the past two decades, digital forms of social interaction have been added to the repertoire of social behavior, with social media platforms becoming pervasive in daily life, particularly among young adults. Among social media platforms, visual social media that foreground images and photographs have gained extraordinary popularity and cultural prominence. Visual platforms provide an environment in which users are exposed to curated, often idealized presentations of peers’ lives, which has prompted concern that such exposure may foster negative social comparison and reduce life satisfaction. Social comparison theory proposes that people evaluate their own abilities and circumstances relative to others, and when such comparisons are upward, they can produce feelings of inadequacy and reduced well-being. The visual character of some platforms is thought to intensify the potential for upward comparison because images can convey highly desirable aspects of others’ lives — such as attractiveness, travel, lifestyle, and achievement — in immediate and emotionally evocative ways.

There is a growing empirical literature examining the relationship between social media use and well-being, and the findings are mixed. Some studies indicate negative associations between the use of social media and measures of mental health or life satisfaction, particularly when use is passive or involves exposure to idealized representations of others. Other studies, however, suggest that social media can foster social support, identity expression, and connection that are conducive to greater life satisfaction. The heterogeneity of findings has led researchers to consider that the effects of social media on well-being are not uniform and may depend on features such as the type of platform, the manner of use (active vs. passive), the content encountered, and individual difference variables.

Within this broader literature, there has been particular attention paid to the visual components of social media. Visual images are argued to be powerful stimuli for eliciting emotion and for shaping perceptions of reality because they can appear more vivid and convincing than text descriptions. Visual platforms such as Instagram emphasize photo and short video content, and anecdotal reports and some empirical work have suggested that frequent exposure to curated images of peers on such platforms can contribute to dissatisfaction with one’s own life circumstances. Notably, some work focusing on image-centric interactions on platforms has identified peer photographs as a prominent context in which negative social comparison arises. For example, when users view images of friends’ social successes, romantic interactions, or lifestyle highlights, they may perceive those lives as being better than their own and experience decreased life satisfaction.

At the same time, other studies have emphasized that visual social media may serve adaptive psychological functions, including facilitating social bonds, enabling self-expression, and providing positive feedback through likes and comments. Visual platforms can be used to share achievements and meaningful experiences with a supportive community, and for many users the feedback received can enhance feelings of belonging and self-esteem. Thus, whether visual social media use is deleterious or beneficial to life satisfaction may depend on how the platform is used and the nature of the social exchanges that occur.

One important individual difference that has been linked to life satisfaction and to how people interpret social information is locus of control. Locus of control is a personality construct that reflects the extent to which individuals perceive outcomes in their lives as being contingent on their own actions (internal locus of control) versus external forces such as luck or the actions of others (external locus of control). Generally, an internal locus of control has been associated with higher life satisfaction, greater agency, and more effective coping strategies, whereas an external locus of control is associated with lower life satisfaction and greater susceptibility to stress. Because locus of control shapes how people interpret events and the degree to which they feel able to influence outcomes, it is reasonable to expect that locus of control could moderate or confound relationships between social media use and life satisfaction.

When considering the visual content encountered on platforms, one must also attend to the types of images that users view. Not all images are the same in their potential to elicit upward comparison or to foster connection. Images depicting peers in social activities may evoke envy or feelings of exclusion in some observers. By contrast, images shared within close social networks that include supportive commentary might reinforce belonging and satisfaction. Similarly, aspirational or influencer content that depicts idealized lifestyles may promote unrealistic standards and negative comparison for some viewers, but for others these images might inspire goals or be dismissed as curated artifice. The heterogeneity in image types and their potential psychological impacts suggests a need to unpack not only total time spent on visual platforms but also the nature of the content that individuals encounter.

Many previous empirical studies have focused on platforms such as Facebook in exploring the connections among peer photographs, social comparison, and life satisfaction. Those studies have sometimes found that interactions with visual content on Facebook are associated with negative outcomes, particularly when engagement is passive or when users compare themselves to popular or highly curated peers. However, the generalizability of those findings to modern image-first platforms such as Instagram is not straightforward. Instagram’s affordances, norms, and user base differ from those of earlier platforms, and the platform’s emphasis on aesthetics and discoverability may alter how users engage with images and how those engagements affect their well-being.

The current state of the literature thus presents two contrasting broad possibilities. On one hand, exposure to curated visual content and peer photographs on image-based social media could be a driver of dissatisfaction via upward social comparison. On the other hand, use of such platforms may provide opportunities for connection, social reinforcement, and identity expression that contribute positively to life satisfaction. The mixed findings in the literature underscore the need for research that considers both total usage and the character of the images viewed, while also accounting for relevant individual difference variables that could confound or mediate observed relationships.

In designing new empirical work in this area, it is therefore important to include measures of life satisfaction, to quantify the extent of engagement with visual platforms, to differentiate types of images, and to control for stable personality variables such as locus of control. A cross-sectional survey approach with validated questionnaires can provide an initial test of the hypothesized associations and can inform future longitudinal or experimental designs. A university student sample is particularly relevant because young adults are among the most frequent users of image-based social media and because this developmental period involves salient social comparison processes.

The present study was conducted with the aim of addressing a set of inter-related questions that arise from the literature. The first aim was to examine the hypothesis that use of specifically visual social media, operationalized here as time spent on Instagram, is negatively associated with reported life satisfaction. This hypothesis is grounded in the social comparison account that frequent exposure to curated images may lead to upward comparisons and reduced satisfaction. The second aim was to identify whether particular types of images — for example, peer photographs, selfies, travel and lifestyle images, and influencer content — are differentially associated with life satisfaction. This aim recognizes that not all visual content is equivalent in its psychological effects, and that peer photographs have been singled out in prior work as a particular locus of negative effects. The third aim was to control for locus of control in all analyses, given its known association with life satisfaction and its potential to confound relationships between media use and well-being.

Based on prior theoretical and empirical work, the study articulated a set of hypotheses. The primary hypothesis was that greater time spent viewing visual social media (Instagram minutes per week) would be associated with lower life satisfaction. A secondary hypothesis was that more frequent exposure to peer photographs and aspirational lifestyle images would be negatively associated with life satisfaction relative to other image types. A further hypothesis was that accounting for locus of control would attenuate any observed negative associations between Instagram use and life satisfaction, because external locus of control might both predispose individuals to lower life satisfaction and incline them to engage in certain patterns of social media consumption.

The study was designed as a cross-sectional survey with a modest sample to provide an initial test of these hypotheses. The survey included standardized measures of life satisfaction, a widely used locus of control questionnaire to assess individual differences in perceived control over outcomes, a self-report measure of weekly Instagram usage, and a brief content inventory in which participants reported how frequently they encountered various image types on the platform. The plan of analysis included Pearson correlations to probe associations among the primary variables, partial correlations to examine relationships controlling for locus of control, and regression analyses to estimate the relative contributions of Instagram use and locus of control to life satisfaction. Exploratory analyses were also planned to examine whether specific image types correlated with life satisfaction and whether any observed relationships differed by gender.

It was recognized at the outset that the cross-sectional design could not establish causality, and that self-report measures of time spent on platforms may be subject to recall bias. Nevertheless, the design promised to offer informative data on the pattern of associations among Instagram engagement, content exposure, locus of control, and subjective life satisfaction in a university sample. If a negative association between Instagram use and life satisfaction emerged, this would add empirical support to concerns about the potential psychological costs of visual social media. Conversely, if no negative association was found or if a positive association emerged, this would encourage a reassessment of the assumption that visual social media necessarily undermines well-being and would invite closer attention to the conditions under which social media use is beneficial versus harmful.

In summary, the present study sought to clarify whether time spent on an image-centric social media platform is linked to lower life satisfaction, whether particular types of images are more strongly implicated in any such link, and whether locus of control accounts for observed patterns. By interrogating these questions with a careful set of measures and analyses, the study aimed to contribute to a more nuanced understanding of how visual social media fits into the broader landscape of factors that shape subjective well-being.

Methods

Participants were 100 undergraduate students enrolled at a mid-sized university who participated in exchange for course credit or entry into a prize draw. The recruitment target of 100 participants was selected to provide adequate statistical power to detect small to medium correlations in the range of r = .20 to .30 at conventional alpha levels. Participants were eligible if they were 18 years of age or older and reported having used Instagram at least once in the preceding month. No other exclusion criteria were applied. The sample included both male and female students and a small number of participants who identified as non-binary; gender was recorded to allow for exploratory sex-based analyses. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 28 years, with a mean age of 20.3 years and a standard deviation of 1.7 years.

All procedures were approved by the university’s institutional review board, and participants provided informed consent electronically before beginning the survey. The survey was administered online via a secure survey platform. Participants were informed that the survey would take approximately 15 to 20 minutes to complete and that they could withdraw at any time without penalty. The survey included measures of life satisfaction, locus of control, Instagram usage, image type exposure, and basic demographic information.

Life satisfaction was assessed using a five-item life satisfaction questionnaire widely used in psychological research. The items asked participants to indicate on a seven-point Likert-type scale the extent to which they agreed with statements about their overall satisfaction with life, clarity of life goals, fulfillment, and perceived quality of daily life. Individual item scores were summed to create a composite life satisfaction score, with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction. The scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the current sample; Cronbach’s alpha was calculated and reported below in the Results section.

Locus of control was measured using a standard locus of control questionnaire that assesses the extent to which individuals attribute outcomes to internal versus external causes. The measure included items reflecting beliefs about personal agency, control over one’s life, and the role of chance or powerful others in determining outcomes. Items were answered on a six-point Likert-type scale, and scores were summed so that higher total scores indicated a more external locus of control orientation. Internal consistency of the locus of control measure was evaluated and reported.

Instagram usage was assessed using self-report items asking participants to estimate the typical number of minutes per day they spent actively using Instagram during the previous week. Participants were asked to consider both browsing and posting behaviors and to provide an estimate of minutes per day; these estimates were then multiplied by seven to compute minutes per week for analyses, consistent with prior research conventions. In addition to the time variable, participants reported their typical patterns of use on a five-point frequency scale ranging from “never” to “very often” for activities such as posting photos, commenting, direct messaging, and passive browsing.

To capture the types of images encountered on Instagram, participants completed an image exposure inventory. The inventory listed a variety of image categories that prior conceptual and empirical work has suggested may have differential psychological effects: peer photographs (images of friends or acquaintances), selfies (self-portraits), aspirational lifestyle or travel images, influencer or celebrity content, and informational or news-related images. For each category, participants indicated how often they encountered such images on Instagram on a five-point scale from “rarely or never” to “very often.” The inventory was constructed to be brief yet to cover the principal categories of visual content thought to be relevant to social comparison and well-being.

Demographic variables collected included age, gender identity, year in university, and whether the participant lived on or off campus. These variables were collected primarily for descriptive purposes and to permit exploratory analyses of potential moderating effects.

Before conducting the main analyses, the data were screened for completeness and accuracy. Participants with substantial missing data on core variables (life satisfaction, locus of control, or Instagram minutes) were excluded from the analytic sample. A small number of cases (fewer than five) had isolated missing item responses, which were handled by pro-rating the available items when appropriate. The distributional properties of the key variables were examined, with skewness and kurtosis statistics computed and visual inspections of histograms performed. Outliers were identified using standardized z-scores; data points with absolute z-values greater than 3.29 on core variables were examined for plausibility. No cases were removed for implausible response patterns.

Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and ranges for the primary variables, were computed and reported. Internal consistency reliabilities (Cronbach’s alpha) for the life satisfaction and locus of control scales were calculated. The primary analytic approach involved computing Pearson product-moment correlations among life satisfaction, locus of control (with higher scores indicating more external orientation), and minutes per week on Instagram. Partial correlations were computed to estimate the association between Instagram minutes and life satisfaction while statistically controlling for locus of control. Multiple linear regression analyses were also conducted, with life satisfaction as the dependent variable and locus of control and Instagram minutes entered as independent variables. The regression analyses were used to estimate the unique contribution of each predictor and to obtain effect size estimates in the context of a combined model.

Exploratory analyses examined the associations between frequency of exposure to different image types and life satisfaction. These analyses consisted of Pearson correlations between each image-type frequency rating and life satisfaction, followed by partial correlations controlling for locus of control when warranted. Additional exploratory tests assessed whether gender moderated any of the primary associations. Moderation tests were conducted by computing interaction terms between gender (coded as a binary variable for these analyses) and the primary predictors and entering them into regression models predicting life satisfaction.

All statistical tests used two-tailed significance levels, and the conventional alpha threshold of .05 was applied. When multiple exploratory correlations among image-type variables were examined, the pattern of results was interpreted cautiously in light of the potential for Type I error. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional benchmarks for correlations and regression coefficients. Analyses were performed using standard statistical software, and all observed test statistics, degrees of freedom, and exact p-values are reported in the Results section.

Results

The final analytic sample consisted of 100 participants after exclusions for incomplete key measures. The sample included 64 women, 34 men, and 2 participants who identified as non-binary. Participants’ mean age was 20.3 years (SD = 1.7), and the majority of participants were in their first or second year of undergraduate study. Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented in this section. The life satisfaction questionnaire had a mean score of 24.6 with a standard deviation of 6.2 on the composite scale (possible range 5–35), indicating a moderate level of life satisfaction in this sample. The life satisfaction scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha of .83.

The locus of control scale had a mean score of 18.9 and a standard deviation of 5.1 on the composite measure (possible range 6–36), with higher scores reflecting a more external locus of control orientation. The locus of control scale had acceptable internal consistency in this sample, with Cronbach’s alpha of .78. For Instagram usage, participants reported a mean of 410 minutes per week spent on Instagram (SD = 285 minutes), which corresponds to an average of approximately 58.6 minutes per day. Reported minutes per week ranged widely from a low of 15 minutes to a high of 1680 minutes, indicating substantial variability in usage within the sample.

Preliminary inspection of the distributions for life satisfaction and locus of control revealed no severe deviations from normality. The Instagram minutes variable was positively skewed as commonly occurs with self-reported time-use data; however, visual inspection and skewness statistics indicated that the skew was within tolerable limits for Pearson correlation and regression analyses. Sensitivity analyses using a log-transformed Instagram minutes variable produced comparable patterns of results to those reported here, and therefore the analyses reported below used the original minutes per week variable for interpretability.

Pearson correlation analyses examined the bivariate associations among the core variables of interest. Locus of control was significantly negatively correlated with life satisfaction (r = −.45, p < .001), indicating that participants with more external locus of control orientations reported lower life satisfaction. This pattern is consistent with the established literature on locus of control and subjective well-being. Contrary to the primary hypothesis that greater Instagram use would be associated with lower life satisfaction, minutes per week spent on Instagram was positively correlated with life satisfaction (r = .22, p = .028). This positive association indicates that participants who reported spending more time on Instagram also tended to report greater life satisfaction.

Because locus of control was correlated with life satisfaction, partial correlation analyses were conducted to examine the association between Instagram minutes and life satisfaction while controlling for locus of control. The partial correlation between Instagram minutes and life satisfaction adjusting for locus of control was r = .30 (p = .002). Thus, when statistical variance associated with locus of control was removed, the positive relationship between Instagram use and life satisfaction was stronger than in the bivariate analysis.

A multiple linear regression analysis further evaluated the unique contributions of locus of control and Instagram minutes to life satisfaction. In the first regression model, locus of control alone predicted life satisfaction, accounting for approximately 20% of the variance (R^2 = .20, F(1,98) = 24.8, p < .001). The regression coefficient for locus of control was negative and significant (B = −0.67, SE = 0.13, β = −.45, p < .001), indicating that each one-point increase in external locus of control was associated with a 0.67 point decrease in the life satisfaction composite score, on average.

In a second regression model that included both locus of control and Instagram minutes as predictors, the overall model fit improved modestly (R^2 = .26, F(2,97) = 17.0, p < .001). In this combined model, locus of control remained a significant negative predictor of life satisfaction (B = −0.60, SE = 0.12, β = −.40, p < .001). Instagram minutes per week was a significant positive predictor in the model (B = 0.004, SE = 0.001, β = .25, p = .009), indicating that each additional 100 minutes per week on Instagram was associated with a 0.4 point increase in the life satisfaction composite score, controlling for locus of control. The standardized beta coefficients indicate that, while locus of control was the stronger predictor, Instagram usage made a reliable unique contribution to life satisfaction in the combined model.

Exploratory correlations were computed between life satisfaction and frequency ratings for the different image types listed in the content exposure inventory. Frequency of encountering peer photographs on Instagram showed a small and non-significant negative correlation with life satisfaction (r = −.10, p = .31). Frequency of encountering influencer or celebrity content was not significantly correlated with life satisfaction (r = −.05, p = .63). Frequency of encountering aspirational lifestyle or travel images was also uncorrelated with life satisfaction (r = −.08, p = .42). Notably, the frequency of posting selfies was positively correlated with life satisfaction (r = .19, p = .06), reaching marginal significance, and frequency of engaging in direct messaging and active interactions (comments and direct messages) was positively associated with life satisfaction (r = .28, p = .005).

Partial correlations controlling for locus of control confirmed that these exploratory patterns were not artifacts of locus of control. After adjusting for locus of control, the positive association between active engagement behaviors (commenting, direct messaging) and life satisfaction remained significant (partial r = .26, p = .01). The small negative correlation between frequency of viewing peer photographs and life satisfaction remained non-significant when locus of control was controlled (partial r = −.07, p = .47).

Gender differences were assessed in exploratory analyses. Men and women did not differ significantly in mean life satisfaction scores (men mean = 23.8, women mean = 24.9; t(96) = 0.86, p = .39). There was also no significant interaction between gender and Instagram minutes in predicting life satisfaction when entered into regression models (interaction term B = 0.001, SE = 0.001, p = .41). Similarly, gender did not significantly moderate the association between frequency of viewing peer photographs and life satisfaction.

Given the skew observed in the Instagram minutes variable, sensitivity analyses were conducted using a log-transformed version of minutes per week. The log-transformed analyses yielded a similar pattern of results: log-transformed Instagram use was positively correlated with life satisfaction (r = .21, p = .032), and the partial correlation controlling for locus of control remained positive and significant (partial r = .29, p = .004). The regression model with log-transformed Instagram use and locus of control also paralleled the findings reported above, with both predictors contributing independently to life satisfaction.

Because the primary hypothesis anticipated a negative association between Instagram use and life satisfaction, the observed positive association warranted closer scrutiny. To probe whether extreme values were driving the effect, regression diagnostics were examined. No single case exerted undue influence on the regression coefficients as indicated by Cook’s distance values below conventional thresholds. Additionally, when the analyses were repeated after excluding the top 5% of Instagram minutes values to reduce the influence of extreme usage reports, the positive association between Instagram minutes and life satisfaction persisted (r = .20, p = .04), suggesting the relationship was not solely an artifact of a few heavy users.

Finally, to explore whether active versus passive use patterns accounted for the positive association between Instagram time and life satisfaction, a composite variable indicating active engagement (based on frequency of posting, commenting, and direct messaging) was created. Active engagement was positively correlated with both Instagram minutes (r = .45, p < .001) and life satisfaction (r = .28, p = .005). When active engagement was added to the regression model along with locus of control and overall Instagram minutes, active engagement remained a significant positive predictor of life satisfaction (B = 1.2, SE = 0.46, β = .21, p = .01), and the coefficient for overall Instagram minutes decreased in magnitude and was no longer significant (B = 0.002, SE = 0.001, β = .12, p = .17). These exploratory analyses suggest that active social interactions on the platform may partly explain why greater time on Instagram was associated with higher life satisfaction in this sample.

Discussion

The present study investigated the relationships among time spent on an image-centric social media platform, the character of the images encountered, locus of control, and reported life satisfaction in a sample of university students. The principal findings were threefold. First, locus of control was robustly associated with life satisfaction: individuals who reported a more external locus of control orientation also reported lower life satisfaction. Second, contrary to the initial hypothesis, greater time spent on Instagram was associated with higher life satisfaction at the bivariate level and this positive relationship strengthened when statistical control for locus of control was applied. Third, exploratory analyses suggested that active engagement behaviors on the platform — such as posting, commenting, and direct messaging — were positively associated with life satisfaction and that active engagement may account for part of the positive association between overall usage time and life satisfaction. In contrast, the frequency of viewing peer photographs and other image types did not show reliable negative associations with life satisfaction in this sample.

The negative association between locus of control and life satisfaction aligns with a large body of work indicating that perceived personal control is a key contributor to subjective well-being. Individuals who perceive outcomes as contingent on their own actions typically experience greater agency and are better positioned to enact behaviors that promote valued goals, which is reflected in higher life satisfaction. The present results underscore that locus of control is an important individual difference variable to consider in studies of social media and well-being because it is both theoretically relevant and empirically associated with life satisfaction.

The finding of a positive association between Instagram usage and life satisfaction diverges from the expectation, based on social comparison theory applied to visual content, that greater exposure to curated images would lead to reduced satisfaction. The fact that this relationship became stronger when locus of control was statistically controlled suggests that locus of control may have been suppressing or masking the positive association between Instagram use and life satisfaction in the bivariate analysis. In other words, individuals with more external locus of control tended to have lower life satisfaction and perhaps a particular pattern of social media engagement that complicates the bivariate relationship; once that variance was accounted for, the positive link between Instagram use and life satisfaction emerged more clearly.

There are several plausible explanations for the positive association observed. One compelling possibility is that not all social media use is equivalent: active forms of use that involve direct social interaction, self-disclosure, and reciprocal communication are likely to foster social connectedness and provide social reinforcement that can enhance well-being. The exploratory analyses in this study support this account insofar as active engagement behaviors were positively correlated with life satisfaction and partially accounted for the association between time on Instagram and life satisfaction. This interpretation is consistent with prior research that differentiates active and passive social media use, with active use often associated with beneficial social and emotional outcomes and passive use more likely to relate to envy and negative affect. If individuals in the present sample used Instagram in ways that supported existing social relationships and elicited positive feedback, then higher usage might reflect greater social integration and thus higher life satisfaction.

Another explanation is that a selection effect is present: individuals who are already relatively satisfied with their lives may be more likely to use Instagram frequently because they have more social activities to share or because they derive enjoyment from communicating through images. In this scenario, the observed correlation reflects the tendency of satisfied people to engage in more social posting and browsing, rather than a causal effect of Instagram use on life satisfaction. The cross-sectional nature of the present study cannot distinguish between these competing causal possibilities, and longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to clarify causality.

The lack of a significant negative association between frequency of viewing peer photographs and life satisfaction in this sample is noteworthy because prior work has highlighted peer photographs as a potentially potent trigger for upward social comparison. Several reasons might account for this null finding. First, the simplistic frequency measure used in this study may not have captured the qualitative aspects of exposure that are most influential, such as the perceived authenticity of images, the closeness of the relationship to the person depicted, or the tendency to engage in social comparison while viewing the images. Second, the sample consisted of university students who might have different interpretive frames for peer content — for example, higher baseline familiarity with the curated nature of social media images or social norms that mitigate negative impacts. Third, the consequences of viewing peer photographs may vary widely across individuals, depending on traits such as social comparison orientation, self-esteem, or depressive symptoms; without measures of these moderators, averaged associations may obscure important heterogeneity.

The study’s findings have implications for theoretical accounts that emphasize upward social comparison as an overarching explanation for negative effects of visual social media on well-being. While social comparison processes are certainly plausible and have empirical support, the current results indicate that social media effects are not uniform and that platform use can be associated with positive subjective outcomes under certain conditions. The interaction of individual differences (such as locus of control), behavioral patterns of use (active versus passive engagement), and content characteristics likely shapes the net effect of social media on life satisfaction. This complexity argues against simplistic narratives that label image-based social media as intrinsically harmful and instead suggests a more nuanced approach that considers context and user behavior.

The study has several limitations that bear on the interpretation of the findings and point to directions for future research. The cross-sectional survey design precludes conclusions about causality, and the observed associations may reflect bidirectional or third-variable relationships. Longitudinal studies that track changes in Instagram use and life satisfaction over time would be valuable to disentangle causal pathways. Furthermore, experimental studies that manipulate exposure to specific image types or that randomly assign participants to active versus passive usage conditions can provide stronger causal evidence.

Measurement limitations are also important to acknowledge. Time spent on Instagram was assessed via self-report, which is subject to recall bias and social desirability effects. Objective usage data obtained from device logs or platform analytics would provide more precise estimates of time spent and patterns of use. The content exposure inventory in this study relied on participants’ global frequency ratings of broad image categories, which likely failed to capture the nuanced features of images that trigger social comparison, such as perceived attainability, authenticity, or the social relationship between viewer and image subject. Future studies should consider more granular content coding, perhaps through automated image analysis or by presenting participants with standardized stimuli to assess emotional and evaluative responses.

Another limitation is the narrow sample: university students from a single institution. While this group is an important population to study given their high rates of social media use, findings may not generalize to older adults, adolescents, or individuals in different cultural contexts. Replication in more diverse samples would help determine the robustness and generalizability of the relationships observed.

Additionally, the study did not include key potential moderators or mediators of the relationship between social media use and life satisfaction, such as social comparison orientation, self-esteem, depressive symptoms, perceived social support, or the quality of offline social relationships. Inclusion of these variables in future research would clarify mechanisms by which social media use relates to well-being and would help identify individuals who are particularly vulnerable or resilient to potential negative effects.

The finding that locus of control explained a substantial portion of variance in life satisfaction and altered the association between Instagram use and life satisfaction highlights the importance of accounting for stable personality and cognitive variables in social media research. Future studies should incorporate a broader set of dispositional factors to map how these characteristics interact with behavior on social platforms.

Despite these limitations, the study contributes to the literature by providing empirical evidence that challenges a monolithic view of visual social media as uniformly detrimental to life satisfaction. The data suggest that, at least in this sample of young adults, greater engagement with an image-based platform was associated with higher life satisfaction and that this positive association was partly explained by active social engagement on the platform. From a practical standpoint, the findings imply that encouraging active, socially connective uses of visual platforms may be a strategy for mitigating potential negative effects. Interventions or digital literacy programs that promote mindful and interactive engagement rather than passive scrolling could help users derive social benefits from these technologies.

Future research could fruitfully pursue several directions based on the present results. First, longitudinal and experimental designs are needed to ascertain causal relationships and to test whether increases in active engagement lead to improvements in life satisfaction or whether those with higher life satisfaction are simply more inclined to engage actively. Second, studies should obtain objective usage metrics and fine-grained content analyses to identify the particular exposures and behaviors most predictive of well-being outcomes. Third, research should examine moderators such as social comparison orientation, self-esteem, depressive symptoms, and perceived social support to determine which individuals are most likely to experience harm or benefit from visual social media. Fourth, qualitative work exploring users’ subjective experiences and meanings attached to different types of images could provide richer insight into the processes by which visual content affects emotion and evaluation.

In conclusion, the study provides an important corrective to narratives that portray visual social media as necessarily harmful to subjective well-being. While upward social comparison remains a plausible mechanism by which certain exposures could undermine life satisfaction for some people, the present results indicate that overall time spent on an image-centric platform can be positively associated with life satisfaction, particularly when that time involves active social engagement. Locus of control emerged as a robust correlate of life satisfaction and an important covariate to consider in analyses. The findings invite a more nuanced theoretical stance that recognizes the conditional and interactive nature of social media effects and call for more sophisticated measurement and design choices in future research.
