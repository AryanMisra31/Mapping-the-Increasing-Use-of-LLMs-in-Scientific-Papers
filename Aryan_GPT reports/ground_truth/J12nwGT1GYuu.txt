Introduction

Caffeine is the most widely used psychoactive substance in the world and its consumption is especially common among students as they enter and progress through university life. Patterns of caffeine use among students have changed in recent decades as coffee shop culture has expanded and as students adopt caffeine-containing products to manage academic demands and irregular sleep schedules. The ubiquity of caffeine and its diverse effects on cognition, mood, and physiology make it an important target for research into mental wellbeing. Research on caffeine’s effects on psychological functioning is extensive but heterogeneous, yielding findings that range from beneficial impacts on alertness and mood to adverse effects such as increased anxiety and sleep disruption. The pharmacology of caffeine provides a rationale for expecting effects on mental wellbeing because caffeine is a central nervous system stimulant that acts primarily as an antagonist at adenosine receptors. Blocking adenosine receptors generally increases arousal and can indirectly modulate dopaminergic and noradrenergic systems implicated in motivation and mood. At low to moderate doses, caffeine commonly produces improved subjective alertness and transient improvements in some aspects of cognitive performance, while at higher doses it can precipitate nervousness, jitteriness, and sleep problems. The net effect of habitual caffeine consumption on global measures of wellbeing such as life satisfaction is therefore uncertain because acute stimulant effects might improve momentary mood while chronic or high-dose patterns could impair sleep and increase anxiety. Additionally, individual differences in sensitivity and tolerance to caffeine mean that the same level of intake may be beneficial to some people and detrimental to others, introducing further variability into population-level associations.

A substantial body of research has examined the short-term mood and cognitive effects of caffeine using experimental designs, often focusing on acute administration in laboratory settings. These studies commonly report transient increases in alertness and reductions in subjective fatigue following caffeine intake. However, experimental effects observed under controlled conditions do not always translate directly to relationships between habitual consumption and global wellbeing in naturalistic samples. Observational studies that examine habitual caffeine use and measures of psychological health yield mixed results, with some reports finding small positive associations between moderate caffeine consumption and mood or lower rates of depressive symptoms, and other reports finding no association or small negative associations with anxiety and sleep quality. Differences in findings across studies may stem from methodological variability including how caffeine is quantified, whether the focus is on coffee, tea, energy drinks, or all sources, how wellbeing is operationalized, and whether studies control for confounding factors such as sleep, stress, socioeconomic status, or substance use. Cross-sectional designs, in particular, are vulnerable to confounding and reverse causation, because people with lower wellbeing might change their caffeine habits to compensate, or people with particular lifestyle patterns that affect wellbeing might also be more likely to consume caffeine.

The university student population warrants specific attention because students often adopt distinctive consumption patterns that reflect academic pressures, social norms, and the transition to independent living. During the university years, students may increase caffeine intake to manage study demands and irregular schedules, and they may also experience fluctuations in mental wellbeing as they adapt to new academic and social environments. Understanding whether habitual caffeine consumption among students is associated with general life satisfaction has implications for public health messaging and university wellbeing services. If caffeine consumption is related to higher life satisfaction, this could reflect adaptive coping strategies or beneficial stimulant effects that support functioning. If caffeine consumption is either unrelated or associated with lower life satisfaction, that would suggest caution in recommending caffeine use for wellbeing and would highlight the need to consider other aspects of lifestyle that contribute to students’ psychological health.

Existing literature presents several mechanistic pathways through which caffeine could plausibly influence global life satisfaction. One pathway is through effects on sleep. Caffeine can delay sleep onset and reduce sleep quality when consumed later in the day, and chronic sleep disruption in turn undermines mood and life satisfaction. Another pathway is through anxiety and physiological arousal: for some individuals, especially at high doses, caffeine can precipitate or exacerbate anxiety, which might lower well-being. Conversely, caffeine’s cognitive enhancing and mood-elevating effects may lead to better daytime functioning, increased productivity, and improved social engagement, potentially promoting higher life satisfaction. Beyond physiological effects, social and cultural factors may mediate associations between habitual caffeine intake and wellbeing; for example, coffee-drinking can be a social ritual that supports friendships and routines, which could indirectly boost life satisfaction. Therefore, whether habitual consumption is ultimately associated with greater or lesser life satisfaction likely depends on a balance of these mechanisms and on contextual factors such as dose, timing, individual sensitivity, and patterns of use.

Many prior studies have limitations that justify further investigation. Experimental studies demonstrate acute effects but do not capture habitual patterns or broader life satisfaction. Observational studies sometimes rely on single-item measures or imprecise measurement of caffeine intake, and they often do not disaggregate sources of caffeine such as coffee or tea. Studies of student populations are valuable but frequently use convenience samples or small sample sizes that limit power to detect modest associations. There is therefore a need for studies that measure habitual consumption with reasonable precision, assess global life satisfaction as an indicator of overall wellbeing, and apply transparent statistical methods to test for relationships in samples of typical university students. Investigations that specifically examine both coffee and tea consumption are particularly relevant in cultural contexts where tea remains a significant source of caffeine.

The present study addresses these issues by examining the association between habitual caffeine consumption in the form of coffee and tea and general life satisfaction in a sample of university students. We recruited one hundred students at a single university and asked them to complete two separate questionnaires. The first questionnaire assessed caffeine consumption habits with items that captured frequency and typical serving sizes for coffee and tea. The second questionnaire assessed general life satisfaction using a commonly used multi-item self-report instrument. The primary analysis involved computing total scores from each questionnaire to yield one caffeine consumption variable and one life satisfaction variable and then conducting a bivariate correlational analysis between these two variables. This analytic approach allows us to test whether, in this sample, higher habitual caffeine consumption is associated with higher or lower ratings of overall life satisfaction.

Given the mixed literature and potential for opposing mechanisms, firm a priori directional expectations are difficult to specify. On the one hand, there are reasons to predict a positive association between moderate caffeine use and life satisfaction, mediated by improved daytime functioning and social rituals. On the other hand, higher or late-day consumption could impair sleep and raise anxiety, suggesting possible negative associations. Because of these competing hypotheses, the present analysis treats the expected association as an empirical question without a strong directional prediction. We also note that a null finding would not necessarily prove the absence of any effect in all contexts, and interpretation of non-significant results must consider issues of measurement sensitivity, statistical power, and sample characteristics. Our study thus aims to provide an informative test of the association between typical coffee and tea consumption and general life satisfaction among university students and to discuss the implications of the findings for theory and future research.

Methods

Participants

A total of one hundred students from a single university participated in the study. Participants were recruited through campus notices and social media groups associated with the university, and recruitment materials invited volunteers to complete two short online questionnaires. All participants were current undergraduate or postgraduate students enrolled at the university at the time of data collection. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 29 years, with a mean age of 20.4 years and a standard deviation of 2.1 years. The sample included students from a variety of academic programs and a mix of years of study. Gender distribution in the sample consisted of 63 female participants and 37 male participants. Participation was voluntary and no students were excluded based on their level of caffeine consumption. Participants received a modest incentive in the form of a chance to win one of several small gift vouchers as compensation for completing both questionnaires. All procedures were approved by the university’s ethics committee and participants provided informed consent online prior to participation.

Measures

Caffeine consumption questionnaire. Habitual caffeine consumption was assessed with a brief purpose-designed questionnaire that focused specifically on coffee and tea consumption. The questionnaire asked participants to report typical frequency of consumption for coffee and for tea separately (with response options ranging from “never” to “more than five times per day”), typical serving size (small, medium, large), and whether they typically consumed caffeinated or decaffeinated varieties. These items were combined into a composite caffeine consumption score representing habitual intake from coffee and tea. Scores were computed by converting frequency and serving size responses into numeric indicators and summing across the coffee and tea items, yielding a composite range that in this study spanned the observed minimum to maximum values. The composite score therefore reflected relative levels of habitual caffeine consumption from these two beverage sources. Internal consistency of the multi-item composite was assessed with Cronbach’s alpha in the present sample.

Life satisfaction questionnaire. General life satisfaction was assessed using a widely used multi-item self-report scale designed to capture global cognitive evaluations of one’s life as a whole. The instrument consists of several items that ask respondents to indicate their agreement with statements about overall satisfaction with life. Responses are given on a Likert-type response format and are summed to produce a total life satisfaction score, with higher scores indicating greater perceived life satisfaction. The scale has been extensively used in research on wellbeing and has demonstrated good psychometric properties in diverse populations. In the present sample the life satisfaction instrument’s internal consistency was computed using Cronbach’s alpha.

Demographics and covariates. Participants also completed a brief demographic questionnaire that recorded age, gender, year of study, and whether they consumed other caffeinated products on a regular basis (e.g., energy drinks, cola). Participants were asked not to alter their usual caffeine habits for the purpose of the study and were free to complete the questionnaires at times of their choosing within a specified window.

Procedure

Data collection occurred via online questionnaires administered through the university’s secure survey platform. Participants first completed the caffeine consumption questionnaire and demographic items and then completed the life satisfaction questionnaire. To reduce order effects, the two questionnaires were presented in counterbalanced order across participants, with approximately half of participants receiving the caffeine questionnaire first and the rest receiving the life satisfaction questionnaire first. The two questionnaires could be completed within the same session and typically took participants less than 15 minutes in total. Data collection took place over a four-week period during a term when no major examination period was underway, to minimize the influence of acute academic stressors on self-reported wellbeing.

Data screening and scoring

All returned questionnaires were screened for completeness. Cases with incomplete responses on either the caffeine composite items or the life satisfaction items were excluded from analyses. The final analytic sample consisted of one hundred participants with complete data on both questionnaires. The caffeine composite score was computed by standardizing and summing frequency and serving-size indicators for coffee and tea and then rescaling to an interpretable metric; this approach yielded scores where higher values corresponded to greater habitual intake. The life satisfaction score was computed according to the instrument’s standard scoring procedure, summing the item responses into a single index. The internal consistencies of both scales were assessed with Cronbach’s alpha.

Statistical analysis

The primary analysis tested the bivariate association between the composite caffeine consumption score and the life satisfaction score using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Prior to computing the correlation, the distributions of both variables were examined for normality using descriptive statistics and visual inspection of histograms and quantile plots. Potential outliers were identified using standard approaches including inspection of z-scores and leverage statistics in follow-up regression diagnostics. If assumptions for Pearson correlation were meaningfully violated, a nonparametric Spearman rank-order correlation was also planned as a robustness check. In addition to the primary bivariate correlation, secondary analyses included partial correlations controlling for age and gender and exploratory correlations that separated coffee-only and tea-only consumption. To aid interpretation of the observed correlation, we computed a 95% confidence interval for the Pearson correlation using Fisher’s z transformation. We also conducted a post-hoc power analysis to estimate the minimum effect size (correlation) that our sample of 100 participants had adequate power to detect at conventional alpha and beta thresholds. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software.

Ethics and transparency

The study protocols were reviewed and approved by the university’s institutional review board. All participants provided informed consent and were debriefed at the end of the study with general information about caffeine use and wellbeing. Analytic decisions, including scoring rules for the caffeine composite and criteria for excluding incomplete responses, were specified prior to inspecting the primary correlation and were followed consistently. The data that underlie the analyses are stored on the university’s secure servers and are available for re-analysis upon reasonable request and subject to ethical governance.

Results

Descriptive statistics and scale properties

The final analytic sample included one hundred participants with complete data on both the caffeine consumption composite and the life satisfaction measure. The caffeine composite score had an observed mean of 19.8 with a standard deviation of 9.6 in the sample. The life satisfaction total score had an observed mean of 24.7 and a standard deviation of 5.4. The internal consistency of the caffeine composite, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was 0.71, indicating acceptable internal reliability for a brief multi-item composite. The life satisfaction scale demonstrated good internal consistency in the present sample, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86. Visual inspection of histograms suggested that both variables had approximate unimodal distributions without extreme skewness. Skewness and kurtosis statistics for both variables were within conventional bounds for normal approximation, with skewness values close to zero and kurtosis values near those expected for a normal distribution.

Assumption checks and outlier analysis

Prior to conducting correlation analyses, the data were screened for influential observations. No single data point produced an unduly high leverage or Cook’s distance in a simple regression of life satisfaction on caffeine consumption. Standardized z-scores indicated that no case exceeded an absolute value of 3.29 on either variable, suggesting an absence of extreme univariate outliers. Bivariate scatterplots did not reveal any clear non-linear patterns or clusters that would violate the assumption of a linear association between the two variables. Given these diagnostics, Pearson’s correlation was judged to be an appropriate primary analytic test. As a robustness check, Spearman rank correlations were computed to ensure that findings were not sensitive to modest departures from normality or to the influence of ordinal scaling in the caffeine composite.

Primary bivariate correlation

The primary analysis tested the bivariate association between the caffeine composite score and the life satisfaction score using Pearson’s correlation. The correlation coefficient was r = 0.08. The p-value associated with this correlation was p = .43, indicating that the correlation was not statistically significant at conventional alpha levels. Using Fisher’s z transformation, the 95% confidence interval for the population correlation was calculated and ranged from approximately -0.12 to 0.27. This interval indicates that the data are compatible with small negative correlations, zero correlation, and small positive correlations, but rule out moderate or large negative or positive correlations with reasonable confidence given the sample size. A Spearman rank-order correlation produced a similar result (rho = 0.07, p = .46), providing a nonparametric corroboration of the Pearson result.

Secondary analyses controlling for demographics

To evaluate whether the bivariate association might be confounded by simple demographic factors, partial correlations controlling for age and gender were conducted. The partial correlation between caffeine consumption and life satisfaction controlling for age and gender was r_partial = 0.06 with a p-value of .55. This partial correlation remained non-significant and did not materially differ from the crude bivariate result, suggesting that basic demographic variables did not mask a substantive association in this sample.

Exploratory beverage-specific analyses

Given the possibility that coffee and tea might have different correlational patterns with life satisfaction, exploratory analyses were conducted separating coffee-only and tea-only consumption estimates. The correlation between coffee-only consumption and life satisfaction was r = 0.05 (p = .62). The correlation between tea-only consumption and life satisfaction was r = 0.09 (p = .37). Neither beverage-specific correlation reached statistical significance. Confidence intervals for these beverage-specific correlations were wide enough to include small negative and small positive effects, underscoring uncertainty about very small effects in the sample.

Effect size interpretation and post-hoc power

The observed correlation of r = 0.08 corresponds to a very small effect size, explaining less than 1% of the variance in life satisfaction (R^2 ≈ 0.006). To contextualize the null finding, a post-hoc power analysis indicated that with an alpha of .05 and a sample size of 100, the study had approximately 80% power to detect a correlation of r ≈ 0.28 or larger. Conversely, the study had low power to detect correlations smaller than approximately 0.20 to 0.25 with high confidence. Thus, the study was well-powered to detect small-to-moderate associations but underpowered for detecting very small effects.

Interpretation of non-significant findings

Taken together, the results indicate that in this sample of university students, habitual caffeine consumption from coffee and tea showed no statistically significant association with general life satisfaction. Both parametric and nonparametric correlations were close to zero and controlling for simple demographic variables did not uncover a substantive relationship. The confidence interval for the correlation excluded moderate-to-large associations but did not preclude very small effects in either direction. Accordingly, the primary conclusion from the present data is that there is no evidence of a meaningful linear association between habitual coffee-and-tea-derived caffeine consumption and overall life satisfaction among the students sampled in this study.

Additional checks and robustness

Several additional checks were performed to ensure the robustness of the primary conclusion. Separating caffeinated versus decaffeinated habitual consumers did not reveal systematic differences in life satisfaction scores. Examination of the pattern of consumption by study year did not reveal consistent trends that would confound the main association. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, causal inferences are not warranted, and the analyses are presented as correlational descriptions of the observed sample. Overall, the set of robustness checks supported the conclusion that there was no detectable meaningful linear relationship between habitual coffee and tea consumption and life satisfaction in this cohort.

Results summary

In summary, descriptive statistics showed acceptable internal consistencies for the study measures. Assumption checks supported the use of Pearson correlation, and both parametric and nonparametric correlations indicated very small and non-significant associations between habitual caffeine consumption from coffee and tea and general life satisfaction. Secondary analyses controlling for age and gender and exploratory beverage-specific analyses were consistent with the primary null finding. Post-hoc power considerations indicate that the study could detect small-to-moderate correlations but had limited sensitivity to very small associations. The results therefore suggest that, within the limits of the present sample and measurement approach, there is no evidence of a substantive relationship between typical caffeine intake in the form of coffee and tea and students’ overall life satisfaction.

Discussion

The present study examined the association between habitual caffeine consumption from coffee and tea and global life satisfaction in a sample of one hundred university students. The primary analysis revealed a very small, non-significant correlation between caffeine consumption and life satisfaction (r = 0.08, p = .43), and secondary and exploratory analyses did not identify systematic associations when controlling for basic demographics or when separating coffee and tea sources. The confidence interval around the correlation excluded moderate or large associations but left open the possibility of very small effects. These findings provide no evidence that habitual consumption of coffee and tea is meaningfully associated with life satisfaction in this particular student sample.

Interpreting a non-significant correlation requires careful attention to measurement, sampling, and theoretical considerations. First, it is important to distinguish between the absence of evidence for an association and evidence for the absence of any effect. The current analysis did not provide evidence for a meaningful linear association, but the confidence interval indicates that very small relationships could exist and would not be detectable at the present sample size with high confidence. Thus, we cannot rule out the possibility that habitual caffeine consumption is associated with life satisfaction at a very small effect size. However, the results do suggest that any such relationship is unlikely to be strong or clinically large in this demographic of students.

Second, measurement considerations may limit the sensitivity of the test. The caffeine composite used in this study focused on coffee and tea and combined frequency and serving size items to estimate habitual intake. While this approach captures the primary sources of caffeine for many individuals in the cultural context of the sample, it does not provide a precise measurement of milligrams of caffeine consumed per day. Further, self-report estimates of frequency and serving size are subject to recall error and social desirability bias. Similarly, the life satisfaction instrument captures a global cognitive appraisal of one’s life but does not index momentary mood states or domain-specific satisfaction (e.g., academic satisfaction, social satisfaction). It is possible that habitual caffeine consumption relates more strongly to short-term affective states, cognitive functioning, or domain-specific aspects of wellbeing rather than to a global cognitive evaluation of life as a whole. Future studies that combine more fine-grained measurement of caffeine intake (for example, daily diaries, ecological momentary assessment, or objective biomarkers) with multiple indicators of wellbeing could provide a more nuanced picture of potential associations.

Third, timing and dose of caffeine consumption are important moderators that were not fully captured by the present design. The effects of caffeine on sleep and mood depend strongly on the timing of consumption; caffeine consumed late in the day is more likely to interfere with sleep and thereby negatively affect wellbeing. Dose-response relationships are also non-linear, with low-to-moderate doses producing beneficial effects for many users and higher doses producing adverse effects such as increased anxiety. The composite score used in this study aggregated across timing and dose information, potentially obscuring heterogeneous effects. For example, two individuals could have similar composite scores but very different temporal patterns of intake—one consuming most caffeine in the morning and the other consuming it in the evening—leading to different implications for their sleep and wellbeing.

Fourth, individual differences in sensitivity and tolerance to caffeine may moderate associations with wellbeing. Genetic polymorphisms in caffeine metabolism (e.g., variation in the CYP1A2 gene) and in adenosine receptor functioning can influence both the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of caffeine, producing variable subjective and physiological responses across individuals. Habitual consumers also develop tolerance to certain effects of caffeine, which could reduce both positive and negative acute effects. These individual differences mean that simple comparisons of habitual intake may obscure meaningful heterogeneity in how caffeine relates to wellbeing. Future work that measures genetic markers or uses within-subject designs to assess individual response profiles could shed light on these moderator effects.

Fifth, contextual and social factors likely shape the relationship between caffeine consumption and life satisfaction. Coffee consumption, in particular, is often embedded in social rituals and routines that contribute to social connectedness and structure in daily life. These social aspects could produce indirect positive associations between certain patterns of consumption and wellbeing that do not reflect pharmacological effects per se. Conversely, heavy reliance on caffeine as a coping mechanism for stress or overwork might reflect broader lifestyle patterns that are detrimental to wellbeing. The cross-sectional design of the present study cannot disentangle such processes or establish whether caffeine consumption influences life satisfaction or whether underlying life satisfaction influences caffeine use.

Sixth, caffeine is one of many substances and lifestyle factors that affect mental wellbeing, and its effects may interact with factors such as sleep quality, alcohol and tobacco use, dietary patterns, physical activity, and stress. The present study controlled for only basic demographic variables and did not comprehensively measure these potential confounds or mediators. As a result, the observed lack of association between caffeine consumption and life satisfaction might mask complex indirect pathways in which caffeine exerts small direct effects that are amplified or mitigated by other behaviors and contextual factors.

Seventh, the sample characteristics limit generalizability. The participants were drawn from a single university and were predominantly young adults in a specific cultural context where both coffee and tea consumption are common. The patterns observed in this cohort may not generalize to older adults, non-student populations, or populations in different cultural settings where sources of caffeine differ. Moreover, the sample size, while adequate to detect small-to-moderate correlations, had limited sensitivity for very small effects. Larger, multi-site studies would be valuable for assessing the robustness and generalizability of null findings.

Eighth, the implications of the null finding for public health and student wellbeing depend on the substantive effect sizes of interest. If the practical concern is whether habitual caffeine consumption strongly and consistently affects overall life satisfaction among students, the present results suggest that it does not. However, if stakeholders are concerned about smaller effects that could accumulate across large populations or about specific adverse effects (e.g., sleep disruption, anxiety) in vulnerable subgroups, then the study’s null finding should not be interpreted as providing reassurance in all circumstances. University wellbeing services should still consider individual students’ patterns of caffeine use as part of broader assessments of sleep, stress, and coping.

Ninth, the study points to several directions for future research. Longitudinal designs would be particularly informative for untangling causal pathways and temporal ordering between caffeine use and wellbeing. Experimental studies that manipulate caffeine intake under ecologically valid conditions, combined with measures of sleep, anxiety, mood, and daily functioning, could test mechanistic hypotheses about how caffeine influences life satisfaction. Ecological momentary assessment methods that capture within-person fluctuations in caffeine intake, sleep, and affect would permit examination of short-term associations that cross-sectional global measures may miss. Incorporating objective measures of caffeine exposure such as salivary caffeine concentrations or wearable sensors that track physiological arousal could enhance measurement precision. Investigating moderators such as timing of consumption, dose, genetic markers, tolerance, and social context would further clarify when and for whom caffeine use may have beneficial versus detrimental consequences.

Tenth, methodological improvements to measurement are important. Developing standardized and validated brief instruments for estimating habitual caffeine intake in terms of milligrams per day, calibrated across beverage types and serving sizes, would help harmonize research findings. Using validated multi-dimensional wellbeing measures rather than single global scores could reveal domain-specific associations that are hidden when only overall life satisfaction is considered. Researchers should also be explicit about the distinctions between acute effects, habitual use, and dependence or withdrawal phenomena, which have different implications for wellbeing and functioning.

Eleventh, considering substitution and co-use of other stimulants is relevant. The landscape of caffeine consumption includes emerging products such as energy drinks, pre-workout supplements, and caffeinated foods, which may differ in dose, rate of consumption, and co-occurrence with other substances. Studies that consider the full spectrum of caffeine sources and poly-substance patterns will be better positioned to capture the real-world exposure profiles relevant to students.

In conclusion, the present study found no evidence of a substantive linear association between habitual caffeine consumption from coffee and tea and general life satisfaction in a sample of one hundred university students. The result is informative in indicating that any association, if present in similar populations, is likely to be small. However, measurement limitations, sample characteristics, and the cross-sectional design caution against overgeneralizing this null finding. Future research that employs longitudinal and experimental designs, improves measurement precision, and examines moderators and mediators will be necessary to clarify the complex relationship between caffeine use and wellbeing. In the meantime, recommendations for students should emphasize moderating caffeine consumption as part of broader strategies to maintain healthy sleep patterns, manage stress, and support overall wellbeing, while recognizing that moderate caffeine use alone is unlikely to have a large impact on global life satisfaction for most students.
