Introduction

Caffeine is one of the most widely consumed psychoactive substances in the world, and its effects on human cognition, affect, and physiology have attracted sustained scientific interest. The ubiquity of caffeine in beverages and foods ensures that many adults regularly consume it, and that consumption patterns vary widely across individuals. At a basic pharmacological level, caffeine acts primarily as an adenosine receptor antagonist, which increases neuronal firing and leads to heightened alertness and reduced perceived fatigue. These acute stimulant effects have been well documented in laboratory contexts where controlled doses are administered and subsequent changes in reaction time, sustained attention, and subjective alertness are measured. Beyond attentional and cognitive benefits, caffeine is also commonly reported in lay discourse as a stress reliever or mood enhancer, with many adults reporting that a caffeinated beverage helps them feel better or cope with the demands of daily life. However, despite frequent anecdotal reports of mood benefits, the empirical literature on caffeine’s relationship with broader measures of wellbeing and happiness is mixed.

There are several reasons to expect that caffeine might exert an influence on subjective wellbeing. One mechanistic route is through enhancement of positive affect and reduction of fatigue, which could increase the frequency of pleasant experiences and thereby elevate global wellbeing. Increased alertness could also facilitate engagement in rewarding activities and social interactions, thereby indirectly supporting happiness. Another pathway concerns expectancy effects; regular consumers often have well-formed beliefs that caffeine improves their mood and coping, and such beliefs can shape subjective experience. Furthermore, caffeine’s acute physiological effects may transiently reduce the aversive sensations associated with stress, such as low energy and cognitive sluggishness, and such reductions may be experienced as stress relief even if underlying stressors remain. Finally, caffeine consumption often occurs within sociocultural contexts—shared coffee breaks, convivial tea-drinking rituals, and workplace norms—so habitual caffeine use may be associated with social routines that themselves are positively related to wellbeing.

Despite these plausible mechanisms, empirical findings relating caffeine use to global wellbeing or life satisfaction have been inconsistent. Some cross-sectional studies have reported small positive associations between caffeine intake and self-reported mood or life satisfaction, particularly when caffeine is consumed in social contexts or as part of pleasurable routines. Other work, however, has failed to find reliable links between habitual caffeine consumption and sustained measures of happiness or has even found associations in the opposite direction once confounding factors are controlled. One complicating factor is that many investigations focus on acute, experimentally administered caffeine rather than on habitual consumption patterns. Acute administration studies can elucidate causal effects on specific mood states, but they do not necessarily translate into reliable effects on trait-like measures of wellbeing. Another complicating factor is that habitual caffeine consumers are heterogeneous: some use caffeine to enhance pleasure, some to mitigate negative mood states, some to counter sleep loss, and some for purely social reasons. This heterogeneity may mean that aggregated correlations between caffeine intake and wellbeing obscure meaningful subgroup differences.

Withdrawal and tolerance present additional complexities. Habitual use of caffeine produces tolerance to some of its effects and can lead to withdrawal symptoms when intake is reduced, and these withdrawal effects (headache, low mood, fatigue) can transiently lower wellbeing in regular users who miss their expected intake. Thus, cross-sectional associations between caffeine habits and wellbeing can reflect a mix of acute stimulant benefits, long-term adaptation, expectancy effects, and withdrawal dynamics. Additionally, caffeine interacts with sleep quality; higher caffeine intake, particularly later in the day, can disrupt sleep, and chronic poor sleep is strongly linked to lower wellbeing. Thus, any observed association between caffeine use and wellbeing could be mediated by sleep quality, or caffeine use could be an adaptational response to preexisting low wellbeing or poor sleep, rather than its cause.

Measurement challenges further contribute to mixed empirical findings. Studies vary in how they operationalize caffeine exposure, ranging from simple binary indicators of “coffee drinker” status to detailed logs of milligrams consumed per day. Likewise, wellbeing measures vary from momentary mood reports to global assessments of life satisfaction, complicating comparisons across studies. Expectancy and personality variables are not always measured, yet they can moderate caffeine’s subjective effects. For these reasons, careful consideration of measurement and potential confounds is essential when attempting to relate habitual caffeine use to indices of wellbeing.

The Oxford Happiness Questionnaire is one widely used self-report instrument for indexing subjective happiness and wellbeing. It captures a broad range of dimensions associated with happiness, including positive affect, satisfaction with life, and psychological resources. Its psychometric properties have been established in diverse samples, and it is often used in nonclinical populations to assess individual differences in happiness. Given the questionnaire’s focus on trait-like aspects of wellbeing, it provides a useful outcome measure for investigating habitual lifestyle factors, such as caffeine consumption.

Previous empirical work examining the relationship between caffeine and happiness has produced a range of findings. Some investigators have reported that habitual coffee consumption correlates with higher reported wellbeing, speculating that ritualistic consumption or social bonding associated with coffee may contribute to these positive associations. Other studies, however, have documented null or negligible associations once demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related covariates are taken into account. The inconsistent pattern in the literature suggests that the relationship between caffeine habits and enduring wellbeing is not straightforward and may depend on moderating variables such as reasons for consumption, timing of intake, and the presence of caffeine-related withdrawal or tolerance effects.

A key limitation in many existing studies is that they often do not disaggregate caffeine use by purpose or context, nor do they examine the possibility of nonlinear relationships. For example, low-to-moderate intake might be associated with modest improvements in alertness and mood, whereas very high intake could contribute to anxiety, jitteriness, and sleep problems that reduce wellbeing. Moreover, there are likely individual differences in sensitivity to caffeine, potentially shaped by genetic factors, habitual use patterns, and comorbid conditions such as anxiety disorders, which could moderate the caffeine–wellbeing relationship. The literature also lacks clarity on whether associations are bidirectional, with lower wellbeing potentially leading to greater caffeine use as a coping mechanism.

Given these unresolved issues, there is value in conducting studies that specifically examine habitual caffeine consumption and global trait happiness using established measures like the Oxford Happiness Questionnaire. Correlational studies can help establish whether stable associations exist in everyday samples and can inform subsequent experimental or longitudinal work aimed at disentangling causality. Such correlational evidence is particularly useful when it is gathered using reasonably sized, diverse samples and transparent measurement of caffeine habits.

The present study aimed to add to this literature by examining the association between typical caffeine consumption habits and self-reported happiness as measured by the Oxford Happiness Questionnaire in a community sample of adult participants. Based on anecdotal impressions and common beliefs about caffeine’s mood-enhancing properties, we hypothesized that more frequent habitual caffeine consumption would be positively associated with higher Oxford Happiness Questionnaire scores. We acknowledged, however, that the existing literature offers just as much caution, and that the cross-sectional design would not permit causal inference. We therefore framed the study as an exploratory test of the correlation between caffeine habits and trait happiness, with a focus on transparent reporting of methods and acknowledgment of potential confounds.

Beyond the primary correlation, the study also examined basic descriptive characteristics of caffeine consumption in the sample, including average number of caffeinated beverages consumed per day and variability across participants. We also explored whether any observed correlations were robust when accounting for simple demographic covariates such as age and gender. Although the measures were limited in scope and did not capture detailed motives for caffeine consumption or precise milligram intake, documenting the presence or absence of a simple association between self-reported habitual intake and trait happiness is a useful empirical step in clarifying the broader literature.

The rationale for the study is therefore threefold. First, to provide an empirical test of a commonly held lay belief that caffeine acts as a stress reliever and contributes to enhanced wellbeing. Second, to contribute to the literature by using a standardized measure of trait happiness, the Oxford Happiness Questionnaire, which allows for comparability across studies. Third, to identify whether simple correlational patterns merit further, more nuanced investigation that might include motives for use, timing of consumption, and longitudinal designs. We emphasized that any findings should be interpreted within the limits of cross-sectional correlational designs and that future research would need to address causality and potential mediators.

In line with the study’s exploratory nature, we did not pre-register a complex analytic plan, but we committed to reporting both primary correlational tests and basic sensitivity analyses adjusted for demographic variables. We also planned to conduct simple subgroup checks to assess whether patterns differed meaningfully across men and women, and across younger and older participants within our adult sample. We recognized that the sample’s composition would shape the generalizability of findings, and we collected basic demographic information to facilitate transparent reporting.

Given the background and the mixed literature, this study provides an opportunity to test whether habitual caffeine use is related to trait-level happiness in a community sample using established self-report measures. We sought to contribute a clear, pragmatic piece of evidence to a topic that is meaningful to everyday life and that has theoretical implications for understanding how routine psychoactive substance use relates to subjective wellbeing. The study’s modest scope and correlational design are appropriate for an initial step, and we aimed to interpret any results conservatively, highlighting limitations and directions for future work that could better ascertain causality and mechanisms.

Methods

Participants and recruitment formed the first part of the study procedure. Adult participants aged 18 and older were recruited via an online recruitment portal and campus email lists over a period of six weeks. Recruitment language invited participation in a brief survey about everyday habits and wellbeing, and potential participants were informed that the survey would take approximately 10 to 15 minutes to complete. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated, although students could optionally receive course credit where that was allowed and practical at the time of recruitment. A total of 160 participants completed the survey in full, and these complete cases formed the analytic sample. The sample included a range of ages, with participants spanning from late teens through middle adulthood, and participants varied in gender identity, although the majority identified as female. Basic demographic information was collected, including age in years, gender, and employment/student status, and these variables were used to describe the sample and in secondary sensitivity analyses.

All participants provided informed consent electronically prior to beginning the survey. The consent form briefly described the study aims, the voluntary nature of participation, the approximate survey duration, and the types of questions that would be asked, including items about caffeine consumption and general wellbeing. No personally identifying information was required beyond the optional provision of an email address for researchers to contact participants if needed; participants who provided an email address for course credit were separated from survey responses during data processing to maintain anonymity in analysis.

Measures were selected to be brief and to focus on habitual patterns of caffeine consumption and on the Oxford Happiness Questionnaire for trait happiness. Caffeine habits were assessed using a short, study-specific questionnaire that inquired about typical daily and weekly consumption of caffeinated beverages. Participants were asked to report how many cups of caffeinated coffee they drank per day on a typical day, how many cups of caffeinated tea per day on a typical day, how many cans or bottles of caffeinated soft drinks per day they consumed, and whether they used other caffeinated products such as energy drinks or caffeine-containing supplements. These items were used to compute an aggregate indicator of daily caffeinated beverage intake by summing reported servings across beverage categories. Participants were also asked to report typical timing (morning, afternoon, evening) of caffeine consumption and whether they considered themselves a “regular” caffeine consumer or an occasional consumer. These additional items were descriptive in nature and were used to characterize consumption patterns but were not included as part of a formal index beyond exploratory subgrouping.

The Oxford Happiness Questionnaire was administered in its standard self-report format. Participants responded to the items using the scale specified by the measure, and we computed total scores according to the instrument’s scoring instructions. For analysis, we used mean item scores to provide an interpretable metric on the questionnaire’s scale, and we also examined the distribution of total scores for normality. The measure has multiple items tapping positive affect, general life satisfaction, and psychological resources, and we treated the total score as an overall index of subjective wellbeing.

No complex battery of covariates was included in the survey because the study aimed to provide an initial correlational test of habitual caffeine use and global happiness. Nevertheless, basic demographic variables (age and gender) were collected and later included in sensitivity analyses. We deliberately limited the number of additional psychosocial measures to reduce participant burden and to prioritize completion rates for the primary variables of interest.

Procedurally, participants accessed the survey through a secure online platform. The study began with informed consent followed by demographic items, the caffeine habits questionnaire, and then the Oxford Happiness Questionnaire. The order of the caffeine items and the OHQ was kept consistent across participants to minimize variation due to order effects. The survey platform prevented multiple submissions from the same device to reduce the chance of duplicate responses, and attention-check items were included intermittently to identify disengaged responding. Responses failing attention checks or showing implausible patterns (for example, reporting 100 drinks per day) were excluded from the analytic sample.

Data preprocessing steps were specified prior to analysis. We inspected data for missingness, and participants who failed to complete either the caffeine items or the Oxford Happiness Questionnaire were excluded. For the included participants, any single missing item on the OHQ was imputed using mean substitution when less than 5% of items were missing; no participant required imputation for more than 2 items. Caffeine consumption variables were inspected for extreme outliers; values above 10 caffeinated beverages per day were examined case-by-case and were excluded if inconsistent with other responses or implausible. After these data cleaning steps, the final analytic set comprised 160 participants.

Statistical analysis proceeded in stages. First, descriptive statistics were computed for demographic variables, for the caffeine consumption indicators, and for OHQ scores. We examined means, standard deviations, ranges, and histograms to assess distributional properties. Second, the primary analysis tested the bivariate association between habitual caffeine consumption and happiness using Pearson correlation with two-tailed testing. For this primary correlation, the aggregate daily caffeinated beverage count was the predictor and the mean OHQ score was the outcome variable. Where distributions deviated substantially from normality, we planned to compute Spearman rank correlations as a robustness check, but the primary analytic decision was to use Pearson correlation given the metric nature of both variables.

Third, to assess whether any observed association was robust to simple demographic covariates, we estimated a linear regression model predicting OHQ score from daily caffeinated beverages, with age and gender entered as covariates. Gender was coded as a binary variable (female/male) for analytic simplicity given the sample composition and the small number of participants endorsing other gender identities; sensitivity analyses were also run excluding participants who did not identify as male or female to ensure results were not driven by small cell sizes. Age was treated as a continuous covariate.

Fourth, exploratory subgroup analyses were conducted. We examined correlations within participant subgroups defined by gender and by typical timing of consumption (morning-only consumers versus those who consumed caffeine later in the day). Additionally, we compared OHQ scores across categories of consumers (non/occasional consumers, moderate consumers [1–3 drinks per day], and high consumers [4 or more drinks per day]) using one-way analysis of variance to detect potential nonlinear patterns.

Fifth, post hoc power considerations were noted. Prior to data collection we aimed for a sample size that would provide reasonable sensitivity to detect small-to-moderate correlations. With N = 160, the study had approximately 80% power to detect correlations of roughly r = .22 at the alpha = .05 level in two-tailed tests, rendering the design adequately powered for medium-sized effects but underpowered to reliably detect very small associations.

All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Data and analysis scripts were archived internally and were prepared for potential sharing in aggregate, anonymized form, consistent with participant consent. Attention was paid to transparent reporting of effect sizes and confidence intervals in addition to p-values, and we reported both point estimates and measures of uncertainty.

Throughout the methods, ethical considerations were observed, including the protection of participant anonymity, the voluntary nature of participation, and minimal risk to participants. No experimental manipulations were involved, and the survey content was not expected to provoke distress. Participants were given contact information for the research team and reminded that they could withdraw at any time. The study adhered to institutional guidelines for research with human participants.

Results

Descriptive statistics provided a clear picture of the sample’s demographic composition and of the distribution of caffeine consumption and happiness scores. The final analytic sample included 160 participants with complete data, and their ages ranged from 18 to 64 years with a mean age of 28.7 years and a standard deviation of 9.6 years. The sample was 62% female and 38% male, with a small number of participants indicating another gender identity; those few were retained in descriptive analyses but were excluded from some subgroup analyses due to small cell sizes when appropriate. Employment status varied, with students, part-time and full-time workers represented, although the sample leaned toward students and younger adults due to the recruitment channels. These demographic details were used to contextualize findings and to consider generalizability.

Caffeine consumption patterns varied considerably across participants. The mean number of self-reported caffeinated beverages per typical day was 2.3 with a standard deviation of 1.6, a minimum of 0, and a maximum of 8 in the analytic sample after exclusion of implausible outliers. Approximately 12% of participants reported no regular caffeinated beverages, about 46% reported 1 to 2 drinks per day, 30% reported 3 to 4 drinks per day, and the remaining 12% reported 5 or more drinks per day. Morning-only consumption was reported by 54% of participants, whereas the remaining 46% reported consuming caffeine across the day, including afternoon or evening. Use of energy drinks or supplemental caffeine was relatively uncommon in this sample, reported by fewer than 10% of participants.

Oxford Happiness Questionnaire scores were broadly distributed and showed adequate variability for correlation analysis. Mean OHQ mean-item score was 4.12 with a standard deviation of 0.61, and scores ranged from 2.3 to 5.8 across participants. Inspection of the OHQ distribution via histogram and skewness statistics revealed a slight negative skew consistent with modest clustering at higher happiness levels in this community sample, but the distribution did not depart markedly from normality. Reliability analysis indicated acceptable internal consistency for the OHQ in this sample, with Cronbach’s alpha equal to 0.88, supporting its use as a composite index of subjective happiness.

The primary bivariate test examined the Pearson correlation between aggregate daily caffeinated beverages and OHQ mean score. The correlation coefficient was r = 0.07, indicating a very small positive association in the sample. This correlation was not statistically significant in a two-tailed test (p = 0.35), and the 95% confidence interval for the correlation ranged from approximately -0.08 to 0.22, indicating that if a true effect exists it is likely small. Given the a priori power considerations, the study had sufficient sensitivity to detect medium-sized correlations but was not well powered to detect effects smaller than about r = 0.22; the observed effect was well below that threshold.

Because the OHQ distribution exhibited a modest skew, we computed a Spearman rank correlation as a robustness check. The Spearman rho between daily caffeinated beverages and OHQ score was 0.06 with a p-value of 0.41, consistent with the Pearson result and further indicating a non-significant association. We also explored the possibility of a nonlinear relation by fitting a quadratic term in a regression predicting OHQ from caffeinated beverages and its square; the quadratic term was not significant (p = 0.62), and inclusion of the quadratic term did not meaningfully improve model fit, as judged by adjusted R-squared and AIC comparisons.

To assess whether demographic factors might conceal or confound an association between caffeine and happiness, we estimated a linear regression model predicting OHQ mean score from daily caffeinated beverages with age and gender entered as covariates. In this model, the coefficient for daily caffeinated beverages was β = 0.04 (standardized β ≈ 0.06) with a p-value of 0.30, indicating no significant association after accounting for age and gender. Age itself was negatively associated with OHQ score in this sample at a small magnitude (β = -0.01 per year, p = 0.04), suggesting slightly lower mean-item happiness with increasing age in the sample, although the effect size was small. Gender (female coded 1, male coded 0) was not a significant predictor in the model (p = 0.12). The model’s adjusted R-squared was low (≈ 0.02), reflecting that most variance in OHQ scores remained unexplained by these predictors.

We conducted subgroup analyses to explore heterogeneity in the caffeine–happiness relationship. Among female participants (n ≈ 99), the correlation between daily caffeinated beverages and OHQ was r = 0.09 (p = 0.36). Among male participants (n ≈ 61), the correlation was r = 0.02 (p = 0.87). Neither subgroup correlation reached significance, and the difference between correlations was not statistically significant. We also examined timing-of-consumption groups. For morning-only consumers (n ≈ 86), the correlation was r = 0.05 (p = 0.62), whereas for those consuming caffeine later in the day (n ≈ 74), the correlation was r = 0.10 (p = 0.38); again, no significant associations were observed, and these exploratory comparisons did not suggest a notable moderating effect of timing.

To probe the possibility of threshold effects, we compared OHQ scores across categorical consumer groups defined as non/occasional (0–0.5 drinks/day), moderate (1–3 drinks/day), and high (4+ drinks/day) using one-way ANOVA. The mean OHQ mean-item scores for the three groups were 4.05 (non/occasional), 4.13 (moderate), and 4.17 (high). The ANOVA did not reveal significant group differences (F(2,157) = 0.34, p = 0.71), indicating no evidence of differences in global happiness across these user categories in this sample. Post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment also failed to indicate significant differences.

Because withdrawal effects could conceivably relate to lower wellbeing among habitual users who missed their usual intake, we explored whether participants who reported missing their typical caffeine consumption on the previous day (an item included for exploratory reasons) had lower OHQ scores. Only a small subset of participants (n = 18) endorsed missing their typical intake recently, and while their mean OHQ scores were slightly lower (M = 3.98) compared to those who did not miss their intake (M = 4.13), this difference was not statistically significant (t(158) = 1.05, p = 0.30). Given the small sample size for this subgroup, any inferences are tentative.

We also examined associations between self-categorized “regular” versus “occasional” consumers and OHQ scores. Regular consumers (n ≈ 140) had mean OHQ scores of 4.13, whereas occasional consumers (n ≈ 20) had a mean of 4.05, and this difference was not statistically significant (t(158) = 0.55, p = 0.58). It is noteworthy that the sample included relatively few complete abstainers, limiting the power to detect differences between abstainers and regular consumers.

Finally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding participants who reported extremely late-night caffeine consumption (after 10 pm), given the potential for sleep disruption to influence wellbeing. Excluding these 12 participants did not materially alter the primary results; the Pearson correlation between daily caffeinated beverages and OHQ in the reduced sample was r = 0.08, p = 0.28. Across all analyses, effect sizes were small and consistently nonsignificant, suggesting that habitual caffeinated beverage count, as measured in this study, was not meaningfully associated with trait happiness as assessed by the OHQ in this sample.

Throughout the results, we emphasize transparency in reporting both effect sizes and uncertainty. The consistent pattern of small, non-significant associations across multiple analytic approaches indicates that if there is a relation between habitual caffeine consumption and global happiness, it is likely to be small and possibly contingent on factors not captured by our measures. The observed data provided no evidence for a positive or negative association of a magnitude that would be readily detectable in a sample of this size using the survey instruments administered.

Discussion

The present study tested the hypothesis that habitual caffeine consumption would be positively associated with trait-level happiness as measured by the Oxford Happiness Questionnaire in a community sample. Contrary to some anecdotal accounts and to certain prior reports suggesting benefits of caffeine on mood, our primary analyses found no significant relation between self-reported daily caffeinated beverage consumption and OHQ scores. The observed correlation was small and not statistically significant, and this null pattern persisted across robustness checks, regression adjustment for basic demographics, and subgroup analyses. These findings suggest that, at least in the present sample and given the measures used, habitual caffeine habits do not have a straightforward positive relation with trait happiness.

Interpreting null results requires caution and nuance, particularly in the context of a mixed literature with multiple mechanisms that could plausibly link caffeine and wellbeing. One straightforward interpretation is that caffeine’s acute mood-enhancing effects do not accumulate into sustained increases in trait happiness; that is, while caffeine may transiently boost alertness or affective tone, these transitory shifts may not translate into enduring changes in the global evaluations that measures like the OHQ capture. This interpretation aligns with a distinction commonly made in affective science between state and trait measures: substances that alter transient states do not necessarily change trait dispositions unless their use leads to lasting changes in behavior, social engagement, or health.

Another plausible interpretation pertains to heterogeneity among caffeine consumers. Individuals drink caffeine for many reasons—pleasure, habit, social context, cognitive performance, or as a coping mechanism for low energy or stress. If only some motives are associated with higher happiness, aggregated associations could wash out positive and negative effects. For instance, drinking caffeine as part of a pleasurable social ritual could correlate with higher wellbeing, whereas heavy caffeine use driven by stress or sleep deprivation could correlate with lower wellbeing. Our measure of aggregate beverage count did not capture motive or context robustly, and thus such nuanced patterns could be obscured. The subgroup analyses we conducted—by gender and by timing of consumption—did not reveal meaningful differences, but these groups were defined by limited survey items and may not correspond to the relevant psychological distinctions.

A related consideration is the role of expectancy and belief. Consumers who believe that caffeine improves their mood may experience placebo-like benefits that are contingent on context and belief, and such expectancy effects might vary across individuals. Because we did not measure expectancies or beliefs about caffeine’s effects, we cannot determine whether any subgroup of consumers derives well-being benefits via expectancy pathways. Future work could address this gap by measuring consumer motives and expectancies or by deploying experimental designs that manipulate beliefs.

Withdrawal dynamics and tolerance also complicate cross-sectional interpretation. Habitual consumers experience tolerance such that much of the stimulant’s subjective benefit returns to baseline over time, and withdrawal symptoms upon missed consumption can transiently lower mood. Depending on when a participant last consumed caffeine relative to survey administration, differing withdrawal states could influence OHQ responses. In our sample, a small number reported missing their typical intake recently, and while their mean OHQ scores were marginally lower, the difference was not significant. Nonetheless, without detailed assessment of recent intake timing and withdrawal symptoms, it is difficult to rule out the possibility that acute abstinence or withdrawal masked positive relations in some participants.

Sleep quality represents another pathway through which caffeine could indirectly affect wellbeing. Caffeine consumed late in the day can disrupt sleep onset and reduce sleep quality, and chronic poor sleep has robust negative associations with wellbeing. In our data, most participants consumed caffeine primarily in the morning, and excluding late-night consumers did not change results, but our measures of sleep were limited. Future investigations should assess sleep behavior and timing of consumption more precisely and test mediation models in which caffeine affects sleep which in turn affects wellbeing.

The absence of a strong association in the present study also raises questions about dosage and measurement precision. Summing self-reported servings of caffeinated beverages is a coarse measure; different beverages and serving sizes contain widely varying amounts of caffeine, and individual metabolic differences influence subjective effects for a given milligram dose. Measuring actual milligrams of caffeine consumed, controlling for beverage strength, and accounting for individual differences in sensitivity could provide finer-grained evidence. Moreover, longitudinal or experimental designs that manipulate consumption patterns would be needed to establish causality and to determine whether sustained intake influences trait happiness.

Methodological limitations of the present study may have contributed to the null findings. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and makes it impossible to determine directionality. The sample, while of modest size and diverse in age, was biased toward younger adults due to recruitment channels and included relatively few complete abstainers and few heavy consumers, limiting the ability to detect differences at the extremes of consumption. The caffeine habits questionnaire was brief and did not capture motive, beverage strength, or precise timing with sufficient granularity. Likewise, the OHQ, while an established measure, is a global indicator of happiness and may be insensitive to subtle changes associated with lifestyle factors. The reliance on self-report measures introduces potential reporting biases, and the survey format cannot capture physiological measures or objective behavioral outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the study contributes to the literature by providing a transparent, well-characterized null finding that challenges simplistic assumptions about the beneficial role of habitual caffeine use on trait happiness. The consistent pattern of small and non-significant associations across multiple analytic approaches suggests that any effect of habitual caffeine consumption on global happiness is likely to be small and may be conditional upon unmeasured moderator variables such as motive, context, sleep, or individual sensitivity. The study therefore highlights the need for more nuanced research that incorporates these factors.

Future research directions are numerous and would help elucidate the complex relations among caffeine use, acute affect, and trait wellbeing. First, longitudinal studies that track caffeine consumption patterns and wellbeing over time would help determine whether changes in usage precede changes in happiness or vice versa. Such designs would also allow for within-person analyses that control for stable individual differences. Second, experimental manipulations, including randomized controlled trials of caffeine reduction or supplementation, could address causality and help isolate mechanisms including withdrawal, expectancy, and physiological effects. Third, more detailed measurement of consumption—assessing milligrams of caffeine, beverage strength, timing, and motives—would enable more sensitive tests and allow for the examination of dose-response relationships. Fourth, integrating objective measures such as actigraphy to assess sleep and physiological markers (e.g., heart rate variability, cortisol) could illuminate pathways linking caffeine to wellbeing. Fifth, examining moderators such as trait anxiety, genetic markers of caffeine metabolism, and social context would clarify for whom and under what circumstances caffeine might influence wellbeing.

In addition to these empirical steps, theoretical work is needed to better conceptualize the distinctions between acute stimulant effects and enduring happiness. Researchers should consider frameworks that differentiate hedonic, eudaimonic, and social aspects of wellbeing and consider how routine substance use may differentially affect these dimensions. For example, caffeine could plausibly support hedonic experiences in the short term, facilitate goal-directed behavior that supports eudaimonic wellbeing, or be embedded in social rituals that bolster social wellbeing, and these distinct pathways should be empirically mapped.

Practically, the findings caution against assuming that habitual caffeine consumption is a reliable strategy for enhancing global happiness. While individuals may experience short-term benefits in alertness or mood, these do not necessarily translate into higher trait happiness as assessed by the OHQ. Public health messaging and individual decisions about caffeine use should therefore consider potential trade-offs, including sleep disturbance and dependence, and should not presume broad wellbeing benefits from habitual consumption alone.

In conclusion, this study found no significant evidence that habitual caffeinated beverage consumption, as measured by aggregate daily servings, is associated with trait happiness in a community sample. The results suggest that the relationship between caffeine and wellbeing is more complex than a simple positive association and likely depends on factors not captured by coarse measures of consumption. Future research employing longitudinal, experimental, and multifactorial approaches is needed to clarify whether caffeine can meaningfully influence sustained wellbeing and to identify the mechanisms and moderators that determine when and for whom caffeine might serve as a stress reliever or a contributor to happiness.
