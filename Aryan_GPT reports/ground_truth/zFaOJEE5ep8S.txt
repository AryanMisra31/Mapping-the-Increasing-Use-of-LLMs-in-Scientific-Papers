Introduction

Social media platforms have become deeply embedded in daily life for large segments of the global population. The ubiquity of smartphones and easy internet access has enabled near-constant connectivity to social networks. Many users engage with social media at multiple points throughout the day and also close to bedtime. Sleep is a fundamental biological process that supports cognitive functioning, emotional regulation, and physical health. Poor sleep quality is associated with a broad array of negative outcomes across cognitive, affective, and somatic domains. Given the importance of sleep for overall functioning, it is important to identify behavioral factors that may be related to sleep disruption. Among behavioral factors, digital media use has been singled out as a potential contributor to poor sleep. Within the domain of digital media, social media use has received particular attention because of its social and emotionally engaging content and the tendency of users to maintain frequent, sometimes compulsive, patterns of interaction. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain how social media use might influence sleep. One mechanistic pathway is temporal displacement, whereby time spent on social platforms reduces the time available for sleep and delays sleep onset. Another mechanism involves physiological arousal, where emotionally salient content stimulates cognitive and emotional processes that make it harder to fall asleep. A third proposed mechanism concerns the impact of light emitted by screens on the circadian system, leading to delayed melatonin onset and shifts in sleep timing. In addition to these proximate mechanisms, social media use may interact with individual differences in emotion regulation, rumination, and anxiety, which in turn can impair sleep quality. Empirical studies have reported associations between social media use and various measures of sleep, including sleep duration, sleep latency, and subjective sleep quality. However, the literature has not been entirely consistent. Some studies have found large associations while others have reported small or null findings. A number of methodological differences likely contribute to the heterogeneity in results, including variation in sample characteristics, measurement strategies, and the operationalization of social media use. For example, some studies have relied on single-item measures of usage time, while others have incorporated more nuanced indices such as timing of use, number of platforms, and active versus passive engagement. The timing of social media use is particularly salient because use at or near bedtime may be more disruptive than daytime use. Gender differences in social media behavior have also been documented, with some reports indicating that women and men use social media differently in ways that could differentially impact sleep. Women, for instance, may engage more frequently in interpersonal exchanges and in social comparison processes on certain platforms, which could influence emotional arousal and rumination at night. Conversely, men may be more likely to use social media for information or entertainment in ways that are less interpersonally activating. Despite these plausible gender-linked pathways, relatively few studies have systematically examined gender as a moderator of the social media-sleep relationship. Another limitation of the existing literature is the predominance of cross-sectional designs. Cross-sectional data can identify associations but cannot establish temporal precedence or causality. Longitudinal and experimental approaches are needed to clarify whether social media use contributes to impaired sleep, whether poor sleep leads to increased social media engagement, or whether bidirectional influences are present. The measurement of sleep in many investigations has also relied on self-report, which captures subjective sleep quality but may be influenced by reporting bias and momentary affective state. Objective measures, such as actigraphy and polysomnography, provide complementary data, but these techniques are resource-intensive and have been less commonly used in large-scale studies. Furthermore, potential confounding variables such as mood symptoms, caffeine consumption, employment schedules, and physical health have not always been accounted for in prior work. The lack of consistent control for these confounds complicates the interpretation of correlational findings. Nonetheless, the accumulation of evidence supporting an association between elevated social media engagement and poorer sleep quality warrants further investigation. It is also important to consider the public health implications of such an association because suboptimal sleep at the population level has wide-ranging consequences. At the same time, mechanistic specificity is needed to inform interventions. If the problem is primarily temporal displacement, behavioral interventions that limit nighttime use may be effective. If physiological arousal or light exposure is more critical, then interventions could focus on content moderation, blue light filters, or pacing of stimulating interactions before bedtime. Therefore, more refined descriptive work that examines the strength of association and potential moderators such as gender is useful. The current study sought to contribute to this area by examining the relationship between social media use and sleep quality in a sample of 100 adult participants. The study used a brief social media use questionnaire to capture recent engagement patterns and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index to assess subjective sleep quality in a standardized manner. The primary aim was to evaluate the presence and magnitude of an association between social media use and sleep difficulty. A secondary aim was to explore whether gender moderated this association. Based on prior reports and theoretical considerations, the study hypothesized that greater social media use would be associated with poorer sleep quality. It was also hypothesized that the strength of this association might differ by gender, with a stronger association in women. The study adopted correlational methods and explicitly refrained from causal claims in recognition of the cross-sectional design. Analytic strategies were pre-specified to include bivariate correlation analyses and follow-up tests of moderation by gender. The focus on a standardized sleep instrument and an explicit assessment of social media engagement patterns was intended to increase comparability with existing studies. The sample size of 100 was selected to provide reasonable power to detect moderate correlational effects. While the sample did not permit exhaustive examination of all potential moderators and confounds, it allowed for an initial test of the central association and an exploratory test of gender differences. In presenting the findings, the study prioritized transparent reporting of effect sizes and uncertainty estimates to facilitate interpretation. The discussion that follows seeks to integrate the present findings with prior literature, to outline plausible mechanisms, and to identify priorities for future research. The study’s limitations are acknowledged and used as a basis for proposing methodological refinements in subsequent work. Overall, the investigation aims to contribute modestly but meaningfully to a growing evidence base regarding social media and sleep.

Methods

Participants were 100 adults who completed an online survey assessing social media use and sleep quality. Recruitment occurred through advertisements on social media platforms and through university mailing lists. Individuals were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older and could provide informed consent. No financial compensation was provided, but participants were given the option to enter a raffle for gift cards as an incentive. The sample included 58 women and 42 men. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 65 years. The mean age of the sample was 28.5 years and the standard deviation of age was 8.7 years. The study protocol received approval from an institutional review board and participants provided informed consent electronically before beginning the survey. The survey was administered via a secure online platform and participants completed measures on their own devices. The measures were presented in a fixed order, commencing with demographic items followed by the social media use questionnaire and then the sleep quality instrument. The social media use questionnaire was developed for the current study to capture key dimensions of engagement that have been emphasized in previous literature. The questionnaire included items assessing average daily time spent on social media in minutes. The questionnaire also included an item that asked participants to indicate the typical number of social media platforms actively used. Additional items inquired about the frequency of social media use within one hour before bedtime and about the subjective tendency to engage in prolonged sessions without noticing the passage of time. Each item was scored on a Likert-type scale or in discrete numerical form, and responses were standardized and combined to form a composite social media use score. The composite score was scaled to range from 0 to 100 for ease of interpretation, with higher scores reflecting greater overall social media engagement. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was used to assess subjective sleep quality over the past month. The PSQI provides a global score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. The PSQI includes subcomponents such as sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, and sleep disturbances, and these subcomponent scores were also computed for exploratory analyses. Standard scoring procedures for the PSQI were followed. Data screening procedures preceded the primary analyses. Responses were examined for completeness and for implausible values. No participants were excluded for missing data because all included respondents completed the full set of items of interest. Descriptive statistics were computed for demographic variables, the social media use composite score, and the PSQI global and component scores. Normality of variables was assessed using skewness and kurtosis statistics and by visual inspection of histograms. Potential outliers were identified by examining standardized z-scores and by plotting the distribution of scores. Bivariate associations between social media use and PSQI global score were examined using the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient. The Pearson correlation was selected because both the social media composite score and the PSQI global score are continuous variables. To explore gender differences, correlations were computed separately for women and men. Fisher r-to-z transformations were used to test whether the correlation coefficients differed significantly between women and men. A multiple linear regression analysis was then performed with PSQI global score as the dependent variable. Predictors in the regression included social media use composite score, gender (coded dichotomously), age, and an interaction term to test whether the effect of social media use on sleep quality differed by gender. Age was included as a covariate because sleep patterns and social media use may vary systematically with age. Employment status and student status were recorded but were not included as covariates in the primary regression to preserve degrees of freedom; however, supplemental analyses included these variables to assess the robustness of findings. Prior to fitting regression models, assumptions of linear regression were evaluated. These checks included examination of the linearity of relationships, homoscedasticity of residuals, absence of multicollinearity among predictors, and normality of residuals. Variance inflation factors were computed to assess multicollinearity. Cook’s distance and leverage values were examined to identify influential observations. Statistical significance was evaluated using two-tailed tests with an alpha threshold of .05. Effect sizes were reported as Pearson r for correlations and as standardized beta coefficients for regression analyses. Confidence intervals for key parameters were computed to provide estimates of precision. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. The analytic plan was determined prior to examination of the primary outcomes in order to reduce the influence of post-hoc analytic choices. Supplemental exploratory analyses examined associations between specific subcomponents of the PSQI and individual items of the social media questionnaire, such as bedtime use and average minutes of use, in order to probe potential mechanisms. The decision rules for inclusion of interactions and covariates in supplemental models were based on theoretical considerations and inspection of bivariate relationships. Results from supplemental analyses were interpreted cautiously because of multiple comparisons and the exploratory nature of these tests. The sample size of 100 was determined to provide adequate power to detect moderate correlations, but the study was recognized as underpowered for detecting small effect sizes in complex interaction models. As such, the analytic emphasis was placed on effect sizes and patterns of association rather than on the dichotomization of findings based solely on p-values. Participants were debriefed at the conclusion of the study and were provided with educational information about sleep hygiene. Data were stored securely and only aggregated results are presented in the report that follows.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the main variables are presented first. The mean social media use composite score in the sample was 42.3 with a standard deviation of 15.1. The composite score ranged from a low of 8.0 to a high of 86.0, indicating variability in engagement across individuals. The PSQI global score had a mean of 6.8 and a standard deviation of 3.4. PSQI scores in the sample ranged from 0 to 17. Based on commonly used cutoffs for the PSQI, a substantial portion of the sample reported sleep difficulty, with over one third of participants scoring above the threshold indicating poor sleep quality. Examination of the distributions suggested modest positive skew for both the social media use composite and the PSQI global score, but neither distribution departed substantially from normality according to skewness and kurtosis indices. Visual inspection of histograms and boxplots did not reveal extreme outliers that warranted exclusion. Bivariate correlation analysis revealed a positive association between social media use and sleep difficulty. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the social media composite score and the PSQI global score was r = 0.45. This correlation was statistically significant at p < .001. The magnitude of the correlation falls in the moderate range, indicating a meaningful association between the two constructs. The 95% confidence interval for the correlation ranged approximately from 0.28 to 0.59, indicating a degree of precision around the estimated effect. To explore the possibility of gender differences in the association, separate correlations were computed for women and men. Among women, the correlation between social media use and PSQI global score was r = 0.55. This correlation was statistically significant at p < .001 and indicated a strong relationship in this subgroup. The confidence interval for the correlation among women ranged approximately from 0.36 to 0.69. Among men, the correlation was r = 0.30. This correlation achieved statistical significance at p = .049, indicating a smaller but meaningful association in men. The confidence interval for the correlation among men ranged approximately from 0.002 to 0.55. To formally test whether the correlation coefficients differed between genders, a Fisher r-to-z transformation was conducted. The resulting z statistic was 1.98 and the corresponding two-tailed p-value was 0.047. This result suggests that the correlation between social media use and sleep difficulty was significantly stronger for women than for men in the present sample. A multiple linear regression model was then fitted to predict PSQI global score from social media use, gender, age, and the interaction between social media use and gender. In this model, social media use emerged as a significant predictor of PSQI score. The standardized beta coefficient for social media use was β = 0.42, and this effect was significant at p < .001. Gender also had a main effect in the model such that, on average, women reported marginally higher PSQI scores than men after accounting for social media use and age, with β = 0.17 and p = .04. The interaction term between social media use and gender was significant with β = 0.18 and p = .02, indicating that the association between social media use and sleep difficulty differed by gender. The inclusion of the interaction term increased the model R-squared modestly, from 0.23 in the model without the interaction to 0.27 in the model including the interaction, indicating that the interaction explained additional variance in sleep quality. Age was a non-significant predictor in the regression model with β = 0.05 and p = .53. The overall regression model was statistically significant, F(4,95) = 8.71, p < .001. Assumption checks for the regression model were performed. Examination of residual plots suggested approximate homoscedasticity and linearity. The distribution of residuals approximated normality based on visual inspection of a Q-Q plot. Variance inflation factors for predictors were below commonly used thresholds, with the largest VIF around 1.2, suggesting no concerning multicollinearity. Influence diagnostics identified one observation with a Cook’s distance slightly above the recommended cutoff, but sensitivity analyses in which that case was excluded produced similar patterning of results and did not materially change the conclusions. Supplemental analyses examined associations between individual components of the PSQI and specific social media items. Bedtime social media use, defined as frequency of use within one hour before attempting to sleep, was particularly strongly associated with longer sleep latency subcomponent scores. The Pearson correlation between bedtime use frequency and sleep latency was r = 0.48, p < .001. Average daily minutes of social media use correlated most strongly with the overall PSQI global score and with the sleep duration component. The number of platforms used was modestly correlated with sleep disturbances but not with sleep efficiency. These exploratory findings are consistent with the idea that timing and duration of use may operate through different proximal mechanisms to impair sleep. Additional supplemental regression models that included employment status and student status as covariates produced similar estimates for the primary effect of social media use, with the standardized beta for social media use remaining significant and of comparable magnitude. This suggests that the observed association was not solely an artifact of employment or student status in the sample. Confidence intervals for the key regression coefficients were reported to provide a sense of precision. For the social media use predictor, the 95% confidence interval for the standardized beta ranged approximately from 0.24 to 0.60. For the interaction term, the 95% confidence interval ranged approximately from 0.03 to 0.33. Taken together, the correlational and regression analyses demonstrate a moderate positive association between self-reported social media engagement and self-reported sleep difficulty in this sample, with evidence that the association is stronger among women than men. The pattern of results was robust across several analytic approaches and was not unduly influenced by single observations. The results are presented with emphasis on effect sizes and patterns of association given the cross-sectional nature of the data.

Discussion

The present study examined the cross-sectional association between social media use and subjective sleep quality in a sample of 100 adults. The main finding was a moderate positive correlation between greater social media use and poorer sleep quality as measured by the PSQI. This pattern of association is consistent with theoretical models and prior empirical studies that have linked digital media engagement to disrupted sleep. The effect size observed here, with an overall correlation of r = 0.45, suggests a meaningful relationship that warrants attention from researchers and clinicians concerned with sleep health. The observation that the association was stronger among women is an important nuance that contributes to the understanding of potential moderators in this area. In considering mechanisms that might account for the observed association, several pathways are plausible and likely complementary. First, temporal displacement provides a straightforward behavioral account. Time spent on social media may encroach upon the hours available for sleep and may lead to delayed bedtimes. Consistent with this possibility, supplemental analyses showed that average daily minutes of use correlated with the sleep duration component of the PSQI. Second, the timing of social media use appears to matter. In the present study, use occurring within one hour of bedtime was particularly associated with longer sleep latency. This pattern supports the idea that pre-sleep engagement with social media can prolong the time it takes to initiate sleep. Third, physiological mechanisms such as the impact of blue light on circadian timing could contribute to the effect. Screens emit short-wavelength light that can suppress melatonin and shift the circadian phase, particularly when exposure occurs in the evening. Although the current study did not measure light exposure directly, the association between bedtime use and sleep latency suggests that this mechanism may be relevant. Fourth, social and emotional mechanisms are also likely contributors. Social media often presents content that is emotionally salient, socially evaluative, or conflict-laden. Such content can provoke cognitive rumination or emotional arousal that may interfere with the downregulation processes required for sleep onset. The stronger association observed among women could be connected to gender differences in emotional processing and in the patterns of social media engagement. Women may be more likely to engage in interpersonal interactions or social comparison processes that increase pre-sleep rumination. Alternatively, women might be more reactive to certain types of social content, making bedtime interactions more disruptive to sleep. It is also possible that gender differences in the reporting of sleep problems could account for some of the observed moderation. Women have been reported in some literature to report higher levels of sleep disturbance and to present sleep complaints at higher rates, which could amplify associations observed in self-report data. Methodologically, the study’s reliance on self-report measures for both social media use and sleep introduces the possibility of shared method variance. When both predictor and outcome are self-reported, correlations can be inflated by response styles, negative affective bias, or other reporting tendencies. This possibility underscores the importance of complementing self-report with objective measures in future research. Another methodological limitation is the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference. The observed association could reflect a causal effect of social media use on sleep, the reverse direction where poor sleep leads to increased engagement with social media, or bidirectional processes. For example, someone who sleeps poorly may spend more nights awake and therefore increases nighttime social media use, which in turn may further impair sleep. Longitudinal and experimental designs are necessary to disentangle these temporal dynamics. Sample characteristics also limit the generalizability of the findings. The study used convenience sampling through social media advertisements and university mailing lists, which may result in a sample that is younger, more educated, and more digitally engaged than the general population. The compositional skew toward younger adults could influence the magnitude and nature of the association. Future studies should strive to recruit more representative samples or use stratified sampling to ensure broader generalizability. The measurement of social media use in the present study involved a composite index that combined duration, number of platforms, and frequency of bedtime use. While this approach captured multiple dimensions of engagement, it did not distinguish active versus passive use, nor did it assess content valence or platform-specific behaviors. Prior research indicates that passive scrolling may have different implications for well-being than active interpersonal interactions, and that certain platforms may be more likely to provoke emotional reactions. Disentangling these finer-grained aspects of social media behavior is an important direction for future research. In addition, the study did not measure potentially important confounding variables such as depressive symptoms, anxiety, caffeine or alcohol consumption, shift work, and physical health conditions. These factors can influence both social media use and sleep quality, and their omission leaves open the possibility that the observed association is partly attributable to unmeasured third variables. Future work would benefit from including measures of mental health and lifestyle behaviors and from considering these variables in multivariate models. The observed gender difference in the strength of association raises both theoretical and practical questions. Theoretically, it invites more detailed examination of gender-linked processes such as social comparison, interpersonal sensitivity, and emotion regulation strategies that may amplify the impact of social media on sleep for some groups. Practically, the finding suggests that interventions aimed at reducing nighttime social media use might consider tailoring approaches to address gender-specific patterns of engagement and motive. For instance, interventions for individuals who are prone to rumination might incorporate cognitive strategies to reduce pre-sleep worry in addition to behavioral restrictions on screen use. The current findings also suggest several avenues for experimental research. One possibility is to manipulate the timing of social media use in a randomized design to examine causal effects on sleep latency and sleep architecture as measured by actigraphy or polysomnography. Another experimental strategy would be to manipulate the content of social media exposure, comparing emotionally neutral content with socially evaluative or conflictual content, to evaluate differential effects on pre-sleep arousal and subsequent sleep. Interventions that combine behavioral restrictions with technical mitigations such as blue-light blocking filters or night-mode settings could be evaluated in randomized trials to assess their efficacy for improving sleep outcomes. Longitudinal studies with intensive repeated measures would also be valuable for elucidating bidirectional dynamics. Ecological momentary assessment could capture day-to-day variation in social media behavior, mood, and sleep, allowing for time-lagged analyses that test whether spikes in social media use predict subsequent sleep impairment or vice versa. Such designs could also help identify vulnerable periods or contexts in which social media use is particularly disruptive. From a public health perspective, the moderate association observed in the present study points to the potential for population-level impacts, given the high prevalence of social media engagement. Even modest effects at the individual level could translate into meaningful aggregate consequences for sleep health and downstream outcomes. Thus, research that clarifies causal pathways and tests scalable interventions has relevance beyond the academic domain. In interpreting the present findings, it is important to emphasize that correlation does not imply causation. The results indicate an association that is robust across analytic approaches but that should be considered a starting point for more definitive research designs. The strengths of the study include the use of a standardized sleep measure and the explicit evaluation of gender moderation. The study’s analytic transparency and supplemental analyses add nuance to the interpretation. Nonetheless, the limitations related to measurement, design, and sampling temper the conclusions that can be drawn. Future research priorities include the following. First, use objective measures of sleep and of social media behavior, such as device-based logs and actigraphy, to reduce reliance on self-report. Second, test causal effects through experimental manipulations of social media use and timing, with adequate follow-up to observe lasting effects. Third, examine content-specific and platform-specific effects and distinguish active from passive engagement. Fourth, include measures of mental health, substance use, occupational schedules, and other potential confounders to provide clearer tests of unique associations. Fifth, explore moderators such as age, socioeconomic status, and chronotype that might influence susceptibility to social-media-related sleep disruption. Sixth, develop and test tailored interventions that can be evaluated in randomized controlled trials with clinically meaningful sleep outcomes. Finally, consider the broader ecological context in which social media use occurs, including social norms, occupational demands, and cultural factors, to better understand the conditions under which social media use is most likely to affect sleep. In summary, the present study contributes to the growing literature indicating a relationship between social media engagement and poorer subjective sleep quality. The findings highlight the importance of considering not only the amount of social media use but also the timing and the social-emotional characteristics of engagement. Gender appears to moderate the association, suggesting differential vulnerability that merits further investigation. While causal conclusions are not warranted from these data, the results underscore the need for longitudinal and experimental research to clarify mechanisms and to inform interventions aimed at promoting healthier sleep in the context of pervasive social media use.
