Introduction

Attachment theory has provided a rich framework for understanding how early relational experiences shape interpersonal functioning across the lifespan. The general premise of attachment theory is that repeated interactions with primary caregivers lead to the internalization of relationship schemas that guide expectations, emotional regulation, and behaviour in adult relationships. Secure attachment is described as a pattern in which individuals comfortably seek support and disclose emotions, whereas insecure styles such as anxious and avoidant attachments are characterized by contrasting strategies for managing threat and intimacy. Anxious attachment is typically marked by hyperactivation of attachment systems, elevated fears of abandonment, and a heightened sensitivity to relationship cues. Avoidant attachment is typically marked by deactivation strategies, discomfort with closeness, emotional suppression, and a greater emphasis on autonomy. These attachment-derived strategies have been linked to a wide range of outcomes, including relationship satisfaction, conflict management, stress reactivity, and broader personality traits. Beyond interpersonal functioning, attachment orientations can plausibly shape how individuals respond to social influence and cues in group contexts. Social conformity is one of the classic manifestations of social influence, and it refers to the tendency to align one’s attitudes, beliefs, or behaviours with those of a majority or an influential other. Conformity can be driven by informational motives, where individuals accept information from others as valid evidence about reality, or normative motives, where individuals conform to gain social acceptance or avoid exclusion. The processes that underlie conformity are multifaceted, involving cognitive assessments of others’ knowledge, affective concerns about belonging, and strategic adjustments to social standing. Given the overlap in mechanisms—particularly the emphasis on perceived social acceptance and sensitivity to relational cues—it is theoretically plausible that individual differences in attachment orientation could be associated with variation in susceptibility to social conformity. Attachment anxious individuals, with their heightened concern about rejection and strong motivation to preserve relationships, may be particularly likely to conform when social acceptance is perceived to be at stake. Their hypervigilance toward relational cues and tendency to seek reassurance could lead them to adopt majority views or behaviours that they believe will secure social inclusion. By contrast, avoidant individuals, who devalue closeness and emphasize independence, may be less susceptible to normative pressures and more resistant to conformist cues, especially when conformity is perceived as compromising autonomy or authenticity. These opposing predictions generate a clear theoretical contrast that invites empirical investigation. A small body of work has begun to examine links between attachment orientation and conformity, but the literature remains fragmented. Some studies suggest that attachment anxiety predicts greater susceptibility to normative influence, particularly in contexts where social approval is salient. Other work has found little or no relationship, or context-dependent effects that hinge on whether conformity confers informational utility or social reward. Similarly, the relation between avoidant attachment and conformity is not consistently documented; some studies indicate reduced conformity among avoidant individuals, while others find no reliable association. Methodological heterogeneity across these studies likely contributes to inconsistent findings. Different studies have used diverse measures of conformity, ranging from behavioural laboratory paradigms to self-report inventories, and these measures capture distinct aspects of social influence. Likewise, attachment has been assessed with variations in scales and scoring approaches that emphasize anxiety, avoidance, or categorical classifications. Sample characteristics such as age, gender composition, cultural background, and relationship status also vary widely and can moderate the observed relationships. Gender, in particular, is a plausible moderator because of documented sex differences in relational orientation and socialisation. Females are often socialized to prioritize relational harmony and interpersonal sensitivity, which could amplify links between attachment anxiety and conformity for women. Males may manifest anxious attachment in ways that are less overtly linked to conformity, or other social norms may reduce the expression of conformity tendencies even in the presence of relational concerns. These gendered socialization patterns suggest that any association between attachment orientation and conformity could be conditional on gender, and failure to test for moderation may mask important nuances. In addition to gender, relationship status could moderate the association because being in a committed relationship may activate attachment-related concerns differentially and influence the incentive structure for conformity. For example, individuals currently in a relationship might be more motivated to maintain harmony and thus more susceptible to conformity under certain conditions than single individuals. Age and life-stage factors may also contribute because older adults may exhibit different patterns of conformity and attachment expression than younger adults. Given these complexities, a study that systematically examines relationships between attachment orientations, measured continuously, and a self-report measure of social conformity, while also testing potential moderators such as gender and relationship status, would make a valuable contribution. The present study adopts such an approach. Participants completed validated self-report instruments indexing attachment anxiety and avoidance, as well as a scale assessing general tendencies toward social conformity. Data were analysed to test the primary hypothesis that higher levels of attachment anxiety would be associated with greater self-reported conformity, whereas attachment avoidance would either be unrelated or negatively related to conformity. A secondary aim was to evaluate whether these associations differed by gender and to explore the potential influence of relationship status and age. To enhance measurement credibility, scale reliability was assessed and internal consistency reported for each instrument. Statistical analyses included bivariate correlations, multiple regression models, and formal tests of moderation using interaction terms. Recognizing the possibility that associations might be modest and context-dependent, the study also examined whether controlling for demographic covariates altered the magnitude or significance of primary effects. The study design was correlational and cross-sectional, which allows for the identification of associations but does not permit causal inference. Nonetheless, correlational evidence can provide an important first step in identifying systematic links and informing subsequent experimental or longitudinal work. Because prior research has reported mixed findings and sometimes detected gender differences, the study was also intent on examining whether the predicted relations are present across both sexes or whether they are primarily driven by one gender. Sampling constraints commonly encountered in social science research can influence the ability to detect gender-specific effects, so careful attention was paid to reporting gender distributions and discussing implications of imbalance for interpretation. The study further intended to be transparent about limitations, including sample representativeness, measurement constraints inherent to self-report data, and the cross-sectional nature of the design. Despite these limitations, demonstrating a reliable association between attachment anxiety and conformity within a given sample would be theoretically meaningful because it would connect attachment theory, typically applied to dyadic contexts, to broader social influence processes. Such findings would have implications for understanding the interpersonal mechanisms that translate internal working models into group-level social behaviour. They would also suggest potential pathways by which attachment insecurity may shape social decision-making, with downstream consequences for social integration, susceptibility to misinformation, and susceptibility to peer influence. Conversely, a null relationship between avoidant attachment and conformity would corroborate predictions that deactivating strategies buffer individuals against normative social pressures. If gender differences emerge, they could point to the importance of socialization and gendered expectations in the expression of attachment-related tendencies outside dyadic romantic contexts. The study therefore aimed to document not only whether attachment orientations relate to conformity but also the boundary conditions and potential moderators of any observed relations. In summary, the present investigation explored the associations between attachment anxiety and avoidance and a self-report measure of social conformity in a sample of adult participants. The primary hypotheses were that attachment anxiety would be positively associated with conformity and that attachment avoidance would be negatively associated or unrelated to conformity. Secondary analyses tested whether these relationships varied by gender, relationship status, and age, and sensitivity analyses tested the robustness of effects to the inclusion of demographic covariates. The study sought to provide clear, well-documented evidence about the presence and nature of links between attachment orientation and social conformity, thereby contributing to a more integrated understanding of how individual differences in attachment shape broader social comportment.

Method

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and local community advertisements, and a total of 180 adults provided complete data for the primary analyses. Of these 180 participants, 140 self-identified as female and 40 self-identified as male, producing a gender-imbalanced sample that is described and examined in the Results and Discussion sections. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 62 years, with a mean age of 23.8 years and a standard deviation of 6.1 years. Relationship status was assessed categorically and included options for single, casually dating, in a committed relationship, and married; for analysis purposes relationship status was dichotomized into currently in a committed relationship (including married) versus not in a committed relationship. All participants completed the study online via a secure survey platform after providing informed consent. The study procedure was approved by an institutional review board, and participants received either course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. Attachment orientations were assessed using a widely used self-report instrument that yields continuous scores on two dimensions: attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance. The questionnaire asked participants to rate the extent to which statements describing feelings and behaviours in close relationships applied to them, using a Likert-type response format. Items for the anxiety scale indexed concerns over abandonment, need for reassurance, and preoccupation with relationships; items for the avoidance scale indexed discomfort with closeness, reluctance to depend on others, and emphasis on autonomy. Scale items were summed or averaged according to standard procedures to produce continuous anxiety and avoidance scores for each participant. Internal consistency for each scale was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding alpha = .89 for the anxiety scale and alpha = .86 for the avoidance scale in the present sample. Social conformity was measured with a self-report conformity scale designed to capture general tendencies to adopt the attitudes, opinions, or behaviours of others in social situations. The conformity scale included items that tapped into both normative and informational motives for conformity and asked participants to indicate how often they behaved in ways consistent with conforming to others. The response format was Likert-type, and items were combined to form a total conformity score for each participant. The conformity scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in this sample, with Cronbach’s alpha = .82. Demographic information collected included age, gender, education level, and relationship status. Participants also reported the length of their current relationship if applicable, but relationship length was not a primary focus of the present analyses and was used only in exploratory follow-up checks. The online survey included validity checks such as attention items and response pattern screening to reduce the likelihood of careless responding; participants who failed these checks were excluded before analysis. Data were screened for missing values, outliers, and violations of normality. No participant had more than 5% missing data on scale items, and missing responses were handled using mean imputation at the scale level if only a single item was missing. A small number of extreme outliers on the conformity scale were evaluated and winsorized to reduce undue influence; however, sensitivity analyses confirmed that the pattern of results was not dependent on winsorization. The primary analytic strategy began with descriptive statistics to characterize sample distributions for attachment anxiety, attachment avoidance, and conformity. Bivariate Pearson correlations were computed to test the predicted associations between attachment dimensions and conformity across the full sample and separately within gender groups. Given the a priori interest in gender differences, separate correlations for females and males were emphasized, and Fisher r-to-z transformations were used to formally compare correlation coefficients between genders. Multiple linear regression models were then estimated to evaluate the unique contributions of attachment anxiety and avoidance to conformity scores while controlling for age and relationship status. Interaction terms between gender and attachment dimensions were included in regression models to test whether gender moderated the associations. Regression diagnostics were examined to ensure that assumptions of linearity, homoscedasticity, and absence of multicollinearity were tenable. Variance inflation factors were computed and found to be below conventional thresholds, indicating no problematic multicollinearity among predictors. In addition to the main analyses, sensitivity analyses were conducted. These included repeating key analyses using nonparametric Spearman correlations to ensure robustness to potential deviations from normality and estimating regression models excluding participants in the smallest demographic categories to test the influence of sparse cells. Given the gender imbalance, power analyses were conducted post hoc to estimate the minimum detectable effect sizes for males and females separately, and these are reported in the Results section. Effect sizes for correlations were interpreted using standard benchmarks, and p-values were reported with two-tailed tests. Given the exploratory nature of some secondary analyses, findings that were marginally significant were interpreted cautiously and placed in the context of statistical power. All analyses were carried out using standard statistical software, and data management followed transparent procedures that would allow for reproducibility. No participants reported severe psychological distress as assessed by screening items, and none were excluded for clinical reasons. The study adhered to ethical standards for research with human participants, including confidentiality and voluntary participation.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. Across the full sample of 180 participants, attachment anxiety scores had a mean of 3.21 and a standard deviation of 0.92 on the 1–5 scale used. Attachment avoidance scores had a mean of 2.87 and a standard deviation of 0.81. The conformity scale had a mean of 3.05 and a standard deviation of 0.69. Examining gender subgroups, female participants (n = 140) had mean anxiety = 3.31 (SD = 0.88), mean avoidance = 2.80 (SD = 0.78), and mean conformity = 3.12 (SD = 0.66). Male participants (n = 40) had mean anxiety = 2.85 (SD = 0.96), mean avoidance = 3.10 (SD = 0.88), and mean conformity = 2.74 (SD = 0.70). Independent-samples t-tests indicated that females scored significantly higher than males on attachment anxiety, t(178) = 3.32, p = .001, whereas males scored marginally higher on attachment avoidance, t(178) = -1.99, p = .048. Females also scored higher on conformity than males, t(178) = 2.82, p = .005. Internal consistency values, reported in the Methods, suggested that the scales were reliable in the sample. Initial bivariate correlations in the full sample revealed a small but reliable positive association between attachment anxiety and conformity, r(178) = .22, p = .003. By contrast, attachment avoidance showed no meaningful correlation with conformity in the full sample, r(178) = -.04, p = .61. Because of theoretical interest in gender differences, correlations were computed separately for females and males. Among female participants, attachment anxiety was moderately positively correlated with conformity, r(138) = .34, p < .001. The association between attachment avoidance and conformity for females was small and non-significant, r(138) = .09, p = .29. Among male participants, attachment anxiety was not significantly correlated with conformity, r(38) = .06, p = .67, and attachment avoidance showed no correlation with conformity, r(38) = -.02, p = .88. These gender-stratified correlations thus indicated a clear pattern in which anxious attachment tracked conformity for females but not for males. To formally test whether the anxiety–conformity correlation differed by gender, Fisher r-to-z transformations were computed comparing the female and male correlation coefficients. The Fisher z for the comparison was z = 2.09, p = .036, indicating that the correlation between attachment anxiety and conformity was significantly larger for females than for males. Given the unequal gender groups, a caveat is that the male correlation estimate is less precisely estimated, and a post hoc power analysis indicated that the male sample (n = 40) had only 62% power to detect a correlation of r = .30 at alpha = .05, two-tailed, whereas the female sample (n = 140) had greater than 99% power to detect the same effect size. Multiple regression models were then estimated to evaluate whether attachment anxiety uniquely predicted conformity after accounting for attachment avoidance, age, and relationship status, and whether gender moderated this effect. In a first model predicting conformity with anxiety and avoidance as predictors, anxiety emerged as a significant positive predictor, beta = .25, t = 3.34, p = .001, whereas avoidance did not, beta = -.03, t = -0.41, p = .68. The model explained R² = .07 of variance in conformity scores. Adding age and relationship status as covariates did not substantially alter the pattern; anxiety remained a significant predictor, beta = .22, t = 2.95, p = .003, while avoidance remained non-significant, beta = -.04, t = -0.55, p = .58. In a subsequent model that included gender and the interaction term anxiety × gender, the main effect of anxiety remained significant, and the interaction between anxiety and gender was significant, b = .27, SE = .10, t = 2.55, p = .012. This interaction indicated that the positive association between anxiety and conformity was stronger for females than for males. Simple slopes analyses indicated that the slope for anxiety predicting conformity was significant for females, b = .30, p < .001, and non-significant for males, b = .03, p = .68. The full model including the interaction explained R² = .12 of the variance in conformity. Regression diagnostics showed acceptable residual distributions and no evidence of multicollinearity, with variance inflation factors below 1.5 for all predictors. Sensitivity analyses used nonparametric Spearman correlations and produced comparable results: Spearman rho for anxiety and conformity among females was .31, p < .001, and among males was .05, p = .73. Excluding participants who were not in a committed relationship did not substantially change the female results; among females in committed relationships the correlation between anxiety and conformity was r = .36, p < .001. Exploratory analyses considered whether the relation between anxiety and conformity could be accounted for by differences in the two subcomponents of conformity (normative versus informational motives) included in the scale. Item-level analyses suggested that the association with anxiety was stronger for items tapping normative motives than for items tapping informational motives, with a within-female correlation of r = .29 for normative items versus r = .15 for informational items; the former was significant at p < .001 and the latter was marginal at p = .07. Among males, neither subcomponent correlated meaningfully with anxiety. These subcomponent findings are consistent with the hypothesis that anxious attachment may be particularly linked to conformity driven by concerns about social acceptance. Additional exploratory checks examined whether the association between anxiety and conformity differed across age, and no significant age by anxiety interaction was detected, p = .48. When relationship length among those in a relationship was included as a covariate, it did not attenuate the female anxiety–conformity association. Finally, because females scored higher on both anxiety and conformity on average, a mediation-like concern was considered whereby gender differences in mean anxiety might account for mean conformity differences; however, when anxiety was included in a model predicting gender differences in conformity, the direct effect of gender on conformity became non-significant, suggesting that individual differences in anxiety at least partially account for observed gender differences in conformity in the present sample. Overall, the pattern of results provides convergent evidence that attachment anxiety is positively associated with self-reported social conformity among female participants in this sample, whereas no reliable relationships were observed for males or for attachment avoidance.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether adult attachment orientations are associated with general tendencies toward social conformity, and whether such associations vary by gender. The results provide evidence that attachment anxiety is positively associated with self-reported conformity in female participants. This association persisted when controlling for attachment avoidance, age, and relationship status, and it was driven more strongly by items tapping normative motives for conformity than items tapping informational motives. By contrast, attachment avoidance did not relate to conformity for either gender, and among male participants there was no reliable relation between attachment anxiety and conformity. The observed gender moderation was supported by both correlational contrasts and regression interaction tests, suggesting that the link between anxious attachment and conformity is meaningfully stronger among women in this sample. These findings contribute to theoretical understanding at the intersection of attachment theory and social influence by documenting an empirical linkage between internal working models that emphasize relational security concerns and a social behavioural tendency to align with others. The results are consistent with conceptual accounts that view anxious attachment as characterized by hypervigilance to social evaluation and an elevated motivation to secure acceptance. Conformity can be an efficient behavioural strategy to reduce the risk of rejection and to signal alignment with group norms, and anxious individuals may therefore be predisposed to use conformity as a social regulation strategy. The stronger association between anxiety and normative, rather than informational, motivations in the subcomponent analyses supports this account and suggests that normative concerns—worries about being accepted—are particularly implicated. This interpretation aligns with the idea that anxious individuals prioritize relational maintenance and may be willing to adjust their expressed attitudes and behaviours to preserve social bonds. The absence of a relationship between avoidant attachment and conformity conforms with theoretical expectations that avoidant individuals, who prioritize autonomy and downregulate attachment needs, would be less motivated to conform simply to gain social acceptance. Avoidant orientation may thus serve as a buffer against normative social influence when conformity would entail emotional disclosure or dependence. The present null findings for avoidance are consistent with some prior work and suggest that avoidance is not a robust predictor of general conformity tendencies measured via self-report. However, it is possible that avoidant individuals may still conform under specific conditions not captured by a general conformity scale, such as when conformity is instrumental to achieving material goals or when it occurs at a behavioural rather than attitudinal level. The gender differences observed herein raise important questions about the socialization and expression of attachment-related motives. Females in many cultures are socialized to value interpersonal harmony and relational sensitivity, which could amplify the behavioural manifestation of attachment anxiety in contexts where social approval is at stake. Consequently, anxious women may show greater alignment with others’ opinions or behaviours because conformity is a practical means of reducing the perceived threat to relationship security. Males, even when experiencing attachment anxiety, may be constrained by gender norms that discourage overt expression of conformity or may channel their relational concerns into different behavioural strategies. Additionally, the male sample was relatively small in the present study, which reduces power to detect effects and increases uncertainty around the true magnitude of any anxiety–conformity relationship among men. The significant interaction effect does suggest a real difference between genders in this sample, but replication in samples with greater gender balance is necessary to ensure generalizability. Several methodological considerations temper the conclusions that can be drawn. The study relied on cross-sectional self-report measures, which precludes causal inference and invites concerns about shared method variance. For instance, individuals who are generally more inclined to report dispositional tendencies toward dependence or sociability might score higher on both anxiety and conformity scales regardless of an underlying causal mechanism. Longitudinal studies or experimental designs manipulating perceived social acceptance could help clarify temporal ordering and causal pathways. The gender imbalance in the sample is another limitation. The overrepresentation of females enhances confidence in the female-specific findings but limits the precision of estimates for males and makes it difficult to generalize conclusions across sexes. Future research should aim for balanced sampling or oversample underrepresented genders to allow for robust comparisons. The use of a general self-report conformity scale captured broad tendencies to align with others, but it cannot substitute for behavioural measures of conformity in specific contexts. Laboratory conformity paradigms that differentiate between normative and informational influence in situ, or field studies that observe real-world conformity behaviours, would provide complementary evidence and help determine whether self-reported tendencies translate into actual conformity. The measure used also assessed both normative and informational motives, but a more fine-grained assessment of the situational moderators that elicit each motive could deepen understanding of when attachment anxiety manifests as conformity. The study controlled for age and relationship status, but other potentially relevant covariates such as cultural background, socioeconomic status, and personality traits like agreeableness and neuroticism were not included. These variables could confound or mediate observed associations. For example, cultural norms regarding conformity vary widely and could interact with attachment orientation to influence behaviour; thus cross-cultural replication would be informative. In addition, the study focused on adult attachment orientations broadly rather than attachment to specific relationship partners; it is possible that attachment security with a particular partner or within a particular social domain could produce different patterns of conformity. The findings suggest several promising directions for future research. One avenue would be to use experimental paradigms to test whether anxious attachment causally increases conformity under conditions that make social acceptance salient. For instance, manipulating the perceived social cost of dissent or the salience of evaluative feedback could reveal whether anxious individuals are more likely to conform when rejection risk is emphasized. Such work could also test whether interventions that reduce perceived rejection threat, such as bolstering secure relational cues, reduce conformity among anxious individuals. Another important direction is to investigate the behavioural consequences of conformity linked to attachment anxiety. If anxious individuals conform more readily, this may have downstream effects on decision quality, susceptibility to misinformation, and the likelihood of engaging in risky group behaviours. Longitudinal designs could also explore whether chronic conformity in anxious individuals predicts trajectories of relationship satisfaction or social integration over time. Gender-specific mechanisms merit deeper exploration as well. Qualitative research or mixed-methods studies could illuminate how men and women interpret and express attachment-related concerns in social contexts, and whether social norms moderate the translation of anxiety into conformity behaviours. Research that includes non-binary and gender-diverse participants would extend understanding beyond the binary gender framework used here. Clinical and applied implications also emerge from the findings. Awareness that anxious attachment is linked to greater conformity among women could inform therapeutic interventions aimed at fostering assertiveness, autonomy, and adaptive boundary setting, especially when conformity undermines personal values or leads to maladaptive outcomes. In educational and organizational contexts, understanding how attachment-related motives shape conformity could guide strategies for promoting independent thinking and reducing undue peer influence without undermining social cohesion. It would be valuable to explore whether attachment-informed interventions can reduce harmful conformity, for example by enhancing perceived belonging through non-contingent acceptance rather than through pressured compliance. Finally, the lack of a relationship between attachment avoidance and conformity suggests heterogeneity in how insecure attachment styles relate to social influence; future theory development should account for these divergent pathways. Models that incorporate motivational, affective, and cognitive mechanisms may better capture why anxious and avoidant attachment styles predict distinct social outcomes. Such integrative frameworks could be tested empirically by combining self-report, behavioural, neurophysiological, and ecological momentary assessment methods. In conclusion, the present study provides evidence that attachment anxiety is associated with elevated self-reported social conformity among females in the sample, with the association appearing particularly tied to normative motives for conformity. Attachment avoidance did not predict conformity for either gender. These results suggest that attachment-derived concerns about acceptance and abandonment may extend beyond dyadic contexts to influence alignment with group norms, particularly among women. Caveats related to sampling, measurement, and causal inference underscore the need for further research using more balanced, multimethod, and longitudinal designs to clarify the robustness and mechanisms of these associations.
