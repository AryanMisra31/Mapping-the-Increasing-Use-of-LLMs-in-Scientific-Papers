Introduction

Social media platforms have become deeply embedded in many people's daily routines and social practices. The integration of social media into everyday life has altered how individuals maintain relationships, gather information, and experience social comparison. One psychological construct that has received attention in relation to social media engagement is the so-called fear of missing out, commonly abbreviated as FOMO. FOMO refers to a pervasive apprehension that others might be having rewarding experiences that one is absent from, and it often motivates behavior aimed at monitoring and participating in social activities. Empirical work has linked FOMO to a broad array of outcomes, including mood disturbances, decreased well-being, and heightened social monitoring behaviors. The mechanisms by which social media use and FOMO are connected likely include frequent exposure to curated positive social information, upward social comparison, and the rapid availability of social opportunities that highlight potential exclusion. Past research has commonly observed positive associations between time spent on social media platforms and reports of FOMO. Those findings have been interpreted to suggest that heavier social media engagement increases the likelihood that individuals will perceive others as being engaged in rewarding activities that they themselves are not part of. However, much of the literature documenting these relationships has been conducted with samples drawn from younger populations, such as adolescents and young adults. Younger users have historically formed the most visible group of social media participants, and many initial studies therefore focused on university students or adolescents. It follows that the generalizability of the positive link between social media use and FOMO to older adult populations remains uncertain. Age is a factor that may shape both patterns of social media use and the emotional concomitants of that use. Older adults often report different motives for using social platforms, including maintained relationships and information seeking, rather than social status seeking or impression management to the same extent as younger users. Additionally, older adults may have different affective responses to social comparison, possibly attenuating the link between exposure to others' activities and feelings of envy or exclusion. Cognitive changes that accompany aging may also influence the frequency and intensity of upward social comparison online, and life course transitions such as parenthood, caregiving, and stable employment may reduce sensitivity to events that occur outside one’s immediate social circle. The differential patterns of social media engagement across age groups also extend to the ways in which platforms are used. Younger users have been found to engage more in active self-presentation behaviors, such as posting updates and photos, whereas older users may engage more in passive content consumption or use platforms primarily for keeping in touch with established contacts. The distinction between active and passive use is theoretically important because passive consumption of social content might foster upward comparisons more readily than active interaction, although active behaviors may also expose users to social evaluation and hence can influence FOMO as well. Beyond usage style, the amount of time spent on a specific platform is an intuitively straightforward metric that has been linked repeatedly to FOMO in prior work. Time spent may increase exposure to social information and thus raise the opportunities for social comparison and the perceived salience of social opportunities one is missing. At the same time, frequency of checking and the particular features used on a platform (for example, browsing newsfeeds versus messaging) may have independent relationships with FOMO beyond overall time. Given the multifaceted nature of social media engagement, a nuanced examination of usage patterns is necessary when assessing FOMO across the adult lifespan. Age may therefore play a role as a moderator or mediator of the relationship between platform use and FOMO. If age moderates the relationship, the strength or direction of the association between social media use and FOMO would vary across age ranges. If age mediates the relationship, age-related differences in platform use could account for the association between use and FOMO, implying that the relation is at least partly explained by differential use patterns across ages. To date, the available evidence suggests that both mechanisms could be plausible, but empirical tests that isolate the role of age in samples composed exclusively of adults older than the typical young adult research subjects are scarce. The present study seeks to address this gap by examining the relationship between social media use, specifically Facebook use, and levels of FOMO among individuals aged 25 years and older. Facebook remains one of the most widely used social networking sites among older adults and provides a stable context to examine usage patterns and emotional outcomes. Measuring Facebook use as a composite of time spent, frequency of checking, and the balance of active versus passive behaviors affords the opportunity to capture multiple aspects of engagement that may be relevant to FOMO. The rationale for focusing on individuals older than 24 years is twofold. First, restricting the sample to adults reduces confounding due to developmental factors tied to adolescence and early adulthood that are not representative of later life stages. Second, limiting the age range to older adults allows an explicit test of whether the patterns observed in younger samples generalize to more mature age groups or whether age attenuates the association between Facebook use and FOMO. Based on prior findings among younger samples, the current study hypothesizes that greater Facebook use will be associated with higher reported levels of FOMO. This hypothesis reflects the broad literature linking exposure to curated social information to feelings of missing out. A secondary hypothesis is that age will reduce or partially account for this association. Specifically, we expect that when age is taken into account, the magnitude of the relationship between Facebook use and FOMO will be weakened, indicating that older participants either use the platform in ways that produce less FOMO or are less susceptible to the emotional effects of social comparison on that platform. We further explore whether different facets of Facebook use—such as passive versus active engagement—differentially predict FOMO and whether these patterns vary across age subgroups within the adult sample. Establishing whether and how age influences the Facebook–FOMO link has theoretical implications for understanding social comparison processes across the adult lifespan and practical implications for tailoring interventions aimed at reducing maladaptive social media outcomes in different age groups. In addition, clarifying the role of platform-specific use patterns contributes to a more precise account of how digital behaviors map onto psychological experiences. The study therefore aims to contribute to both theoretical debates about age-related differences in socio-emotional processing and to applied discussions about mitigating negative outcomes associated with social media engagement. To accomplish these aims, we conducted a cross-sectional survey of adults aged 25 and older who reported active Facebook use. The survey assessed multiple indicators of Facebook engagement as well as self-reported FOMO using an established self-report scale. Analyses included bivariate correlations between Facebook use metrics and FOMO, partial correlations controlling for age, and regression-based mediation tests to examine whether age accounted for part of the association between Facebook use and FOMO. We also conducted supplemental analyses examining active versus passive Facebook behaviors and tested for robustness when adjusting for gender and education. The primary focus was to determine whether the previously observed positive association between social media use and FOMO held in an older adult sample and whether age-related variance changed the strength of that association. Given the complexity of potential pathways, we approached the analyses with both confirmatory hypotheses regarding the overall relation and exploratory analyses to uncover nuanced patterns that may inform future longitudinal research. The following sections describe the methods used, the statistical tests performed, the results obtained, and the broader implications of those results for theory and practice.

Methods

Participants were recruited via online channels that included community forums, social media advertisements, and a research volunteer registry. Eligible participants were required to be at least 25 years old, to report having an active Facebook account used at least once in the past month, and to be proficient in English. The target sample size was set to obtain a reasonably powered correlation and regression analyses, and recruitment continued until 314 participants had completed the survey and passed attention checks. Of the 314 participants, 182 identified as female, 128 as male, and 4 as non-binary or another gender identity. The mean age of the sample was 38.7 years with a standard deviation of 9.8 years, and ages ranged from 25 to 68 years. The sample included individuals with a range of educational backgrounds, with approximately 62% reporting a college degree or higher and the remainder reporting high school or some college. Participants received a small monetary compensation or a raffle entry as incentive for completing the survey, depending on recruitment source. Data were collected using an online questionnaire administered via a secure survey platform. Participants first reviewed an electronic informed consent form that described the study purpose, procedures, potential risks, and confidentiality protections. After providing consent, participants completed demographic questions followed by measures of Facebook use and FOMO. To reduce non-serious responding, the questionnaire included two attention-check items embedded among the scales. Participants who failed either attention check were excluded from analyses. The data collection protocol and materials were reviewed and approved by an institutional ethics committee prior to recruitment. Facebook use was assessed through a multi-item composite measure that captured three facets of engagement. The first facet assessed average daily time spent on Facebook in minutes, reported on a continuous scale. The second facet assessed frequency of checks per day, reported as an integer count with an upper bound option for "more than 20 times" to accommodate heavy users. The third facet assessed the balance of active versus passive behaviors using a set of items asking participants to report how often they engaged in particular activities on Facebook over the past month. Active behaviors included posting status updates, uploading photos, commenting on others' posts, and messaging friends. Passive behaviors included scrolling the newsfeed, viewing others' photos or stories without interacting, and reading posts without responding. Responses for each activity were given on a Likert-type scale from 1 (never) to 5 (very often). An active-passive ratio score was computed by averaging active items and subtracting the mean of passive items, such that higher scores indicated a relatively more active engagement style and lower scores indicated a relatively more passive style. The FOMO measure consisted of a 10-item self-report scale designed to tap into individuals' apprehension about missing out on rewarding experiences. Participants rated each item on a 1-to-5 Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Example items included statements about worrying that others were having more rewarding experiences and feeling compelled to stay connected. The total FOMO score was computed as the mean of the items, with higher scores indicating greater FOMO. The FOMO scale had demonstrated internal consistency in prior work, and internal consistency was assessed in the current sample. Additional covariates were collected for secondary analyses, including self-reported social network size on Facebook (number of friends), employment status, relationship status, and self-reported typical mood over the past two weeks. These covariates were included to examine whether the association between Facebook use and FOMO was robust to potential confounds. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Preliminary analyses involved screening the data for missing values, outliers, and distributional assumptions. Cases with missing data on key variables were excluded listwise if the missingness was limited to a small number of items; however, participants who failed attention checks or had substantial missing data were excluded from the final analytic sample. Descriptive statistics and zero-order Pearson correlations were calculated for the main study variables. Internal consistencies for multi-item scales were computed using Cronbach's alpha. To test the primary hypothesis that greater Facebook use would be associated with higher FOMO, we first examined bivariate correlations between Facebook time, frequency, active-passive ratio, and FOMO scores. To test the secondary hypothesis that age would attenuate the association between Facebook use and FOMO, we computed partial correlations controlling for age. We also ran a series of multiple regression analyses predicting FOMO from Facebook use variables with and without age included as a predictor. These regressions allowed us to observe changes in the coefficients associated with Facebook use when age was entered into the model. For mediation testing, we conducted bootstrap analyses with 5,000 resamples to assess whether age accounted for an indirect effect between Facebook use and FOMO. Although mediation in cross-sectional data does not establish causal pathways, this approach provided an estimate of whether age-related variation accounted for a significant portion of the association. In supplemental analyses, we examined whether the active-passive engagement style interacted with age to predict FOMO by including an interaction term in regression models. We also tested the robustness of the main findings by adding gender, education, relationship status, employment status, and Facebook network size as covariates. Significance thresholds were set at p < .05 for primary tests, and effect sizes were reported using standardized regression coefficients and confidence intervals. Where appropriate, nonparametric checks were applied to ensure that findings were not driven by distributional irregularities.

Results

After data cleaning and application of exclusion criteria, the final analytic sample comprised 304 participants. Ten participants were excluded for failing attention checks or providing incomplete data on key measures. The sample retained for analysis had a mean age of 38.5 years with a standard deviation of 9.6 years and a range from 25 to 68 years. Of the retained participants, 179 identified as female, 122 as male, and 3 as non-binary or another gender identity. Descriptive statistics showed that the mean reported daily time spent on Facebook was 56.2 minutes with a standard deviation of 47.9 minutes. The median number of checks per day was 7 with an interquartile range of 4 to 12. The mean active-passive ratio score was −0.12 with a standard deviation of 0.74, indicating that, on average, participants reported slightly more passive than active engagement. The mean FOMO score in the sample was 2.43 on the 1-to-5 scale with a standard deviation of 0.74. Internal consistency for the FOMO scale was high, with Cronbach's alpha equal to .91 in the current sample, indicating that the items cohered well. The internal consistency of the active and passive item sets was acceptable, with alpha values of .82 for active items and .85 for passive items. Preliminary checks showed that Facebook time had a right-skewed distribution; however, log-transformed time yielded a distribution closer to normal, and results were substantively similar using either raw or log-transformed time. We report analyses using raw time for interpretability but note that tests using log-transformed time produced consistent conclusions. Zero-order correlations among the primary variables indicated that greater daily time on Facebook was significantly associated with higher FOMO, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of r = .32, p < .001. Frequency of checks per day was also positively correlated with FOMO at r = .35, p < .001. The active-passive ratio was negatively correlated with FOMO, indicating that more passive engagement was associated with higher FOMO, with r = −.40, p < .001. Age was negatively correlated with Facebook time (r = −.28, p < .001) and with FOMO (r = −.22, p < .001), such that older participants tended to spend less time on Facebook and reported lower FOMO. Age was also modestly positively correlated with the active-passive ratio (r = .19, p = .001), suggesting that older participants engaged in relatively more active behaviors compared with younger participants in the sample. To examine whether age accounted for part of the association between Facebook use and FOMO, we computed partial correlations controlling for age. The partial correlation between Facebook time and FOMO decreased to r = .18, p = .02 after controlling for age. Similarly, the partial correlation between frequency of checks and FOMO decreased to r = .20, p = .01 when age was controlled. The partial correlation between the active-passive ratio and FOMO remained substantial but was reduced in magnitude to r = −.31, p < .001 when controlling for age. These reductions in correlation coefficients suggested that age accounted for a portion of the relationships between Facebook use metrics and FOMO. We next conducted multiple regression analyses predicting FOMO scores. In a regression model including Facebook time and frequency as simultaneous predictors, both predictors had significant positive coefficients. Facebook time had an unstandardized coefficient of 0.002 per minute (b = 0.002, SE = 0.0006, standardized β = .20, p < .001), and frequency had an unstandardized coefficient of 0.03 per check (b = 0.03, SE = 0.009, standardized β = .22, p < .001). The model accounted for 18% of the variance in FOMO (R^2 = .18). When age was added to the model, the coefficients for Facebook time and frequency decreased. In the adjusted model, Facebook time had a standardized beta of .11 (p = .04) and frequency had a standardized beta of .13 (p = .03), while age had a standardized beta of −.15 (p = .005). The adjusted model explained 22% of the variance in FOMO (R^2 = .22), indicating that including age improved model fit. To probe mediation, we tested whether the relationship between Facebook time and FOMO was partially mediated by age-related variance using bootstrap analyses with 5,000 resamples. The indirect effect of Facebook time on FOMO via age was statistically significant, with an indirect effect estimate of 0.008 (95% bootstrap CI [0.002, 0.015]), indicating that age accounted for a small but significant portion of the association. However, the direct effect of Facebook time on FOMO remained significant in the mediation model, suggesting partial mediation rather than full mediation. A similar pattern emerged when frequency of checks was examined with age as a mediator: the indirect effect estimate was 0.005 (95% CI [0.001, 0.010]) and the direct effect remained significant. For the active-passive ratio, the indirect effect via age was larger, with an indirect estimate of −0.04 (95% CI [−0.07, −0.02]), and the direct effect again remained significant. These mediation analyses support the interpretation that age accounts for part, but not all, of the Facebook use–FOMO connection. Supplemental analyses tested whether the influence of active versus passive engagement on FOMO differed across age groups. We created three age groups for exploratory purposes: 25–34 years, 35–49 years, and 50 years and older. The negative association between active-passive ratio and FOMO was strongest in the youngest group (r = −.46, p < .001), weaker in the middle group (r = −.33, p < .001), and weakest though still significant in the oldest group (r = −.20, p = .02). An interaction term between age (continuous) and active-passive ratio in the regression predicting FOMO was significant (b = 0.01, SE = 0.004, p = .01), indicating that the protective association of active engagement against FOMO weakened with increasing age. We also examined whether the main findings were robust to controlling for gender, education, relationship status, employment status, and Facebook network size. Including these covariates produced only minor changes in the coefficients for Facebook use variables and age; the general pattern of results—positive associations between Facebook use metrics and FOMO and attenuation of these associations when age was controlled—remained unchanged. Finally, we report model diagnostics and sensitivity checks. Residuals in the regression models were approximately normally distributed, variance inflation factors were below 2 suggesting multicollinearity was not a concern, and results were consistent when using log-transformed Facebook time. Overall, the pattern of results supported the primary hypothesis that greater Facebook use is associated with higher FOMO among adults aged 25 and older, and the secondary hypothesis that age accounts for part of this association.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between Facebook use and fear of missing out among adults aged 25 years and older, with a specific focus on the role of age in modifying or explaining that relationship. The principal finding was that greater Facebook engagement—indexed by time spent on the platform, frequency of checks, and a more passive style of use—was positively associated with higher self-reported FOMO. This pattern replicates the broad literature linking social media engagement to heightened concerns about missing out, extending those associations into a sample composed entirely of adults beyond early adulthood. Importantly, age emerged as a meaningful factor in the observed associations. Age was negatively correlated with both Facebook use and FOMO, and when age was statistically accounted for, the strength of the relationships between Facebook use and FOMO was reduced. Mediation analyses suggested that age partially mediated the association between Facebook use and FOMO, indicating that differences in engagement associated with age account for some, but not all, of the link. In other words, while older adults in this sample tended to use Facebook less and to report lower FOMO, there remained a direct association between Facebook engagement and FOMO that was not fully explained by age-related differences. These findings have several implications for theory and practice. Theoretically, the results underscore that FOMO is not exclusively a phenomenon of adolescence and young adulthood; it is detectable among older adults who engage with social media platforms. The presence of a direct association between Facebook use and FOMO after accounting for age suggests that exposure to curated social content and the social comparison processes elicited by platforms remain salient across much of the adult lifespan. However, the attenuation of the association with increasing age points to age-linked differences in how individuals engage with social media and how susceptible they are to FOMO. One plausible interpretation is that life stage factors such as stable social roles, established friendship networks, and differing priorities reduce sensitivity to the kinds of social opportunities highlighted on platforms. Older adults may evaluate content differently, placing less weight on peers' leisure activities or status-driven displays, thereby experiencing less FOMO in response to comparable exposure. Another possibility is that the modes of engagement shift with age in ways that influence FOMO. Our finding that more passive engagement was more strongly associated with higher FOMO and that this relation was particularly pronounced among younger adults suggests that passive consumption exposes users to upward comparisons without the buffering effects of active social interaction. Older adults in the sample reported relatively more active engagement and correspondingly lower FOMO, implying that participation and communication may mitigate feelings of missing out by reinforcing social connectedness. These patterns align with the hypothesis that passive browsing encourages social comparison and envy, whereas active interaction may foster reciprocal communication and support. The observed interaction between age and active-passive engagement further suggests that the protective effect of active engagement diminishes with age, perhaps because older users’ active engagement serves different social functions such as coordination of family events or information sharing rather than impression-centric processes. From a methodological perspective, the use of multiple indicators of Facebook engagement strengthens confidence in the conclusion that both quantity and quality of use matter for emotional outcomes. Time spent and frequency of checking captured exposure to social content, whereas the active-passive ratio provided a more behaviorally nuanced index of how users engage with material. The fact that these different facets each related to FOMO and that age modified these relations underscores the importance of multidimensional measurement in research on social media and well-being. The partial mediation results also highlight the complexity of the relationship. Age explained some of the covariance between Facebook use and FOMO, but not all, suggesting that additional unmeasured variables could play a role. For example, personality traits such as neuroticism or trait envy, differences in offline social connectedness, or platform affordances beyond Facebook could contribute to both social media behaviors and FOMO. Future research would benefit from incorporating broader psychological measures to delineate these pathways more fully. There are several practical implications to consider. First, interventions aimed at reducing maladaptive FOMO might be tailored differently across age groups. For younger adults, strategies that reduce passive browsing or promote active, reciprocal engagement might be particularly useful. For older adults, interventions might focus on aligning platform use with meaningful social goals and reducing time spent on exposure that triggers social comparison. Educational campaigns about the curated nature of online content and tools to manage notification frequency or app usage could be beneficial across the adult lifespan but might be most impactful if targeted based on typical usage patterns and motives. The study's cross-sectional design imposes important limitations on causal inference. Although mediation analysis suggested that age accounts for part of the association between Facebook use and FOMO, causal direction cannot be determined from the present data. It remains possible that individuals with higher FOMO are attracted to more frequent checking and greater time online, or that a bidirectional feedback loop operates where FOMO fuels use which in turn amplifies FOMO. Longitudinal designs and experimental manipulations of platform exposure or usage style would be necessary to unpack causal relationships. Another limitation concerns reliance on self-report measures of both Facebook use and FOMO. Although self-reports provide valuable data on subjective experience and perceived use, objective measures such as logged usage data could complement self-report and reduce potential reporting biases. Future studies that combine self-report with behavioral logs would provide a richer account of the temporal dynamics linking social media engagement and emotional outcomes. The focus on Facebook as the sole platform investigated is another limitation. Although Facebook remains widely used, other platforms with different affordances—for example, image-centered or ephemeral-content platforms—may engender distinct patterns of social comparison and FOMO. Research that examines multiple platforms and cross-platform behavior would clarify whether the observed associations are specific to Facebook or generalize across the social media ecosystem. The sample composition and recruitment also affect generalizability. Participants were recruited online and may overrepresent individuals with higher digital engagement or certain demographic characteristics. Although the sample included a broad age range of adults and a mix of educational backgrounds, nonrandom sampling limits the extent to which findings can be extrapolated to the general population. Future research employing probability sampling or targeted recruitment to underrepresented groups would strengthen external validity. There remain several promising directions for future work. Longitudinal studies could examine developmental trajectories of FOMO and social media use across middle and later adulthood to observe whether the attenuating effect of age observed here reflects maturation or cohort effects. Experimental interventions that reduce passive browsing or modify frequency of checks could test causal hypotheses about the mechanisms by which social media use influences FOMO. Additionally, studies that explore moderators such as personality traits, offline social support, and cultural values would illuminate for whom and under what circumstances social media use is most likely to produce FOMO. Qualitative work could complement quantitative findings by exploring subjective experiences of missing out among older adults and how those experiences are shaped by life course priorities. Finally, research aimed at designing platform features that reduce the social comparison potential of feeds—such as reframing algorithmic curation, increasing visibility of mundane everyday activities, or providing contextual cues about the curated nature of posts—could have practical benefits for reducing FOMO-related distress. In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that Facebook use is positively associated with FOMO among adults aged 25 and older and that age accounts for part of this association. The findings extend the literature on social media and emotional well-being into a less-studied segment of the adult population and underscore that both the quantity and quality of platform engagement matter for psychological outcomes. Although older adults appear less susceptible overall to FOMO and use Facebook differently than younger adults, social media exposure remains relevant to emotional experience across much of the adult lifespan. Addressing the mechanisms that link exposure to social content and feelings of missing out will be an important task for future research and for the development of interventions and platform design choices that promote healthier digital interaction.
