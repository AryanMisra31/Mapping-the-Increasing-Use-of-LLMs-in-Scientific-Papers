Introduction

Understanding the factors that shape eating behavior is a central aim of research in health psychology and behavioral nutrition because eating has substantial consequences for both short-term well-being and long-term physical health. One set of influences that has attracted considerable attention is psychological stress, which is commonly reported to affect appetite and food intake in complex and sometimes inconsistent ways. On some occasions, stress has been associated with increased appetite and a tendency to seek out energy-dense foods, while on other occasions stress is associated with appetite suppression and reduced food intake. This variability in outcomes points to the importance of delineating the conditions and mechanisms through which stress exerts influence on hunger and eating behavior. It also underscores the practical importance of identifying whether everyday stressors reliably shift subjective hunger in the moment because such shifts could contribute to patterns of overconsumption or underconsumption over time.

At a broad physiological level, stress engages neuroendocrine systems that plausibly connect psychological arousal and energy regulation. Acute psychological stress activates the sympathetic-adrenomedullary system as well as the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, resulting in the release of catecholamines and glucocorticoids. These hormones can influence peripheral metabolic processes and central appetite-regulating circuits. For instance, catecholamines tend to suppress nonessential digestive processes during immediate fight-or-flight responses, whereas glucocorticoids such as cortisol can increase appetite and promote the storage of energy under certain conditions. Beyond these physiological pathways, stress can shift attention, alter reward processing, and change the valuation of food cues, any of which could modify subjective feelings of hunger and the likelihood of initiating eating.

Psychological and situational moderators are also important in determining whether stress leads to increased or decreased hunger. The nature of the stressor—its duration, controllability, and social evaluative component—can shape whether an individual experiences stress-induced anorexia or stress-induced overeating. Individual differences such as trait stress reactivity, eating restraint, mood regulation strategies, and metabolic factors likewise play moderating roles. Laboratory investigations frequently use standardized stress induction tasks such as public speaking, social evaluation, or timed cognitive tasks to isolate the acute effects of stress and to examine downstream effects on appetite and food choice.

Timed mental arithmetic tasks are a common experimental tool for inducing acute cognitive stress and negative affect in a controlled setting. When participants are given a limited time to complete challenging calculations, the combination of time pressure, performance evaluation, and cognitive load reliably produces subjective stress and physiological responses in many samples. Such tasks are straightforward to administer, can be standardized across participants, and have ecological validity insofar as they mirror common work- and school-related stressors. In contrast, non-evaluative, low-demand tasks such as coloring or drawing are often used as neutral control conditions that provide a relaxing and low-stress context. Comparing responses across a cognitive stress task and a relaxed control task allows researchers to examine how acute negative affect and perceived stress relate to momentary hunger.

Measurement approaches for momentary hunger commonly rely on self-report scales, and Likert-type items are frequently used to capture subjective hunger and stress immediately following experimental manipulations. Pre- and post-manipulation ratings allow researchers to assess change in subjective states attributable to the experimental condition. While self-report measures have limitations related to introspective accuracy and response bias, they remain a practical and valid method for indexing momentary subjective states when used carefully in controlled studies.

Several lines of empirical work have found positive associations between stress and subsequent increases in appetite or food intake under laboratory conditions. In certain experimental paradigms, participants exposed to acute stressors reported greater hunger and increased consumption of palatable snack foods relative to participants in control conditions. These findings support a model in which acute stress can upregulate appetite in the short term, possibly via glucocorticoid-mediated increases in appetite and hedonic eating. Other experiments, however, have found that acute stress decreases subjective appetite and results in lower immediate food intake, a pattern more consistent with sympathetic activation suppressing digestive processes. The mixed nature of these findings highlights the need to specify the types of stressors, the timing of hunger measurement, and the characteristics of the participant sample that might account for divergent outcomes.

Beyond immediate laboratory observations, correlational studies in naturalistic settings also document complex relations between everyday stress and eating behavior. Some survey-based studies report that people who experience frequent stress are more likely to engage in emotional eating or to consume more calorie-dense foods, whereas other studies indicate that stress can disrupt regular eating patterns and reduce appetite in some individuals. These patterns suggest that stress-induced changes in hunger may be heterogeneous across individuals and contexts, with a meaningful portion of the population exhibiting increases in hunger under stress and others exhibiting decreases.

Clarifying whether a simple laboratory manipulation that produces different levels of perceived stress will lead to corresponding differences in subjective hunger is important for both theoretical and applied reasons. Theoretically, demonstrating a robust correlation between stress and hunger in response to an acute, standardized manipulation would provide evidence for a direct linking mechanism between psychological stress and immediate appetite states. Applied implications include the potential to design low-cost interventions—such as short relaxation or distraction tasks—to modulate hunger states in moments of stress and thereby influence eating behavior.

The present study was designed to examine the relationship between acute stress and subjective hunger using a straightforward experimental contrast. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions: a timed mental arithmetic task intended to induce mild cognitive stress, or a quiet coloring task intended to serve as a relaxed control condition. Both tasks were brief, standardized, and well-suited for administration in a laboratory setting. Participants provided self-reported ratings of stress and hunger on Likert scales before and after the task. The primary empirical question was whether exposure to the timed mental arithmetic task would produce higher post-task stress ratings and whether those higher stress ratings would be associated with higher post-task hunger ratings relative to the coloring task.

We hypothesized that participants in the timed mental arithmetic condition would report greater increases in perceived stress from pre- to post-task than participants in the coloring condition. We further hypothesized that greater perceived stress would be positively associated with increases in subjective hunger across participants, such that higher post-task stress would correspond to higher post-task hunger. Finally, given the possibility that baseline hunger could influence responses, we evaluated whether the observed relationships between stress and hunger held after controlling for pre-task hunger and other relevant covariates such as time since last meal and demographic variables.

This study sought to contribute empirically to the literature by combining a straightforward experimental manipulation, clear self-report measures, and relevant covariate assessment to test the link between induced stress and hunger. The random assignment of participants to the two task conditions reduced concerns about confounding and allowed the comparison of within-person changes as well as between-group differences. The inclusion of a relaxed control task provided a baseline against which to evaluate effects attributable to stress rather than task engagement per se. Together, these design elements provided an appropriate and focused test of whether an acute stressor day beget a measurable change in subjective hunger.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university campus and surrounding community using online announcements and flyers. Participants were eligible if they were between 18 and 35 years old, fluent in English, and reported no current eating disorder diagnosis or metabolic condition that could substantially alter appetite. Participation was voluntary and students received course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. In total, 120 participants completed the study and their data were included in analyses. The final sample contained 74 females and 46 males. The mean age of participants was 21.7 years with a standard deviation of 3.2 years.

All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of the hosting university and procedures conformed to standard ethical guidelines for research with human participants. Participants were informed that they could withdraw at any time without penalty and that their responses would remain confidential. After the experimental session, participants were debriefed about the nature of the manipulations and the study aims.

The experimental design employed a between-subjects manipulation with two conditions: a timed mental arithmetic task (stress induction) and a coloring task (relaxed control). Participants were randomly assigned to one of the two conditions using a simple randomization procedure implemented prior to arrival. Each participant completed the same sequence of measures and tasks in a single laboratory session lasting approximately 20 minutes.

Measures included self-report ratings of stress and hunger administered immediately before and immediately after the experimental task. Stress was measured with a single-item Likert scale asking “How stressed do you feel right now?” Responses ranged from 1 (not at all) to 7 (extremely). Hunger was measured with a single-item Likert scale asking “How hungry do you feel right now?” Responses also ranged from 1 (not at all) to 7 (extremely). Participants also provided information about the time since their last meal, sleeping hours the previous night, and whether they had any current medications that might influence appetite. Demographic information collected included age, gender, and student status.

The timed mental arithmetic task involved a set of moderately difficult arithmetic problems presented on a computer screen. Participants were instructed that they would have five minutes to complete as many problems as possible and that performance would be recorded. The arithmetic stimuli were designed to be more challenging than typical everyday calculations to induce some cognitive strain and mild evaluative pressure. Participants were encouraged to work quickly and accurately. The experimenter provided standardized instructions and remained unobtrusive during task performance.

The coloring task used a set of intricate but non-evaluative coloring templates and colored pencils. Participants were given five minutes to color a mandala-style design and were told that the activity was intended to be relaxing and non-evaluative. The experimenter provided the coloring materials and standardized instructions and did not provide any performance evaluation or feedback. The duration of the coloring task matched the duration of the arithmetic task to equate time on task across conditions.

Procedure unfolded in a standard way for all participants. Upon arrival, participants completed informed consent and demographic questionnaires. They then provided pre-task stress and hunger ratings on the Likert scales. Participants were randomly allocated to one of the two task conditions and were led to a quiet room containing a computer and the relevant materials. They completed the assigned five-minute task under standardized instructions. Immediately following task completion, participants provided post-task stress and hunger ratings. Finally, participants completed brief questions about time since last meal and other covariates, were debriefed, and compensated.

To assess whether the arithmetic task successfully induced stress relative to coloring, a manipulation check compared pre- to post-task changes in stress between conditions. The primary analyses examined (a) between-group differences in post-task hunger, (b) within-subject changes in hunger from pre- to post-task across conditions, (c) correlations between post-task stress and post-task hunger across the full sample and within each condition, and (d) a multiple regression predicting post-task hunger from post-task stress while controlling for pre-task hunger and time since last meal. Effect sizes and confidence intervals were computed for main tests. Statistical analyses used standard parametric tests appropriate for Likert-type measures treated as approximately continuous in this sample; reported tests included paired and independent-samples t-tests, Pearson correlations, mixed-design ANOVA, and linear regression.

Data screening procedures were carried out prior to hypothesis testing. Data were inspected for missing values, which were negligible, and for outliers on the primary variables. No participant had missing pre- or post-task hunger or stress ratings. Time since last meal varied across participants but was included as a covariate when appropriate. All variables approximated normal distributions adequate for parametric analysis given the moderate sample size. Statistical significance was evaluated against an alpha level of .05, and two-tailed tests were used unless otherwise noted.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first to provide an overview of the data. Across the full sample, mean pre-task stress rating was 3.16 with a standard deviation of 1.05. Mean post-task stress rating for the entire sample was 3.50 with a standard deviation of 1.28. Pre-task hunger across the full sample had a mean of 3.46 and a standard deviation of 1.15. Post-task hunger across the full sample had a mean of 3.56 and a standard deviation of 1.20. These overall means conceal systematic differences between the experimental conditions, which are reported below.

Random assignment appeared to be effective in balancing baseline hunger and stress across conditions. The arithmetic (stress) group had a pre-task hunger mean of 3.45 with a standard deviation of 1.16 and the coloring group had a pre-task hunger mean of 3.47 with a standard deviation of 1.14. An independent-samples t-test comparing pre-task hunger between conditions produced t(118) = -0.11, p = .91, indicating no significant baseline difference. Pre-task stress was also similar across groups, with the arithmetic group showing a mean of 3.18 (SD = 1.07) and the coloring group showing a mean of 3.13 (SD = 1.03); the t-test produced t(118) = 0.30, p = .77.

The manipulation check confirmed that the timed mental arithmetic task increased perceived stress more than the coloring task. The arithmetic group’s mean post-task stress was 4.20 (SD = 1.10), while the coloring group’s mean post-task stress was 2.80 (SD = 0.98). An independent-samples t-test indicated that the arithmetic group reported significantly higher post-task stress than the coloring group, t(118) = 7.06, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.29. A paired-samples t-test within the arithmetic group showed a significant increase in stress from pre-task to post-task, t(59) = 7.20, p < .001, whereas the coloring group showed a small but statistically significant decrease in stress from pre-task to post-task, t(59) = -2.91, p = .005.

Turning to hunger, there was a notable difference in how hunger changed across the two conditions. In the arithmetic group, post-task hunger averaged 3.85 (SD = 1.21), compared with the pre-task average of 3.45 (SD = 1.16). The paired-samples t-test for that group indicated a significant increase in hunger, t(59) = 3.24, p = .002. In the coloring group, mean post-task hunger was 3.28 (SD = 1.09) compared with the pre-task mean of 3.47 (SD = 1.14), and the paired-samples t-test indicated a small but significant decrease in hunger, t(59) = -2.05, p = .045.

An independent-samples t-test comparing post-task hunger across groups showed that participants in the arithmetic condition reported significantly higher hunger than those in the coloring condition, t(118) = 2.86, p = .005, Cohen’s d = 0.52. The group means and standard deviations described above suggest a moderate effect size favoring an association between induced stress and increased hunger.

Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the association between post-task stress and post-task hunger across the full sample and within each condition. Across all participants, post-task stress and post-task hunger were positively correlated, r = .45, p < .001. Within the arithmetic group, the correlation between post-task stress and post-task hunger was r = .52, p < .001, indicating a strong positive association in the stress condition. In the coloring group, the correlation was weaker though still positive, r = .28, p = .03.

To test whether the observed association between stress and hunger held after controlling for pre-task hunger and time since last meal, a hierarchical multiple regression was conducted predicting post-task hunger. In the first step, pre-task hunger and time since last meal (in hours) were entered as predictors. The model explained 32% of the variance in post-task hunger, R2 = .32, F(2,117) = 27.74, p < .001, with pre-task hunger as a significant predictor (β = .52, p < .001) and time since last meal also significant in the expected direction (β = .24, p = .005). In the second step, post-task stress was added to the model and accounted for an additional 9% of variance, ΔR2 = .09, ΔF(1,116) = 15.48, p < .001. In the full model, post-task stress remained a significant predictor of post-task hunger (β = .38, p < .001), even after accounting for baseline hunger and time since last meal.

An exploratory analysis tested for an interaction between condition and time on hunger using a mixed-design ANOVA with time (pre, post) as the within-subjects factor and condition (arithmetic, coloring) as the between-subjects factor. The analysis revealed a significant time × condition interaction, F(1,118) = 11.32, p = .001, ηp2 = .09, indicating that the change in hunger over time differed by condition. Follow-up simple effects showed that the arithmetic group experienced a significant increase in hunger from pre to post, whereas the coloring group experienced a small decrease.

Additional exploratory checks addressed potential gender differences and the role of other covariates. When gender was added as an additional predictor in the regression model, gender did not significantly predict post-task hunger (β = .06, p = .38), nor did it significantly moderate the relationship between post-task stress and post-task hunger (interaction term β = .05, p = .44). Similarly, self-reported sleep hours the previous night did not materially alter the main pattern of results when included as a covariate.

To summarize the key quantitative results, the timed mental arithmetic task successfully induced greater subjective stress compared to the coloring control. Participants assigned to the stress task reported a statistically significant increase in hunger from pre- to post-task, whereas participants assigned to the coloring task reported a small decrease in hunger. Across the full sample and within the stress condition in particular, higher levels of post-task stress were reliably associated with higher levels of post-task hunger. The relationship between stress and hunger remained significant after controlling for pre-task hunger and time since last meal, indicating that acute perceived stress contributed uniquely to variation in momentary hunger.

The pattern of results supports the primary hypotheses and suggests that acute cognitive stress, as induced by a short timed arithmetic task, is associated with immediate increases in subjective hunger. The findings also indicate that a simple relaxation-oriented task can lead to stable or slightly reduced hunger in the short term. These outcomes together imply that the psychological context of brief stressful episodes may be important in determining immediate appetite states, and that the association between stress and hunger is observable using brief self-report measures in a controlled laboratory context.

Discussion

The present study examined whether a brief, standardized induction of cognitive stress would be associated with changes in subjective hunger in a sample of young adults. The timed mental arithmetic task successfully increased subjective stress relative to a coloring control task, and this increase in stress was associated with a concurrent increase in hunger. These effects were apparent in both between-group comparisons and in correlational analyses, and they held after controlling for baseline hunger and time since the last meal. The results therefore support the idea that acute psychological stress can be positively associated with short-term increases in perceived hunger in many individuals.

These findings extend prior evidence concerning the links between stress and eating-related states by demonstrating that a brief, performance-oriented stressor can produce reliably higher subjective hunger in the moment. Importantly, the experimental contrast included a matched-duration, low-stress control activity, which strengthens the inference that the observed changes in hunger were related to stress and not merely to time on task or mere engagement in an activity. The manipulation check data confirmed that the arithmetic task produced substantially higher perceived stress than the coloring task, and the statistical tests documented correlated changes in hunger that were consistent with theoretical models that posit glucocorticoid and reward-related pathways linking stress to increased appetite.

The present results are consistent with a theoretical framework that emphasizes both physiological and psychological mechanisms. Physiologically, acute stress releases hormones and neurochemicals that can increase appetite and alter reward sensitivity, potentially biasing behavior towards seeking palatable food. Psychologically, stress may increase the motivation to engage in comfort-seeking behaviors and to obtain immediate relief, and eating can be perceived as a rapid means of mood regulation. In this study, the arithmetic task likely increased both physiological arousal and cognitive evaluation pressure, producing an internal state in which participants reported greater hunger.

That said, the relationship between stress and hunger is not uniform across all contexts or individuals, and the present study sheds light on some boundary conditions. The coloring task did not produce an increase in hunger and in fact was associated with a slight decrease, perhaps reflecting the calming nature of a non-evaluative creative activity. This pattern suggests that interventions that foster short episodes of relaxation or mindful engagement could potentially reduce momentary appetite in situations where stress might otherwise push people towards eating. The differential effects observed across conditions highlight that stress is not a unitary influence on eating behavior; rather, the specific type of stressor and the accompanying cognitive and emotional experience matter.

While the current study focused on subjective hunger as the primary outcome, hunger is only one proximal antecedent of actual food intake. The observed increase in the hunger ratings indicates a change in motivated state, but whether this would translate into greater caloric consumption or poorer dietary choices outside the laboratory remains to be empirically tested. Future research could extend the design by including direct measures of intake, food choice tasks, or longer monitoring of real-world eating behavior following induced stressors. Such work would help determine the ecological significance of the present findings and whether short-term shifts in hunger lead to cumulative effects on energy balance.

The results also invite consideration of individual differences that were not the primary focus of this study but which may moderate stress–hunger links. For example, trait-level tendencies such as emotional eating, dietary restraint, stress reactivity, and habitual coping strategies may shape whether acute stress increases or decreases hunger for a given person. Similarly, biological differences, including sex, baseline cortisol levels, and metabolic rate, could influence the magnitude of the effect. Although gender did not moderate the stress–hunger association in the present sample, the study was not powered to detect small interaction effects and did not measure many biological moderators; these areas are promising avenues for future investigation.

Methodologically, the use of single-item Likert measures for stress and hunger offers both advantages and limitations. Single-item scales are brief, reduce participant burden, and capture subjective experience directly, which is appropriate for pre-post laboratory manipulations. However, they do not provide the multi-faceted assessment offered by longer validated instruments and may be more susceptible to measurement error. Researchers seeking greater precision may want to include physiological measures of stress (e.g., salivary cortisol) and multi-item hunger scales in future studies to triangulate subjective reports with objective markers. Combining subjective and biological measures would strengthen causal inferences about the pathways linking stress to appetite.

Another important limitation concerns the generalizability of the findings. The sample consisted predominantly of young adults from a university setting, and results may differ in older populations or in samples with greater variability in socioeconomic status, dietary habits, or health conditions. Additionally, participants self-reported the time since their last meal, and although this variable was controlled for in analyses, objective verification of fasting status would provide greater rigor. The laboratory environment and the brief nature of the task may limit ecological validity; stressors in real life can be chronic, multi-dimensional, and embedded in social contexts that were not captured here.

Despite these limitations, the present findings have practical implications. If brief cognitive stressors reliably increase subjective hunger, then workplace and educational environments that place repeated time pressure on individuals may inadvertently promote heightened appetitive states throughout the day. Interventions that incorporate short breaks for relaxation or non-evaluative creative activities might serve to counteract stress-induced increases in hunger and thereby reduce the risk of stress-related overeating. Public health messaging and organizational policies could consider including stress-management strategies as part of broader efforts to support healthy eating behaviors.

Future research should pursue several clear directions. First, replication with larger and more diverse samples would establish the robustness and boundary conditions of the stress–hunger relationship. Second, longitudinal designs could examine whether repeated exposure to brief stressors produces cumulative increases in hunger and, consequentially, in food intake and body weight over time. Third, experimental variations could probe the mechanisms more directly by including physiological measures such as cortisol and heart rate variability to distinguish between sympathetic and HPA-axis contributions. Fourth, intervention studies could test whether short relaxation tasks administered in real-world contexts attenuate stress-induced hunger and reduce subsequent unhealthy snacking.

Another interesting avenue is to explore the specificity of stress-induced hunger for certain types of foods. It is well established that stress can increase preference for energy-dense, sweet, or fatty foods in some contexts; future studies could examine whether the hunger increase evidenced in the present study is selective for hedonic food cues or general across food categories. Understanding the interplay between stress, subjective hunger, and food choice would have direct relevance for dietary interventions aimed at reducing stress-related unhealthy eating.

Finally, research that incorporates ecological momentary assessment would bridge the laboratory and real-world contexts by tracking momentary stress and hunger in daily life and relating fluctuations to subsequent eating episodes. Such work would help clarify the temporal dynamics and real-world significance of stress-related changes in appetite. Integrating experimental manipulations with ambulatory monitoring could provide a comprehensive picture of how momentary stress influences hunger and behavior across contexts and time scales.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that a brief timed mental arithmetic task increases subjective stress and that higher stress is associated with higher subjective hunger in the short term. The findings support a model in which acute psychological stress is linked to immediate increases in appetite-related states, at least under the conditions tested here. These results contribute to a nuanced understanding of the stress–eating relationship and point to multiple avenues for future research and practical interventions aimed at mitigating stress-related impacts on eating behavior.
