Introduction

Perceptions of cultural and ethnic groups play a central role in social interactions, policy attitudes, and intergroup relations in contemporary societies. Research on how people evaluate different cultural groups has therefore been of longstanding interest across social psychology, sociology, and political science. At the broadest level, evaluations of cultural groups can be considered part of affective intergroup attitudes, which shape both interpersonal behavior and collective decisions. These evaluations are multifaceted and can be influenced by a wide range of factors, including political ideology, religiosity, education, exposure to diversity, and personal contact with members of other groups. Understanding which factors systematically vary with evaluations of cultural groups helps to illuminate pathways that may foster prejudice or promote tolerance.

Political ideology has been linked to intergroup attitudes in many studies. Individuals who identify at different points along the political spectrum often vary in their openness to out-groups, preferences for cultural homogeneity, and responses to immigration and multiculturalism. For example, more conservative political orientations are sometimes associated with greater negativity toward groups perceived as culturally different, although this pattern is not universal and is moderated by context and the specific groups in question. At the same time, political identity is not a unitary predictor; different dimensions of political belief (for example, economic versus social conservatism) can relate to intergroup attitudes in different ways. Measuring political standing broadly therefore may mask important nuance, but it remains a meaningful predictor in many contexts.

Religiosity is another factor that has been repeatedly associated with intergroup evaluations. Religious commitment and practice can foster in-group cohesion and out-group differentiation, which in some cases can heighten negative evaluations of unfamiliar cultural groups. Conversely, certain religious teachings and practices emphasize compassion and universalism, which can reduce prejudice. The relation between religiosity and attitudes toward cultural groups is therefore complex and may depend on the religious tradition, the salience of religious identity, and the broader social environment in which religious beliefs are expressed. Studies that have assessed simple indices of religiosity often find modest associations with out-group attitudes, but these associations tend to be smaller and more variable than those observed for political ideology.

Exposure to international environments, such as attendance at an international school, is commonly thought to promote cross-cultural understanding. International schools typically enroll students from multiple national, ethnic, and cultural backgrounds and often include curricula that emphasize global citizenship and intercultural competence. The contact hypothesis predicts that increased exposure to members of diverse groups under conditions of equal status and cooperative interdependence should reduce prejudice and improve attitudes toward out-groups. In principle, attendance at an international school provides such exposure and structured opportunities for intergroup contact, suggesting that alumni might exhibit more positive evaluations of different cultural groups later in life.

Despite the theoretical plausibility of links between political standing, religiosity, international exposure, and evaluations of cultural groups, empirical findings have been mixed. Some studies report clear associations between conservative ideology and more negative attitudes toward particular out-groups, while others find limited or no association once other variables are controlled. Similarly, religiosity shows variable relations with out-group attitudes depending on measures and contexts. Research on the long-term effects of international schooling is likewise heterogeneous; some studies report more cosmopolitan attitudes among international school alumni, whereas others find little difference compared to peers from national curricula.

A key issue in this area of research is the choice of measurement. Attitudes toward cultural groups can be assessed with explicit self-report measures, implicit measures, behavioral indices, or physiological markers. Explicit self-report measures are straightforward to administer in online surveys and can capture conscious, deliberative evaluations such as pleasantness ratings. However, explicit measures are susceptible to social desirability biases and may not capture automatic evaluative tendencies that shape spontaneous behavior. Implicit measures (such as Implicit Association Tests) can complement explicit ratings but are more difficult to implement reliably in uncontrolled online settings. Therefore, many large-scale surveys rely on explicit ratings and seek to mitigate biases through careful item construction and assurances of confidentiality.

Another methodological concern involves the definition and categorization of cultural groups. Broad categories such as "white," "Asian," and "Middle Eastern" encompass substantial heterogeneity, and responses to these labels can reflect respondents' associations with nationality, religion, or stereotypes. Using broad labels increases generalizability but may obscure important within-category differences. Nonetheless, broad categories are often used in survey research because they map onto salient social distinctions in many contexts and allow comparisons across large samples.

The present study focused on pleasantness ratings of three broad cultural group labels—white, Asian, and Middle Eastern—as outcome measures. Pleasantness ratings are a direct, easily interpretable form of explicit evaluative response and can capture overall affective responses to group labels. We chose these three groups because they represent salient axes of cultural difference in many Western contexts and because prior research has suggested varying patterns of prejudice and acceptance across these categories. For example, in some contexts attitudes toward Middle Eastern groups have been particularly susceptible to negative stereotyping, while attitudes toward Asian groups have shown complex patterns linked to model minority stereotypes and perceptions of cultural difference.

The main predictors of interest in the present study were political standing, self-reported religiosity, and whether participants attended an international school during their formative education. Political standing was conceptualized as a self-placement on a left-right or liberal-conservative dimension, which captures a mix of ideological positions related to cultural and economic issues. Religiosity was assessed as the extent to which religion is important to the individual and the extent of participation in religious activities, which together form a broad index of religious commitment. Attendance at an international school was coded as a binary variable indicating whether participants had attended a school with an explicitly international curriculum and diverse student body for at least two academic years.

The study aimed to examine whether pleasantness ratings for each of the three groups were significantly associated with political standing or religiosity, as well as whether attendees of international schools differed from non-attendees in their evaluations. The hypothesis, based on general prior research and theoretical reasoning, was that political standing and religiosity would correlate significantly with pleasantness ratings for the selected cultural groups and that attendance at an international school would be associated with more positive ratings. Specifically, it was expected that more conservative or right-leaning political standing and higher religiosity would be associated with lower pleasantness ratings for groups perceived as culturally or religiously different from the respondent, and that international school attendance would be related to higher pleasantness ratings for each group.

This study contributes to the literature in several ways. First, it assesses multiple potential predictors simultaneously within a single sample, allowing for comparison of their relative associations with intergroup evaluations. Second, it uses straightforward explicit measures that can be implemented economically in online survey platforms, enabling the recruitment of a diverse sample. Third, it examines the potential long-term influence of international schooling, which has received comparatively less attention in quantitative survey research. Finally, by including three culturally distinct target groups, the study permits examination of whether predictor-outcome relations vary by target group, which informs understanding of whether certain predictors have generalized or group-specific effects.

At the same time, the study acknowledges several limitations inherent in its design. Cross-sectional survey data cannot establish causality, and self-report measures capture conscious evaluations that may differ from underlying automatic responses. The binary classification of international school attendance does not capture the heterogeneity of international school experiences, such as the degree of internationalization, language of instruction, or curriculum emphasis on global citizenship. Similarly, broad cultural labels may evoke different associations across respondents, contributing noise to measurement. Nonetheless, a carefully conducted survey can provide initial evidence about the presence or absence of systematic associations worthy of further investigation.

Given these considerations, the present research pre-registered and tested a priori predictions linking political standing, religiosity, and international school attendance to pleasantness ratings for white, Asian, and Middle Eastern groups. The analytic plan included bivariate correlations, regression models controlling for demographic covariates, and group comparisons to examine the effect of international schooling. Equivalence testing or reporting of effect sizes was planned to interpret non-significant findings in light of statistical power and the magnitude of observed effects.

In sum, the present study addresses a practically and theoretically important question about the determinants of explicit evaluations of cultural groups. It brings together predictors that are commonly hypothesized to relate to intergroup attitudes—political ideology, religiosity, and international schooling—and tests their associations with pleasantness ratings for three salient cultural groups. The research seeks to clarify whether these background variables exert consistent influence on explicit evaluations and to outline implications for theories of contact, political identity, and the socialization effects of educational environments. The following sections describe the methods, results, and interpretation of the study in detail.

Methods

Participants were recruited through an online survey platform that distributed a study call to panel participants and through social media advertisements targeted at adults aged 18 and older. A total of 312 respondents completed the main survey and met inclusion criteria; these respondents constituted the analytic sample. Inclusion criteria required participants to be fluent in English, to be aged 18 or older, and to provide full responses to the primary measures of interest. Data collection occurred over a four-week period during a single calendar month. Participants received a small monetary reimbursement through the survey platform or were entered in a prize draw, depending on recruitment channel.

Demographic data were collected from all participants. The sample's mean age was 24.7 years (SD = 6.3), with a range from 18 to 58. Female participants comprised 56% of the sample (n = 175), male participants 42% (n = 131), and 2% identified as non-binary or preferred not to specify (n = 6). Participants reported a range of educational backgrounds: 48% were current undergraduate students, 32% had completed an undergraduate degree, and the remainder had other or no higher education. The sample included participants from multiple countries, though the largest proportions were from English-speaking countries; 61% reported being citizens of the country in which they were recruited, while the remainder were international residents.

Attendance at an international school was assessed with a single binary item asking whether the respondent had attended an international school for at least two academic years prior to age 18. Of the 312 participants, 74 (23.7%) reported such attendance, and 238 (76.3%) reported no such attendance. The international school item included a brief definition of an international school as one that enrolls students from multiple nationalities and which typically delivers an internationally oriented curriculum, to reduce misclassification.

Political standing was measured using a single-item self-placement scale. Participants were asked to place themselves on a 7-point scale from 1 (very liberal/left) to 7 (very conservative/right). This approach is commonly used in survey research to capture general ideological orientation. In the present sample, the mean political standing was 3.8 (SD = 1.5), indicating a distribution around the mid-point with modest variability. Respondents were also asked two follow-up items to validate the single-item measure, including a forced-choice item about whether they primarily thought of themselves as left or right and an item assessing specific positions on social issues, but the primary analytic variable was the single-item self-placement.

Religiosity was assessed with a three-item composite designed to capture importance of religion, frequency of religious service attendance, and frequency of private religious practice. Each item used a 7-point scale, and the composite score was computed as the mean of the three items. Internal consistency for the three-item religiosity scale was acceptable in this sample (Cronbach's alpha = .78). The mean religiosity score in the sample was 2.9 (SD = 1.6), reflecting a generally low-to-moderate level of reported religiosity across participants.

The outcome measures were pleasantness ratings for three cultural group labels: "white," "Asian," and "Middle Eastern." For each target label, participants were instructed: "Please indicate how pleasant you find people who are described using the following term" and responded on a 7-point scale from 1 (very unpleasant) to 7 (very pleasant). The order of the target labels was randomized across participants to control for order effects. The pleasantness items were treated as continuous variables for analysis. Descriptive statistics showed mean pleasantness ratings of 5.1 (SD = 1.0) for "white," 4.9 (SD = 1.2) for "Asian," and 4.3 (SD = 1.4) for "Middle Eastern."

To reduce the influence of socially desirable responding, the survey included an implicit reassurance statement about confidentiality and the separability of responses from personal identity. No direct social desirability scale was included to minimize participant burden, although participants were reminded that their honest responses were valuable. The survey also collected information on contact frequency with people from diverse backgrounds during childhood and current contact, which were included as exploratory covariates in later analyses.

The survey was programmed to require responses to all primary items to avoid missing data on main variables. Completion time averaged 12.5 minutes (SD = 4.3). Data were screened for response inconsistency and attention; 17 additional responses were excluded from the initial pool of respondents because of rapid completion (less than 3 minutes) or failing attention-check items. The 312 participants reported above-threshold engagement and were retained for primary analyses.

Ethical approval for the study protocol was obtained from an institutional review board prior to data collection, and participants provided informed consent electronically before beginning the survey. Consent materials described the voluntary nature of participation, the confidentiality of data, and the ability to withdraw at any time.

The analytic plan was specified in advance. Primary analyses tested bivariate Pearson correlations between pleasantness ratings for each cultural group and the two continuous predictors: political standing and religiosity. For the binary predictor of international school attendance, independent-samples t-tests compared mean pleasantness ratings between attendees and non-attendees for each group. Secondary analyses used multiple linear regression models predicting each pleasantness rating from political standing, religiosity, international school attendance, and demographic covariates (age, gender, and current country residence). All regression models were examined for assumption violations; residual plots were inspected and variance inflation factors (VIFs) were computed to assess multicollinearity.

Given that the original hypotheses anticipated associations in specific directions, one-tailed tests could have been justified, but two-tailed tests were used to maintain conservative inference. Alpha was set at .05 for significance testing. Effect sizes (Pearson r for correlations, Cohen's d for t-tests, and standardized beta coefficients for regressions) and 95% confidence intervals were reported to aid interpretation. When non-significant results were observed, effect size estimates and confidence intervals were emphasized to assess the plausibility of meaningful effects.

Exploratory follow-up analyses examined potential interactions between political standing and religiosity, and whether the relation of predictors to pleasantness ratings differed across the three target groups. Additional exploratory checks investigated whether current contact with members of each target group moderated any observed relations. Finally, sensitivity analyses examined whether excluding participants from outside the main national context altered the pattern of results.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first to provide context for subsequent analyses. The mean pleasantness rating for "white" targets was 5.10 (SD = 1.02). For "Asian" targets the mean pleasantness rating was 4.92 (SD = 1.18). For "Middle Eastern" targets the mean pleasantness rating was 4.29 (SD = 1.38). The distribution of political standing spanned the full 1–7 range, with a mean of 3.80 (SD = 1.50). Religiosity scores ranged from 1.0 to 7.0 with a mean of 2.87 (SD = 1.59). International school attendance was reported by 74 participants (23.7%) and not reported by 238 participants (76.3%).

Bivariate correlations were conducted between the two continuous predictors (political standing and religiosity) and the three pleasantness ratings. Political standing was not significantly correlated with pleasantness ratings for "white" targets (r = -0.03, 95% CI [-0.12, 0.06], p = .52). Political standing was not significantly correlated with pleasantness ratings for "Asian" targets (r = -0.04, 95% CI [-0.13, 0.05], p = .39). Political standing was not significantly correlated with pleasantness ratings for "Middle Eastern" targets (r = -0.06, 95% CI [-0.15, 0.03], p = .18). All correlations were small in magnitude and confidence intervals included zero.

Religiosity similarly showed no significant bivariate correlations with pleasantness ratings. The correlation between religiosity and pleasantness for "white" targets was r = 0.02 (95% CI [-0.07, 0.11], p = .71). The correlation between religiosity and pleasantness for "Asian" targets was r = -0.05 (95% CI [-0.14, 0.04], p = .26). The correlation between religiosity and pleasantness for "Middle Eastern" targets was r = -0.08 (95% CI [-0.17, 0.01], p = .09). Although the association with Middle Eastern targets approached the conventional threshold, it did not reach statistical significance and the effect size remained small.

Independent-samples t-tests compared pleasantness ratings for participants who did and did not attend an international school. For "white" targets, international school attendees had a mean pleasantness of 5.06 (SD = 1.03) and non-attendees had a mean of 5.12 (SD = 1.02), yielding t(310) = -0.45, p = .65, Cohen's d = -0.06, 95% CI for d [-0.29, 0.17]. For "Asian" targets, attendees had a mean of 4.95 (SD = 1.20) and non-attendees had a mean of 4.91 (SD = 1.17), yielding t(310) = 0.28, p = .78, Cohen's d = 0.05, 95% CI for d [-0.17, 0.28]. For "Middle Eastern" targets, attendees had a mean of 4.36 (SD = 1.42) and non-attendees had a mean of 4.26 (SD = 1.36), yielding t(310) = 0.62, p = .54, Cohen's d = 0.11, 95% CI for d [-0.12, 0.34]. None of these comparisons reached statistical significance and the estimated effect sizes were small.

Given the potential for demographic confounds, multiple linear regression models predicted each pleasantness rating from political standing, religiosity, international school attendance (coded 0/1), age, gender (dummy coded as female = 1, male = 0, non-binary excluded from dummy coding due to small n), and current country residence (domestic vs international relative to recruitment country). Variance inflation factors were below 2.0 in all models, indicating low multicollinearity. Residuals were approximately normally distributed and plots did not reveal major outliers.

In the regression predicting pleasantness for "white" targets, the overall model was not significant, R^2 = .02, F(6,305) = 1.25, p = .28. Political standing had an unstandardized beta of -0.02 (SE = 0.03), standardized beta = -0.03, p = .52. Religiosity had an unstandardized beta of 0.01 (SE = 0.03), standardized beta = 0.02, p = .71. International school attendance had an unstandardized beta of -0.04 (SE = 0.11), standardized beta = -0.03, p = .71. None of the covariates were significant predictors.

The regression predicting pleasantness for "Asian" targets also yielded a non-significant overall model, R^2 = .03, F(6,305) = 1.61, p = .14. Political standing had an unstandardized beta of -0.03 (SE = 0.03), standardized beta = -0.04, p = .38. Religiosity had an unstandardized beta of -0.04 (SE = 0.03), standardized beta = -0.06, p = .23. International school attendance had an unstandardized beta of 0.05 (SE = 0.12), standardized beta = 0.05, p = .69. Age and gender were not significant predictors.

For "Middle Eastern" targets, the regression model explained slightly more variance but remained non-significant overall, R^2 = .05, F(6,305) = 2.49, p = .02; however, when correcting for multiple comparisons across the three models, this effect did not survive conservative correction. In this model political standing had an unstandardized beta of -0.07 (SE = 0.04), standardized beta = -0.09, p = .07. Religiosity had an unstandardized beta of -0.06 (SE = 0.04), standardized beta = -0.08, p = .10. International school attendance had an unstandardized beta of 0.09 (SE = 0.16), standardized beta = 0.06, p = .57. Although the coefficients for political standing and religiosity were in the expected negative direction for Middle Eastern targets, neither reached statistical significance at the .05 level.

Because the relation between political standing and attitudes might be non-linear or might differ across specific issue domains, follow-up exploratory analyses examined the two follow-up political items and the social-issues measure. These exploratory correlations were small and non-significant for all three target groups (all |r| < .10, all p > .10), providing no support for a stronger relation when political standing was operationalized differently in the present sample.

Interaction terms between political standing and religiosity were tested in regression models to determine whether combinations of ideological and religious identity predict pleasantness ratings. Interaction terms were added separately to each model, and none of the interactions were significant predictors for any of the three target groups (all p > .20). Simple slopes analyses did not reveal notable patterning of effects across combinations of high and low religiosity and political standing.

Exploratory moderation analyses tested whether current contact frequency with members of each target group moderated any relations between predictors and pleasantness ratings. Contact frequency was modestly positively associated with pleasantness ratings for each group (rs = .12 to .18, p-values between .02 and .06), but when included as a covariate the primary relations between political standing, religiosity, and international school attendance remained non-significant. Interaction terms between contact and the primary predictors were uniformly non-significant.

Sensitivity analyses excluded the 41 participants who reported residence outside the principal recruitment country at the time of the survey. These restricted-sample analyses produced the same pattern of null results as the full sample: bivariate correlations and group comparisons remained small and non-significant. Similarly, analyses that excluded participants who reported limited English fluency (n = 12) showed no meaningful change.

Effect size estimates for the primary predictors across the various analyses were uniformly small. The largest standardized beta observed in any of the primary regression models was -.09 for political standing predicting pleasantness for Middle Eastern targets, and the 95% confidence interval for this estimate included zero. Confidence intervals for the effect sizes generally excluded large effects, suggesting that the study had sufficient power to detect small-to-moderate effects but not minute ones. Post-hoc power calculations indicated that with N = 312 the study had 80% power to detect correlations of approximately r = .16 or larger at alpha = .05 (two-tailed). Observed correlations were below this threshold.

Given the non-significant findings, equivalence testing was conducted for the main bivariate relations to assess whether the observed effects were statistically equivalent to zero within a small effect bound (r = .10). Results of equivalence tests suggested that for pleasantness ratings of "white" and "Asian" targets the observed correlations with political standing and religiosity could be considered statistically equivalent to zero at the chosen bound. For "Middle Eastern" targets the equivalence tests were inconclusive for some predictors, given slightly larger observed effect estimates, but still did not support meaningful non-zero relations.

Overall, the results indicated no significant correlations between pleasantness ratings of the three target groups and political standing or religiosity, and no significant effect of attendance at an international school on these ratings. Secondary and exploratory analyses did not reveal robust moderators or interactions that altered this conclusion.

Discussion

The present study set out to examine whether self-reported political standing, religiosity, and attendance at an international school were associated with explicit pleasantness ratings of three broad cultural groups: white, Asian, and Middle Eastern. In contrast to the initial hypotheses, the pattern of results was largely null. Bivariate correlations between the continuous predictors and each pleasantness rating were small and non-significant. Comparisons between those who had and had not attended international schools likewise uncovered no significant differences. Regression analyses controlling for demographic covariates reproduced the same pattern of non-significance, and exploratory interaction and moderation tests did not identify consistent conditional effects. Overall effect sizes were small and confidence intervals commonly included zero, and equivalence testing suggested that for many relations the effects were statistically indistinguishable from negligible within a small-effect bound.

These null findings have several implications for theory and empirical work on predictors of explicit intergroup attitude. First, they suggest that in this sample and with these measures, explicit pleasantness ratings of broad cultural group labels were not strongly patterned by general political orientation or by a global index of religiosity. This stands in contrast to some prior literature reporting associations between conservatism or high religiosity and negative attitudes toward certain out-groups. The discrepancy may reflect several factors, including differences in measures, sample composition, social desirability, or cultural context. Political ideology is multifaceted, and a single-item left-right self-placement may dilute the variance associated with cultural issue positions specifically relevant to evaluations of these groups. Similarly, the three-item religiosity composite may not have captured the specific doctrinal or normative aspects of religious commitment that influence intergroup attitudes in other studies.

Second, the absence of a detectable international-school effect raises questions about the robustness and longevity of any socializing influences of international education on explicit attitudes. While international schools are theorized to foster cosmopolitan perspectives and cross-cultural empathy, not all international school experiences are equal, and the simple binary indicator used here cannot capture quality, curriculum emphasis, peer composition, or the age at which international exposure occurred. It may be that international schooling influences other outcomes—such as cosmopolitan identity, intercultural skills, or openness to experience—that are not directly reflected in a single pleasantness rating of group labels. Alternatively, any influence of international schooling might attenuate over time, particularly if individuals subsequently live and work in less cosmopolitan contexts.

Third, the methodological choices inherent in the present design likely influenced the pattern of results. Explicit pleasantness ratings are subject to social desirability and may reflect normative responses rather than deep-seated evaluative tendencies. In many contexts, people are motivated to report positive or neutral attitudes toward various cultural groups due to social norms against prejudice. This social desirability pressure could compress variance in responses and weaken correlations with individual differences in political ideology or religiosity. Implicit measures, behavioral indicators, or richer attitudinal scales might reveal associations that explicit pleasantness ratings mask.

Sample composition may also have played a role. The sample in this study skewed young and relatively well-educated, with many participants recruited through online channels and including a sizable proportion of current students. This demographic profile could be associated with more liberal social norms and generally positive affect toward different cultural groups, reducing variability in pleasantness scores. Additionally, though the sample included participants from multiple national backgrounds, the majority were from English-speaking countries, which may limit generalizability and reduce the opportunity to detect cross-cultural variability in the predictors of interest.

The choice of target categories is another important consideration. Broad labels such as "Asian" and "Middle Eastern" encompass diverse nationalities, ethnicities, and religious traditions. Respondents may respond differently to, for example, "Japanese," "Indian," "Turkish," or "Iranian" labels, but these distinctions are lost in broad categories. It is possible that political standing or religiosity relates differentially to attitudes toward specific national or religious sub-groups within these broader categories, and such variation would be obscured by aggregation. Future research could investigate more fine-grained target labels or examine whether predictors relate to specific stereotypes or perceived threat dimensions rather than global pleasantness.

Another plausible explanation for the null results concerns the match between predictor and outcome levels of specificity. Political orientation and religiosity are broad constructs that may more strongly predict responses to politically salient issues (such as immigration policy or religious freedoms) than general pleasantness judgments. If participants conceptualized the pleasantness items as unrelated to policy or symbolic conflicts, then ideological predictors might not be strongly engaged. Conversely, measures capturing perceptions of threat, symbolic distance, or policy support might align more closely with political standing and reveal meaningful relations.

It is also worth considering measurement sensitivity and statistical power. Although the sample size provided adequate power to detect small-to-moderate correlations, very small effects could remain undetected. Equivalence testing suggested that observed effects for some relations were sufficiently small to be practically negligible, but for predictors where confidence intervals were wider, there remains uncertainty about extremely small effects. Future studies with larger samples could more precisely estimate small associations or test for heterogeneity across subpopulations.

The lack of interaction effects between political standing and religiosity indicates that combinations of ideological and religious identity did not jointly predict pleasantness ratings in this dataset. However, interactions might emerge under different operationalizations—for instance, if religiosity were measured in terms of orthodoxy or fundamentalism rather than general religiosity, or if political standing were separated into social and economic dimensions. The present null interactions therefore do not rule out more complex conditional patterns under alternative measures.

Despite the predominantly null outcomes, the observed modest correlations between contact frequency and pleasantness ratings hint at the continuing relevance of direct intergroup contact. Contact was positively related to pleasantness across target groups, albeit weakly. This is consistent with contact theory and suggests that current, concrete interactions with members of other groups may exert more influence on explicit affect than broad background variables such as past schooling or general ideological placement. Interventions seeking to improve intergroup attitudes might therefore do well to facilitate quality cross-group contact rather than rely solely on educational or identity-based mechanisms.

The study has several limitations that should be acknowledged and that point to directions for future research. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Longitudinal or experimental designs could better assess whether international schooling causally shapes attitudes, or whether pre-existing traits select individuals into particular educational pathways. Second, reliance on explicit self-reports limits insight into implicit or behavioral expressions of prejudice; future work should incorporate multiple methods, including implicit measures and real-world behavioral indicators. Third, the binary assessment of international school attendance and the broad cultural categories used for targets could be refined in follow-up studies. Detailed histories of international exposure, including duration, curriculum type, and cultural mix, along with more specific target labels, would yield greater specificity.

Future research could also examine potential mediators of any prospective associations. For instance, if international schooling is related to attitudes, mediating variables could include intercultural competence, language proficiency, or global identity. Similarly, political standing might influence intergroup evaluations through perceived symbolic threat or endorsement of hierarchical social values; measuring these mediators could illuminate pathways. Experimental manipulations that activate political or religious identities before measuring attitudes could test whether these identities causally influence evaluations in specific contexts.

Comparative cross-national research would also be valuable. The relations between ideology, religiosity, and intergroup attitudes are context-dependent, and studies across diverse national contexts could reveal heterogeneity in patterns. In political climates where cultural issues are highly salient, ideology may be a stronger predictor than in contexts where such issues are less contested. Sampling from a broader range of demographic strata and geographic regions would therefore help clarify the boundary conditions for the predictors examined here.

Methodologically, researchers should consider supplementing pleasantness ratings with measures of perceived threat (economic, symbolic, or security-related), perceived cultural distance, and stereotype content (warmth and competence). These constructs may better capture the cognitive and affective processes by which ideology and religiosity relate to evaluations of groups. Moreover, examining downstream outcomes such as policy preferences, voting behavior, or willingness to engage in intergroup cooperation would connect attitudinal findings to social consequences.

Practically, the null findings in the present study suggest that simple assumptions about the predictive power of general political standing or religiosity for explicit affective evaluations may be unwarranted in some samples and contexts. Policymakers and practitioners aiming to foster social cohesion should be cautious about relying on broad identity appeals or educational credentials alone, and should instead focus on fostering meaningful contact and addressing specific sources of intergroup tension. Educational programs that incorporate structured intergroup cooperation, critical reflection on stereotypes, and long-term engagement may be more likely to engender durable positive attitudes than merely exposing students to diverse populations absent pedagogical frameworks.

In conclusion, the present study found no evidence that political standing, religiosity, or attendance at an international school were strongly associated with explicit pleasantness ratings of white, Asian, or Middle Eastern groups in a sample of adult respondents. While these null results do not rule out context-specific or small effects, they suggest that the determinants of explicit intergroup affect may be more complex and contingent than often presumed. Future research should employ more nuanced measures, longitudinal and experimental designs, and multi-method approaches to clarify the conditions under which ideology, religion, and educational background shape evaluations of cultural groups.
