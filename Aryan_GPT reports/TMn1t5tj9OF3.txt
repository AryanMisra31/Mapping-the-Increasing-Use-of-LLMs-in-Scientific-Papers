Introduction

Memory for spoken language operates at multiple levels of representation, and the durability of those representations varies with the level of representation and the functional demands placed on them. Early work in cognitive psychology demonstrated that information presented in language can be stored briefly in surface form, whereas deeper semantic representations persist longer and are more robust over time. One influential strand of research suggests that the syntactic and lexical form of sentences is retained only transiently and that, once a semantic interpretation has been constructed, the surface form tends to be forgotten. This pattern was described in classic recognition studies in which participants listened to passages and later judged whether test sentences were identical to earlier target sentences or had been changed in various ways. In those studies, participants were relatively insensitive to form changes after a short delay but remained sensitive to semantic changes, a dissociation that has been taken as evidence for multiple levels of representation in language memory. The distinction between surface form, propositional content, and situation model has shaped theoretical views about how language comprehension and memory interact, with many models positing that an initial veridical trace of form decays while the abstracted meaning remains. The proposal that form is transient while meaning persists is not only interesting for theoretical analyses of comprehension but also has implications for understanding individual differences in language processing and memory. If the maintenance of form requires active resources, then individual differences in working memory capacity may influence the probability of retaining surface structure versus abstract meaning. Working memory has been implicated in many aspects of language comprehension, particularly for tasks that require maintenance of information over brief intervals or that impose processing demands beyond automatic integration. Memory for syntactic structure could plausibly draw on short-term capacity to a greater degree than memory for meaning, which may be consolidated into a more stable representation of the discourse. Consequently, individuals with greater working memory capacity might be better able to hold onto surface syntactic or lexical details for longer, whereas individuals with limited capacity might lose these details more rapidly but nonetheless retain the semantic gist. Empirical evidence concerning the relationship between working memory capacity and different levels of linguistic memory has been mixed, and systematic replications of classic paradigms with explicit measures of capacity are relatively scarce. The classic experiments that inspired this line of reasoning typically focused on group-level phenomena and did not examine individual differences in capacity systematically, leaving open the question of whether capacity is a reliable predictor of memory for form versus meaning. Moreover, advances in theory have emphasized the need to consider task demands, timing, and the nature of the memory test when interpreting patterns of forgetting across representational levels. Some recent studies have used recognition paradigms with parametric manipulations of delay and type of change and have reported results consistent with the pattern that surface form decays rapidly while meaning is retained, but finer-grained analyses suggest that the boundary between form and meaning is influenced by attention, rehearsal, and the availability of working memory resources. Given the longstanding theoretical importance of the surface-versus-meaning distinction and the potential role of working memory capacity, it is valuable to revisit classic paradigms with explicit measures of individual differences and with systematic manipulations of temporal position and change type. A direct replication and extension of the classical recognition paradigm can address several open questions simultaneously: it can test whether prior findings about the persistence of semantic content replicate under current conditions; it can examine whether syntactic and lexical forms decay at different rates; and it can assess whether individual differences in working memory capacity predict performance on recognition tasks that tap different representational levels. In addition, it is important to manipulate the position of the target sentence in a passage because position influences temporal delay before the retention test and therefore interacts with forgetting processes. Past studies placed target sentences at various positions and reported differential patterns of retention as a function of serial position, suggesting that some of the apparent decay of surface form could be due to temporal separation rather than an intrinsic property of representation. A well-controlled replication should therefore include target sentences at the beginning, middle, and end of short passages to examine how delay influences memory at each representational level. The design must also distinguish between types of change at test, including identical, syntactic, semantic, and lexical alterations, so that the specific patterns of detection and misclassification can be analyzed. To probe individual differences in working memory, a standard and widely used measure such as forward digit span can provide a simple index of capacity that has been linked to verbal maintenance and rehearsal processes. Although digit span is not a comprehensive measure of working memory, it is appropriate for an initial test of whether short-term verbal capacity relates to the retention of linguistic form and content. If digit span correlates positively with accuracy in detecting syntactic or lexical changes relative to semantic changes, this would support the idea that capacity helps preserve surface structure. Conversely, a lack of association would argue against a strong role for capacity in maintaining surface details once meaning has been derived. Beyond correlations, the design enables factorial analyses to test for main effects and interactions of change type and position, and for potential moderation by working memory capacity. In planning such a study, it is also important to consider potential sources of variability that might attenuate effects relative to classic reports, including procedural differences, stimulus quality, participant attention, and practice effects, all of which can reduce power or alter patterns of response. Therefore, a careful replication should pre-register, where possible, the analyses and should include sufficient sample size to detect effects that are of plausible magnitude based on earlier work. The present study therefore set out to replicate and extend the classic recognition paradigm by presenting participants with spoken passages that contained a target sentence placed at the beginning, middle, or end, and by following each passage with a test sentence that was either identical or changed in one of three ways: syntactic form, semantic content, or lexical items. Participants were asked to state whether the test sentence was identical to the earlier sentence and, if they judged it to be changed, to classify the type of change. In addition to the recognition task, participants completed a digit span test to quantify verbal working memory capacity. The aims of the study were threefold: first, to test whether the classical pattern of stronger retention of semantic information relative to surface form replicates; second, to examine how serial position affects retention of different representational levels; and third, to investigate whether working memory capacity, as indexed by digit span, predicts recognition performance for syntactic and lexical forms relative to semantic content. It was hypothesized that, in line with earlier findings, participants would detect semantic changes more reliably than syntactic or lexical changes, particularly when the target sentence was separated from the test by a longer interval. It was also predicted that increased digit span would be associated with better detection of syntactic and lexical changes, reflecting a capacity advantage in maintaining surface-level linguistic detail. Finally, it was anticipated that syntactic changes might be more detectable than purely lexical substitutions because syntactic violations could affect sentence structure in a manner that disrupts the mapping to meaning, whereas lexical changes might sometimes be assimilated into the existing situation model. Methodological details were carefully specified to approximate earlier procedures while incorporating improvements in stimulus control and measurement of individual differences. Sampling aimed to recruit enough participants to afford reasonable power for within-subject comparisons and correlational analyses. Stimuli were constructed to allow multiple variants of each target sentence so that change types could be implemented without introducing confounds such as prosodic differences or semantic oddity. The procedure included practice items and explicit instructions to ensure that participants understood the distinction between identical and changed sentences and the subtypes of changes. Response options were coded to capture both the initial identical/changed judgment and the subsequent classification when a change was reported, permitting analyses of sensitivity and classification accuracy at multiple representational levels. Data analysis plans included repeated-measures ANOVAs to test for main effects of change type and position, as well as Pearson correlations and regression analyses to test for associations between digit span and recognition accuracy. Given the possibility that categorical ANOVAs might fail to detect subtle individual differences, continuous analyses of digit span were prioritized alongside any median-split comparisons. The overall project thus combined a principled replication of a classic experimental paradigm with an explicit test of the role of working memory capacity in maintaining different levels of linguistic representation.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from a local community sample, and participation rewarded with course credit or a modest monetary payment. A total of 94 individuals participated in the experiment after screening for native English fluency and normal hearing or corrected-to-normal hearing. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 55 years, and both sexes were represented. All participants provided informed consent and the protocol was approved by the institution's human subjects review board. The experiment took place in a quiet laboratory room for the majority of participants and in a controlled testing suite for the remainder; all participants were tested in a distraction-minimized environment. Stimuli consisted of 36 short passages, each approximately three to five sentences in length, and each passage contained one designated target sentence that could appear in the first, second, or final position. For each target sentence, four test-sentence variants were constructed: an identical version that matched the recorded target sentence verbatim; a syntactic-change version in which grammatical structure was altered while preserving core propositional content; a semantic-change version in which the core meaning of the sentence was altered; and a lexical-change version in which one or two content words were substituted with synonyms or near-synonyms to create a surface change without changing the propositional content substantially. Care was taken to construct lexical substitutions such that the resulting sentence remained plausible in context but differed at the lexical level, and syntactic changes involved reversible changes in active/passive or phrase order that preserved meaning where intended or altered meaning where intended. All recorded stimuli were produced by a trained speaker in a neutral prosody and normalized for amplitude; the speaker produced the sentences with natural, fluent intonation to mimic ordinary listening conditions. Passage versions were counterbalanced across participants using a Latin square design so that each target sentence appeared at each serial position equally often across the sample and so that each test-sentence type followed a target sentence equally often across participants. The experimental design was therefore within-subjects for both position (three levels: beginning, middle, end) and change type (four levels: identical, syntactic, semantic, lexical). Each participant completed 36 experimental trials, with the distribution of target positions and change types balanced across trials. In addition to the main trials, each participant completed four practice trials with feedback to familiarize them with the task and the response format. The procedure on each trial began with listeners hearing the passage through headphones at a comfortable listening level. Following the end of the passage, a brief fixed delay of 3 seconds was imposed before the test sentence was played, with the delay intended to standardize the interval between passage and test across trials while preserving the temporal differences induced by target sentence position. After the test sentence, participants were prompted to make a binary judgment about whether the test sentence was identical to the earlier target sentence or had been changed. Responses were collected via keyboard press and were self-paced, but participants were instructed to respond as accurately as possible. If participants indicated that the sentence had been changed, they were then prompted to categorize the change as syntactic, semantic, or lexical; this follow-up classification response was also collected via keyboard. No feedback was provided during the experimental trials. The digit span test was administered following the recognition task to avoid strategic rehearsal during the main trials. Digit span was assessed using a standard forward digit span procedure in which sequences of digits of increasing length were read aloud by the experimenter and participants were required to repeat the sequence in order. The test began with strings of 2 digits and increased up to 9 digits, with two trials at each length; the test terminated after two failures at a given length. The digit span score was defined as the longest string length at which the participant correctly recalled at least one of the two trials, consistent with common scoring methods. Additionally, total correct items were recorded to provide a continuous measure of performance for correlational analyses. The experimenter monitored for compliance and ensured that participants were comfortable and attentive during the digit span administration. Data were collected and logged for each participant, including response times for the identical/changed judgment and for the change-type classification when applicable, although the main analyses focused on accuracy rather than latencies. Prior to the main analyses, data underwent screening for outliers and for participants who failed to follow instructions, such as by responding at chance across all trials or by having excessive missing responses; four participants were excluded for these reasons, leading to a final sample size of 90. Accuracy on the practice trials was examined to ensure task comprehension; all included participants achieved above-chance performance on practice items. For the primary analyses, accuracy was computed as the proportion of trials on which participants correctly identified whether the test sentence was identical or changed, collapsed across change types for the overall metric and computed separately for each change type for finer-grained analyses. For trials where participants indicated that the test sentence had changed, classification accuracy was computed as the proportion of correct change-type labels relative to the total number of change decisions. Statistical analyses included repeated-measures ANOVAs with within-subject factors of position (beginning, middle, end) and change type (identical, syntactic, semantic, lexical), and with Greenhouse-Geisser corrections where sphericity assumptions were violated. Effect sizes were reported in terms of partial eta squared for ANOVA effects and Cohen's d for simple contrasts. Pearson correlations between digit span scores and accuracy metrics were calculated to test the hypothesized associations between working memory capacity and recognition performance. In addition, hierarchical linear regression was used to test whether digit span explained variance in recognition accuracy above and beyond potential covariates such as age and educational attainment, which were recorded at intake. Secondary analyses included median-split comparisons of high and low digit-span groups to provide an ANOVA-friendly test of capacity effects, although primary inferential claims rested on continuous-variable analyses. All statistical tests were two-tailed and an alpha level of 0.05 was used for significance, with Bonferroni adjustments applied for multiple post-hoc comparisons where appropriate. Finally, exploratory item-level mixed-effects analyses were conducted to assess whether specific stimulus features, such as sentence length or lexical frequency of substituted words, influenced recognition performance; these exploratory models included random intercepts for participants and items and were fit using standard maximum-likelihood procedures. The entire testing session lasted approximately 45 minutes for most participants, and participants were debriefed at the end of the session concerning the goals of the study.

Results

Descriptive statistics for recognition accuracy indicated that overall performance varied with change type in a pattern that qualitatively resembled the classic findings but with attenuated differences. Mean proportion correct across all trials was 0.72 (SD = 0.11) when participants made the identical/changed judgment; however, performance varied by change type, with semantic changes detected at higher rates than syntactic or lexical changes. Specifically, mean correct detection rates were 0.85 (SD = 0.10) for semantic change trials, 0.68 (SD = 0.14) for syntactic change trials, and 0.65 (SD = 0.15) for lexical change trials. Identical trials, on which the test sentence matched the target exactly, were correctly judged as identical on 0.70 (SD = 0.13) of trials, reflecting some degree of false alarms and conservative responding on the part of some participants. When accuracy was broken down by serial position of the target sentence, the pattern suggested diminished detection of syntactic and lexical changes when the target sentence had been presented earlier in the passage, but the effect sizes were small. For semantic changes, detection rates were relatively stable across positions, with means of 0.86, 0.84, and 0.85 for beginning, middle, and end positions, respectively. For syntactic changes, detection rates were 0.70, 0.68, and 0.66 for beginning, middle, and end positions, respectively, and for lexical changes the corresponding values were 0.67, 0.66, and 0.62. These cell means suggested the predicted rank order of change-type detection but did not show strikingly large serial-position effects. A repeated-measures ANOVA with factors of change type (identical, syntactic, semantic, lexical) and position (beginning, middle, end) was conducted on the proportion correct scores. The main effect of change type was in the predicted direction but did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance, F(3, 267) = 2.12, p = .099, partial eta squared = .023, indicating a small effect size and variability across participants. The main effect of position was also non-significant, F(2, 178) = 1.48, p = .231, partial eta squared = .016, suggesting that the serial position of the target sentence did not produce robust differences in recognition accuracy in this sample. The interaction between change type and position was likewise non-significant, F(6, 534) = 1.20, p = .305, partial eta squared = .013. Inspection of the Greenhouse-Geisser corrected values produced similar null conclusions. Given the non-significant omnibus ANOVA, post-hoc contrasts were performed cautiously and interpreted as exploratory; pairwise comparisons indicated that semantic change detection was higher than lexical change detection across positions, t(89) = 2.05, p = .043, Cohen's d = 0.22, but this effect was modest and would not survive conservative correction for multiple comparisons. Classification accuracy for trials judged as changed showed a comparable pattern: when participants indicated that a sentence had changed, they correctly labeled semantic changes 0.82 (SD = 0.12) of the time, syntactic changes 0.60 (SD = 0.17) of the time, and lexical changes 0.58 (SD = 0.18) of the time. These classification data suggest that participants were relatively good at identifying semantic changes when they occurred but had difficulty discriminating between syntactic and lexical change types. Reaction time data on the identical/changed decision were examined but not subjected to formal hypotheses; median response times were approximately 1.8 seconds after test-sentence offset and did not differ significantly across change types, F(3, 267) = 1.04, p = .375. Digit span scores in the final sample ranged from 4 to 9 digits with a mean of 6.1 (SD = 1.2), which is consistent with typical young adult samples for forward digit span. Pearson correlations between digit span and overall recognition accuracy revealed a modest positive association, r = .24, p = .023, indicating that individuals with higher digit span tended to perform better on the recognition task overall. The correlation between digit span and detection of syntactic changes was r = .27, p = .011, which was slightly larger than the correlation with lexical change detection, r = .20, p = .056, and larger than the correlation with semantic change detection, r = .12, p = .25. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that working memory capacity is more strongly associated with retention of surface-level syntactic form than with retention of semantic content. However, when digit span was entered as a covariate in an ANCOVA alongside the within-subject factors of change type and position, the main effect of digit span was not significant, F(1, 88) = 2.79, p = .098, and there were no significant interactions between digit span and either within-subject factor. To probe the discrepancy between the significant correlations and the non-significant ANCOVA, a median-split was performed to create high and low digit-span groups; an independent-samples t-test comparing overall recognition accuracy between groups showed a marginal difference, t(88) = 1.65, p = .102, Cohen's d = 0.35, indicating that categorical grouping reduced sensitivity relative to the continuous analysis and was underpowered to detect the modest effect size. Hierarchical regression analyses were conducted to test whether digit span explained incremental variance in syntactic change detection after accounting for age and education; digit span accounted for an additional 4.8% of variance in syntactic detection accuracy after controlling for age and education, ΔR2 = .048, Fchange(1,86) = 4.35, p = .040, suggesting a small but statistically reliable contribution in the regression framework. Item-level mixed-effects exploratory models indicated that sentence length and lexical frequency of substituted words contributed modestly to variance in detection rates, with longer sentences being slightly harder to monitor for surface changes and low-frequency substitutions slightly easier to detect as changes; however, these item-level predictors did not alter the pattern of correlations between digit span and syntactic detection. To assess potential speed-accuracy tradeoffs, correlations between response times and accuracy were computed and were generally small and negative (higher accuracy associated with slightly faster responses), suggesting that better performers were not simply trading speed for correctness. Analyses excluding trials with extreme response times yielded similar accuracy means and did not qualitatively change the inferential outcomes. Finally, a reliability analysis of the recognition task indicated acceptable internal consistency across trials, with Cronbach's alpha of .72 for the overall accuracy measure, supporting the suitability of the task for correlational analyses with working memory measures.

Discussion

The present study revisited a classic paradigm examining the durability of surface form versus semantic content in memory for spoken language and extended that paradigm by including a direct measure of verbal working memory capacity. The data produced a pattern that was broadly consistent with the theoretical distinction between transient surface traces and more persistent semantic representations, but the effects were subdued relative to earlier reports and did not reach statistical significance in omnibus ANOVAs. Correlational analyses revealed a modest but statistically reliable association between digit span and recognition performance, particularly for syntactic change detection, suggesting that individuals with greater short-term verbal capacity tended to retain some aspects of surface structure better than individuals with lower capacity. At the same time, semantic changes were detected at relatively high rates across positions, and classification of semantic changes was more accurate than classification of syntactic or lexical changes. Taken together, these results provide tempered support for the general claim that meaning is relatively enduring and that form fades, while also pointing to the importance of individual differences in capacity for preserving surface-level details. It is important to acknowledge that the omnibus ANOVAs did not yield significant main effects or interactions, a finding that contrasts with some classical findings and invites careful consideration of methodological and theoretical factors that might account for the discrepancy. One possibility is that the magnitude of the form-versus-meaning dissociation is smaller than originally estimated and therefore requires larger samples or more sensitive designs to detect robustly. The sample in the present study was moderate in size and sufficient to detect small-to-moderate correlations, but the within-subject cell sizes for particular combinations of position and change type may have limited power to detect subtle interactions. Another methodological factor is that the delay between the passage and the test sentence was fixed at three seconds across trials in order to standardize temporal intervals, whereas some classical studies allowed variable delays that might have accentuated decay effects for surface form. The choice to standardize delay was made to control for extraneous variance, but it may have reduced the opportunity to observe strong serial-position effects driven by longer intervening material. Additionally, the recording quality and prosodic naturalness of the stimuli can influence listeners' encoding and retention of surface features, and even small deviations from natural speech patterns can alter how participants attend to prosodic or lexical cues. Although stimuli were produced by a trained speaker and normalized for amplitude, the laboratory setting and the use of a single speaker may have yielded relatively homogeneous prosody that reduced salient surface markers that participants could use at test. It is also possible that modern participants, exposed to frequent multitasking and brief auditory snippets in daily life, approach listening tasks differently than earlier cohorts and may be less inclined to attend to verbatim surface form unless specifically instructed to do so. The modest correlations between digit span and recognition performance, especially for syntactic detection, are theoretically meaningful even though they are small in magnitude. These correlations are consistent with the idea that maintaining syntactic form requires active short-term storage or rehearsal-like processes and that individuals with higher capacity have a slight advantage in preserving syntactic structure over short intervals. The lack of a similarly robust correlation with semantic detection accords with the view that semantic representations are consolidated more readily into a stable discourse representation that is less dependent on transient maintenance. However, correlation does not establish causality, and capacity may covary with other traits, such as attentional control, language experience, or motivation, which could also influence performance on recognition tasks. The hierarchical regression analysis indicated that digit span contributed incremental variance to syntactic detection beyond demographic covariates, which strengthens the interpretation that capacity has a role, but future work should include multiple measures of working memory and executive control to disentangle their respective contributions. Regarding the null omnibus ANOVAs, it is noteworthy that the continuous analyses (correlations and regression) revealed effects that were not mirrored by categorical group comparisons or by the within-subjects ANOVA. This divergence highlights the statistical advantage of preserving individual differences as continuous variables rather than dichotomizing them, as loss of information can reduce sensitivity to detect meaningful associations. The median-split comparison of high versus low digit-span groups produced only marginal differences, illustrating how categorical grouping can obscure real but modest relationships detectable in continuous data. From a theoretical perspective, the overall pattern aligns with multi-level models of language memory that posit an initial shallow surface trace and a more stable propositional-level or situation model representation. The present results replicate the key qualitative insight that semantic content remains accessible even when finer surface details are less reliably recognized, but they also suggest that the decay of surface form is not absolute and can be modestly moderated by working memory capacity. Such moderation implies a dynamic interplay between the nature of the representational content and the cognitive resources available to maintain it. The practical implications of these findings extend to contexts such as eyewitness testimony, language instruction, and memory rehabilitation, where the fidelity of surface-level recall versus gist memory matters. If working memory capacity helps preserve syntactic and lexical details, then individuals with lower capacity may be more prone to paraphrase or to lose precise wording over time, whereas individuals with higher capacity may retain verbatim forms longer when needed. However, because the present effects were modest, these implications should be considered tentative and contingent on replication in larger and more diverse samples. Several specific limitations of the current study warrant emphasis and point to directions for future research. First, the use of forward digit span as the sole index of working memory provides a narrow window on capacity and does not capture the executive-attentional components of working memory implicated in complex language processing. Future studies should include a broader battery of working memory tasks, such as complex span measures, operation span, and tasks that index attentional control, to parse which aspects of capacity are most relevant to surface retention. Second, the present study focused on short delays with a standardized 3-second interval and varied serial position via where the target occurred within the passage; future work should manipulate delay parametrically and include longer intervals to map the time course of decay for different representational levels more fully. Third, the classification task required participants to label the type of change when they perceived one, but some misclassification could reflect difficulty in metacognitive labeling rather than failures of memory per se; future designs could incorporate forced-choice recognition where participants select among multiple alternatives to reduce reliance on metalinguistic labeling. Fourth, item-level variability in sentence complexity, lexical frequency, and plausibility likely influenced detection rates, and while exploratory mixed-effects analyses addressed some of these factors, future studies should systematically manipulate these stimulus properties to understand their impact. Fifth, the present sample, drawn largely from a university community, limits generalizability, and replication in more diverse populations would test the robustness of the observed associations. Beyond these methodological refinements, there are several promising theoretical extensions. One avenue is to examine the neural correlates of the retention of surface form versus semantic content using neuroimaging methods; such studies could reveal whether sustained activity in language-related regions or frontoparietal working memory networks predicts verbatim retention. Another direction is to investigate developmental changes in the relationship between working memory and language memory across the lifespan, as children and older adults may show different patterns of reliance on capacity for maintaining surface form. Additionally, experimental manipulations that encourage or discourage rehearsal of surface form at encoding could clarify to what extent active maintenance strategies mitigate decay and whether strategy use interacts with capacity to predict retention. It would also be informative to test whether training interventions targeting working memory lead to improved maintenance of surface detail in language tasks, though prior training studies suggest limited transfer, so such effects should be evaluated cautiously. Finally, integrating computational models that simulate the decay of surface traces and the consolidation of meaning with individual- difference parameters for capacity could provide a formal framework for understanding the observed patterns and for making quantitative predictions about conditions under which form is retained or lost. In sum, the present study offers a cautious endorsement of the classical claim that meaning outlasts form in memory for spoken language while emphasizing that working memory capacity modestly supports the preservation of surface-level syntactic detail. The findings underscore the importance of combining replication of foundational paradigms with contemporary individual-differences approaches, and they highlight the need for larger-scale, methodologically rigorous investigations before strong conclusions are drawn. Future research that incorporates richer measurement of working memory, parametrically varied delays, and finely controlled stimulus manipulations will be necessary to delineate more precisely how cognitive capacity shapes the fidelity of language memory.
