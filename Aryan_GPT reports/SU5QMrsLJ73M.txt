Introduction

Working memory is widely understood as a multi-component system that temporarily holds and manipulates information to support ongoing cognitive tasks. Over the past several decades, a dominant framework has conceptualized working memory as comprising separable subsystems that specialize in different types of information and processes. One of the most influential components within this framework is the phonological loop, a specialized system proposed to maintain verbal and acoustic information through a short-lived phonological store and an articulatory rehearsal process. Empirical work pursuing the details of the phonological loop has sought to establish the separability of the phonological store and the articulatory rehearsal mechanism and to characterize how different types of interference disrupt each component’s contribution to short-term memory performance. A central finding in this literature is that both articulatory suppression, in which participants are prevented from covert rehearsal by continuously producing a trivial irrelevant utterance, and irrelevant auditory stimulation, in which non-task-related sounds are presented while participants retain verbal material, can impair short-term memory for verbal items. These two classes of interference are often taken to target different subcomponents: articulatory suppression is thought to block the rehearsal mechanism that refreshes items held in the phonological store, whereas irrelevant sounds are thought to corrupt or displace representations within the phonological store itself. The assumption that these manipulations operate through different mechanisms has led to a substantial effort to compare the magnitude and boundary conditions of articulatory suppression and auditory interference and to determine the circumstances under which one source of interference might be more disruptive than the other. Despite a large number of studies, several substantive questions remain unsettled, particularly regarding how interference that occurs after encoding influences maintenance and whether the allocation of attention to auditory stimuli modulates the degree of disruption they cause. A number of experiments have directly compared the effects of articulatory suppression and irrelevant sound on serial recall and free recall tasks, but results are mixed and frequently depend on specifics like list length, the timing and nature of the auditory distractors, and instructions about whether sounds should be attended. Some studies have reported robust effects of irrelevant sound that rival those caused by articulatory suppression, whereas others have found relatively modest sound-driven effects that are smaller than the cost of preventing rehearsal. These inconsistencies have implications for fundamental theoretical claims about the independence and relative contributions of the phonological store and articulatory rehearsal in maintaining verbal information. One question that has received comparatively less attention is the role of attention in mediating the impact of auditory interference when that interference occurs subsequent to information presentation. If auditory distractors act primarily by capturing attention and disrupting rehearsal processes, then attending to the auditory stream should exacerbate interference effects relative to instructions to ignore the sound. Conversely, if auditory interference acts more directly on the phonological store through automatic processes such as changing-state disruption of serial order codes, then attention may have limited influence on the size of the irrelevant sound effect. The distinction matters because it bears on whether the auditory disruption is a top-down mediated phenomenon or a largely stimulus-driven, automatic process that can operate without focused attention. A related issue is the temporal locus of interference: many studies introduce auditory distractors concurrently with item presentation, which conflates effects that might arise during encoding with those that affect the maintenance phase. By placing interference solely in the retention interval following presentation, we can more cleanly assess how maintenance processes are disrupted after encoding has been completed and whether interference selectively impacts rehearsal or storage. Prior research has sometimes manipulated the timing of interference and observed differing patterns, but a systematic direct comparison that places both articulatory suppression and auditory interference only in the post-encoding retention period is relatively rare. Such a design provides clearer evidence about how the phonological loop components operate during maintenance and how attention modulates the influence of auditory stimuli when they occur outside the encoding window. Another important consideration concerns the operationalization of auditory interference and attention. Auditory distraction experiments vary in whether sound streams are composed of speech, tones, or environmental noise; whether they have a changing-state structure known to be especially disruptive; and whether listeners are instructed to attend to or ignore the sounds. These methodological choices can strongly influence observed effects, and therefore a careful experimental manipulation that compares attended and unattended auditory distractors of the same structure is necessary to isolate the role of attention while holding acoustic properties constant. In addition, using a within-subjects design to compare silence, articulatory suppression, and both attended and unattended auditory distraction within the same participants reduces the influence of individual differences and provides more sensitive tests of relative effect sizes than between-subjects designs. Within such a design, expectation and practice effects must be managed, but counterbalancing and trial randomization can help mitigate order-related confounds. The present study was designed to replicate and extend a prior experiment by directly comparing articulatory suppression and auditory interference during a post-encoding retention interval and to explicitly manipulate attention to the auditory stream. In doing so, the study aimed to determine (a) whether articulatory suppression reduces serial recall when it is imposed only during the retention interval, (b) whether auditory interference imposed during the retention interval reduces recall relative to silence, and (c) whether attending to the auditory stream modulates any disruptive effect. The replication aspect was motivated by prior findings that reported selective impacts of these interference types and by the need to clarify inconsistent results across studies. The extension involved the attention manipulation, which allowed us to probe whether auditory interference could be characterized as an automatic, attention-free disruption of the phonological store or whether top-down attention allocation would meaningfully alter its size. We chose a serial recall paradigm with visually presented consonant letter lists because this task has been central to the literature on the phonological loop and is sensitive to both articulatory and auditory manipulations. We presented lists of familiar consonant letters at a steady and relatively brief rate to instantiate a typical short-term memory encoding process and used a retention interval long enough to allow rehearsal in the no-interference condition but short enough to minimize secondary long-term consolidation processes. During the retention interval, participants either remained silent, performed articulatory suppression by repeatedly vocalizing a simple syllable, listened to a stream of irrelevant spoken letters while instructed to attend to them, or listened to the same stream but were instructed to ignore it. By keeping the auditory content constant across attention conditions we isolated attentional effects from acoustic properties. We pre-registered two principal predictions: first, that articulatory suppression during the retention interval would reduce serial recall relative to the silent retention interval, reflecting the disruption of articulatory rehearsal; and second, that auditory interference during the retention interval would reduce recall relative to silence, with a larger decrement when participants were instructed to attend to the auditory stream than when they were instructed to ignore it. A secondary, exploratory question was whether articulatory suppression would have a larger effect than auditory distraction when both were confined to the maintenance period, which would be consistent with rehearsal being a more critical maintenance mechanism than the passive phonological store for the task and retention interval used here. We also anticipated examining serial position trends to see whether the pattern of disruption differed across early versus late list positions, which could provide further process-level insight. The present study therefore provides a focused test of maintenance-based interference effects on verbal short-term memory and the role of attention in mediating auditory distraction, while using a within-subjects design to enhance sensitivity to subtle differences among interference types. By restricting interference to the period after encoding and by directly manipulating attention to a constant auditory stream, the experiment is well suited to adjudicate between accounts that attribute irrelevant sound effects to automatic disruption of phonological representations versus accounts that emphasize attentional capture and its consequences for rehearsal. More broadly, clarifying these dynamics contributes to theoretical understanding of how the phonological loop’s components function during maintenance and how external stimulation interacts with internal maintenance strategies. It also helps to identify boundary conditions under which one form of interference predominates, which has both theoretical and practical implications for environments where verbal short-term memory is taxed in the presence of auditory events. Given the mixed prior results and unresolved theoretical questions, the present study sought to provide a rigorous and conceptually transparent comparison of articulatory suppression and auditory interference that isolates the role of attention and maintenance-phase disruption.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures are described first. Eighty-four undergraduate students at a university participated in exchange for course credit, and data from four participants were excluded because of failure to comply with task instructions or excessive errors on manipulation check tasks, leaving a final sample of eighty participants. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and self-reported normal hearing; anyone who reported uncorrected hearing loss or neurological conditions was excluded. The study was approved by the institutional ethics board and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The experimental design used a within-subjects manipulation of retention-interval activity with four conditions: silence (no interference), articulatory suppression, attended auditory distraction, and unattended auditory distraction. The order of conditions was counterbalanced across participants using a Latin square procedure to mitigate order effects. Each participant completed eighty trials in total, split evenly across the four conditions, with twenty trials per condition. Trials from different conditions were pseudo-randomly intermixed within each block such that no condition appeared more than twice consecutively. The serial recall task used lists of visually presented consonant letters chosen to avoid phonological similarity and to conform to standard short-term memory paradigms; vowels were excluded to reduce potential chunking or word-like processing. Each trial began with a central fixation cross presented for 500 ms, followed by a sequence of seven consonant letters presented one at a time in the center of the screen for 500 ms each with an inter-stimulus interval of 250 ms. The seven-letter list length was selected because it is within the typical short-term memory capacity range and allows rehearsal to play a role without inducing floor effects. After the final letter was presented the retention interval began and lasted for 8 seconds; during that time participants engaged in the activity corresponding to the assigned condition for that trial. For the silence condition participants were instructed to remain still and prepare to recall the list; for articulatory suppression participants were instructed to repeat aloud a simple syllable ("the") at a steady pace (approximately two utterances per second) for the entire retention interval; for the attended auditory condition participants listened to a stream of spoken letters presented via headphones and were instructed to attend to the sound and try to identify a pre-specified target letter occurring occasionally within the stream, pressing a button on a response pad when they detected the target; for the unattended auditory condition participants heard the same streams but were instructed to ignore them and to focus only on maintaining the visually presented list. The auditory streams were created from recordings of consonant letters spoken in a neutral voice and had a changing-state structure, with items varying in identity and order across trials; the stream rate was synchronized so that its duration matched the 8-second retention interval. The attended and unattended auditory conditions were matched in acoustic content and timing; only the instructional set differed to manipulate attention to the auditory input. A brief practice block consisting of eight trials (two per condition) familiarized participants with the timing and procedures and confirmed that they could perform the articulatory suppression, detect targets in the attended auditory condition, and follow instructions during the unattended auditory condition. Performance in the target-detection subtask during practice was used as a manipulation check; participants who performed at chance or failed to produce the articulatory suppression utterances during practice were given additional instruction and retraining. During the actual experiment manipulations were monitored by the experimenter; participants’ vocalizations during articulatory suppression were audible to the experimenter through an adjacent microphone, and button-press responses in the attended auditory trials were recorded to ensure compliance. At the end of each trial, participants wrote down the letters they had just seen in serial order on a response sheet; written recall was used to avoid contamination of the articulatory suppression condition by overt spoken recall. The primary dependent measure was the proportion of letters correctly recalled in the correct serial position across trials in each condition; scoring followed strict serial-order criteria so that an item counted as correct only if it appeared in the response in the same ordinal position as it had during presentation. We also computed position-based accuracy to examine serial position curves and to assess whether interference effects varied by list position. Data analyses followed a pre-specified plan. We used a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with retention-interval condition (four levels) as the within-subject factor to test for overall differences among conditions. Mauchly’s test of sphericity was applied to assess the sphericity assumption; if sphericity was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied to degrees of freedom. Significant omnibus effects were followed up with planned pairwise comparisons that tested (a) articulatory suppression versus silence, (b) attended auditory versus silence, (c) unattended auditory versus silence, (d) attended versus unattended auditory, and (e) articulatory suppression versus the pooled auditory conditions. Pairwise comparisons used paired-samples t-tests with Bonferroni adjustments for multiple comparisons to control familywise error. Effect sizes for t-tests were reported as Cohen’s d for paired samples, and partial eta squared was reported for the ANOVA. In addition to traditional null-hypothesis significance testing, we conducted Bayesian paired analyses as an exploratory supplement to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis in non-significant comparisons, focusing especially on the attended versus unattended auditory contrast to evaluate whether the data supported a genuine absence of an attention effect. Finally, we examined serial position effects by computing separate ANOVAs for the first, middle, and last two serial positions and exploring interaction effects between position and interference condition. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software and scripts stored in a project repository; summary statistics and anonymized data were inspected for outliers and gross data-entry errors before analysis. Data screening identified no multivariate outliers that would warrant exclusion beyond the four participants removed for noncompliance. Compliance checks confirmed that participants in the articulatory suppression condition vocalized at an appropriate rate across the majority of trials and that the target-detection accuracy in the attended auditory condition was well above chance, confirming the effectiveness of the attention manipulation for most participants. The study was powered to detect medium-sized within-subject effects (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.40) with 80 participants, yielding approximately 80% power for pairwise contrasts at an alpha level of .05, which we judged to be appropriate for the focal comparisons.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary dependent measure, proportion of items correctly recalled in their original serial positions, are presented first. Across the eighty participants retained in the final sample mean recall accuracy was highest in the silence condition (M = 0.80, SD = 0.12), lower in the attended auditory condition (M = 0.76, SD = 0.14), slightly lower still in the unattended auditory condition (M = 0.75, SD = 0.14), and lowest in the articulatory suppression condition (M = 0.72, SD = 0.13). These means reflect the average proportion of correctly recalled items across twenty trials per condition. Inspection of the within-subjects pattern showed that for most participants the silent condition supported the highest or near-highest recall performance, consistent with the expectation that interference of any kind tends to reduce recall relative to a quiet retention interval. Individual variability was present but not extreme; the distribution of difference scores between conditions was approximately symmetric without heavy skew that would suggest systematic subgroups of participants. Mauchly’s test of sphericity for the main repeated-measures ANOVA returned W = .93 and a non-significant result (p = .12), indicating that the sphericity assumption was not strongly violated, so we report uncorrected degrees of freedom for the omnibus test. The repeated-measures ANOVA comparing the four retention-interval conditions produced a significant overall effect, F(3,237) = 4.58, p = .004, partial eta squared = .055, indicating reliable differences in recall performance across the four conditions. Given the omnibus effect, we proceeded to our pre-specified pairwise comparisons, applying Bonferroni correction for five planned contrasts. The contrast comparing articulatory suppression to the silence condition was significant; paired t-test results were t(79) = 3.60, p < .001 (Bonferroni-corrected), Cohen’s d = 0.40, indicating that articulatory suppression during the retention interval significantly reduced serial recall relative to silence. The mean difference for this comparison was 0.08 (95% CI [0.03, 0.13]) in proportion-correct terms, which corresponds to an average loss of approximately 0.56 items per list given seven-item lists. The contrast comparing attended auditory distraction to silence yielded t(79) = 1.92, p = .06 (uncorrected) and did not reach significance after Bonferroni correction, Cohen’s d = 0.21, indicating that attending to the auditory stream did not significantly reduce recall relative to silence in this dataset. The corresponding comparison between unattended auditory distraction and silence produced t(79) = 1.84, p = .07 (uncorrected), Cohen’s d = 0.20, which was again non-significant after correction. Direct comparison between attended and unattended auditory conditions produced t(79) = 0.80, p = .43, Cohen’s d = 0.09, indicating no reliable difference as a function of attentional instruction. Finally, the comparison of articulatory suppression against the pooled auditory conditions (averaging attended and unattended) resulted in t(79) = 1.98, p = .051 (uncorrected), Cohen’s d = 0.22, which did not survive correction for multiple comparisons and therefore provided only weak, non-conclusive evidence that articulatory suppression might be more disruptive than auditory distraction when pooled. To supplement these null pairwise outcomes, especially the attended versus unattended auditory comparison, we conducted Bayesian paired-samples analyses to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis. The Bayesian analysis comparing attended versus unattended auditory conditions returned a Bayes factor BF01 = 3.8 in favor of the null over the alternative, which provides moderate evidence that there is no meaningful difference between attending to and ignoring the auditory stream in this paradigm. For the attended auditory versus silence contrast the Bayesian factor BF10 was approximately 1.3, indicating only anecdotal evidence for a difference and therefore retaining substantial uncertainty. For the articulatory suppression versus silence contrast the Bayes factor BF10 was approximately 18.6, indicating strong evidence in favor of a true difference and corroborating the frequentist result. We also explored serial position effects to determine whether interference effects were localized to particular list positions or operated uniformly across the list. Figure-level inspection of serial position curves showed the expected bowed shape, with relatively higher recall for early (primacy) and late (recency) positions compared to middle positions. When we examined early (positions 1–2), middle (positions 3–5), and late positions (positions 6–7) separately, the interference by position interaction in a 4 × 3 repeated-measures ANOVA was non-significant, F(6,474) = 1.42, p = .21, partial eta squared = .018, suggesting that the overall pattern of disruption did not differ markedly across positions. Planned comparisons within position bins showed that the articulatory suppression versus silence difference was numerically present across early, middle, and late bins but reached statistical significance only in the middle positions where rehearsal is often critical for maintaining order (t(79) = 3.01, p = .003 uncorrected for the middle positions), whereas differences in early and late positions were smaller and non-significant after correction. This pattern is consistent with articulatory suppression preferentially undermining rehearsal processes that support mid-list serial order maintenance. We also checked for potential carryover or fatigue effects by analyzing performance across blocks. A block (4) × condition (4) repeated-measures ANOVA revealed no significant block by condition interaction, F(9,711) = 0.87, p = .56, indicating that condition effects did not systematically vary across the experiment and suggesting that counterbalancing procedures were successful in mitigating order artifacts. Compliance and manipulation checks supported the integrity of the experimental manipulations. Participants’ rates of vocalization during articulatory suppression were monitored and, based on the experimenter’s ratings and occasional acoustic checks, the majority of participants produced utterances at the target rate on over 85% of suppression trials; data from the excluded participants reflected persistent noncompliance. In the attended auditory condition the mean target-detection hit rate during the retention interval was 0.78 (SD = 0.11), well above chance, which indicates that participants were generally attending to the stream when instructed to do so. False alarm rates for target detection were low (M = 0.06, SD = 0.04), and reaction times for detected targets averaged 610 ms (SD = 120 ms) after target onset, consistent with engaged processing of the auditory material. In exploratory analyses we examined whether individual differences in target-detection performance correlated with the magnitude of memory disruption in the attended auditory condition; we found a weak negative correlation (r = −.15, p = .19) that did not reach significance, suggesting that those who were better at detecting auditory targets did not exhibit reliably larger decrements in serial recall. We also checked whether participants who reported using overt rehearsal strategies or other mnemonic aids during debriefing showed different patterns of disruption; no robust patterns emerged in these self-report comparisons. Overall, the pattern of results indicates that articulatory suppression imposed during the retention interval reliably impairs serial recall relative to silence, whereas the irrelevant auditory streams used in this study produced small, non-significant declines in performance and attention instructions did not materially alter this outcome.

Discussion

The present experiment tested how two commonly studied forms of interference, articulatory suppression and irrelevant auditory stimulation, influence verbal short-term memory when they are restricted to the retention interval following encoding, and whether attention to auditory streams modulates their impact. The first major finding was that articulatory suppression during the maintenance period produced a clear and reliable reduction in serial recall relative to a silent retention interval. This outcome aligns with theoretical accounts that posit articulatory rehearsal as an active maintenance process that sustains phonological representations over brief delays and that can be disrupted by overt or covert speech production. The effect size for articulatory suppression versus silence was moderate, and Bayesian analysis provided strong support for the alternative hypothesis, which together indicate a robust influence of preventing rehearsal on maintenance-phase memory performance. The fact that suppression was effective even when applied only after item presentation emphasizes that rehearsal during the retention interval contributes meaningfully to successful serial recall and is not solely required during encoding. A second major finding is that irrelevant auditory stimulation presented during the retention interval produced numerically modest decreases in recall compared to silent retention, but these decreases did not reach statistical significance in the planned comparisons after correction for multiple tests. In addition, whether participants were instructed to attend to the auditory stream or to ignore it had little effect on the size of the (small) auditory interference, and the attended versus unattended contrast produced Bayes factors that moderately favored the null. Taken together, these outcomes suggest that in this paradigm and with the specific auditory materials used, auditory interference during maintenance was a weaker disruptor of serial recall than articulatory suppression and was not strongly modulated by explicit attentional instructions. These results have several theoretical implications for understanding the phonological loop and for adjudicating between competing mechanistic accounts of how external auditory events influence verbal short-term memory. The robust impact of articulatory suppression supports the idea that a rehearsal process materially contributes to the maintenance of serial order information in short-term memory. Preventing rehearsal after encoding deprives the phonological store of refreshment, leading to decay or loss of temporal order information, particularly in mid-list positions where rehearsal plays a central role in refreshing items. This is consistent with processes whereby articulatory rehearsal sustains and reactivates phonological traces and thereby helps preserve serial position information. That articulatory suppression applied only during maintenance produced a measurable cost reinforces the notion that rehearsal is an ongoing process during delays. The limited effect of auditory distraction in this study complicates simple assumptions that irrelevant sound will always exert a strong disruptive effect on the phonological store. One possibility is that auditory streams primarily disrupt encoding or concurrent presentation rather than maintenance when they are introduced only after the to-be-remembered items have been presented. If the phonological store can retain a representation of recently encoded items for a brief period that does not depend heavily on the presence or absence of similar sounding material, then auditory distraction that begins only after encoding may have a diminished impact. Another possibility is that the type of auditory material and its salience determine the magnitude of disruption, and the changing-state spoken-letter streams we used were not sufficiently potent in this post-encoding context to produce large effects. Prior literature indicates that changing-state sequences can be particularly disruptive, but the effect may depend on concurrent presentation or on specific acoustic characteristics; here, despite using varying letters, the retention-only placement perhaps reduced overlap with the stored representations in a way that weakened the disruptive process. Moreover, the fact that attention instructions did not modulate auditory disruption suggests that, at least under the present conditions, top-down allocation of attention to the auditory stream was not the principal determinant of any disruptive influence. The attended auditory condition included a target-detection task that most participants performed above chance on, indicating that attention was plausibly directed to the stream when requested, yet differences between attending and ignoring were negligible. This pattern is informative because it weighs against accounts that attribute the irrelevant sound effect primarily to general attentional capture and subsequent disruption of rehearsal. If attentional capture were the key mechanism, then explicitly attending to the auditory stream should have increased interference. The observed data better fit a framework in which any auditory disruption that does occur is not strongly attention-dependent in this retention-only setup; rather, disruption might operate through automatic interactions with phonological representations that are relatively insensitive to task-driven attention. Nonetheless, the very small auditory effects and the Bayesian evidence moderately favoring the null for the attended versus unattended comparison make clear that, in this experiment, attention had a minor or negligible role in determining the size of auditory interference. An additional interpretative angle considers the potential interdependence of the phonological store and articulatory rehearsal. The stronger effect of articulatory suppression could indicate that the rehearsal mechanism is more essential for the task and retention duration used here than the phonological store per se. That is, rehearsal may be the process that transposes transient phonological traces into a maintained form for serial order retention, whereas the phonological store alone may not be sufficient, especially for preserving order information across a multi-second delay. The small auditory effects might then reflect the fact that the store, though present, does not play the dominant role in supporting order retention for visually presented consonants when rehearsal is available. However, the data are also compatible with an integrated perspective in which the store and rehearsal interact closely; disrupting rehearsal may lead to a more immediate and pronounced loss of order-maintaining processes, while auditory disruption of stored phonological traces may be partially mitigated by intact rehearsal if participants can refresh representations despite noisy input. The lack of a statistically significant difference between articulatory suppression and the pooled auditory conditions in our analyses suggests that auditory interference was not dramatically less influential overall, and a larger sample or more potent auditory streams might produce different results. Methodological considerations temper the inferences that can be drawn and suggest avenues for future work. First, although the attended auditory condition included an active detection task, the requirement for responding during the retention interval introduced a secondary task that could have subtly altered the allocation of cognitive resources in ways not entirely comparable to suppression. Even though suppression was an overt motor task and the attended auditory task was perceptual and response-driven, differences in cognitive load and modality might have influenced relative effects. Future studies could equate task demands more closely, for example by using an attended auditory task that requires only covert monitoring without motor responses, or by including a motor control task that matches vocalization demands without interfering with rehearsal. Second, our use of seven-letter lists and an 8-second retention interval was intended to give rehearsal room to operate, but different list lengths and interval durations might yield different balances between store- and rehearsal-based maintenance. It is possible that longer lists or longer delays would accentuate the effects of auditory disruption or change the relative impact of articulatory suppression. Third, the specific acoustic properties of the auditory streams may matter; the spoken-letter streams we used were linguistically plausible distractors but perhaps lacked certain dynamic qualities (e.g., background noise, rhythmically salient features, or intensity variations) that make changing-state sequences more disruptive when presented during maintenance. Systematically varying acoustic complexity and similarity to the to-be-remembered material could test whether there are threshold conditions for effective disruption of the phonological store in the post-encoding interval. Fourth, although the attended versus unattended manipulation appeared to work as judged by target-detection performance, the attention instruction may not have induced completely discrete attentional states. Some participants may have monitored the auditory stream even in the ignore condition, or conversely some participants may have partially ignored the stream even when instructed to attend, leading to a reduction in the contrast between conditions. Experience sampling or momentary probes of attention during the retention interval could help quantify the degree of attention allocation across conditions in future work. Fifth, while our within-subjects design increased sensitivity, it also risks carryover effects; counterbalancing mitigated this risk, but independent replication with a between-subjects design could assess the robustness of the pattern under alternative methodological constraints. Despite these caveats, the present findings contribute to the literature by demonstrating that articulatory rehearsal is an important component of maintenance during short retention intervals and that, at least under the tested conditions, auditory distraction during retention produces only modest impairment that is not strongly influenced by instructions to attend. The results therefore favor a view in which rehearsal processes are central to maintaining order information for short verbal lists, and where the phonological store’s vulnerability to auditory interference depends on specific task parameters and the temporal relationship between the distractors and the encoded material. Several directions for future research arise naturally from this study. One productive route would be to vary the onset of auditory distraction relative to encoding across conditions, comparing distraction during encoding, immediately after encoding (as in the present study), and later in the retention interval to map out when the store is most susceptible to disruption. Another approach would contrast different types of auditory distractors, such as steady-state tones, speech at varying degrees of semantic relatedness to the memoranda, and environmental noise, to determine which acoustic features drive phonological store disruption and whether these features interact with attention. It would also be fruitful to manipulate the cognitive load of the articulatory suppression and attended tasks to equate non-specific resource demands and thereby ascertain whether modality-specific interference or general attention/executive load accounts better explain the observed effects. Neurophysiological measures, such as electroencephalography or functional neuroimaging, could provide converging evidence about whether auditory distraction and articulatory suppression engage distinct neural circuits during maintenance and whether attention alters their engagement. Longitudinal or developmental studies might examine whether reliance on rehearsal versus store-based maintenance changes with age or cognitive maturation and whether susceptibility to auditory distraction changes accordingly. Finally, given that our findings point to a potentially minor role for attention in modulating post-encoding auditory interference, it would be valuable to probe boundary conditions under which attention does become influential—for example, when auditory distractors are semantically meaningful, emotionally salient, or highly deviant from ongoing context. In sum, the present study replicates and extends prior evidence that articulatory suppression disrupts maintenance when applied during the retention interval and finds only modest and non-attention-dependent effects of auditory distraction under the tested conditions. These outcomes underscore the importance of articulatory rehearsal for preserving serial order in short-term memory and suggest that the susceptibility of the phonological store to auditory interference is contingent on the timing and nature of the distraction as well as the role of rehearsal. Future research that systematically manipulates timing, acoustic features, attentional demands, and list characteristics will help refine mechanistic accounts of how external sound interacts with internal memory maintenance processes and will clarify the precise relationship between the phonological store and articulatory rehearsal within the working memory architecture.
