Introduction

Understanding why individuals vary in their willingness to engage in risky behavior is a central question in psychology and public health. Risk taking has consequences that span from relatively benign recreational activities to behaviors that can dramatically affect health, finances, and social relationships. Decades of research have attempted to identify stable individual differences that predict propensity for risk taking. Among the most enduring psychological constructs used to explain such differences are personality traits. Personality traits provide a parsimonious description of recurrent patterns of thought, feeling, and behavior that may predispose some people toward greater engagement in risky behaviors than others. The current work situates itself within this tradition and seeks to advance understanding by examining multiple personality dimensions simultaneously in relation to domain-specific risk taking across a broad adult age range. Historically, many studies have focused on single personality traits in isolation when relating personality to risk behavior. While such approaches offer important information about specific pathways, they can obscure the broader profile of traits that, together, predict risk preferences and behaviors. A more comprehensive approach that assesses several major personality dimensions concurrently can illuminate which traits uniquely contribute to risk taking after accounting for overlap among traits. The Eysenck model of personality offers a useful framework for such an approach because it operationalizes individual differences along several theoretically meaningful axes that have been linked to behavioral tendencies relevant to risk taking. Eysenck’s model classically distinguishes among extraversion, neuroticism, and psychoticism. Extraversion is conceptualized as a tendency toward sociability, assertiveness, and sensation seeking. Neuroticism reflects emotional instability and proneness to negative affect. Psychoticism has been associated with aggressiveness, impulsivity, and nonconformity. Early theorists argued that extraverted individuals would be more likely to seek out stimulation and therefore engage in more risk taking. This prediction has found support in numerous studies that link extraversion to sensation seeking and certain forms of risk, particularly recreational and social risks. At the same time, psychoticism is theoretically tied to impulsive and antisocial tendencies that could facilitate a range of risky behaviors, including those that are more consequential for health and law-abiding behavior. Empirical work on psychoticism and risk taking is less voluminous than that on extraversion, however, and has produced mixed findings. Some investigations found psychoticism-related traits such as impulsivity and low constraint to predict risky decision-making, whereas others reported only modest or domain-limited associations. Neuroticism is often conceptualized as a risk factor for maladaptive behavior, but its relation to risk taking is complex. High neuroticism could either increase risk taking through poor emotion regulation and reckless coping or reduce engagement in risk because of heightened anxiety and threat sensitivity. Thus, simultaneous consideration of extraversion, psychoticism, and neuroticism can be especially informative. In addition to trait-level predictors, age is a key correlate of risk taking that cannot be ignored. Research across developmental stages consistently shows that risk taking is higher in adolescence and young adulthood than in later adulthood. The neurodevelopmental and social explanation for this pattern emphasizes the maturation of cognitive control and shifts in social motivations as people age. Nonetheless, much work on personality and risk has been conducted in restricted age groups such as college students, limiting generalizability across the adult life span. Examining personality–risk relations across a broad adult age range allows investigation of whether personality traits predict risk behavior independent of age and whether their influence is moderated by age. Domain specificity of risk taking is another important consideration. People who take financial risks may not necessarily take health risks, and willingness to engage in social risks such as speaking up in a group setting may be distinct from willingness to engage in recreational or ethical risks. Tools such as the Domain-Specific Risk-Taking (DOSPERT) scale operationalize risk taking across several life domains and allow researchers to examine whether personality traits relate differently to distinct types of risk. Prior literature suggests that extraversion might be particularly relevant for social risk, while psychoticism-related traits might be more strongly linked to recreational or financial risk. However, empirical comparisons of these domain-specific associations are relatively sparse, especially in samples covering a wide age spectrum. Measurement considerations also play a central role. The Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ) and its revisions provide reliable indices of psychoticism, extraversion, and neuroticism, but psychometric properties can vary with the form used and the scoring procedure. Likewise, self-report measures of risk taking capture willingness or propensity rather than actual behavior, and so linking trait predictors to domain-specific self-reported propensity complements, rather than replaces, behavioral or ecological measurement approaches. Moreover, the overlap among personality traits necessitates multivariate analyses that can parse out unique contributions. Previous studies that focused on a single trait such as extraversion have sometimes found associations with risk taking that are attenuated when other traits or demographic covariates are included. This pattern underscores the importance of studying multiple traits concurrently. The present research sought to address several gaps in the literature. First, we administered a revised version of the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire to obtain concurrent measures of psychoticism, extraversion, and neuroticism. Second, we measured risk taking using the DOSPERT to capture domain-specific willingness to take risks. Third, we recruited a broad adult sample aged 18 years and older to evaluate personality–risk relations across the adult life span. Fourth, we examined whether age accounts for variance in risk taking above and beyond personality and whether personality traits predict risk taking independent of basic demographics. Based on theoretical and empirical precedents, we preregistered two primary expectations. We expected that higher psychoticism scores would be associated with greater overall propensity to take risks given psychoticism’s links to impulsivity and nonconformity. We also expected extraversion to show a positive association with overall risk taking driven by social and recreational domains, although we noted that prior findings have been inconsistent. In addition to these primary predictions, we anticipated that age would be negatively associated with overall risk taking and with several risk domains. Finally, we planned exploratory analyses to examine domain-specific associations and to evaluate whether personality traits uniquely predicted risk taking in multivariate models controlling for age, gender, and education. The study’s design and analytic approach were chosen to provide a robust test of these hypotheses and to contribute to a more nuanced understanding of how major personality traits relate to different forms of risk taking across adulthood. We also sought to provide reliable psychometric information about the measures used in a broadly recruited adult sample so subsequent investigations can draw on these estimates. By examining multiple personality traits at once, we hoped to clarify the relative importance of psychoticism versus extraversion and neuroticism in accounting for individual differences in risk taking. In doing so, the study addresses a practical need for clearer predictors of risk-related behaviors that can inform prevention and intervention aimed at reducing harm. The integration of age and personality in a single framework also speaks to developmental perspectives on risk and personality. If personality traits are stable predictors across ages, interventions might focus on trait-related propensities. Conversely, if age alters the impact of personality on risk, different strategies may be required at different life stages. Finally, although neurobiological mechanisms are beyond the immediate scope of this survey study, the observed patterns can generate hypotheses for future neuroscience-driven work on the biological bases of risk-related trait differences. The present paper reports the results of these investigations and discusses their implications for personality theory, risk research, and applied efforts aimed at understanding and mitigating hazardous behaviors among adults.

Methods

Participants were recruited via an online research platform and local community advertisements. All participants were required to be aged 18 years or older and to be fluent in English in order to complete the questionnaires. A total of 620 individuals initiated the survey. Data cleaning procedures removed participants who failed embedded attention-check items, provided implausibly fast completion times, or had large amounts of missing data on key measures. After these exclusions, the final sample consisted of 512 participants. The sample ranged in age from 18 to 75 years with a mean age of 35.6 years and a standard deviation of 12.4 years. The gender composition of the sample was 54% female, 45% male, and 1% identifying as nonbinary or preferring not to say. Participants reported a range of educational backgrounds from high school completion to postgraduate degrees, with the modal level being a bachelor’s degree. The study received ethical approval from the institution’s review board, and electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participants completed the survey on their personal devices and were compensated with a small monetary honorarium or course credit when applicable. Personality was assessed using a revised version of the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ-Revised) adapted for contemporary use. The EPQ-Revised yielded scores on three primary dimensions: psychoticism, extraversion, and neuroticism. Items were presented in a Likert format ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) to increase sensitivity and reduce ceiling effects relative to binary formats. The psychoticism scale included items tapping impulsivity, unconventionality, and aggression. The extraversion scale assessed sociability, assertiveness, and a tendency to seek stimulation. The neuroticism scale assessed emotional instability, worry, and mood lability. Subscale scores were computed as the mean of constituent items and scaled so that higher values indicated stronger endorsement of the trait. Risk taking was measured using the Domain-Specific Risk-Taking (DOSPERT) scale. The DOSPERT instrument measures the likelihood of engaging in risky activities across five domains: ethical, financial, health/safety, recreational, and social. For each domain there were six items, yielding a 30-item overall scale. Participants rated how likely they would be to engage in each activity on a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (very unlikely) to 7 (very likely). Domain-specific scores were computed by averaging the items within each domain; an overall mean risk-taking score was also computed by averaging across all items. Prior to analyses we checked the reliability of all scales. Cronbach’s alpha was computed for each EPQ-Revised subscale and for the DOSPERT overall scale and subdomains. Item-total correlations were examined to confirm that individual items contributed meaningfully to the scales. Descriptive statistics for age, gender, education, and all questionnaire scores were computed. The analytic strategy focused on evaluating associations between personality traits and risk taking at multiple levels. First, zero-order Pearson correlations were computed between EPQ dimensions, overall risk taking, and each DOSPERT domain. Second, hierarchical multiple regression was used to test whether personality traits predicted overall risk taking after controlling for demographic covariates. Demographic variables were entered in the first step of the regression model; age was included as a continuous predictor and gender and educational attainment were included as covariates. The EPQ-Revised psychoticism, extraversion, and neuroticism scales were entered in the second step. Standardized beta coefficients, t-statistics, and significance values were reported. Variance inflation factors were computed to assess multicollinearity. Third, domain-specific regressions were conducted to test whether personality dimensions differentially predicted risk taking across the DOSPERT domains. Fourth, exploratory interaction tests examined whether age moderated the relation between psychoticism and risk taking by including an age-by-psychoticism interaction term in regression models. All continuous predictors were mean-centered before computing interaction terms. Supplemental analyses included robust regression methods to ensure results were not driven by outliers and sensitivity analyses excluding participants over 65 years of age to assess the influence of potential cohort effects. Statistical significance was evaluated at the conventional alpha level of .05, and effect sizes were reported alongside p-values to aid interpretation. To facilitate interpretation of the magnitude of associations we computed Cohen’s d equivalents for key findings and reported R-squared changes for hierarchical models. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Data and analysis scripts were archived in a university repository and will be made available upon reasonable request, consistent with participant consent and data privacy considerations.

Results

The final analytic sample included 512 participants after data cleaning. The sample’s mean age was 35.6 years with a range from 18 to 75 years and a standard deviation of 12.4 years. Females comprised 54% of the sample, males 45%, and 1% identified as nonbinary or preferred not to say. Educational attainment varied with 18% reporting high school or equivalent as the highest level, 39% reporting some college or an associate degree, 33% reporting a bachelor’s degree, and 10% reporting postgraduate education. Descriptive statistics for the key measures showed adequate spread. The mean psychoticism score on the EPQ-Revised was 2.41 (SD = 0.63) on the 1–5 scale. Extraversion had a mean of 3.26 (SD = 0.71), and neuroticism had a mean of 2.98 (SD = 0.77). The DOSPERT overall mean score was 3.45 (SD = 0.92) on the 1–7 scale. Domain means for DOSPERT were: ethical 2.74 (SD = 1.05), financial 3.18 (SD = 1.22), health/safety 3.05 (SD = 1.18), recreational 4.21 (SD = 1.25), and social 4.04 (SD = 1.20). Internal consistency estimates indicated acceptable reliability for the primary measures. Cronbach’s alpha for the psychoticism scale was .78, for extraversion was .82, and for neuroticism was .84. The DOSPERT overall scale had an alpha of .86, and domain subscales ranged from .72 to .81. Item-total correlations were all in acceptable ranges, suggesting that individual items contributed meaningfully to each scale. Zero-order Pearson correlations provided the first set of tests of the primary hypotheses. Overall DOSPERT risk taking correlated positively with psychoticism (r = .28, p < .001). The correlation between overall risk taking and extraversion was small and not statistically significant (r = .05, p = .17). Neuroticism had a small negative correlation with overall risk taking (r = -.12, p = .006). Age was negatively correlated with overall risk taking (r = -.31, p < .001). Gender showed a small effect such that males reported slightly higher overall risk taking than females (point-biserial r = .09, p = .03). Correlations among personality scales were as expected; extraversion and neuroticism were negatively correlated (r = -.18, p < .001), psychoticism and extraversion showed a small positive correlation (r = .10, p = .02), and psychoticism and neuroticism were modestly positively correlated (r = .14, p = .001). We next conducted a hierarchical multiple regression predicting overall DOSPERT score. In Step 1, demographic covariates were entered. Age was a significant predictor of overall risk taking (standardized beta = -.30, t = -7.03, p < .001). Gender had a small but significant effect (beta = .07, t = 2.00, p = .046), with males reporting higher risk taking. Education was not a significant predictor in Step 1 (beta = -.02, t = -0.52, p = .60). The model with demographics explained 12.1% of the variance in overall risk taking (R^2 = .121, F(3,508) = 23.28, p < .001). In Step 2, the three personality dimensions were added. The full model explained 22.3% of the variance (ΔR^2 = .102, F change(3,505) = 22.48, p < .001). In the full model, psychoticism emerged as a significant unique predictor of overall risk taking (standardized beta = .22, t = 5.24, p < .001). Extraversion was not a significant unique predictor when other traits and demographics were included (beta = .04, t = 1.05, p = .294). Neuroticism showed a small negative unique effect that approached but did not reach conventional significance (beta = -.07, t = -1.85, p = .064). Age remained a significant predictor in the full model (beta = -.26, t = -5.95, p < .001). Variance inflation factors for all predictors were below 1.8, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. To probe domain-specific associations, five separate multiple regressions were conducted with each DOSPERT domain as the outcome. For the recreational domain, psychoticism was a strong predictor (beta = .31, t = 7.45, p < .001) after controlling for age, gender, and education. Extraversion was not a significant predictor of recreational risk (beta = .06, t = 1.48, p = .14). Age negatively predicted recreational risk (beta = -.24, t = -5.75, p < .001). For the financial domain, psychoticism again positively predicted risk (beta = .19, t = 4.12, p < .001). Extraversion did not significantly predict financial risk (beta = .02, t = 0.46, p = .65). Age negatively predicted financial risk (beta = -.21, t = -4.65, p < .001). For the social domain, none of the personality variables reached significance when entered simultaneously with covariates; psychoticism had a marginal effect (beta = .09, t = 1.95, p = .052) and extraversion remained nonsignificant (beta = .04, t = 0.98, p = .33). Age again negatively predicted social risk but with a smaller effect size (beta = -.11, t = -2.48, p = .013). For the health/safety domain, psychoticism positively predicted risk (beta = .15, t = 3.45, p = .001), while extraversion did not (beta = -.01, t = -0.21, p = .83). Age was a robust negative predictor of health/safety risk (beta = -.29, t = -6.76, p < .001). For the ethical domain, psychoticism did not significantly predict risk (beta = .07, t = 1.58, p = .12), and extraversion had no effect (beta = .03, t = 0.68, p = .50); age negatively predicted ethical risk (beta = -.17, t = -3.92, p < .001). Across domains, the pattern indicated that psychoticism was most consistently associated with recreational, financial, and health/safety risk taking, while extraversion was not a unique predictor of any domain when other traits were accounted for. Exploratory interaction analyses tested whether the relation between psychoticism and overall risk taking was moderated by age. The age-by-psychoticism interaction term did not reach statistical significance (beta = -.05, t = -1.12, p = .26), indicating no evidence that the psychoticism–risk link varied systematically with age in our sample. Sensitivity analyses using robust regression methods produced similar conclusions, with psychoticism remaining a significant predictor of overall and domain-specific risk taking. Excluding participants aged 66 and older did not materially affect the results; psychoticism still predicted overall risk taking (beta = .23, p < .001) and extraversion remained nonsignificant (beta = .05, p = .21). Taken together, the results provide converging evidence that psychoticism is associated with higher self-reported propensity to take risks, that extraversion does not uniquely predict risk taking when psychoticism and neuroticism are controlled, and that age is a consistent negative predictor of risk propensity across domains.

Discussion

This study examined the associations between multiple personality dimensions and domain-specific risk taking in a broad adult sample. The main finding was that psychoticism, as measured by a revised EPQ, was positively and uniquely associated with overall risk taking and with several DOSPERT domains, particularly recreational, financial, and health/safety risk. Extraversion, contrary to some expectations and prior reports, did not uniquely predict risk taking in models that included psychoticism and neuroticism. Age was a robust negative predictor of risk taking across models and domains. These findings have several theoretical and practical implications. The positive association between psychoticism and risk taking supports the view that traits related to impulsivity, nonconformity, and aggression are relevant to understanding why certain individuals exhibit greater propensities for risk. Psychoticism’s links to impulsivity and lower constraint may lower the threshold for engaging in behaviors that carry potential harms or losses, hence explaining the observed relations with financial, recreational, and health/safety risks. The consistency of psychoticism’s associations across multiple domains suggests that it captures a general behavioral tendency toward risk-seeking that transcends specific contexts. This generality contrasts with the idea that personality influences on risk are purely domain-specific and underscores the importance of considering dispositional tendencies that operate across situations. The finding that extraversion did not uniquely predict risk propensity when psychoticism and neuroticism were included indicates that some prior associations between extraversion and risk may reflect confounded variance with related traits. Extraversion is often associated with sensation-seeking and social boldness; however, when measures of impulsivity and nonconformity are controlled, the residual component of extraversion may not drive decisions to engage in risky behaviors. This pattern is important because it highlights that superficially similar constructs—sensation seeking, extraversion, impulsivity—are not interchangeable and can have distinct relations with outcomes. For researchers and practitioners, the implication is that targeting impulsivity-related features captured by psychoticism may be more relevant for risk reduction efforts than altering social stimulation preferences per se. Age emerged as a reliable negative predictor of risk propensity, mirroring a large literature showing declines in many forms of risk taking across the life span. The age effect remained significant after accounting for personality, indicating that developmental or cohort-based processes contribute to lower risk taking independent of stable dispositional differences. Possible mechanisms for age-related declines include improved cognitive control, shifts in socioemotional goals, changes in perceived costs and benefits, and accumulated experience that informs decision-making. The absence of a significant age-by-psychoticism interaction suggests that psychoticism’s influence on risk propensity is relatively stable across adulthood rather than being confined to younger age groups. This stability points to the possibility that interventions targeting psychoticism-related features could have utility across a wide age range. Several aspects of the domain-specific results merit further comment. Psychoticism was most strongly associated with recreational risk taking, which is consistent with the idea that impulsive, sensation-oriented tendencies would promote engagement in physically exciting or novel activities. Its relation to financial risk may reflect a willingness to accept monetary uncertainty or a reduced sensitivity to potential losses. The association with health/safety risk may reflect poor risk assessment or prioritization of immediate rewards over long-term well-being. The relatively weaker and inconsistent relations of psychoticism with ethical and social domains suggest that moral considerations and social norms may temper the expression of impulsivity in those contexts, or that different trait processes underlie willingness to take ethical or socially risky actions. The lack of a unique extraversion effect across domains, including social risk, invites further investigation. Previous literature often links extraversion to social risk taking and sensation-seeking behaviors; however, the present findings indicate that when impulsivity-related variance is accounted for, extraversion’s predictive power diminishes. It is possible that facets of extraversion that were not captured by the EPQ-Revised in our study—such as positive affectivity or activity level—may relate to specific risk behaviors. Alternatively, measurement differences across studies and cultural context may contribute to inconsistencies in extraversion–risk findings. The current study has several strengths. It assessed multiple personality dimensions concurrently rather than focusing on a single trait, used a domain-specific instrument for risk propensity, and recruited a wide age range in a moderately large sample. These features improve the study’s ability to detect unique associations and to explore whether trait effects are robust across adulthood. The psychometric properties of the measures were acceptable, lending confidence to the observed relations. Nevertheless, limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design restricts causal inference; personality traits and self-reported risk propensity were measured at the same time, and longitudinal data would be needed to determine temporal precedence. Self-report measures capture perceived or intended behavior rather than actual risk-taking actions; hence, future work should incorporate behavioral tasks, ecological momentary assessment, or objective records (e.g., driving violations, medical histories) to corroborate self-report findings. The study sample, while diverse in age, may not fully represent the general population in terms of socioeconomic, cultural, or racial/ethnic diversity, which could limit generalizability. Additionally, the EPQ-Revised’s psychoticism scale assesses a broad construct that intersects with clinically relevant features; careful interpretation is required to avoid stigmatizing individuals who score higher on this scale. The current findings also raise questions for neuroscience-informed research. The relation between psychoticism and risk taking suggests possible underlying neural mechanisms involving reward processing and impulse control. Research has implicated dopaminergic pathways and frontostriatal circuits in both impulsivity and risky decision-making. Future studies combining personality assessment with neuroimaging or pharmacological challenges could test whether individuals higher in psychoticism exhibit heightened reward responsivity or reduced prefrontal regulatory activation during risk-related tasks. Genetic and neurochemical markers associated with impulsivity and novelty seeking may also shed light on biological substrates that link psychoticism to risk behavior. Interventions aiming to reduce harmful risk taking could benefit from targeting processes related to impulsivity and constraint. Cognitive training designed to strengthen executive control, behavioral interventions that restructure contingencies and delay immediate reward, and brief motivational strategies that increase valuation of long-term outcomes may be particularly useful for individuals high in psychoticism. The absence of a unique extraversion effect suggests that interventions focused solely on social-extraversion aspects (e.g., reducing social novelty) may be less effective for reducing risky behavior when impulsivity remains unaddressed. Several avenues for future research emerge from this study. Longitudinal designs are needed to examine how personality traits and risk propensity co-develop and whether changes in life circumstances moderate these relations. Experimental manipulations that elicit risk taking under controlled conditions and measure corresponding neural activity would clarify causal mechanisms. Inclusion of other personality frameworks, such as the Five-Factor Model and more granular measures of impulsivity and sensation seeking, could delineate which facets account for the associations observed here. Cross-cultural investigations would determine whether the pattern of psychoticism predicting risk taking replicates in different societies with varied norms around risk. Moreover, studying younger cohorts and tracking them into adulthood would illuminate whether psychoticism’s influence on risk is stable or changes across developmental transitions. Policy and applied implications should be considered cautiously. Understanding that psychoticism-related traits predispose individuals to higher risk taking does not imply determinism; many people with elevated scores do not engage in harmful levels of risk, and contextual factors such as peer influence, stress, and opportunity strongly shape behavior. Nonetheless, identifying trait-based vulnerabilities can inform targeted prevention efforts, for instance by tailoring education and risk-communication strategies to individuals’ dispositional profiles. Screening tools that combine personality assessments with behavioral indicators could help allocate resources to those most likely to benefit from interventions. In sum, the present study contributes to personality and risk research by demonstrating that psychoticism is a consistent predictor of self-reported risk propensity across several life domains and that extraversion does not uniquely explain risk taking when considered alongside psychoticism and neuroticism. Age remains an important independent predictor, with older adults reporting less willingness to engage in risky activities. These findings underscore the value of multivariate personality assessment in understanding risk behavior and point to impulsivity-related trait processes as promising targets for future empirical and applied work. Continued efforts to integrate personality, developmental, and neurobiological perspectives will advance a more complete account of why people differ in their propensity to take risks and how those tendencies can be addressed to promote healthier, safer outcomes.
