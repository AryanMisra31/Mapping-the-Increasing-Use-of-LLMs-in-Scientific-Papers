Introduction

Human language contains many regularities that are not strictly arbitrary, and one of the most striking classes of these non-arbitrary relations links aspects of speech sounds to non-linguistic properties of objects, a phenomenon commonly described as sound symbolism. Sound-symbolic correspondences appear in many languages and cultures and take multiple forms, ranging from systematic links between particular phonemes and semantic domains to iconic mappings in which a phonetic shape bears resemblance to a perceptual or motor pattern. Among the most widely discussed and experimentally robust instances of sound symbolism is the so-called bouba-kiki effect, in which people reliably map rounded, curvilinear shapes onto words like “bouba” or other sounds containing voiced sonorants and back vowels, and angular, jagged shapes onto words like “kiki” or sounds with voiceless obstruents and high front vowels. The bouba-kiki effect has been repeatedly observed in diverse participant populations and with a variety of tasks, suggesting that it reflects a fundamental crossmodal correspondence between auditory or articulatory patterns and visual shape contours. Such correspondences have been taken to suggest that language contains a non-negligible proportion of iconic links that may support language learning and grounding.

Beyond the bouba-kiki phenomenon, researchers have documented other consistent sound-symbolic mappings, including associations between sound properties and magnitude or size. For example, smallness is often associated with high front vowels or certain diminutive morphology, and largeness with low back vowels or more open articulations. This pattern has been invoked to explain why diminutive suffixes often contain high front vowel elements in many languages and why adults as well as infants may generalize size-based word meanings from phonological cues. Thus, two broad classes of sound symbolism can be distinguished: contour-based correspondences that tie sound shape to visual contour qualities like roundedness versus pointedness, and magnitude-based correspondences that tie sound properties to perceived size or magnitude.

These two classes of correspondences invite several further questions that are relevant theoretically and empirically. One question concerns the modality-general status of contour-based sound symbolism: does the bouba-kiki effect depend on a particular mode of stimulus presentation, for example requiring visual access to the shapes, or is it equally robust when stimuli are delivered in other modalities such as auditory presentation of words or even haptic exploration of shapes? Clarifying the modality dependence of bouba-kiki is important for understanding its underlying mechanisms, because modality-general effects would suggest a more abstract crossmodal mapping or a multisensory representational basis, whereas modality-specific effects could point to matched perceptual processes tied to particular sensory channels.

A related question concerns the relative contribution of contour congruency versus size congruency when both dimensions vary. Many experimental demonstrations of bouba-kiki have used controlled stimuli that isolate curvature while minimizing variance in size, but everyday objects vary along multiple axes including both contour and magnitude. If a listener is asked to assign a novel word to a novel object that differs from alternatives in both contour and size, which sound-symbolic cue will predominate in their mapping? Understanding the hierarchical weighting of these cues could reveal whether contour information is privileged in sound-to-shape mapping or whether size cues—perhaps as a reflection of more generalized magnitude symbolism—can override contour cues under some conditions.

Another crucial consideration is the acoustic and articulatory basis of these mappings. Some accounts emphasize low-level perceptual similarities between acoustic properties and visual contours—for example, that sonorous, low-frequency, continuous sounds map to rounded contours because both are perceived as smooth and continuous, whereas sharp, transient sounds map to angular contours because both are perceived as discontinuous and spiky. Alternative accounts emphasize articulatory gestures: rounded vowels and bilabial consonants involve lip rounding and closure gestures that may be readily associated with round shapes, while alveolar plosive or fricative articulations might be associated with sharper edges. Whatever the proximate mechanism, the empirical pattern that emerges across many studies is that contour-based mappings are robust and often automatic.

Despite the robust demonstrations, there remain open empirical debates that motivate the present work. First, while many studies have shown the bouba-kiki effect with visual shapes and spoken words presented together, fewer have systematically compared modalities by presenting words and/or shapes in isolation or in different sensory channels. Second, even when both contour and size vary, the relative salience of contour versus size cues has not been thoroughly quantified in adult participants using tightly controlled manipulations of sound-symbolic features that isolate contour-congruent and size-congruent elements of words. Third, while magnitude symbolism has sometimes been characterized as being driven by phonetic features that convey diminutive meanings, it is unclear whether such diminutive cues can compete with strong contour cues in determining preferential word-to-shape assignment.

The current set of experiments was designed to address these issues in a systematic way. The first aim was to test whether the bouba-kiki mapping is robust across different presentation modalities. To do so, two parallel experiments were conducted with adult participants: one presenting the classic rounded and angular shapes visually and asking participants to assign spoken or written nonsense words, and the other delivering only auditory stimuli or other sensory configurations. By comparing performance across these two modalities, we sought to determine whether the bouba-kiki effect depends on co-presentation of visual and auditory cues or whether it persists even when stimuli are experienced in a single modality. The expectation, based on prior literature and the crossmodal nature of the effect, was that participants would show a robust bouba-kiki preference regardless of modality.

The second aim pursued a related but distinct question about cue hierarchy: when participants are presented with shapes that vary orthogonally in contour (rounded vs angular) and size (small vs large), and when words are designed to possess sound-symbolic features that are either contour-congruent or size-congruent, which type of feature will primarily govern participants’ preferential assignment of words to shapes? To answer this, we created sets of nonsense words manipulated to vary independently on contour-congruency (via consonant and vowel composition that has been associated with rounded vs angular mappings) and on diminutive-like features that have been associated with smallness. Participants were presented with individual shapes and asked to choose which of two nonsense words better fit the shape. If contour symbolism is the dominant driver, participants should preferentially select words whose sound-symbolic features match the shape’s contours, regardless of the size cues embedded in the word. Conversely, if magnitude symbolism is dominant and primarily driven by diminutive elements, then words bearing diminutive-like features should be selected for small shapes even when their contour features are incongruent.

A further theoretical motivation for differentiating contour and magnitude symbolism is that they may reflect distinct cognitive and neural processes. Contour-based mappings could be grounded in sensorimotor correspondences between articulatory gestures and visual shape perception, whereas magnitude-based mappings might reflect more abstract magnitude systems that generalize across dimensions such as size, loudness, and pitch. Distinguishing their relative contributions in a single behavioral paradigm could therefore inform theories of iconicity in language and the cognitive architecture of crossmodal correspondences.

Methodologically, the present study adopted both categorical forced-choice tasks and mixed-effects statistical models to quantify participants’ mappings, thereby allowing inferential conclusions at both the group and item levels and controlling for participant- and item-level variability. The design also included manipulations to ensure that contour and size features were orthogonal and that words were balanced along phonological dimensions other than the targeted features. This rigorous control enabled a focused test of the hypothesis that contour-congruent sound-symbolic features will predict preferential assignment more strongly than size-congruent features, and that the bouba-kiki effect will appear across modalities.

In addition to the primary hypotheses, the experiments examined potential moderating variables such as participant language background, explicit awareness of sound-symbolic relations, and response times as an index of processing fluency. If the bouba-kiki effect is automatic and modality-general, it should be observed across participants regardless of explicit awareness and with relatively fast response times. Conversely, if mappings depend heavily on strategic reasoning or on verbal labels, then explicit awareness might moderate effects and responses might be slower.

Overall, the present research tests two interrelated claims. The first claim is that the bouba-kiki effect is robust in adults regardless of the sensory modality in which stimuli are presented. The second claim is that when contour and size cues compete, preferential assignment of nonsense words to nonsense shapes will be driven primarily by sound-symbolic features that are congruent with the shape’s contours, rather than by size-related features, with magnitude-based mappings emerging mainly when diminutive features are present. The experiments reported here set out to test these claims using controlled stimuli, balanced experimental designs, and appropriate statistical modeling.

Methods

Overview and participants

Two initial experiments were run to test modality effects on the bouba-kiki mapping. These two experiments will be referred to collectively as Experiment 1, with Experiment 1a and Experiment 1b indicating the visual and auditory variants, respectively. Experiment 1a and 1b each tested 30 adult participants, yielding a combined total of 60 participants for the modality comparison. A separate experiment, Experiment 2, examined the relative effects of contour and size sound-symbolic features and tested a separate sample of 52 adult participants. All participants were recruited from a university participant pool and through community advertising and were paid or received course credit for their participation. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing and were native speakers of the local majority language. The sample sizes were selected to provide adequate power to detect medium to large effects that are typical for the bouba-kiki effect and for sound-symbolic preferences more generally.

Stimuli: visual shapes

Visual stimuli were grayscale line drawings of abstract shapes designed to vary orthogonally in contour and size where required. For the modality experiments (Experiment 1a and 1b), the classic pair of shapes was used: one rounded, curvilinear shape with smooth contours, and one jagged, angular shape with sharp points. The rounded shape had several smooth bulges and no pointed vertices, while the angular shape had multiple acute angles and pronounced points. For Experiment 2, a larger set of four shapes was created by crossing contour (rounded vs angular) with size (small vs large). The small shapes occupied approximately 1.5 degrees of visual angle when presented on the display used, and the large shapes occupied approximately 4.5 degrees of visual angle, ensuring a clear perceptual size difference while keeping contour features prominent. All shapes were equated for overall luminance and were presented on a neutral gray background.

Stimuli: nonsense words

Nonsense words were constructed to manipulate two types of sound-symbolic features independently: contour-congruent phonological features and size-congruent (diminutive-like) features. Contour-congruent features were operationalized through consonant and vowel choices that previous work suggests map onto rounded or angular visual contours. Rounded-sounding words incorporated voiced sonorants and back vowels (e.g., sequences with /m/, /b/, /o/), whereas angular-sounding words incorporated voiceless obstruents and high front vowels (e.g., /k/, /t/, /i/). Size-congruent features were operationalized through the presence or absence of a diminutive-like high vowel or suffix, such as an /i/ or /-i/ ending, which has been associated with smallness in many languages. Words were constructed in pairs such that contour congruency and size congruency could be independently manipulated. For example, a rounded-contour-congruent but non-diminutive word might be “mobo,” whereas a rounded-contour-congruent and diminutive word might be “mobi.” Likewise, an angular-contour-congruent and diminutive word could be “kiki,” while an angular but non-diminutive counterpart could be “kaku.” Stimuli were recorded by a single native speaker with neutral prosody for auditory presentation, and orthographic forms were presented in a consistent font for visual presentation when required.

Procedure: Experiment 1a (visual)

In Experiment 1a, participants sat at a computer in a quiet testing room and viewed the rounded and angular shapes on a monitor. On each trial, a pair of nonsense words (one rounded-sounding and one angular-sounding) was displayed beneath the central shape, and participants were asked to choose which word they believed best matched the shape by pressing a corresponding button. Trials included both the classic “bouba-kiki” style pairing (rounded vs angular word forms) and randomized presentations to avoid position bias. The task proceeded over 40 trials, with stimuli randomized across trials. Trial timing allowed participants to respond at their own pace, but response times were recorded.

Procedure: Experiment 1b (auditory)

Experiment 1b used a strictly auditory presentation for the words and visual presentation for the shapes; however, to isolate modality effects we also ran trials in which words were presented auditorily without written labels and participants indicated their choice while viewing only the shape. On each trial, a shape was displayed centrally and two spoken nonsense words were presented sequentially through headphones, with a short inter-stimulus interval. Participants pressed a button to indicate which of the two spoken words they felt best matched the shape. Trials and timing were matched to Experiment 1a, and response times were recorded from the offset of the second spoken word.

Procedure: Experiment 2 (contour vs size)

In Experiment 2, participants performed a two-alternative forced-choice task in which they were presented with a single shape on each trial and two candidate nonsense words on the screen or presented auditorily. The two words differed orthogonally in contour-congruency and size-congruency relative to the shape, such that one word could be contour-congruent but size-incongruent, whereas the other could be size-congruent but contour-incongruent. There were four types of trials crossing rounded/angular and small/large shapes, and each trial type was presented multiple times with different word pairs to avoid item-specific effects. In total, each participant completed 96 trials, with trials randomized across participants. Participants were instructed to choose the word they felt best matched the shape and to respond as quickly and accurately as possible. After the forced-choice block, participants completed a debriefing questionnaire probing whether they noticed any systematic relations between sounds and shapes.

Data recording and preprocessing

Responses and response times were recorded for all trials. Trials with response times below 200 ms or above 10,000 ms were excluded as outliers. For each participant and each condition, proportions of contour-congruent choices and size-congruent choices were calculated. For inferential analyses, mixed-effects logistic regression models were fit to trial-level data with random intercepts for participants and items, and with fixed effects for contour congruency, size congruency, presentation modality, and their interactions as appropriate. Standard diagnostic checks were applied to verify model assumptions and goodness of fit.

Ethics

All experimental procedures were approved by the institutional review board, and participants provided informed consent prior to participating.

Results

Overview and data quality

All participants completed the tasks, and data from all participants were included after excluding occasional trials with extreme response times as noted in the preprocessing section. Less than 2% of trials were removed for response-time outliers across experiments, leaving a robust dataset for analysis. Participants’ debriefing responses indicated that while some individuals reported noticing an intuitive fit between sounds and shapes, the majority did not report an explicit strategy for choosing certain words; this suggests that many of the observed effects likely reflect implicit correspondences rather than overt reasoning.

Experiment 1a: visual presentation

In Experiment 1a, which presented shapes visually with words shown orthographically beneath the target shape, the proportion of trials in which participants selected the rounded-sounding word for rounded shapes was high. Specifically, 26 out of 30 participants selected the rounded-sounding word for the rounded shape in the majority of trials, yielding a group-level mean proportion of 0.86 (SD = 0.11). This proportion is substantially above chance (0.50), as assessed by a one-sample t-test on participant-level proportions, t(29) = 22.4, p < .001. A paired analysis comparing choices for rounded versus angular shapes revealed a symmetrical pattern: participants overwhelmingly associated rounded-sounding words with rounded shapes and angular-sounding words with angular shapes. A binomial test aggregating across individual trials further confirmed that the probability of choosing the contour-congruent word exceeded chance at p < .001.

Response times in Experiment 1a were informative as well. Mean response time for correct contour-congruent matches was 842 ms (SD = 343 ms), and for non-congruent matches was 998 ms (SD = 412 ms). A paired t-test showed that responses for congruent trials were significantly faster than for incongruent trials, t(29) = -3.75, p = .001, suggesting processing fluency for contour-based matches in the visual presentation mode.

Experiment 1b: auditory presentation

In Experiment 1b, where words were presented auditorily and shapes were shown visually while participants heard two spoken words sequentially, results paralleled those of the visual-word condition. In this modality, 25 out of 30 participants chose the rounded-sounding word for rounded shapes in the majority of trials, yielding a group-level mean proportion of 0.83 (SD = 0.13). A one-sample t-test showed that this was significantly above chance, t(29) = 17.9, p < .001. Aggregated binomial analyses also confirmed a strong bouba-kiki pattern, p < .001.

Response time analyses showed that mean response time for contour-congruent matches in the auditory condition was 994 ms (SD = 401 ms) and for incongruent matches was 1,152 ms (SD = 436 ms). The difference was significant, t(29) = -2.9, p = .007, indicating faster responses when auditory words matched shape contours. Comparing response times across modalities indicated that responses were moderately faster in Experiment 1a than in Experiment 1b overall, consistent with the additional processing time needed to parse auditory stimuli.

Combined analyses across Experiments 1a and 1b

To formally test the modality-general claim, trial-level mixed-effects logistic regression was applied to the combined dataset from Experiments 1a and 1b with a fixed effect for contour congruency and modality (visual-orthographic vs auditory) and their interaction, and random intercepts for participant and item. The model revealed a strong main effect of contour congruency (β = 2.95, SE = 0.34, z = 8.68, p < .001), indicating a high likelihood of selecting the contour-congruent word. The main effect of modality was not significant (β = -0.12, SE = 0.21, z = -0.57, p = .57), suggesting that the strength of the bouba-kiki mapping did not differ significantly between the visual-orthographic and auditory presentation modes. The interaction between contour congruency and modality was also nonsignificant (β = -0.18, SE = 0.30, z = -0.60, p = .55), providing further evidence that the bouba-kiki effect is robust across the two tested modalities.

Experiment 2: contour versus size

Experiment 2 manipulated contour congruency and size congruency orthogonally in both shape stimuli and nonsense words, allowing the direct comparison of their influences on preferential word assignment. At the participant level, the mean proportion of choices consistent with contour congruency was 0.72 (SD = 0.18), whereas the mean proportion of choices consistent with size congruency was 0.54 (SD = 0.20). A paired t-test comparing these proportions confirmed that contour congruency was chosen significantly more often than size congruency, t(51) = 4.89, p < .001.

Trial-level mixed-effects logistic regression with fixed effects for contour congruency and size congruency and random intercepts for participant and item revealed a strong effect of contour congruency (β = 1.81, SE = 0.39, z = 4.64, p < .001), indicating that contour-congruent words were substantially more likely to be chosen. By contrast, the main effect of size congruency was small and did not reach conventional significance (β = 0.23, SE = 0.17, z = 1.35, p = .18), indicating that mere size-congruent features in words did not strongly predict choices when contour and size cues were in conflict.

Because size symbolism is often linked to diminutive-like phonological features, an additional analysis incorporated a binary predictor coding the presence of a diminutive-like high vowel suffix in the word form and its interaction with size congruency. This model showed a significant interaction: words with an explicit diminutive suffix were more likely to be chosen for small shapes (β = 0.68, SE = 0.27, z = 2.52, p = .012), whereas the simple size congruency effect without a diminutive suffix remained nonsignificant. These results suggest that magnitude-driven mappings in this paradigm are primarily carried by diminutive-like morphology rather than by the other phonological features that were used to cue size in some stimuli.

Further subsidiary analyses examined whether the contour effect persisted when restricting the data to trials in which the size cues were particularly salient (i.e., maximal size differences). Even in these subsets, contour congruency remained the dominant predictor (β = 1.57, SE = 0.45, z = 3.49, p = .0005), whereas size congruency remained non-significant unless accompanied by a diminutive suffix. An analysis of response times showed that choices consistent with contour congruency were made faster (M = 821 ms, SD = 298 ms) than choices consistent with size congruency (M = 937 ms, SD = 350 ms), t(51) = -3.02, p = .004, again indicating a processing advantage for contour-based matches.

Interactions with participant awareness and language background

Participants’ responses to the post-task questionnaire indicated varied levels of explicit awareness of sound-symbolic relations. A supplemental analysis examined whether explicit awareness modulated the observed effects by including a between-subjects factor coding whether participants reported noticing sound-shape correspondences. The contour congruency effect remained robust irrespective of reported awareness (interaction term non-significant, p > .3), indicating that the mapping was not contingent on explicit strategy. Similarly, although participants were all native speakers of the dominant local language, there was no appreciable variation in effects across different dialect or language backgrounds within the sample.

Summary of key results

Across Experiments 1a and 1b, a robust bouba-kiki effect was observed, and this effect did not differ between visual-orthographic and auditory presentation modalities. In Experiment 2, contour-congruent sound-symbolic features strongly predicted participants’ preferential assignment of words to shapes, whereas size-congruent features had only a weak influence unless instantiated as a diminutive-like suffix. These results together indicate that contour-based sound symbolism is both modality-general and hierarchically dominant relative to size-based mappings in adult participants under the tested conditions.

Discussion

The present set of experiments offers converging evidence on two interrelated aspects of sound symbolism: the modality-general robustness of the bouba-kiki mapping and the relative dominance of contour-congruent features over size-related features when both types of cues are available. Across two parallel modality experiments comprising sixty adults in total, participants consistently mapped rounded shapes onto rounded-sounding words and angular shapes onto angular-sounding words regardless of whether words were presented orthographically or auditorily. A separate experiment with fifty-two participants demonstrated that when shapes varied orthogonally in contour and size and when words contained independent contour-congruent and size-congruent features, participants predominantly assigned words to shapes on the basis of contour congruency; size congruency exerted a secondary influence and only showed reliable effects when conveyed through explicit diminutive-like morphology.

These findings have several implications for theoretical accounts of sound symbolism and the organization of crossmodal correspondences in language. First, the modality-general nature of the bouba-kiki effect supports the idea that contour-based sound symbolism reflects an abstract mapping between auditory/articulatory patterns and visual shape properties that is not tightly bound to a single sensory modality. The lack of a significant interaction between modality and contour congruency in the combined analysis suggests that the cognitive representation that underlies bouba-kiki is accessible and operational whether the relevant phonological information is presented in written or spoken form. This cross-modal stability aligns with theories that posit amodal or supramodal representations linking phonological forms and visual shape schemas, and it also complements evidence that children and individuals across cultures demonstrate similar mappings.

Second, the relative dominance of contour cues over size cues in Experiment 2 suggests a hierarchical processing preference for contour information when mapping sounds to shapes. While size and magnitude are prominent dimensions in many crossmodal correspondences, they appear to play a subordinate role relative to contour when both are available and in conflict. This outcome suggests that contour-related features of words—such as consonant manner and vowel backness—may provide more diagnostic information for shape identity than do phonological indicators of magnitude. One reason for this pattern could be that contour cues directly mirror spatial and gestural properties that are central to perceiving an object’s shape, whereas size cues may require additional interpretive mapping onto scalar magnitude systems that are more abstract.

The observation that diminutive-like morphological cues can sometimes drive size-related mappings, despite the general dominance of contour, indicates that different mechanisms may underlie contour and magnitude symbolism. Diminutive morphology is an explicit linguistic marker that often conveys smallness, endearment, or reduction, and it is plausible that speakers have learned robust associations between specific phonological markers and diminutive semantics through linguistic experience. Consequently, when such cueing is present, it can influence participants’ judgments by tapping into learned morphological conventions. In our data, diminutive suffixes increased the likelihood of selecting a word for a small shape, whereas non-morphological size cues were insufficient to produce a comparable effect. This pattern lends support to the notion that magnitude-based correspondences may be more language-specific and mediated by lexical or morphological learning, whereas contour-based correspondences are more grounded in sensorimotor or perceptual analogies and thus more universal.

From a processing perspective, the observed differences in response times provide additional insight: contour-congruent matches were executed faster across both modality and contour/size choice experiments, indicating that contour-based correspondences are not only preferred but also processed more fluently. This fluency suggests that contour congruency facilitates rapid associative mapping, potentially reflecting automatic crossmodal resonance between sound and shape representations. The slower responses for size-based matches, especially those lacking explicit diminutive morphology, may reflect the extra cognitive steps needed to derive magnitude from phonological cues.

Theoretical models of iconicity often debate whether sound-symbolic correspondences are primarily driven by low-level sensory similarities, articulatory resonance, statistical learning, or communicative utility. The present findings can be integrated with each of these perspectives. The modality-general bouba-kiki effect is consistent with accounts that posit crossmodal sensory similitude or shared metric properties across modalities—rounded sounds and rounded shapes may share temporal continuity or spectral smoothness that makes them perceptually compatible. The prominence of contour cues suggests that articulatory gestures that produce rounded versus sharp acoustic signatures may be directly mapped onto visual contour heuristics; for instance, lip rounding and nasal resonance could be experienced as sonically smooth and match curved shapes, whereas abrupt articulatory releases may resonate with jagged visual edges. At the same time, the fact that diminutive morphology exerts influence over magnitude judgments highlights the role of learned, language-specific conventions and statistical regularities in shaping sound-symbolic associations.

Future work should explore several open questions that follow from the current results. One direction is to examine developmental trajectories: do infants and children show the same contour-dominant mapping when contour and size cues conflict, and at what age do diminutive morphological cues begin to influence size assignments? Longitudinal or cross-sectional developmental studies could chart the relative emergence of sensorimotor-based contour correspondences versus learned morphological magnitude correspondences. Another avenue is cross-linguistic research: given that diminutive markers vary across languages, cross-cultural comparisons could test whether the influence of diminutive morphology on size symbolism is consistent across linguistic communities or mediated by the canonical phonological shape of diminutives in a given language.

Neuroscientific approaches could also help disentangle the mechanisms, for example by using neuroimaging to identify whether contour-based mappings engage sensorimotor or visual-shape processing regions, while magnitude-based mappings recruit more abstract magnitude or language-related circuits. Electrophysiological measures could reveal the time course of mapping and whether contour congruency effects arise earlier than size-driven effects. Additionally, studies could manipulate the salience and reliability of contour and size cues to test whether contour dominance is robust across a wide range of sensory conditions or if certain manipulations can elevate the influence of size cues.

Another promising direction involves expanding the range of modalities beyond vision and audition to include haptic exploration and multisensory combinations. If bouba-kiki mappings are truly modality-general, then haptic presentations of rounded versus angular shapes should yield similar mappings to rounded- and angular-sounding words. Preliminary work in other contexts suggests this may be the case, but direct experimental tests with orthogonal contour-size manipulations would strengthen conclusions about sensory generality.

The present findings also have practical implications for areas such as brand naming, product design, and education. Understanding which phonetic features communicate shape or size characteristics most effectively can inform the creation of product names or educational materials that exploit sound-symbolic cues to convey properties intuitively. For instance, names for soft, rounded consumer goods may profit from rounded-sounding phonemes, whereas names for sharp or angular products could leverage more obstruent-rich phonologies; however, designers should be cautious in assuming that diminutive-sounding endings will necessarily convey smallness unless such morphology is explicitly recognized by the target audience.

Several limitations of the current work should be noted. Although the sample sizes were adequate for detecting medium and large effects, larger and more diverse samples would increase confidence in generalizability. The stimuli, while carefully controlled, nevertheless represent a limited slice of the possible phonological and visual variations that could be explored; broader stimulus sets might reveal subtler interactions. Finally, participants in our study were drawn from a fairly homogeneous language community; future research should sample globally to confirm cross-cultural generality.

In conclusion, the experiments reported here demonstrate that contour-based sound symbolism exemplified by the bouba-kiki effect is robust across modalities and that contour congruency predominates over size congruency in guiding preferential assignment of nonsense words to nonsense shapes. Magnitude symbolism can exert influence, but primarily when instantiated through explicit diminutive-like morphological cues rather than through more diffuse phonological features. These results suggest a layered architecture of sound-symbolic mapping in which sensorimotor and perceptual contour correspondences provide a strong, modality-general foundation, and language-specific morphological conventions modulate magnitude-related inferences. Future research that traces the development, neural basis, and cross-linguistic variation of these mappings will further elucidate the role and origins of iconicity in human language.
