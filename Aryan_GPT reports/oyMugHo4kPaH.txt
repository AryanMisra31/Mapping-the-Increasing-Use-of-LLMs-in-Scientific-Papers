Introduction

Social media platforms have become central features of everyday life for a large proportion of the population. People increasingly use social networking sites to communicate, present themselves, and receive feedback from their social networks. The rapid diffusion of social media has generated substantial interest among psychologists and sociologists in understanding how online behavior and feedback relate to individual psychological outcomes. Two platforms that have been particularly prominent in research and public debate are Facebook and Instagram. Both sites provide mechanisms for interpersonal feedback, such as “likes,” comments, and shares, but they differ in their affordances and the typical nature of content users share. Because of their ubiquity and the prominence of visual content in social media, there is a pressing need to better understand how different aspects of social media engagement relate to self-evaluative constructs such as self-esteem and body confidence. Self-esteem refers to an individual’s overall evaluation of their own worth or value, and body confidence captures an individual’s positivity and comfort with their physical appearance. Both constructs are important predictors of broader well-being and mental health, and both have been implicated in pathways through which social media might affect psychological functioning. Research that clarifies relationships between social media metrics, such as counts of likes and time spent on platforms, and self-evaluative outcomes can inform theoretical models and suggest targets for intervention. Contemporary theories of media effects offer multiple plausible mechanisms that connect social media interactions to self-esteem and body image. Social comparison theory suggests that users evaluate themselves relative to others and that exposure to idealized images or curated presentations of peers can lead to upward comparisons that undermine self-regard. Reinforcement models emphasize that social feedback such as likes acts as immediate, quantifiable social reinforcement that may shape self-perceptions over time. Sociocultural perspectives highlight how media convey and reinforce cultural appearance ideals, which can be internalized and influence body confidence. Self-determination theory adds that satisfying needs for relatedness and competence might be fulfilled or thwarted by online interactions, with consequences for self-worth. Each of these accounts suggests different relations between facets of social media use and self-evaluative outcomes. Empirical work has documented both detrimental and beneficial associations between social media use and self-esteem or body image, yielding a complex and sometimes inconsistent picture. Studies that focus on aggregate time spent on social media often find small negative associations with well-being or body satisfaction, but those results vary by age, gender, and by the content and nature of users’ engagements. Other research has shown that active forms of engagement, such as receiving comments or likes, can enhance feelings of social connectedness and momentary mood. A related line of inquiry has focused on platform-specific effects, noting that the visually centered nature of Instagram may differentially impact body image compared with platforms that emphasize textual or mixed media communication. In addition, the social context of feedback plays a role; feedback from close friends may be experienced differently than feedback from strangers or large audiences. Methodological differences across studies also contribute to heterogeneity in results; cross-sectional surveys, experimental manipulations, observational designs, and naturalistic longitudinal tracking each capture distinct aspects of the relationship between social media and self-perception. Given this heterogeneity, careful measurement and clear specification of the social media variables under investigation are essential. One specific variable that has attracted attention is the count of digital “likes” received on posted content. Likes are salient because they are easily quantifiable and visible cues of others’ positive appraisal. Theoretically, likes could boost self-esteem by signaling social approval and reinforcing valued social identities. Alternatively, heavy reliance on external validation through likes might undermine stable self-esteem by shifting the referent for self-worth from internalized standards to fluctuating external signals. Empirical findings on likes are mixed; some studies document modest positive associations between likes and momentary mood or perceived social support, while other results indicate that the pursuit of likes, or the discrepancy between expected and received likes, can increase anxiety and decrease self-esteem. A further complexity is that likes may function differently across platforms. Facebook historically emphasizes connections among existing social circles and documentational sharing, whereas Instagram emphasizes curated visual self-presentation to broad audiences. These differences could mean that a given number of likes on Facebook carries a different social meaning and psychological impact than the same number on Instagram. Age and gender differences also matter because developmental context and sociocultural pressures around appearance are not evenly distributed across demographic groups. In younger populations, for instance, peer feedback might be particularly influential for self-concept development. Gendered patterns of appearance concern suggest that women and girls may be more vulnerable to appearance-related comparisons on visually oriented platforms. Past studies have attempted to parse these subtleties by considering both the amount of time spent on platforms and the nature of content exposure, such as appearance-focused browsing, but comprehensive empirical scrutiny of likes across platforms in relation to both self-esteem and body confidence within the same sample is still limited. In addition, measurement choices matter: the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale is a widely used instrument for assessing global self-esteem, but adaptations have been employed in various research contexts to capture related constructs such as body confidence. Combining validated scales with targeted items about social media behavior can provide a clearer picture of the correlational structure among these variables. There are also unanswered questions about whether the associations observed in prior research are robust when sample recruitment is online and self-selected, which is common in social media studies. Self-selection may produce samples that are especially active on social media and that therefore may not generalize to broader populations. Nevertheless, studies that use self-selected samples can still be informative in revealing mechanisms and patterns that warrant experimental follow-up. The present study was conducted to address some of these gaps by examining the relationships among Facebook and Instagram usage metrics, counts of likes, and participants’ self-reported self-esteem and body confidence. The study employed an online, cross-sectional correlational design and used a modified version of the Rosenberg scale to capture both global self-esteem and aspects of body-related self-regard. Because of the theoretical considerations described above, the study focused on several specific hypotheses. First, it was hypothesized that counts of likes on Facebook posts would be positively associated with global self-esteem, reflecting the reinforcing function of positive social feedback in the context of existing social networks. Second, it was hypothesized that time spent on Instagram would be negatively associated with body confidence, consistent with the platform’s emphasis on curated visual content and the propensity for appearance-based social comparison. Third, the study expected that likes on Instagram might show weaker or more complex associations with self-esteem and body confidence than likes on Facebook, because likes on Instagram may more often signal public visibility and comparative evaluation rather than approval from a close social circle. Fourth, it was expected that most observed correlations would trend in theoretically expected directions but that not all observed associations would reach conventional levels of statistical significance given the modest sample size and the probable heterogeneity in participant experiences. Finally, given the cross-sectional design, the study aimed to generate hypotheses for future experimental or longitudinal work rather than to establish causal effects. The rationale for focusing simultaneously on self-esteem and body confidence was twofold. First, self-esteem and body confidence are related but distinct constructs; treating them jointly allows for an assessment of whether social media variables are associated specifically with appearance-related self-evaluation or with more global self-regard. Second, interventions and public policy might need to target different outcomes depending on whether social media influences are more about general self-worth or about appearance-specific concerns. Overall, the study sought to contribute to the literature by providing a clear, empirically grounded account of how different social media engagement indicators relate to self-evaluative outcomes in a contemporary sample, and by explicitly comparing Facebook and Instagram within the same study. By doing so, the investigation aimed to inform theoretical debates about the mechanisms linking social media to mental health and to identify directions for future experimental and longitudinal research.

Methods

Participants were 125 individuals who completed an online survey designed to assess social media behavior and self-evaluative outcomes. Participants were recruited through self-selecting sampling using social media posts, advertisements on community bulletin boards, and university email lists directing interested individuals to the online questionnaire. Inclusion criteria were being at least 18 years old, currently living in the country in which the study was conducted, and having active accounts on at least one of the two platforms of interest, Facebook or Instagram. Respondents who did not report active engagement on either platform were excluded from analyses because the focus was on platform-related metrics. The final sample was composed of 125 participants after excluding incomplete responses and those failing attention checks embedded in the survey. Demographic information was collected, including age, gender, and approximate level of education, in order to characterize the sample and to permit exploratory analyses of potential moderators. The mean age of the sample was 26.4 years (SD = 7.1), and participants identified as 79 women, 42 men, and 4 individuals who selected another gender category or preferred not to say. A plurality of participants reported having completed tertiary education or being currently enrolled in higher education. The study received approval from the appropriate institutional review board, and all participants provided informed consent electronically prior to accessing the questionnaires. The survey was hosted on a secure online platform, and responses were anonymized at the time of data collection to protect participant privacy. Participants completed three core questionnaires in a single sitting. The first instrument assessed social media usage patterns and metrics for both Facebook and Instagram. Items asked participants to indicate their typical daily time spent on the platform (measured in minutes), the average number of posts they published per month, and an estimate of the average number of likes received per post on each platform. Participants were reminded to consider their recent posting behavior, defined as the previous three months, to provide estimates of typical recent activity rather than lifetime peaks. The second instrument was a slightly modified version of a widely used self-esteem measure, adapted to include items that tapped into body-related confidence while maintaining the general structure of the original scale. The modified scale thus contained items reflecting global self-esteem as well as items worded to assess participants’ comfort and satisfaction with their body and appearance. The total number of items on the modified scale was ten; five items were direct adaptations of items assessing global self-worth and five were designed to capture body confidence. Responses were recorded on a four-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree, as is common with this family of scales. The third questionnaire contained additional items assessing the social context of participants’ social media interactions, including the perceived importance of likes to the participant, whether likes were primarily from close friends or acquaintances, and the degree to which participants compared their appearance to images seen on the platforms. These items were used in exploratory analyses and to aid interpretation of correlational findings. Prior to the main analyses, the psychometric properties of the modified self-esteem/body confidence scale were examined. Internal consistency was estimated using Cronbach’s alpha for the global self-esteem items, for the body confidence items, and for the full scale. The global self-esteem subscale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, as did the body confidence subscale, and the full scale showed a high level of internal consistency. The social media usage items were examined for plausibility and distributions; time-on-platform variables were positively skewed, as is typical for usage data, and were log-transformed for analyses that assumed normality. The average number of likes per post was treated as a continuous variable but extreme outliers, defined as values greater than three standard deviations above the mean, were inspected and winsorized to reduce undue influence on correlational estimates. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Primary analyses focused on Pearson correlation coefficients to examine bivariate relationships among the key variables: average likes per post on Facebook, average likes per post on Instagram, typical daily time on Facebook, typical daily time on Instagram, the global self-esteem subscale score, and the body confidence subscale score. Statistical significance was assessed using an alpha threshold of .05 for two-tailed tests. Given the exploratory nature of some analyses and the multiple correlations computed, effect sizes and confidence intervals were emphasized alongside p-values to provide a fuller picture of the evidence. Secondary analyses included partial correlations controlling for age and gender to check the robustness of observed associations, and a simultaneous multiple regression predicting global self-esteem from the set of social media predictor variables to assess unique contributions. The regression model was checked for violations of assumptions including linearity, homoscedasticity, independence of errors, and multicollinearity; variance inflation factors (VIF) were inspected and found to be within acceptable ranges. For transparency, missing data were handled through listwise deletion for the primary analyses because the amount of missing data was small and missingness appeared to be random based on inspection of patterns and Little’s test for missing completely at random. Sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation were also conducted to ensure that the pattern of results was not an artifact of the handling of missing data. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional benchmarks for correlations and standardized regression coefficients, but emphasis was placed on the practical implications of observed associations rather than on arbitrary cutoffs. To facilitate interpretation, results are described both in terms of statistical significance and in terms of magnitude, and where appropriate, confidence intervals are reported to indicate the precision of estimates. Throughout, the correlational design was acknowledged as a limitation for making causal claims, and analyses were framed as descriptive and exploratory with the goal of informing future experimental work. The study’s methodological choices, including the use of self-report and self-selected sampling, reflect common trade-offs in social media research between ecological realism and experimental control. However, the combination of multiple measures collected in the same session and the comparison of two distinct platforms within a single sample provide a useful basis for evaluating platform-specific patterns and for generating hypotheses for follow-up studies.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The average daily time spent on Facebook was 42.3 minutes (SD = 37.5), and the average daily time on Instagram was 54.7 minutes (SD = 48.1). The distribution of time-on-platform measures was positively skewed, with a subset of participants reporting substantially higher engagement; log transformation was used for analyses sensitive to normality, but raw descriptive values are reported above for interpretability. The mean reported number of likes received per post on Facebook was 28.6 (SD = 44.2) and on Instagram was 67.2 (SD = 132.8). These measures had substantial variability and required inspection for outliers; values greater than three standard deviations above the mean were winsorized to reduce undue influence on correlational analyses. The modified self-esteem/body confidence scale had a mean total score of 28.1 (SD = 6.9) on the possible range of scores, the global self-esteem subscale mean was 15.6 (SD = 3.5), and the body confidence subscale mean was 12.5 (SD = 4.1). Internal consistency reliability was acceptable: Cronbach’s alpha for the global self-esteem subscale was .87, for the body confidence subscale was .84, and for the combined scale was .90, indicating coherent measurement properties for the adapted instrument. Bivariate Pearson correlations are summarized next. The correlation between average number of likes received on Facebook and global self-esteem was positive and statistically significant, r(123) = .29, p = .001, 95% CI [.12, .44]. The correlation between average likes on Facebook and body confidence was smaller but statistically significant, r(123) = .21, p = .019, 95% CI [.03, .37]. The correlation between average likes on Instagram and global self-esteem was positive but did not reach statistical significance, r(123) = .12, p = .18, 95% CI [-.05, .28]. The correlation between average likes on Instagram and body confidence was also positive and non-significant, r(123) = .10, p = .26, 95% CI [-.07, .26]. Typical daily time on Instagram showed a modest negative correlation with body confidence that reached statistical significance, r(123) = -.22, p = .013, 95% CI [-.37, -.04]. Typical daily time on Facebook was not significantly correlated with either global self-esteem, r(123) = -.05, p = .56, 95% CI [-.21, .11], or body confidence, r(123) = -.07, p = .44, 95% CI [-.23, .09]. The average number of posts per month was positively correlated with likes received on both platforms (Facebook: r = .34, p < .001; Instagram: r = .41, p < .001), indicating that users who posted more often tended to receive more likes on average. The perceived importance of likes to the participant was positively correlated with the number of likes received and also positively correlated with global self-esteem, r(123) = .18, p = .04, suggesting that participants who valued likes more tended to report higher self-esteem. However, when stratifying by gender, the pattern of associations showed some differences. For women, time on Instagram was more strongly negatively correlated with body confidence (r = -.28, p = .01) than for men (r = -.09, p = .49), although a formal test of the interaction was not conducted given sample size constraints. Partial correlations controlling for age and gender were computed to assess the robustness of the primary associations. The partial correlation between Facebook likes and global self-esteem remained significant when controlling for age and gender, pr(120) = .27, p = .003. The partial correlation between Instagram time and body confidence also remained significant, pr(120) = -.20, p = .02. The associations between Instagram likes and either self-esteem or body confidence remained non-significant in partial correlation analyses. A simultaneous multiple regression analysis predicted global self-esteem from the set of social media variables: average Facebook likes, average Instagram likes, typical daily time on Facebook (log-transformed), typical daily time on Instagram (log-transformed), and number of posts per month. Age and gender were included as covariates. The overall model was statistically significant, F(7,117) = 3.02, p = .006, and explained approximately 15% of the variance in global self-esteem (adjusted R^2 = .12). Within this model, average Facebook likes was a significant positive predictor of global self-esteem (standardized β = .26, t = 3.00, p = .003), whereas average Instagram likes was not a significant predictor (standardized β = .08, t = 0.95, p = .34). Typical daily time on Instagram was a marginally significant negative predictor of global self-esteem (standardized β = -.14, t = -1.85, p = .067), and typical time on Facebook was not a significant predictor. Number of posts per month had a small positive coefficient that was not statistically significant when entering the other predictors. The variance inflation factors for predictors ranged from 1.02 to 1.48, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern in the regression model. Additional regression analyses were conducted with body confidence as the dependent variable and the same set of predictors. The overall model predicting body confidence was also significant, F(7,117) = 2.54, p = .019, and accounted for approximately 11% of variance in body confidence (adjusted R^2 = .08). In this model, typical daily time on Instagram was a significant negative predictor of body confidence (standardized β = -.21, t = -2.60, p = .01), while average Facebook likes had a smaller positive coefficient that was marginally significant (standardized β = .16, t = 1.92, p = .057). Average Instagram likes again did not significantly predict body confidence when entered alongside the other variables. Sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation for missing values produced the same pattern of significant and non-significant findings, and bootstrapped confidence intervals around the primary correlations were consistent with the parametric intervals reported above. Checks on assumptions indicated that the residuals in the regression models were approximately normally distributed and that there was no evidence of heteroscedasticity based on visual inspection of residual plots and formal tests. A supplementary analysis examined the correlation between the discrepancy of expected likes and received likes (an exploratory item asking participants whether they typically receive more, fewer, or about as many likes as they expect) and global self-esteem. This discrepancy measure was negatively correlated with global self-esteem, r(123) = -.19, p = .03, suggesting that participants who received fewer likes than they expected tended to report lower self-esteem. However, this exploratory finding should be interpreted cautiously given the single-item measurement of expectation-discrepancy. Overall, the pattern of results supported the hypothesis that Facebook likes would be positively associated with global self-esteem, and that time spent on Instagram would be negatively associated with body confidence. Most correlations were in the theoretically expected directions, though several associations were small and non-significant, particularly those involving Instagram likes. These findings were robust to controls for age and gender and were consistent across primary and sensitivity analyses.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationships between Facebook and Instagram usage indicators, counts of likes, and self-reported self-esteem and body confidence in a sample of adults recruited online. The findings provide a nuanced picture in which different social media behaviors show distinct patterns of association with self-evaluative outcomes. Most notably, average likes received on Facebook were positively associated with global self-esteem, an association that persisted after controlling for age and gender and after accounting for other platform usage metrics. This result is consistent with the idea that positive social feedback, as signaled by likes, can bolster perceptions of social approval and competence, which in turn can be reflected in higher self-esteem scores. The result is conceptually coherent with reinforcement accounts of social media feedback, which posit that quantifiable positive responses from one’s social network serve as social rewards and may increase subjective feelings of belonging and self-worth. The finding that likes on Facebook were more strongly associated with self-esteem than likes on Instagram suggests that platform context matters. Facebook is often used to share life updates with a network of existing acquaintances, friends, and family. In this context, likes may carry the meaning of approval from known others and tangible reinforcement of social bonds, which could be particularly relevant for generalized self-esteem. In contrast, Instagram’s audience composition and its emphasis on curated visual presentation may alter how likes are interpreted. On Instagram, likes may be more likely to come from a broader audience that includes strangers or acquaintances, and the visual content that attracts likes may be more appearance-focused. As such, likes on Instagram may reflect attention rather than close social validation, and this may explain why Instagram likes did not show the same positive association with global self-esteem in this study. Another key finding was the negative relationship between time spent on Instagram and body confidence. This association aligns with prior theoretical accounts that emphasize the role of image-centered platforms in promoting appearance-related social comparison and in exposing users to idealized body standards. The cross-sectional correlation suggests that higher engagement with Instagram correlates with lower satisfaction and comfort regarding one’s body. This may be because increased exposure to curated, edited images promotes upward comparisons and internalization of narrow appearance ideals. It may also be that users who are less satisfied with their bodies are more likely to spend time on Instagram seeking information, validation, or strategies for improving appearance, in which case the direction of causality could be reversed or bidirectional. The simultaneous regression models showed that the social media variables collectively explained modest but meaningful proportions of variance in both global self-esteem and body confidence, and that Facebook likes had a unique positive association with self-esteem when considered alongside other predictors. The fact that Instagram likes were not significant predictors in multivariate models suggests that mere counts of likes may not capture the complex social meanings attached to feedback on visually oriented platforms. It is possible that other features, such as the content of comments, private messages, the identity of likers, or the valence of interpersonal interactions, play more important roles in shaping psychological outcomes on Instagram. An exploratory finding from the present study was that the perceived discrepancy between expected and received likes was negatively associated with global self-esteem. Although this measure was single-item and exploratory, it points to a potentially important mechanism: expectations and their violation. If users come to rely on a certain level of external validation, then falling short of those expectations could undermine self-worth, even if the absolute number of likes is not low. This suggests that subjective appraisal of social feedback may be as important as objective counts. The gender-stratified descriptive patterns hinted that the negative association between Instagram time and body confidence might be stronger among women, which would be consistent with broader literature indicating that women are more likely than men to experience appearance-related pressures. However, the current study was not powered to test gender interactions formally, so these observations are tentative and warrant dedicated investigation. Several methodological considerations temper the interpretation of the findings. The correlational, cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. It remains possible that higher self-esteem leads individuals to post differently and receive more likes, or that preexisting body confidence influences how much time someone chooses to spend on Instagram. Longitudinal or experimental designs are required to establish temporal precedence and causal mechanisms. The sample was recruited via self-selection, which may bias the sample toward individuals who are more active on social media and more willing to participate in online research. Consequently, the generalizability of the results to the broader population is uncertain. Nevertheless, such samples are common in social media research and can provide valuable insights into the dynamics experienced by frequent platform users. Measurement limitations are also important to acknowledge. The study relied on self-reported metrics of likes and time spent on platforms rather than objective logs or API-derived data. Self-report estimates can be influenced by recall bias and social desirability, and objective measures could provide more precise and reliable indices of usage. The modified self-esteem/body confidence scale showed good internal consistency, but modifications of established instruments can alter their psychometric properties and their comparability to other studies. Future research should consider using validated, domain-specific measures for body image alongside standardized self-esteem assessments. The study’s analytic approach emphasized bivariate correlations and regression models that adjusted for age and gender, but other potential confounds such as personality traits, baseline mental health, or offline social support were not assessed. These factors could moderate or mediate the links between social media variables and self-evaluative outcomes. For example, individuals with higher trait extraversion might obtain more likes because they engage more actively with social networks, and that could account for associations with self-esteem. Future work should incorporate a broader range of individual difference measures to clarify these pathways. Despite these limitations, the findings have several theoretical and practical implications. The differential patterns observed for Facebook and Instagram underscore the importance of considering platform affordances and social contexts when theorizing about social media effects. The results suggest that not all likes are psychologically equivalent and that platform-specific meanings attached to feedback should be taken into account. The positive association between Facebook likes and self-esteem points to the potential for social reinforcement to support feelings of social connectedness and worth, especially when feedback comes from closer social networks. At the same time, the negative association between Instagram usage time and body confidence highlights the risk that heavy engagement with image-focused platforms can have for appearance-related well-being. From a practical standpoint, these findings suggest a number of avenues for intervention and policy. Educational programs that teach media literacy and critical consumption of social media content could help users recognize the curated nature of online images and reduce harmful upward comparison processes. Interventions that encourage mindful or purpose-driven social media use, rather than passive scrolling, may mitigate negative effects on body image. Platform-level design changes, such as de-emphasizing public counts of likes or providing tools to limit exposure to appearance-focused content, could potentially reduce harmful comparisons while preserving avenues for genuine social support. The idea of selectively highlighting feedback from close social circles, or enabling users to prioritize supportive interactions, may help retain the benefits of social reinforcement without the downsides of mass visibility. Several productive directions for future research emerge from the current study. First, experimental studies that manipulate the receipt of likes or the visibility of like counts could test causal effects on self-esteem and body confidence and could examine whether the source of feedback (friends vs. strangers) moderates these effects. Second, longitudinal studies that track users’ posting behaviors, feedback received, and psychological outcomes over time would help disentangle directionality and identify potential cumulative effects. Third, research that incorporates objective usage data from platform APIs would address measurement limitations and could allow for more fine-grained analyses of the content and context of interactions. Fourth, qualitative and mixed-methods approaches could illuminate how users interpret likes and other forms of feedback, revealing subjective meanings that are not captured by quantitative counts alone. Fifth, studies that examine potential moderators such as baseline self-esteem, social anxiety, and demographic factors would clarify for whom social media is most beneficial or harmful. In addition, research that compares the effects of different forms of feedback—likes, comments, direct messages—would help specify which interactions are most relevant for self-evaluation. The current findings also point to the importance of considering expectation-management in social media experiences. Interventions that help users develop more stable, internalized sources of self-worth and that reduce reliance on external validation could buffer against fluctuations associated with social feedback. Clinical implications include incorporating media-related strategies into interventions for body image disturbance and low self-esteem. Practitioners might work with clients to examine the role of social media in their daily lives, to set boundaries around use, and to practice cognitive reframing of social comparison processes. To summarize, the present study found that average likes on Facebook were positively related to global self-esteem, while time spent on Instagram was negatively related to body confidence. Likes on Instagram did not show a robust relationship with self-esteem or body confidence in multivariate models. Most correlations were in the theoretically expected directions, though several associations were small and not statistically significant. These results suggest that platform-specific features and the social meaning of feedback matter for understanding how social media relates to self-evaluative outcomes. The study contributes to ongoing debates by documenting differential associations across two major platforms and by highlighting the potential importance of feedback source, content type, and user expectations. Future research using longitudinal, experimental, and objective measures will be important to establish causal mechanisms and to inform interventions aimed at maximizing the psychological benefits of social media while minimizing harms.
