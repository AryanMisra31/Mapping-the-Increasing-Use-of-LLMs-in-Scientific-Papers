Introduction

Social media platforms have become ubiquitous arenas for social interaction in contemporary societies. Instagram is one of the most frequently used social media platforms and is characterized by a strong emphasis on visual content and curated personal presentation. People use Instagram for a variety of goals, including maintaining social connections, self-presentation, information seeking, entertainment, and identity exploration. These differential uses suggest that individual differences in personality may influence how and to what extent people engage with the platform. Personality traits, and particularly the broad trait of extraversion, have long been implicated in influencing social behavior, communication patterns, and preferences for social engagement. Extraverted individuals tend to seek out social stimulation, display greater sociability, and prefer more frequent interpersonal interaction, features that could map onto patterns of social media use. For example, individuals high in extraversion may be more likely to use social media actively to connect with others, post updates, and cultivate large social networks. Conversely, introverted individuals may prefer more controlled or asynchronous forms of online interaction, potentially leading to different patterns of engagement on platforms such as Instagram.

A substantial body of research has examined links between personality traits and social media behavior broadly. Many studies indicate that extraversion predicts greater general frequency of social media use, a greater number of online connections, and more active content production. However, results across studies are not uniform, and the relation between extraversion and platform-specific behaviors can vary depending on the affordances of the platform. For instance, some social media platforms emphasize text-based status updates and group interactions, while others focus on images and curated visual self-presentation. The extent to which extraversion predicts use on a specific platform like Instagram may therefore differ from more generalized findings that aggregate across platforms.

Instagram presents a distinctive context for studying personality and social media behavior. Its visual emphasis may differentially appeal to users who enjoy self-presentation, creative expression, and aesthetic curation. The platform also supports both highly public interactions, such as follower counts and public posts, and more intimate interactions via direct messaging and close friends features. Because the platform affords both public and private modes of interaction, it is plausible that extraversion may correlate more strongly with certain Instagram behaviors than with others. For example, extraversion may be linked to the number of followers or to the frequency of posting public content, but less strongly to passive browsing or the use of direct messaging. The multifaceted nature of Instagram use means that examining multiple indicators of engagement is important for understanding personality correlations.

A second consideration in mapping personality to Instagram use is the heterogeneity of motivations that drive platform engagement. Users may increase their use of Instagram for social reasons, such as maintaining friendships or extending social networks; for personal reasons, such as self-expression or popularity-seeking; or for instrumental reasons, such as professional promotion or information gathering. These diverse motivations imply that correlations between personality traits and simple measures of use may be masked if increased use can arise from multiple, distinct motivations that are differentially associated with personality. For example, extraverts might use Instagram frequently to interact publicly with many contacts, while introverts might use Instagram frequently to observe others or to engage in selective, private interactions. If both groups show high frequency of use for different reasons, a simple correlation between extraversion and frequency could be null, despite meaningful personality-linked differences in use patterns.

Empirical studies that have sought to disentangle these possibilities have yielded mixed evidence. Some investigations show clear positive associations between extraversion and various indices of social media activity, including frequency of posting, number of friends, and engagement in public interactive behaviors. Other studies, however, report limited or no relations between extraversion and measures such as time spent on visual platforms or follower counts, suggesting that the picture is not straightforward. Several methodological factors may account for these inconsistencies, including variability in how extraversion is measured, heterogeneity in sample demographics, the choice of specific platform behaviors as dependent measures, and how usage data are operationalized and transformed. These considerations underscore the need for careful, platform-specific analyses that employ multiple indicators of engagement and that assess psychometric properties of personality measures within the sample.

The current study focuses on Instagram and examines whether objective and self-reported indicators of Instagram use are associated with individual differences in extraversion. We operationalized Instagram engagement in several ways. First, we assessed frequency of use, capturing how often participants report visiting or using the platform. Second, we included network-based indicators, namely the number of followers and the number of accounts followed, which together provide an index of an individual's reach and the size of their immediate social network within the platform. Third, we considered the ratio of followers to following as an indicator of social prominence versus social exploration. These indicators were selected to capture both behavioral frequency and structural aspects of an individual's Instagram presence.

Extraversion was measured using items drawn from a well-established brief personality inventory with demonstrated validity in adult samples. The chosen items emphasize sociability, assertiveness, and preference for social interaction. We computed a composite extraversion score to index individual differences along this dimension. We also collected demographic variables known to relate to social media use, including age, gender, and self-reported hours of non-work screen time, to evaluate potential confounding influences. Our approach permitted both zero-order correlational analyses and multivariate regression models adjusting for these demographic covariates.

Several hypotheses guided the investigation. First, based on prior literature that links extraversion to greater social engagement, we hypothesized that extraversion would be positively correlated with frequency of Instagram use. Second, we hypothesized that extraversion would be associated with network measures, such that higher extraversion would correlate with a greater number of followers and a greater number of accounts followed. Third, given the possibility that motivations for use vary, we predicted that associations might be diminished or absent once demographic covariates are considered and if usage distributions are skewed.

We also anticipated possible alternative outcomes. One alternative is a null association between extraversion and Instagram use indicators, which could reflect a genuine lack of relation or could arise if diverse motivations for high levels of Instagram engagement counterbalance across the extraversion continuum. A second alternative is a more complex pattern in which extraversion is associated with some indicators (e.g., followers) but not others (e.g., frequency), reflecting distinct underlying behaviors being captured. A third possibility is that associations are moderated by demographic variables such as age or gender, reflecting cohort or cultural differences in how Instagram is used.

This study attempts to contribute to the field by offering a focused, multimodal assessment of Instagram engagement in relation to extraversion in a relatively large and diverse adult sample. Our methodological approach includes multiple usage indicators, psychometric evaluation of the extraversion measure, statistical controls for key demographics, and appropriate data cleaning and transformation to address skew and outliers. By taking these steps we aimed to provide a clearer picture of whether extraversion reliably predicts Instagram behaviors in everyday users.

Another rationale for this study is the need to clarify whether the widely cited associations between extraversion and social media use generalize to visual, image-centered platforms with diverse interaction affordances. Clarifying this issue has implications both for theory and for applications such as targeted messaging, digital well-being interventions, and understanding the psychological correlates of online social networks. If extraversion is a consistent predictor of Instagram engagement, then theoretical models of personality and media use can incorporate trait-based predispositions to engage with public visual platforms. Conversely, if extraversion is not associated with Instagram use, then models may need to be revised to account for platform-specific affordances and the multiplicity of motivations that drive engagement.

Finally, it is important to note that correlational designs cannot determine causality. Even if we observed associations, we could not infer whether personality drives Instagram use, whether Instagram use shapes personality expression, or whether unmeasured third variables influence both. Nevertheless, the current study’s aim was to map the presence and magnitude of cross-sectional associations as a foundation for future longitudinal or experimental work. In the presence of null findings, we considered alternative explanations and analytic strategies to probe whether more complex patterns, such as opposing associations for subgroups, could be masking relationships at the aggregate level. Ultimately, the study sought to offer a nuanced assessment by using multiple indicators, robust statistical tests, and careful interpretation of both null and non-null findings.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university community and through an online research participation platform. A total of 253 individuals began the survey. After excluding incomplete responses and participants who reported not using Instagram at all, the final sample comprised 218 active Instagram users. The sample included 128 females, 88 males, and 2 participants who selected a non-binary or other gender option. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 56 years, with a mean age of 26.9 years and a standard deviation of 7.8 years. The sample had a diverse range of educational backgrounds with most participants reporting some college education or higher. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by a local institutional ethics board, and procedures adhered to standard ethical guidelines for research with human participants.

Measures were administered via an online questionnaire programmed to ensure required responses to key items. Extraversion was assessed using eight items adapted from a brief, validated personality inventory. Items included statements such as “I see myself as someone who is talkative,” “I see myself as someone who is outgoing, sociable,” and “I see myself as someone who seeks the company of others.” Participants rated each item on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Items were coded in a consistent direction and summed to create an extraversion composite score, with higher scores indicating greater extraversion. Internal consistency for the extraversion composite was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha.

Instagram use was measured with multiple indicators. Frequency of use was assessed with a single item asking participants to estimate how often they used Instagram, with response options ranging from 0 (never) to 5 (multiple times per day). We also included a free-response item asking participants to enter the approximate number of minutes or hours they spent on Instagram per day; participants entered numerical estimates which were converted to minutes for analysis. Network measures consisted of self-reported number of followers and number of accounts followed. Participants were instructed to round to the nearest ten if exact counts were not known. Because social media network counts are often highly skewed, we planned a priori to inspect distributions and apply log transformations to follower and following counts as needed. We also computed a follower-to-following ratio by dividing the number of followers by the number of accounts followed, with care taken to handle zero values (adding one to numerator and denominator where necessary to avoid division by zero).

Additional covariates included age, gender, and self-reported average daily non-work screen time. Participants also reported whether their Instagram account was public or private. These covariates were included in multivariate analyses to examine whether demographic factors could account for associations between extraversion and Instagram measures.

Data preprocessing included checks for missing data, inconsistent responding, and outliers. Participants who left more than 20% of items blank or provided internally inconsistent responses were excluded. For continuous variables with a small number of missing values, we used mean imputation for descriptive completeness and listwise deletion in primary inferential analyses to avoid introducing bias. We examined distributions for follower and following counts and found the expected positive skew; we therefore applied a natural logarithm transformation to these counts after adding one to handle zeros. Frequency of use, measured on an ordinal scale, was treated as interval for correlational analyses but was also analyzed using nonparametric tests to verify robustness. Extraversion scores were inspected for normality, and skewness and kurtosis values were calculated.

Primary analyses consisted of Pearson correlations between extraversion scores and the primary Instagram indicators: frequency of use, minutes per day, log-transformed follower count, log-transformed following count, and follower-to-following ratio. We supplemented these correlations with Spearman rank-order correlations to assess robustness against non-normal distributions. For each correlation, 95% confidence intervals were computed. We also conducted multiple linear regression analyses predicting each Instagram variable from extraversion while adjusting for age, gender, average daily non-work screen time, and account privacy status. Variance inflation factors were reviewed to assess multicollinearity in regression models.

Because prior literature suggested the possibility of subgroup-specific patterns, we conducted exploratory moderation analyses. Specifically, we tested whether age or gender moderated the relation between extraversion and Instagram measures by entering interaction terms (extraversion × age, extraversion × gender) into regression models. Significant interactions were probed using simple slopes analyses where appropriate. We also conducted sensitivity analyses restricting the sample to younger users (age 18–30) and to users with public accounts to see whether associations differed in these subgroups.

Statistical thresholds were set at alpha = .05 for primary tests, with adjustment for multiple comparisons via the false discovery rate for secondary exploratory analyses. Effect sizes are reported to provide a sense of practical significance. All analyses were conducted in a statistical software environment using standard packages for psychometrics and regression modeling. Assumptions of linear regression, including linearity, homoscedasticity, independence of errors, and normality of residuals, were assessed via diagnostic plots and tests. Where assumptions were violated, nonparametric alternatives were considered.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the main variables are presented initially. The mean extraversion score in the sample was 29.6 on an 8-item scale with a theoretical range of 8 to 40, and the observed standard deviation was 5.1. Cronbach’s alpha for the extraversion composite was .83, indicating good internal consistency. For Instagram frequency, the modal response indicated multiple times per day for 61% of participants, daily use for 27%, and less than daily for the remainder. The median estimated time spent on Instagram was 45 minutes per day, with a mean of 63 minutes and a standard deviation of 48 minutes, reflecting a right-skewed distribution. The number of followers had a median of 320 and a mean of 1,204 with substantial positive skew. The number of accounts followed had a median of 280 and a mean of 1,012, also positively skewed. After log transformation, the distributions of follower and following counts were considerably more symmetric.

Primary Pearson correlation analyses between extraversion and Instagram indicators revealed small and non-significant associations. Extraversion correlated r = .04 with frequency of use (p = .57, 95% CI [-.08, .16]). Extraversion correlated r = .06 with minutes per day (p = .36, 95% CI [-.06, .18]). The correlation between extraversion and log-transformed follower count was r = .03 (p = .68, 95% CI [-.09, .15]). The correlation between extraversion and log-transformed following count was r = .01 (p = .87, 95% CI [-.11, .13]). The association with the follower-to-following ratio was r = .05 (p = .47, 95% CI [-.07, .17]). All of these correlations were close to zero and statistically non-significant at the conventional alpha level.

Spearman rank-order correlations yielded similar patterns, with rho = .05 between extraversion and frequency of use (p = .48), rho = .07 between extraversion and minutes per day (p = .29), rho = .02 between extraversion and follower count (p = .79), rho = .00 for following count (p = .99), and rho = .04 for follower-to-following ratio (p = .60). These nonparametric results corroborate the lack of meaningful monotonic associations.

Multiple linear regression models were estimated to adjust for demographic covariates. In a model predicting frequency of use, extraversion was not a significant predictor after adjusting for age, gender, daily non-work screen time, and account privacy status (β = .03, t(212) = 0.56, p = .58). In the same model, younger age (β = -.28, p < .001) and greater daily non-work screen time (β = .34, p < .001) were significant predictors of higher frequency of use. Gender and account privacy were not significant predictors in this model. The full model explained 29% of the variance in frequency of use (adjusted R2 = .27), primarily driven by age and screen time.

When predicting log-transformed follower count, extraversion again failed to reach significance (β = .02, t(212) = 0.32, p = .75) in a model controlling for covariates. In that model, age was a small but significant negative predictor (β = -.15, p = .02), and public versus private account status was a strong predictor (β = .41, p < .001). Daily non-work screen time had a modest positive association (β = .11, p = .08) that did not achieve conventional significance. The model accounted for 22% of the variance in log follower count (adjusted R2 = .20), with account privacy contributing a large portion of explained variance.

A parallel model predicting log-transformed following count showed no significant extraversion effect (β = .01, t(212) = 0.18, p = .86). Significant predictors of following count included account privacy status (β = .29, p < .001) and daily non-work screen time (β = .19, p = .005). The adjusted R2 for that model was .18. Across these models, variance inflation factors were below 2.0, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern.

Exploratory moderation analyses tested whether the relation between extraversion and Instagram measures differed by age or gender. Interaction terms extraversion × age and extraversion × gender were added to the regression models predicting frequency of use and follower counts. None of the interaction terms were significant at the p < .05 level. For example, the extraversion × age interaction predicting frequency of use yielded β = -.01, p = .78, and the extraversion × gender interaction yielded β = .04, p = .58. Simple slopes analyses therefore were not pursued further because there was no evidence of moderation by the tested demographics.

We conducted subgroup sensitivity analyses restricting the sample to users aged 18–30 (n = 146) and to users with public accounts (n = 112). In the younger subgroup, correlations between extraversion and frequency of use were r = .06 (p = .48) and between extraversion and follower count r = .05 (p = .58). In the public-account subgroup, correlations remained trivial and non-significant. These subgroup results are consistent with the overall null pattern.

Given the possibility that non-linear relations might exist between extraversion and Instagram measures, we tested quadratic terms for extraversion in regression models. Quadratic effects did not approach significance for any of the primary outcomes. We also examined whether splitting extraversion into tertiles would reveal group differences in mean Instagram usage. One-way ANOVAs comparing low, medium, and high extraversion groups showed no significant differences in frequency of use, time spent, follower count, or following count.

We report effect sizes to convey the magnitude of observed associations. The largest zero-order correlation observed between extraversion and any Instagram variable was r = .06, which corresponds to a negligible effect size explaining less than 1% of variance. Regression coefficients likewise indicated minimal practical effects of extraversion once demographic covariates were accounted for.

To ensure the null findings were not due to low reliability of the extraversion scale, we examined item-level statistics and factor structure. All extraversion items loaded moderately on a single factor in exploratory factor analysis, and item-total correlations ranged from .42 to .67. Cronbach’s alpha remained at .83, supporting adequate scale reliability. Therefore, lack of association does not appear to be attributable to measurement unreliability of the extraversion construct in this sample.

We conducted post hoc power analyses given the pattern of null results. With a sample size of 218, the study had 80% power to detect a correlation of approximately r = .19 at alpha = .05. The observed correlations were substantially smaller than this threshold, suggesting that if any true associations exist they are likely to be small in magnitude. Thus, the null results indicate that extraversion is not meaningfully related to the Instagram usage measures examined, at least within the detection limits imposed by our sample size.

Additional exploratory analyses considered different operationalizations of usage. We examined the relationship between extraversion and self-reported number of public posts in the past month and found no significant association. We also tested relations between extraversion and self-reported engagement behaviors such as liking, commenting, and posting stories; these behaviors were measured on Likert scales and showed trivial, non-significant correlations with extraversion as well. Taken together, multiple indicators and analytic approaches converged on a pattern of weak and non-significant associations between extraversion and Instagram engagement in this sample.

Methods and results robustness checks, including nonparametric analyses, log-transformations, covariate adjustment, sensitivity analyses, and inspection of psychometric properties, consistently supported the absence of meaningful associations. No analyses produced evidence that extraversion reliably predicts frequency of Instagram use, follower or following counts, or related engagement behaviors. The consistency of null findings across methods and subgroups increases confidence in the robustness of the observed pattern.

Discussion

The present study examined the relation between extraversion and various indicators of Instagram engagement in a sample of active users. Across multiple measures of usage, including frequency of use, daily minutes of engagement, follower and following counts, and engagement behaviors such as posting and commenting, extraversion showed negligible associations. These null results persisted across Pearson and Spearman correlations, multivariate regression models controlling for age, gender, account privacy, and non-work screen time, subgroup analyses, and non-linear specifications. Furthermore, the extraversion measure demonstrated good internal consistency and clear factor structure, suggesting that measurement unreliability is unlikely to explain the absence of observed relationships. Collectively, the findings suggest that extraversion, as operationalized in this study, is not a meaningful predictor of Instagram usage patterns in this cross-sectional adult sample.

Interpreting null findings requires caution and careful consideration of alternative explanations. One straightforward interpretation is that extraversion simply does not strongly influence how people use Instagram in terms of frequency and basic network size. Instagram’s affordances might attract users across the personality spectrum for diverse reasons that do not align cleanly with the sociability dimension captured by extraversion. Visual platforms provide opportunities for controlled self-presentation and asynchronous consumption that may appeal both to extraverts and to introverts. Extraverts may use Instagram to broadcast and connect with broad networks, while introverts may use it as a low-effort way to maintain ties and observe social life without initiating direct contact. Thus, aggregated measures such as frequency or follower count might reflect different behaviors and motivations across individuals, resulting in a net null association with extraversion.

Another interpretation is that platform-specific features shape the personality relationships in ways that differ from general social media use. Prior literature indicating positive associations between extraversion and general social media activity often aggregates across platforms with varying interactional styles. Instagram's emphasis on images, curated aesthetics, and the option of passive consumption may decouple the expression of extraversion from platform engagement. In other words, extraversion may predict behaviors that are more characteristic of other platforms — such as frequent textual updates or live interactive sessions — but not necessarily those dominant on Instagram. This platform specificity could account for discrepancies between our results and studies that reported positive relations between extraversion and social media use more broadly.

A further possibility is that different motivations for Instagram use operate in opposite directions for extraverts and introverts. For example, extraverts might be attracted to Instagram for its public social affordances and potential for broad engagement, resulting in larger follower counts and more outwardly visible activity. Conversely, introverts might increase their use for observational purposes, such as passive browsing, saving content, or maintaining selective, smaller-scale connections via direct messaging. If both ends of the extraversion spectrum display high usage for different reasons, aggregate correlations can be null despite meaningful subgroup-specific behaviors. Our exploratory analyses of interaction terms and subgroup splits did not detect significant moderating effects by age or gender, but they were not powered specifically for detecting opposing subgroup effects tied to distinct motivations. Future studies employing measures that directly assess motivations and types of activity could clarify whether divergent underlying reasons for use mask trait-behavior associations.

It is also necessary to consider measurement and methodological factors that could influence observed associations. Although our extraversion scale showed good internal consistency, it was derived from a brief inventory and might not capture finer-grained facets of extraversion such as gregariousness, activity, or assertiveness. Different facets of extraversion could relate differently to social media behaviors; for instance, the gregariousness facet might predict follower counts, while the activity facet might predict posting frequency. Using a broader inventory that distinguishes extraversion facets could uncover relations obscured by a unidimensional measure. Similarly, our Instagram usage measures relied primarily on self-report. While self-report is practical and widely used, it can introduce inaccuracy due to recall bias or social desirability. Future studies that combine self-report with objective usage logs from the platform could offer more precise assessments and might detect subtle associations not visible in self-report data.

Demographic factors and cohort effects may also play a role. Instagram usage norms and the social meanings of follower counts can differ across age groups, cultures, and social milieus. In our sample, which was relatively young on average, age was a strong predictor of frequency of use, and account privacy status strongly predicted follower counts. These findings suggest that demographic and account-level variables are important determinants of usage outcomes. It is plausible that personality relationships with Instagram use vary across different demographic strata or cultural contexts. Cross-cultural or life-span research could illuminate whether extraversion predicts Instagram engagement differently among adolescents, older adults, or users in diverse cultural settings.

The null findings also raise questions about the functional consequences of Instagram use for personality expression. Even if extraversion does not predict how frequently people use Instagram or how many followers they have, it may still relate to how they use the platform in terms of content style, interactional tone, or emotional outcomes. Extraverts might post more socially oriented content, receive more immediate engagement from their networks, or experience different subjective effects of platform use, such as feelings of connection or social reward. These nuanced differences would not necessarily be captured by global usage metrics. Therefore, future research should extend beyond quantity of use to examine qualitative aspects of online behavior, emotional responses to engagement, and content analyses of posts and interactions.

Longitudinal and experimental designs would also yield important insights. Cross-sectional correlational designs cannot determine causality or temporal dynamics. It is possible that engaging with Instagram shapes certain social behaviors or that shifts in life circumstances influence both personality expression and platform use. Longitudinal data could reveal whether changes in extraversion predict subsequent changes in Instagram behavior or vice versa. Experimental manipulations that alter patterns of use or that prime social motivations might help identify causal pathways between personality and online engagement.

Another avenue for future research is examining the role of motivations and gratifications in mediating the link between extraversion and Instagram behavior. Uses and gratifications theory posits that users engage with media to satisfy particular needs, such as social connection, information, or entertainment. If extraversion predisposes individuals to seek certain gratifications, and those gratifications in turn shape specific Instagram behaviors, then mediation models could reveal indirect relationships. For instance, extraversion might predict social interaction motives, which then predict active posting behaviors. Collecting measures of motivations alongside usage metrics would therefore enhance understanding of personality-behavior relations.

Methodologically, integrating multilevel approaches could be fruitful. Instagram interactions occur within networks, and individual behavior is influenced by the network context, such as norms, reciprocity, and audience composition. Examining how extraversion interacts with network properties to shape behavior requires network-level data. For example, an extravert in a highly engaged network might exhibit different behaviors than an extravert in a less active network. Multilevel modeling or social network analysis could unpack these contextual influences.

The present study has limitations that should be acknowledged. The sample, while adequate in size for detecting small-to-moderate effects, predominantly comprised young adults and was drawn from a university community and an online volunteer pool, which may limit generalizability. Self-reported measures of follower counts and use time can be imprecise, though we attempted to mitigate this by allowing rounded estimates and by log-transforming skewed counts. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Additionally, our operationalization of extraversion did not parcel its facets, which could obscure facet-level effects.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. It focused specifically on Instagram, thereby addressing platform-specificity in the personality-media literature. We used multiple indicators of usage, performed robustness checks including nonparametric tests and sensitivity analyses, and adjusted for key demographic covariates. The consistent pattern of null findings across these approaches strengthens confidence in the conclusion that extraversion is not a robust predictor of the examined Instagram usage metrics in this sample.

In terms of theoretical contribution, the study suggests that generalizations from broader social media research to platform-specific behaviors should be made cautiously. The absence of substantial associations between extraversion and Instagram use implies that platform affordances and user motivations can override simple trait-behavior mappings. The results encourage a more nuanced theoretical framework that considers the interaction of person-level traits, platform features, motivations, and network contexts in shaping online behavior.

Practically, these findings imply that interventions or features on Instagram that target users based on extraversion alone may be ineffective. Platform designers and practitioners should consider a range of psychological and contextual factors when aiming to predict or influence user behavior. For researchers interested in personality and digital behavior, the findings highlight the value of detailed behavioral metrics and the need to model diverse motivations and social contexts.

Future work could address the open questions identified here by combining objective usage data from platform APIs, richer personality assessments that include facets and related constructs (e.g., loneliness, social anxiety, need for affiliation), and direct measures of user motivations. Longitudinal studies could clarify whether personality predicts changes in use over time or whether sustained platform engagement influences trait expression. Experimental interventions could test causal effects of altering the visibility of posts, the composition of followers, or the affordances for interaction to observe how users with different personality dispositions respond.

In conclusion, this study found no substantial evidence that extraversion predicts basic Instagram usage indicators such as frequency of use, time spent, follower counts, or related engagement behaviors. The null results prompt a reconsideration of how personality traits map onto platform-specific digital behaviors and encourage more nuanced, multi-method research that incorporates motivations, content features, network structure, and longitudinal dynamics. Understanding the complex interplay between personality and social media use remains an important endeavor for psychological science and for designing digital environments that accommodate diverse user needs.
