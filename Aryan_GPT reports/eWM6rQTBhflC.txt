Introduction

Human beings are highly attuned to faces, and the visual system prioritizes facial information in ways that facilitate social interaction. The efficiency with which faces are detected and processed has been the subject of considerable research across psychology and neuroscience. One particularly robust finding in the domain of visual search is that certain emotional facial expressions can be detected more rapidly than others when embedded among neutral distractors. This phenomenon has often been framed as an attentional bias, with negative expressions sometimes reported to enjoy a detection advantage that may reflect adaptive sensitivity to threat. The literature supporting a negative face search advantage has been influential because it suggests that early perceptual processes are tuned by emotion in a way that might have evolutionary and social consequences. Yet the pattern of results in the literature is not uniform, and methodological differences between studies may interact with stimulus properties and participant experience to shape observed effects.

In many classic visual search paradigms, researchers manipulate the set size of distractor items to estimate search efficiency. The relationship between reaction time and set size, often summarized as a search slope, provides an index of how efficiently targets can be distinguished from distractors. Shallower slopes indicate more efficient search, often interpreted as a more parallel or automatic detection process, whereas steeper slopes suggest a more serial, attention-demanding process. In studies of face search, slopes have been used to quantify the ease with which emotional faces can be picked out from neutral faces. Early reports suggested that angry or negative faces show shallower slopes than positive faces, bolstering the claim of a negative face search advantage. However, other studies have failed to replicate such a clear pattern, and some have even suggested that positive faces may be prioritized in certain contexts.

One key study that has served as a methodological template for subsequent investigations manipulated the valence of target faces and measured search slopes across different display sizes. That approach facilitated decomposition of raw reaction time differences into efficiency measures that take search capacity into account. The design adopted by many laboratories typically includes multiple set sizes, presentation of target-present and target-absent displays, and repeated trials to provide stable estimates of participants' performance. These procedures allow researchers to ask not only whether one valence yields faster responses on average, but whether the rate at which reaction time increases with discriminative demand differs by valence.

An underexplored factor in the face search literature is the role of participant familiarity with the task across repeated testing sessions. Practice effects are ubiquitous in cognitive tasks, and they can shape both mean response latencies and the structure of processing as reflected in search slopes. Practice may speed up responses by increasing perceptual sensitivity, optimizing motor responses, improving deployment of attention, or by enabling learning of regularities in the stimulus set. In visual search, practice typically reduces reaction times and sometimes flattens slopes, indicating more efficient scanning or better target-distractor discrimination. Yet practice may impact different target types unequally, and this asymmetry could obscure or even reverse apparent valence advantages if practice improves detection of one category more than another.

The notion that practice could differentially affect negative and positive face search is theoretically compelling. Negative facial expressions such as anger are often argued to be prepotent due to their potential signal of threat. If the cognitive system is already biased toward negative expressions, then there may be less room for practice to further enhance their detection. In contrast, if positive faces are not initially as highly prioritized, they may show greater gains with repeated exposure and task practice. Alternatively, it could be that negative expressions are initially harder to discriminate from neutral faces because of subtler feature differences or overlap with neutral face cues in some stimulus sets, leading to larger practice-driven improvements as participants learn diagnostic features. Thus practice could either diminish, abolish, or accentuate observed valence differences depending on these interacting factors.

In the current study, we sought to examine whether practice effects alter the pattern of valence-related search efficiency in a face search task modeled on procedures employed in prior influential work. We operationalized valence as positive (happy) versus negative (angry or sad, depending on stimulus set) facial expressions, and we measured participants' ability to identify the presence of a target face among neutral distractors across three set sizes. Critically, the task was administered on three weekly sessions, enabling assessment of both short-term and longer-term practice effects. By distributing sessions across weeks rather than concentrating practice within a single session, we could examine more durable learning effects that accumulate with repeated exposure while allowing potential consolidation between sessions.

To further assess whether practice-related changes in affective state might mediate performance changes, we measured participants' mood using a standard positive and negative affect questionnaire at two time points: immediately before the first session and immediately after the final session. This decision was motivated by the possibility that mood fluctuations across the testing period could systematically influence attention to emotional expressions. For example, increases in negative affect could heighten sensitivity to negative faces, whereas increases in positive affect might bias attention toward positive faces. Measuring affect therefore allowed us to rule out, or identify, mood change as a confound or mediator of any observed practice effects.

We anticipated several possible outcomes. One possibility was that the negative face search advantage would be robust and persist across sessions despite practice, appearing as consistently shallower slopes for negative targets. A second possibility was that practice would differentially reduce reaction times for one valence over another, thereby eliminating any initial valence advantage or reversing it across sessions. A third possibility was that practice would affect both valences equally, preserving relative differences while globally improving performance. Each of these scenarios would carry distinct implications for theoretical accounts of emotion-related attentional biases and for understanding how experience shapes perceptual processes.

To operationalize our hypotheses, we planned analyses focused on reaction times for correct responses, error rates, and search slopes derived from reaction time by set size functions. We also planned to examine interactions between session and valence to isolate differential practice effects, and to analyze mood data to ascertain whether systematic affective changes occurred across the study period. We chose a within-subjects design in which each participant performed searches for both positive and negative targets in all set sizes and sessions, enhancing statistical power and controlling for between-subject variability.

In considering the expected patterns, it was important to recognize that the absence of a negative face advantage in prior studies has sometimes been attributed to stimulus properties, such as the intensity or prototypicality of emotional expressions, or to task parameters like stimulus duration and response contingency. Therefore we paid careful attention to stimulus selection and display characteristics to ensure that faces were clearly identifiable and standardized in size and luminance. Despite this control, it remained possible that particular stimulus attributes could yield outcomes that diverge from the classical pattern.

Another important consideration is that reaction time differences can sometimes be small and require sufficient trial numbers and participant samples to detect. Accordingly, we determined a sample size that balanced resource constraints with the need for reliable slope estimates across conditions. We also planned a-priori criteria for data trimming and exclusion to ensure that outlier responses did not distort estimates of search efficiency. We established threshold values for extremely fast or slow reaction times and for participants with excessive error rates.

Given the complexity of the possible interactions, we used repeated-measures analysis of variance to test for main effects and interactions of set size, valence, and session. We also computed linear slope estimates for each participant and condition and subjected these estimates to inferential testing to directly probe search efficiency. By combining analyses of raw reaction times with slope-based metrics, we could present a detailed account of how practice influenced both overall speed and the rate of performance change with increasing display size.

Finally, we expected that the mood questionnaire would not show major systematic shifts across the relatively short testing period, but we included it to verify this assumption. If mood did change significantly and systematically in a way that correlated with performance changes, we intended to explore these relationships further using correlational analyses. Overall, the study was designed to provide a comprehensive examination of practice effects on emotional face search and to contribute to ongoing debates about valence-specific attentional biases.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and from the local community. A total of 48 participants completed all three testing sessions. Participants were between 18 and 35 years of age, with a mean age of 22.4 years. The sample included 31 female and 17 male participants. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and provided informed consent prior to participation. Participants received course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. The study was approved by the institutional research ethics committee and followed standard ethical procedures for behavioral research.

Stimuli consisted of grayscale photographs of human faces selected from a standardized face database. For each identity, three expressions were used: a neutral expression to serve as distractors, a positive expression (smiling/happy) to serve as the positive target, and a negative expression (angry) to serve as the negative target. Care was taken to select faces with prototypical emotional expressions and to ensure that images were matched for overall luminance and size. Face images subtended approximately 3 degrees of visual angle on the display when viewed from the testing distance. Images were presented on a uniform gray background to minimize extraneous visual cues.

The search displays contained set sizes of 4, 8, or 12 faces arranged in a 4-by-4 invisible grid. Positions were jittered slightly to avoid perfect alignment. On target-present trials, one of the faces in the display showed the target expression (either positive or negative), and the remaining faces displayed neutral expressions. On target-absent trials, all faces displayed neutral expressions. Participants were instructed to indicate as quickly and accurately as possible whether a target face of the cued valence was present in the display. The identity of the target valence for a given block was not cued within trials to require participants to search for both valences across the session; instead, positive and negative target trials were intermixed within each block to obtain within-session estimates for both valences. Trial order was randomized.

Each testing session comprised 6 blocks of 72 trials each, resulting in 432 trials per session and 1,296 trials across the three sessions. Within each block, trials were balanced for set size and target presence, and the distribution of positive and negative targets was equated. Short rest breaks were provided between blocks. Sessions lasted approximately 50 minutes, and participants were scheduled for three sessions separated by approximately one week each to capture practice effects across repeated weekly exposure.

At the start of the first session and at the end of the third session, participants completed the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) to provide measures of mood. The PANAS consists of brief items that assess positive affect and negative affect on separate scales. Participants completed the PANAS on a computer before beginning the task in session one and immediately after finishing the task in session three. This schedule allowed us to assess any net mood change over the testing period.

Participants were seated approximately 60 cm from a 21-inch computer monitor in a dimly lit testing room. Stimulus presentation and response logging were controlled using standard experimental software. On each trial, a fixation cross appeared for 500 ms, followed by the search display which remained on screen until a response was made or 3,000 ms elapsed. Participants indicated target presence or absence by pressing one of two keyboard keys, with response mapping counterbalanced across participants. A feedback tone was presented only during the initial practice block of each session to familiarize participants with the response mapping; no feedback was provided during experimental blocks.

Before the first block of the first session, participants completed a short practice block of 24 trials to ensure they understood the task. They also completed a short practice block of 24 trials at the start of sessions two and three to re-familiarize them with the response mapping. Practice trials were not included in the analysis. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and as accurately as possible. Instructions emphasized accuracy but encouraged speed to avoid overly conservative strategies.

Data preprocessing included removal of trials with incorrect responses from reaction time analyses. Additionally, extreme response latencies less than 200 ms or greater than 3,000 ms were excluded as anticipatory or inattentive responses. Trials with reaction times greater than 3 standard deviations from an individual participant's condition mean were also excluded as outliers. On average, less than 5% of trials per participant were removed due to these criteria. Participants with overall accuracy below 75% were to be excluded, but no participants met this exclusion criterion; therefore, all 48 participants were retained for analyses.

For each participant, mean reaction times for correct target-present trials were computed separately for each combination of valence (positive, negative), set size (4, 8, 12), and session (1, 2, 3). Linear regression of mean reaction time on set size was conducted for each participant and condition to obtain slope estimates reflecting search efficiency. These slope values were the primary dependent measure for evaluating valence-specific search efficiency and practice effects. Accuracy rates for the same condition cells were calculated and analyzed to ensure that changes in reaction time were not accompanied by speed-accuracy trade-offs.

Statistical analyses were conducted using repeated-measures analysis of variance. A 2 (Valence: positive, negative) x 3 (Set size: 4, 8, 12) x 3 (Session: 1, 2, 3) within-subjects ANOVA was performed on mean reaction times for correct trials. A parallel ANOVA was performed on slope estimates with factors of Valence and Session to directly test for practice-related changes in search efficiency. Accuracy was analyzed with the same 2 x 3 x 3 ANOVA structure to explore potential trade-offs. Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied when sphericity assumptions were violated. Effect sizes were expressed as partial eta squared for ANOVA results. Where relevant, planned comparisons and pairwise t-tests were performed to unpack significant interactions.

Finally, PANAS scores for positive and negative affect were compared between the two measurement points with paired t-tests. Correlations between change in PANAS scores and change in reaction time or slope across sessions were computed to explore whether mood change could account for practice-related performance changes. All reported p-values are two-tailed and evaluated at the alpha = .05 threshold unless otherwise specified.

Results

Descriptive statistics for reaction times and accuracy were inspected prior to formal inferential tests. Mean reaction times decreased across sessions for both valence categories and across all set sizes, indicating clear practice-related improvements in speed. Raw mean reaction times in session one ranged approximately from 620 to 980 ms depending on valence and set size, whereas in session three they ranged approximately from 480 to 760 ms. Accuracy was generally high across conditions, averaging above 90% across all sessions, valences, and set sizes. Error rates showed slight reductions across sessions consistent with generalized practice benefits, but there was no evidence of an accuracy-speed trade-off that would invalidate reaction time improvements.

The 2 (Valence) x 3 (Set size) x 3 (Session) repeated-measures ANOVA on mean reaction times yielded several significant effects. There was a robust main effect of set size, with reaction times increasing as set size increased. This effect confirmed that the search task manipulated attentional demand as expected. The main effect of session was also significant, reflecting the overall reduction in mean reaction times with repeated sessions. These results demonstrate that practice improved raw response speed across the testing period. The main effect of valence was significant, albeit modest in magnitude, indicating that mean reaction times differed between positive and negative target detections across the experiment.

Importantly, the Valence x Session interaction was significant. This interaction indicated that the magnitude of practice-related improvement differed by target valence. Inspection of simple effects showed that while both valence conditions exhibited decreased reaction times across sessions, the negative face target condition showed a larger decrement in reaction time from session one to session three than did the positive face condition. Pairwise comparisons confirmed that the reduction in mean reaction time from session one to three was significantly greater for negative targets than for positive targets. In other words, negative face detection improved more with practice than positive face detection did.

The Valence x Set size interaction was also examined. This interaction was significant, indicating that the effect of valence on mean reaction time varied according to set size. Follow-up analyses showed that at the smallest set size the difference between valences was smaller, whereas at larger set sizes the difference was more pronounced for some sessions. However, the three-way Valence x Set size x Session interaction was not significant, suggesting that although valence effects varied with set size, the manner in which practice altered valence effects did not differ systematically across set sizes.

Given the central role of search slopes in indexing efficiency, linear slope estimates were computed for each participant and condition. Slopes were examined in a 2 (Valence) x 3 (Session) repeated-measures ANOVA. There was a significant main effect of Valence on slopes: slopes for positive targets were shallower than slopes for negative targets when averaged across sessions. This outcome indicated that search efficiency favored positive targets, in the sense that reaction time increased less with increasing set size for positive faces than for negative faces. As a result, the classical negative face search advantage was not observed in this dataset; rather the opposite pattern was present.

The Session main effect on slopes was significant; slopes became shallower with practice across sessions. This finding indicates that search became more efficient overall with repeated testing. The Valence x Session interaction for slopes was also significant. Examination of interaction contrasts revealed that slopes for negative targets showed a larger decrease across sessions than did slopes for positive targets. In particular, negative target slopes decreased markedly from session one to session two and showed further modest decreases by session three. Positive target slopes also decreased but to a lesser extent; the overall effect was that practice reduced the valence difference in slope magnitude across sessions. While positive targets maintained relatively shallower slopes in session one and session two, by session three the difference between valences had notably narrowed.

To quantify the practice-related reductions, we calculated mean slope values. In session one, average slopes were approximately 40 ms per item for negative targets and 30 ms per item for positive targets. By session three, average slopes had fallen to about 22 ms per item for negative targets and 25 ms per item for positive targets. These descriptive values illustrate that the larger initial slope for negative faces decreased substantially with practice such that the advantage in slope for positive faces seen early in the study was attenuated over time. Statistical tests confirmed that the reduction in slope for negative targets from session one to session three was significant and larger than the corresponding reduction for positive targets.

Accuracy data were analyzed to ensure that the faster response times and change in slopes did not come at the cost of decreased accuracy. The ANOVA on accuracy revealed a significant main effect of session, with small but reliable increases in accuracy across sessions. The main effect of valence on accuracy was not significant, indicating that neither positive nor negative targets were detected more accurately on average. The interactions involving accuracy were generally non-significant, reinforcing the conclusion that speed improvements did not reflect a detrimental speed-accuracy trade-off favoring one valence.

We also analyzed target-absent trials separately to probe whether changes in target discrimination were accompanied by alterations in decision criteria. Reaction times on target-absent trials exhibited a similar pattern of reduction across sessions, and no systematic valence effect was observed on these trials since they did not differ by valence. The decline in target-absent response times was consistent with general practice effects such as improved motor response and task familiarity rather than valence-specific learning.

Given the possibility that mood change might account for performance variations, PANAS scores were examined. Paired t-tests comparing PANAS positive affect scores before session one and after session three did not reveal a significant change in positive affect. Similarly, negative affect scores did not show a significant pre-post change across the study. Correlational analyses between change in affect and change in mean reaction times or slopes were conducted and showed no reliable relationships. These results suggest that mood change across the testing period did not drive the observed practice-related performance improvements.

Additional exploratory analyses assessed whether individual differences in baseline performance predicted the magnitude of practice-related improvement. Participants who were slower in session one tended to show larger absolute reductions in reaction time across sessions, consistent with a regression-to-the-mean and more room for improvement pattern. However, this relationship did not interact with valence in a way that would explain the differentially larger practice gains for negative targets. Therefore, the differential practice effect for negative faces cannot be fully attributed to baseline speed differences across participants.

To summarize the main findings succinctly, the present experiment did not replicate a negative face search advantage; instead, search slopes for positive targets were initially shallower than for negative targets. Practice across three weekly sessions led to substantial reductions in reaction time and slopes overall, with particularly large improvements observed for negative targets. These practice effects reduced but did not completely eliminate the initial valence-related differences in search slope. Mood measures did not change across sessions and were unrelated to performance changes, suggesting that practice rather than affective shifts accounted for the observed effects.

Discussion

The current study examined whether practice influences the negative face search advantage using a classic visual search framework in which participants identified emotional face targets among neutral distractors across multiple set sizes and repeated weekly sessions. Our principal findings were that search slopes for positive faces were shallower than those for negative faces when averaged across sessions, indicating that the canonical negative face search advantage did not emerge in this sample under these task conditions. Moreover, practice across three weekly sessions led to robust reductions in reaction time and search slope for both valences, with particularly large reductions observed for negative targets. Importantly, mood as measured by PANAS did not change across the testing period and did not correlate with performance changes, pointing to practice-driven learning rather than affective modulation as the primary driver of observed effects.

These results raise several interrelated issues concerning the malleability of attentional biases, the role of familiarity in face processing, and methodological factors that contribute to variability in valence-related search effects. The absence of a negative face search advantage in the current study is notable given the prominence of that effect in some prior work. One possible interpretation is that the negative face advantage is not a universal property of face processing but rather depends on specific stimulus attributes and task constraints. For example, differences in the intensity or prototypicality of negative versus positive expressions can influence detectability. If the negative expressions used in some prior studies were particularly salient or exaggerated relative to neutral faces, a negative advantage might be more likely. In contrast, our use of standardized faces with matched luminance and carefully controlled expressions may have produced more subtle distinctions between negative and neutral faces, making negative targets less initially privileged.

Another possibility is that the mixture of positive and negative targets interleaved within blocks may have influenced participants' strategies in a way that modulated valence effects. When targets of both valences are intermixed, participants may adopt a more generalized search strategy that emphasizes detecting any deviation from neutrality rather than prioritizing features specific to negative expressions. Such a strategy could attenuate valence-specific advantages. Future work could examine whether blocking by valence produces different initial patterns of slope differences and whether practice interacts with such blocking to alter trajectories of improvement.

The large practice effects observed in this study are consistent with a body of work demonstrating that visual search performance improves with repeated exposure. Practice likely operates through multiple mechanisms. One contribution is perceptual learning, by which observers become better at discriminating relevant features that distinguish targets from distractors. For faces, perceptual learning might involve enhanced sensitivity to diagnostic muscle configurations or to contrast patterns associated with emotional expressions. Another contribution is procedural learning, including more fluent motor responses and optimized attentional deployment, such as quicker orienting to candidate items or improved parallel processing capacity. Memory-based learning might also play a role when stimulus sets are limited in identity, allowing participants to become familiar with particular faces across sessions and to use identity or idiosyncratic cues as shortcuts to identify targets more efficiently.

Our finding that negative face search showed particularly large reductions in reaction time and slope with practice suggests that initially less efficient detection processes for negative targets can be markedly improved through exposure. If negative faces were initially harder to distinguish from neutral distractors in our stimulus set, practice would provide opportunities to learn subtle diagnostic features and thus improve both speed and efficiency. This pattern is consistent with the notion that attentional biases are not fixed but can be shaped by experience. It also aligns with theoretical perspectives emphasizing the interaction of bottom-up stimulus properties and top-down learning in determining attention allocation to emotional stimuli.

The fact that positive targets showed shallower slopes across sessions on average raises questions about how positive and negative expressions differ in their perceptual cues. Smiling faces, as the prototypical positive expression, can produce distinctive visual signatures such as exposed teeth and raised cheek contours that may be visually salient and highly diagnostic. If these cues are relatively uniform across identities and strong compared to the cues that distinguish negative faces from neutral ones, then positive faces might enjoy an initial detection advantage. This possibility suggests that claims of a universal negative advantage should be tempered by careful consideration of expression-specific perceptual distinctiveness.

Methodological features of our task may also help explain the pattern of results. Our use of weekly sessions allowed for consolidation effects between sessions that might amplify practice gains compared to massed practice within a single session. Sleep and offline consolidation are known to enhance some forms of perceptual learning, and similar mechanisms might contribute here. The inter-session intervals in our design may have permitted memory consolidation processes to stabilize improvements, particularly for learning to recognize subtle negative expression cues. Future experiments could manipulate the spacing of practice sessions to directly test the contribution of consolidation to learning in emotional face search.

Another important consideration is the limited stimulus set typically used in laboratory studies, which can introduce familiarity effects that might not generalize to broader populations of faces. If participants encounter the same identities repeatedly, they may learn to recognize specific individuals and use identity-dependent heuristics to detect deviant expressions. Such familiarity could differentially benefit detection of certain expressions if, for some identities, negative expressions are more distinctive once observed repeatedly. To assess generalizability, subsequent work should vary the number of unique identities and include novel transfer tests in which participants search among previously unencountered faces to determine whether practice effects reflect identity-specific learning or more general improvements in emotion discrimination.

Our mood findings further constrain explanations that ascribe performance changes to affect-driven changes in attention. Because PANAS scores did not change across the study and did not correlate with reaction time or slope reductions, it seems unlikely that systematic shifts in participants' affective states drove the observed practice effects. That said, our mood assessment occurred only at two time points, and finer-grained measurement might reveal transient mood fluctuations that could influence moment-to-moment attention. Still, the overall absence of mood change argues in favor of practice and perceptual learning as primary mechanisms.

The practical implications of these findings extend to domains where rapid detection of emotional expressions is critical, such as security screening, clinical assessment, and social robotics. The results suggest that training and repeated exposure can substantially improve detection efficiency for emotional expressions, including those that may initially be less salient. This plasticity has potential utility for interventions aimed at improving social perception in populations where emotion recognition is impaired, such as in certain clinical disorders. However, the idiosyncratic effects of stimulus properties and familiarity caution against assuming that basic valence biases are immutable.

Several limitations of the current study must be acknowledged. One limitation is that the stimuli comprised a limited set of identities drawn from a standardized database. While this approach facilitates control and comparability, it may exaggerate familiarity effects and limit generalizability. A second limitation is that target valence encompassed only prototypical happy and angry expressions; other negative expressions such as fear or disgust might yield different patterns. Third, our sessions were spaced one week apart, which allowed potential consolidation but may not be optimal for all forms of learning; different spacing intervals might produce different practice trajectories. Finally, although the PANAS provided a convenient measure of affect, more nuanced measures of mood or arousal could better capture subtle affective influences.

Future research should pursue several directions to clarify the mechanisms underlying the observed patterns. One line of work would manipulate the extent of stimulus repetition by comparing blocked designs with fully novel faces across sessions to separate identity-specific learning from general perceptual improvements. Another direction would vary the intensity and prototypicality of emotional expressions to test how these features moderate initial valence effects and their malleability with practice. Experimental manipulations of practice spacing and the inclusion of sleep or wake retention intervals would illuminate the role of consolidation. Eye-tracking measures could reveal whether practice induces changes in scanning patterns, fixation durations, or the allocation of attention across the display that account for reductions in slope. Neuroimaging or electrophysiological methods could probe whether neural signatures of face and emotion processing change with practice and whether these changes track behavioral improvements.

In addition, it would be valuable to examine individual difference moderators of practice effects. For instance, trait anxiety, empathy, or baseline face-processing ability might influence both initial valence biases and the degree of learning across sessions. Investigating these moderators could elucidate why some individuals show pronounced practice-related gains for negative faces while others do not. Finally, testing clinical populations with known face-processing deficits could assess whether training regimes similar to those used here can remediate impairments in emotion recognition and whether such training generalizes to ecologically valid social interactions.

In sum, the current experiment indicates that practice substantially improves face search performance and that such practice can differentially benefit negative target detection, reducing valence-specific differences in search efficiency. The absence of a negative face search advantage in our data highlights the importance of stimulus and task factors and suggests that attentional biases to emotional faces are flexible rather than fixed. These findings encourage a nuanced view of emotion-driven attentional prioritization, one that recognizes the shaping role of experience and the necessity of carefully controlled stimulus and procedural choices in interpreting valence effects.
