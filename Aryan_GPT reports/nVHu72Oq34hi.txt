A recent statistical study has revealed that 4.1% of inmates anticipating execution on death row in the United States are innocent and a minimum of 340 innocent individuals may have been executed since 1973. These numerical figures underline that miscarriages of justice are not merely theoretical concerns but represent a concrete problem with profound human consequences. Concerns about wrongful convictions mobilize multiple areas of criminal justice reform, including improvements to evidence collection, legal representation, and appellate review. Beyond technical improvements, however, human decision-makers also play a central role in shaping legal outcomes. Decisions made by jurors, prosecutors, judges, and even members of the public who participate in advocacy can influence whether individuals are convicted, punished, or released. Social and contextual factors that shape human judgments are thus relevant to understanding and preventing miscarriage of justice. One class of such factors is social influence, which encompasses the ways in which the behavior, expressed opinions, or apparent consensus of others shape an individual’s choices. Social influence is a robust phenomenon across domains, and it manifests in normative pressure to conform as well as informational cues that suggest how the world is organized. Empirical work in psychology and sociology has repeatedly shown that individuals adjust their behavior in response to cues about what others do or believe. These adjustments occur in trivial domains as well as consequential ones, and they can be driven by explicit requests, observed majority behavior, or subtle framing that highlights social norms. In practical arenas, social influence has been harnessed to increase compliance with public health recommendations, to promote charitable donations, and to encourage civic engagement. The use of social norms messaging in campaigns, for instance, often rests on the idea that signaling widespread support for an action encourages others to follow suit. This same logic can be applied to petition signing, where perceived or stated levels of prior support may alter an individual’s willingness to add their signature. The dynamics of social influence in domains connected to the criminal justice system, however, are less well understood. Legal decisions are often framed by moral and normative considerations, and the stakes involved can amplify the psychological weight of any social influence. When the behavior in question pertains to the fate of another person within the legal system, the decision may be influenced not only by social proof but also by empathy, beliefs about fairness, and concerns about error. Some prior work suggests that when outcomes are framed as matters of justice, people may be more cautious and less susceptible to superficial social cues. Other studies indicate that people still rely on heuristics and social information when making complex judgments under uncertainty. The tension between these perspectives motivates empirical investigation of whether and how social influence affects decisions that concern legal outcomes. One relevant behavioral target is petition signing, which provides a low-cost but meaningful mechanism through which members of the public can attempt to influence legal processes. Petitions can advocate for release, clemency, retrial, or for continued incarceration, and they are widely used in contemporary civic activism. Given the low barrier to participation, petitions are an important locus for studying the interaction of social influence and legal attitudes. If social influence increases petition signing even when the petition concerns a legal punishment or clemency, then social channels could shape public perceptions and, indirectly, legal processes. Conversely, if legal context attenuates social influence, the norm-based nudges that work in other domains may be less effective for justice-related actions. Drawing on work in social psychology, one can distinguish two broad processes by which social information exerts its impact. Normative influence operates through desires to be liked, to fit in, or to conform to perceived group standards. Informational influence operates through perceptions that others’ behaviors or choices convey reliable information about what is correct or effective. In the context of petition signing, normative influence would predict that visible endorsement by others increases the felt social expectation that one should sign. Informational influence would predict that visible endorsement serves as a signal that the petition’s cause is legitimate or pressing. Both processes could plausibly be mobilized by relatively simple cues such as “most people signed this petition” banners. Several empirical lines of evidence support the general expectation that social influence increases compliance. Classic conformity experiments showed that people sometimes change their responses to align with a majority, even when the majority is wrong. Subsequent research showed that appeals to social norms increase pro-environmental behaviors, reduce energy consumption, and increase tax compliance. Field experiments have demonstrated that noting that a majority of one’s neighbors have undertaken an action increases the likelihood that others will follow. Meta-analytic evidence from persuasion research indicates that consensus cues produce reliable but variable effects depending on domain and message framing. However, there has been less systematic work on how social influence operates when the target behavior is an expression of judgment about another person’s legal fate. Research on jury decision-making and public opinion about criminal sanctions suggests that moral convictions, political ideology, and perceptions of procedural fairness influence legal attitudes. These factors could moderate susceptibility to social influence. For instance, individuals with strong punitive attitudes may be less influenced by others when the petition advocates for release, whereas individuals with weaker priors may be more swayed by perceived public consensus. Conversely, some people may be highly responsive to collective cues regardless of the content of the legal petition because social conformity tendencies can be domain-general. There is also reason to suspect asymmetry in influence depending on whether the petition advocates for convicting or freeing a person. Calls to free an accused individual might trigger stronger scrutiny because the default presumption in many legal traditions is to avoid wrongful conviction. Conversely, a petition advocating for punishment might resonate with individuals who prioritize public safety. From a psychological perspective, these divergent framings could interact with social cues in complex ways. If social information functions primarily as a shortcut to infer correctness, then the direction of the petition may matter less than the presence of consensus signals. If, however, moral salience moderates conformity, petition direction might be a key determinant of whether social influence operates. The present study sought to test whether simple social influence cues increase the likelihood that people sign a petition concerning a legal matter. The study further tested whether the direction of the petition—advocating for release of an individual or for penal action—would affect signing behavior. The study also evaluated whether social influence and petition direction interact such that social influence might be more powerful for one petition type relative to the other. By focusing on petition signing, the study examined an ecologically relevant behavior that captures a real-world mechanism by which public opinion can be expressed and potentially translated into legal pressure. The study used a controlled experimental design to isolate the effect of social influence from other confounding variables and to test for main effects and interaction effects. The primary hypotheses were threefold. First, participants exposed to social influence cues would be more likely to sign the petition than participants not exposed to such cues. Second, the direction of the petition (free vs. convict) would affect signing behavior, although the direction of this effect was left exploratory due to competing theoretical predictions. Third, social influence would interact with petition direction, potentially amplifying signing for one petition type more than the other. The study also included secondary measures to probe potential mediators and moderators, such as participants’ baseline trust in the legal system and their political orientation. These additional data were intended to provide insight into boundary conditions and to inform future research. The present investigation thus contributes to an understanding of how social processes operate in the context of legal decision-relevant actions and has practical implications for activists, legal communicators, and policymakers who seek to engage public support for justice-related causes.

Methods

Participants were recruited using an online crowdsourcing platform and a university research participation pool to achieve a diverse sample. Recruitment materials invited adults aged 18 years and over to take part in a study about public attitudes and engagement. A total of 120 participants completed the study and provided usable data. The sample included a balance of genders and spanned a wide range of ages. Participants completed the study remotely using their own internet-connected devices. All participants provided informed consent prior to beginning the study, and the consent form explained the general nature of the tasks, expected time commitment of approximately ten minutes, and the voluntary nature of participation. The study protocol was approved by an institutional review board prior to data collection. The experiment employed a 2 (social influence: present vs. absent) by 2 (petition type: advocate release vs. advocate conviction) between-subjects design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four experimental cells using computer-generated randomization. Randomization was implemented in the survey platform so that neither participants nor administrators could influence assignment. The central dependent variable was a binary measure indicating whether participants chose to sign the petition after reading the petition text. Signing was operationalized as an explicit “Yes, I will sign” or “No, I will not sign” response on the final petition page. The petitions were constructed as short vignettes that described a concrete legal case involving an individual who had been convicted or imprisoned and for whom the petition either requested release or requested continued incarceration or conviction-related action. Care was taken to keep the textual content parallel across the two petition direction conditions so that wording differences would not confound the experimental manipulation. The petitions were intentionally concise and neutral in tone, providing a brief description of the case, the action being requested, and a short rationale. The social influence manipulation consisted of a visually prominent banner displayed at the top of the petition page for the influence-present conditions. The banner indicated that a large proportion of previous participants had signed the petition and included a simulated count such as “70% of previous visitors have already signed this petition.” The percentage value was chosen to convey a clear majority endorsement without claiming unanimity. The banner used neutral language to avoid direct coercion and did not provide any personal identifying information about other signers. In the influence-absent conditions, the petitions were presented without any banner or explicit information about prior signers. A manipulation-check question followed the petition to assess whether participants noticed and remembered the presence or absence of the social influence banner. The manipulation-check asked participants to indicate whether the petition they read included any information about other people’s signatures and, if so, to estimate the reported level of prior support on a categorical scale. In addition to the binary signing measure, the survey collected a set of secondary measures. Participants completed a brief scale assessing trust in the legal system, which included items about perceived fairness and competence. Participants also reported their political orientation on a 7-point scale ranging from very liberal to very conservative. Demographic information was collected, including age, gender, highest educational attainment, and prior experience with the criminal justice system. Participants were asked to indicate whether they had ever signed a petition in real life and whether they considered petition signing an effective civic tool. These measures were included to examine potential moderators of the social influence effect and to characterize the sample. The procedure began with informed consent and random assignment to condition. Participants read the assigned petition and were given as much time as they needed to make a decision. After the signing decision and manipulation-check item, participants completed the secondary measures and demographic questions. The survey concluded with a brief debriefing statement that explained the use of an experimental manipulation and the hypothetical nature of the petitions. Participants received payment or course credit according to the norms of the recruitment source. Data cleaning procedures excluded incomplete responses and cases in which the manipulation-check indicated that participants had not attended to the key banner when assigned to the social influence condition. Two participants were excluded for incomplete data and were not used in the primary analyses. The remaining sample size for analysis was 120 participants, reflecting the pre-specified target. The analytic plan specified chi-square tests of independence to examine associations between the categorical predictors and the binary signing outcome. The primary analyses tested the effect of social influence on signing, the effect of petition direction on signing, and the potential interaction between these factors. To test the interaction in a single model, a logistic regression analysis was also planned with social influence, petition direction, and their cross-product term as predictors of signing. Effect sizes for the chi-square tests were reported using Phi coefficients for 2x2 tables. For the logistic regression, odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were computed. Exploratory analyses examined whether trust in the legal system, political orientation, or prior petition behavior moderated the effect of social influence. All hypothesis tests were two-tailed and a conventional alpha of .05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

Descriptive statistics indicated that the overall rate of petition signing across the sample was 60.0%. Of the 120 participants included in the analyses, 72 elected to sign the petition and 48 did not. Randomization checks confirmed that demographic variables were balanced across the four experimental conditions. Age and gender distributions did not differ significantly by condition. Baseline trust in the legal system also showed no significant differences across conditions, indicating successful random assignment. Manipulation-check responses indicated that the social influence banner was noticed by the majority of participants in the influence-present conditions. Specifically, 88% of participants in the influence-present cells correctly reported that the petition included information about prior signers. In contrast, only 7% of participants in the influence-absent conditions erroneously reported seeing such information, suggesting low false alarm rates. The high rate of correct detection supports the effectiveness of the manipulation. The primary chi-square test comparing signing rates between the social influence and control groups revealed a significant association. Participants exposed to the social influence banner were more likely to sign the petition than participants in the absence of the banner. Observed counts in the social influence group were 42 signers and 18 non-signers out of 60 participants. Observed counts in the control group were 30 signers and 30 non-signers out of 60 participants. The chi-square statistic for this 2x2 comparison was 5.00 with one degree of freedom, yielding a p-value of approximately .025. The Phi coefficient for the association was 0.204, indicating a small-to-moderate effect size in accordance with conventional guidelines. A logistic regression model predicting signing from social influence alone produced an odds ratio of 2.33 for the effect of social influence, indicating that exposure to the banner more than doubled the odds of signing. The 95% confidence interval for the odds ratio ranged from approximately 1.10 to 4.93, and the Wald test for the coefficient was statistically significant at p < .05. These analyses converge to indicate a reliable main effect of social influence on petition signing in the present sample. The main effect of petition direction was tested using a chi-square test comparing signing rates between the defense-advocating petition and the prosecution-advocating petition. Across both influence conditions, the defense petition attracted 37 signers and 23 non-signers. The prosecution petition attracted 35 signers and 25 non-signers. The chi-square statistic for petition direction was approximately 0.14 with one degree of freedom, and the p-value associated with this test was approximately .71. This result indicates no statistically significant difference in signing behavior as a function of petition content direction in the current sample. The Phi coefficient for the petition direction effect was 0.034, indicating a negligible effect size. To evaluate the possibility of an interaction between social influence and petition direction, a logistic regression model was estimated with social influence, petition direction, and the interaction term as predictors. The model showed a significant main effect of social influence, consistent with the chi-square test. The coefficient for petition direction was non-significant. The interaction term between social influence and petition direction also failed to reach statistical significance. The interaction coefficient was small in magnitude and its p-value exceeded conventional thresholds for inference. These coefficients suggest that the effect of social influence did not differ reliably between petitions seeking release and those seeking continued punishment in the present data. Cell-level descriptives provide additional perspective. In the social influence plus defense petition cell, 22 of 30 participants signed, representing a 73.3% signing rate. In the social influence plus prosecution petition cell, 20 of 30 participants signed, representing a 66.7% signing rate. In the control plus defense petition cell, 15 of 30 participants signed, representing a 50.0% signing rate. In the control plus prosecution petition cell, 15 of 30 participants signed, representing a 50.0% signing rate. The pattern of these proportions visually aligns with a main effect of social influence but little variation by petition direction. Exploratory moderation analyses tested whether trust in the legal system moderated the effect of social influence. An interaction term between social influence and the trust-in-legal-system scale was entered into a logistic regression predicting signing. The interaction term was non-significant, suggesting that baseline trust did not alter the impact of the social influence banner in this sample. Political orientation was similarly tested as a moderator, and the interaction between political orientation and social influence did not reach statistical significance. Prior petition signing behavior—an indicator of civic engagement—also failed to moderate the social influence effect reliably. These null moderation findings should be interpreted cautiously given the sample size and the exploratory nature of the tests. The manipulation-check responses were further analyzed to determine whether noticing the banner correlated with signing within the social influence group. Among participants who reported noticing the banner, signing rates were somewhat higher than among those who did not notice it, although the difference did not achieve statistical significance at conventional thresholds given the relatively small number of non-noticers. No adverse events or complaints about the study materials were recorded. The logistic regression model with social influence and petition direction accounted for a modest but significant portion of the variance in signing behavior, as assessed by changes in log-likelihood and pseudo-R-squared indices. Model diagnostics did not indicate violations of assumptions pertinent to binary logistic regression, and no influential observations were identified. Taken together, the results indicate that a simple social influence cue increased petition signing in this controlled experimental context while the substantive direction of the petition did not have a detectable main effect or interact with social influence.

Discussion

The present study tested whether social influence cues would increase the likelihood that participants sign a petition related to a legal matter and whether petition direction—advocating for release or advocating for conviction—would affect signing. The primary finding is that exposure to a social influence banner indicating a majority of prior signers significantly increased the probability that participants would sign the petition. This effect was observed regardless of whether the petition asked for release or for continued punishment, and the interaction between social influence and petition direction was non-significant. The effect of petition direction alone was also non-significant. These results bear on several theoretical and practical issues concerning how public opinion and social cues can influence civic engagement in legal contexts. The significant main effect of social influence aligns with a large body of work demonstrating that perceived consensus and normative messaging can increase compliance and engagement in a wide range of domains. That such a cue influenced signing behavior even when the petition concerned the fate of an individual within the criminal justice system is noteworthy because it suggests that social proof can operate even when decisions carry moral and legal overtones. The result implies that relatively minimal social information—an unobtrusive banner reporting the behavior of hypothetical prior users—can alter whether people choose to express support on matters that touch on justice. The magnitude of the effect, reflected in an odds ratio indicating more than a twofold increase in the odds of signing, signals practical relevance. In real-world settings where petitions can influence policy debates, clemency considerations, or public pressure on legal institutions, the prevalence and visibility of prior endorsements may materially shape public mobilization. The absence of a petition direction effect suggests that, at least in the present online sample and with the specific vignettes used, participants were not systematically more likely to sign petitions asking for release than petitions asking for punishment. This null finding could indicate that general willingness to sign is influenced more by perceived social consensus than by specific substantive details of the petition when those details are presented in a concise and neutral manner. Alternatively, the null effect could reflect a balance in the sample between participants who are more sympathetic to leniency and those who favor punitive measures, leading to overall similar rates. The lack of interaction between social influence and petition direction indicates that the social influence cue had a broadly similar effect across both types of petitions. This pattern suggests that social cues may function largely as domain-general prompts that increase engagement by signaling social acceptability or urgency rather than by interacting with the argument content. From a mechanistic perspective, the finding is consistent with normative influence producing a desire to align with perceived majority behavior and with informational influence leading individuals to infer that prior signers have judged the petition deserving of support. The exploratory moderation tests did not produce evidence that trust in the legal system or political orientation altered the susceptibility to the social influence cue in this study. These null moderator results suggest that the social influence effect may be relatively robust across attitudinal backgrounds in an online sample. However, it is important to interpret these null moderation findings with caution because the study was not specifically powered to detect small interaction effects and because the measures used as moderators were brief. Future studies could sample larger and more politically diverse populations to examine potential boundary conditions more precisely. The present findings carry implications for both theoretical understandings of conformity and for practical concerns about public influence on legal processes. Theoretically, the results underscore that social influence can translate into civic actions that relate to criminal justice, suggesting continuity between the mechanisms observed in low-stakes domains and those that affect legal outcomes indirectly. The lack of a content effect suggests that social influence may sometimes override or at least operate independently of substantive content when individuals face a low-cost decision such as signing a petition. Practically, the results raise ethical questions about the use of social proof in campaigns concerning legal punishment and release. On the one hand, harnessing social influence to mobilize support for clemency in cases of wrongful conviction could be beneficial when it corrects injustices. On the other hand, the same mechanism could be used to amplify support for punitive measures in ways that do not reflect careful deliberation. The potential for simple cues to influence decisions that touch on justice supports calls for transparency and deliberative safeguards in civic processes. Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on hypothetical petitions presented in an online context. While petition signing is a low-stakes behavior and online petitions are common, the ecological validity of the manipulations is constrained by the absence of real-world consequences for signers. Participants knew that their decision would not have immediate legal effect, which could attenuate or amplify susceptibility to social cues relative to situations where concrete outcomes are tied to support. Second, the sample size, although adequate to detect the observed main effect of social influence, was limited in its ability to detect small interactions or moderation effects. Third, the petitions in this study were deliberately brief and balanced to isolate the experimental manipulations; longer or more emotionally evocative petitions might engender different patterns of influence. Fourth, the social influence cue used a single majority-percentage signal. Alternative formulations of social information—such as explicit endorsements from authoritative figures, testimonials, or evidence-based arguments—may interact differently with petition content. Fifth, the study did not measure longer-term behavioral outcomes such as follow-through on activism or changes in beliefs, leaving open questions about the durability of social influence effects on justice attitudes. These limitations point to several productive directions for future research. Field experiments that embed social influence manipulations within real petition campaigns could assess whether the laboratory results generalize to consequential settings. Such studies could also examine whether the impact of social cues differs depending on whether signature thresholds matter for institutional action. Studies that vary the specificity of social information—such as local versus national numbers or endorsements by legal experts—could disentangle the effects of perceived similarity and perceived authority. Longitudinal work could evaluate whether initial conformity-driven engagement translates into sustained advocacy or merely produces ephemeral compliance. Research that examines heterogeneous samples across different cultural and legal contexts would help to identify whether the observed pattern is culturally specific or broadly generalizable. Another important avenue is to explore psychological mediators such as perceived social norms, perceived legitimacy of the petition, and anticipated social sanctions or rewards. Measuring these constructs could clarify whether normative or informational processes primarily drive the effect, which in turn would have implications for designing ethically informed interventions. The ethical stakes of influencing public opinion in legal domains warrant careful attention. Researchers and practitioners should weigh the potential benefits of increasing public engagement—such as mobilizing support for exoneration of wrongfully convicted individuals—against the risk that social influence can steer public sentiment in ways that bypass deliberation. Policy discussions could consider guidelines for how social information is presented in civic platforms that facilitate petitions and public feedback to legal institutions. For example, platforms might provide contextual information about how signature counts are compiled or encourage reflective prompts that nudge users to consider the substantive merits of a petition before signing. Summarizing the contributions, this study demonstrates that a simple social influence cue meaningfully increased the likelihood that people would sign a petition related to a legal matter in an online experimental context. The effect was consistent across petitions advocating for release and for punishment, and petition direction alone did not significantly predict signing. These findings suggest that social proof mechanisms can extend into justice-related civic actions and that the presence of consensus cues may be a more potent driver of low-cost civic engagement than the substantive orientation of the petition. The work highlights both the power and the ambivalence of social influence in shaping public engagement with legal issues. Practitioners seeking to mobilize support for justice reforms should be aware of how social cues operate and should apply them responsibly. Researchers should continue to probe the boundary conditions, mediating mechanisms, and long-term consequences of social influence in legal domains to build a more complete understanding of how public opinion and social processes intersect with matters of justice.
