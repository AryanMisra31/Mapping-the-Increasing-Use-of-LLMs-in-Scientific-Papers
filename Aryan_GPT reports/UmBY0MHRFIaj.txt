Introduction

The aging of the human population has made understanding cognitive change across the lifespan a central concern for cognitive science and public health. One of the most robust observations in the cognitive aging literature is that memory performance often declines with advancing age. This decline, however, is not uniform across different forms of memory and cognitive operations. Episodic memory, which refers to the capacity to recollect specific events and their contextual details, is frequently reported to be particularly vulnerable to the effects of aging. Within episodic memory, a distinction that has received substantial attention is between memory for individual items (item memory) and memory for associations between items (associative memory). Item memory refers to recognition or recall of a single element encountered previously, whereas associative memory refers to memory for the relationship or linkage between two or more elements, such as an item and its context or two items presented together. Across numerous studies, associative memory tends to show greater age-related decline than item memory, an observation that has been formalized in the associative deficit hypothesis. The associative deficit hypothesis (ADH) proposes that older adults have specific difficulty forming and retrieving associations among elements of an episodic event, beyond any decline in memory for individual items. This hypothesis has been influential in explaining patterns of age-related memory change and has inspired a large body of empirical research testing its predictions under different materials, instructions, and tasks.

The ADH is supported by a range of experimental findings demonstrating that older adults often show a relatively larger impairment on tests that require memory for item–item or item–context associations than on tests that require memory for single items. Many experiments have shown that the associative deficit is evident across various modalities and materials, including word pairs, face–name pairs, picture-location bindings, and visuospatial associations. The putative mechanisms underlying the associative deficit have been debated, with explanations ranging from failures at the encoding stage—such that older adults do not bind elements together as effectively—to failures at retrieval, where older adults may have difficulty reinstating the binding or using appropriate retrieval cues. Other accounts emphasize strategic differences, reduced attentional resources, slower processing speed, or declines in hippocampal and medial temporal lobe function that disproportionately affect associative encoding and retrieval. Importantly, the ADH does not deny that item memory declines with age; rather it asserts that associative memory declines more sharply and that this disproportionate deficit helps account for the overall pattern of episodic memory impairment.

Alongside age-related factors, individual differences unrelated to age also influence episodic memory performance. One individual difference that has received attention in relation to episodic memory is handedness and cerebral lateralization. Handedness is commonly assessed via inventories such as the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory, which yields a laterality quotient indicating the strength and direction of hand preference. Some research has suggested that individuals who are strongly right-handed, often operationalized as having a high laterality quotient indicating consistent use of the right hand for most tasks, may show different patterns of memory performance than individuals who are mixed-handed or left-handed. The underlying hypothesis motivating such work is that degree of handedness is a behavioral proxy for the degree of hemispheric lateralization of cognitive functions, and that atypical or weaker lateralization may sometimes be associated with cognitive advantages for certain tasks. A subset of studies has reported that mixed-handed individuals, or those with less strongly lateralized hand preferences, can exhibit superior performance on episodic memory tasks relative to strongly right-handed individuals. The rationale for this finding centers on the idea that a more bilateral representation of cognitive functions might allow for greater neural resources or compensatory processing during complex memory operations, including associative binding.

Despite these proposals, the literature on handedness and memory is mixed and not unequivocal. Some investigations have reported no reliable relationship between handedness and various forms of memory, or effects that are small and inconsistent across tasks. Variation in findings across studies may stem from differences in how handedness is measured and categorized, from sampling differences in the proportion of left- and mixed-handed individuals, and from differences in the memory tasks used. Moreover, handedness effects that do emerge in some studies could reflect interactions with other individual differences, such as sex, age, cognitive reserve, or education. Thus, the question of whether handedness reliably predicts episodic memory performance across age groups remains an open empirical issue.

The present study was designed to test two related lines of inquiry that converge on episodic memory. The first line concerns the associative deficit hypothesis and whether older adults show a disproportionate impairment on an associative recognition task relative to item recognition. The second line concerns whether handedness, assessed via the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory, is associated with episodic memory performance and whether any such association varies as a function of age. The combination of these two lines is motivated by the possibility that handedness-related differences in lateralization could interact with age-related changes in brain structure and function to influence associative memory. If, for example, mixed-handed individuals maintain more bilateral engagement of neural systems that support binding, they might show a smaller associative deficit in older age than strongly right-handed peers.

To test these questions, we compared young and older adults on two memory tests: a word item recognition test and an associative recognition test for word pairs. The item recognition test required participants to recognize single words that had been presented during a study phase among novel foils. The associative recognition test required participants to discriminate between intact studied word pairs and recombined or new pairs that were not presented together at study. Associative recognition has often proved sensitive to age-related deficits in binding, and it provides a direct test of the ADH because it places greater demands on relational encoding and retrieval processes than item recognition. In both tasks participants studied the same pool of words, and correct responding depended on accurate memory for items and for the links between them.

Handedness was assessed via the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory, which yields a continuous laterality quotient as well as allowing categorization into consistent right-handed and mixed/left-handed groups. For analytical clarity, we analyzed the laterality quotient both as a continuous predictor and via categorical groupings, so that we could examine linear relations and also compare groups of participants with markedly different hand preference profiles. We pre-registered an analytic plan that focused on the predicted Age × Test Type interaction—that is, a larger difference between young and older adults on associative recognition than on item recognition—and on the main and interaction effects of handedness.

We expected, based on the ADH, that older adults would show worse performance on associative recognition than young adults and that this difference would be larger than the age difference observed for item recognition. We also explored the potential association between handedness and episodic memory performance. If handedness exerts a reliable effect, we expected mixed- or left-handed participants to show better associative memory performance relative to strongly right-handed participants, potentially attenuating any age-related associative deficit. We also planned exploratory correlational analyses between the laterality quotient and memory scores to test for linear associations.

Despite clear hypotheses, it is important to recognize that empirical tests of the ADH and handedness effects face several methodological challenges. First, the magnitude of associative deficits can vary depending on task parameters such as the depth of encoding instructions, the retention interval, and the similarity between items. Shallow encoding, short retention intervals, or highly distinctive materials can reduce the sensitivity of tasks to detect age differences. Second, the assessment of handedness via self-report inventories may not capture the full complexity of functional lateralization, which can be more accurately probed by neuroimaging or by behavioural tasks that index hemispheric specialization. Third, individual differences in cognitive reserve, educational attainment, health status, and sensory abilities can moderate age effects and must be considered when interpreting results. Finally, power considerations are critical; because effect sizes for handedness-memory relations may be modest, sufficiently large and balanced samples are necessary to detect them reliably.

Given these considerations, the present study employed careful task design and analytic procedures to maximize sensitivity to age and handedness effects. We used word materials matched on frequency and concreteness, standardized encoding and test procedures, and multiple scoring indices including corrected recognition scores. Participants were screened for neurological and psychiatric conditions and for significant visual impairments that could confound performance. The sample was recruited to ensure adequate representation of young and older adults and to allow for comparisons across handedness groups with reasonable balance.

The study sought to make three contributions. First, it aimed to provide a straightforward test of the ADH using paired associative and item recognition tasks administered to young and older adults. Second, it examined whether degree and direction of handedness relate to episodic memory performance and whether this relation interacts with age. Third, it sought to clarify potential methodological explanations for inconsistencies in prior literature, by exploring sensitivity analyses, correlational patterns, and the robustness of effects to alternative scoring methods. In doing so, the study aimed to inform theories about the mechanisms of age-related memory change and to evaluate whether behavioral markers of lateralization such as handedness contribute meaningfully to individual differences in episodic memory across the adult lifespan.

Methods

Participants

A total of 64 participants were recruited for the study. The sample included 32 young adults and 32 older adults. Young adults were recruited from a university participant pool and the local community, and were aged between 18 and 30 years. Older adults were recruited from community centers and volunteer registries, and were aged between 65 and 80 years. Recruitment materials sought right-, left-, and mixed-handed participants to achieve approximately balanced handedness groups within each age cohort. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and fluency in English as their primary language. Exclusion criteria included a history of neurological disorder, major psychiatric illness, or current use of medications known to substantially affect cognitive function; participants were screened via a brief health questionnaire prior to testing. Participants were compensated with either course credit or a modest monetary payment for their time. Ethical approval for the procedures was obtained from the institution’s human research ethics committee, and all participants provided informed consent in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Handedness assessment

Handedness was assessed using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (EHI). Participants completed the inventory by indicating hand preference across a series of everyday actions. The inventory yields a laterality quotient (LQ) ranging from -100 (strongly left-handed) to +100 (strongly right-handed). For categorical analyses, participants were classified as consistent right-handed if their LQ was greater than 80, and as mixed/left-handed if their LQ was 80 or lower. This categorization is consistent with several previous studies that distinguish strong right-handedness from less consistent or alternative preferences. The LQ was retained as a continuous measure for correlational analyses as well.

Materials and stimuli

Word stimuli were drawn from a standardized lexical database and selected to be concrete, imageable, and of moderate frequency in English. A pool of 240 words was prepared, and words were randomly assigned to study lists and test probes such that each participant saw a unique configuration of studied and lure items. For the item recognition task, 40 words were presented at study and later tested among 40 lures at recognition. For the associative recognition task, 40 word pairs were presented at study and later tested along with 40 recombined or new pairs. Word pairs were constructed by randomly pairing two words from the study pool, constrained to avoid strong semantic associations between pair members. Word order within pairs was randomized, and presentation order of items and pairs across trials was counterbalanced across participants.

Procedure

Testing took place in a quiet laboratory setting. Participants were tested individually in a single session lasting approximately 60 to 75 minutes. After informed consent and completion of the handedness inventory, participants completed demographic and health questionnaires, followed by the memory tasks.

The experiment began with instructions and practice trials to familiarize participants with the memory tasks. For the study phase of the item recognition task, individual words were presented one at a time on a computer screen for 2.5 seconds each, with a 500 ms inter-stimulus interval. Participants were instructed to attend to each word and to try to remember it for a later memory test; no specific encoding strategy was prescribed, reflecting a standard intentional encoding paradigm. After a brief distractor period of 60 seconds involving simple arithmetic problems to attenuate rehearsal, participants completed the item recognition test. At recognition, single words were presented and participants indicated whether each word was “old” (studied) or “new” (not studied) by pressing designated keys. Response times and accuracy were recorded.

For the associative recognition task, participants studied 40 word pairs. Each pair was displayed on the screen for 5 seconds, with a 1000 ms inter-pair interval. Again, instructions emphasized that participants should try to remember the pairs for a subsequent memory test. No specific relational encoding strategy (e.g., instructed imagery) was imposed, to maintain comparability with many prior ADH studies that assess naturalistic associative encoding. After another brief distractor task of 60 seconds, participants completed the associative recognition test. At test, participants were presented with word pairs that were either intact (exactly as studied), recombined (two words that had been studied but not together), or new (at least one novel word). For analytic purposes we focused on discriminating intact versus recombined pairs, and recombined pairs served as lures to probe associative memory. Participants made forced-choice judgments indicating whether each pair was intact or not.

Practice trials preceding each task comprised 6 items or 6 pairs with feedback to ensure that participants understood the task demands. No feedback was provided during the main study and test phases. The order of the item and associative tasks was counterbalanced across participants to control for order effects; half the participants completed the item task first and the other half completed the associative task first. The words used in the first task were distinct from those used in the second task, to avoid interference between tasks.

Scoring and dependent measures

Memory performance was quantified using corrected recognition scores to control for response bias. For the item recognition task, corrected recognition was computed as hit rate minus false alarm rate for single-word recognition. For the associative recognition task, corrected associative recognition was computed as the proportion of intact pairs correctly identified as intact minus the proportion of recombined pairs erroneously identified as intact. Hit and false alarm rates were computed for each participant and corrected scores were used in the main analyses. We also computed signal detection d′ measures for both tasks as a supplementary index; these produced patterns consistent with the corrected recognition scores and are reported where appropriate.

Analysis plan

The principal analysis was a mixed-design ANOVA with Test Type (item vs. associative) as a within-subjects factor and Age Group (young vs. older) and Handedness Group (consistent right vs. mixed/left) as between-subjects factors. The primary planned comparison was the Age × Test Type interaction, which would indicate a disproportionate age-related deficit for associative relative to item recognition. In addition, we examined main effects and interactions involving Handedness Group to test whether handedness was associated with overall memory performance or moderated age effects. Effect sizes (partial eta squared) and 95% confidence intervals around means were reported. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were checked via examination of residuals and Levene’s test; where violations were observed, nonparametric or robust alternatives were considered. Supplementary analyses included independent-samples t-tests comparing age groups on each test separately, correlation analyses of laterality quotient with memory scores, and exploratory regression models controlling for education and verbal fluency. Statistical significance was evaluated using an alpha level of .05, two-tailed. Power considerations prior to data collection indicated that the sample size provided modest power to detect medium-sized effects, but power to detect small effects—particularly for handedness—was limited.

Results

Participant characteristics and handedness distribution

The demographic and handedness characteristics of the sample are summarized first. The young adult group (N = 32) had a mean age of 22.4 years (SD = 3.1), and the older adult group (N = 32) had a mean age of 71.3 years (SD = 4.2). Education levels varied across participants, with young adults reporting a mean of 14.2 years of formal education (SD = 2.1) and older adults reporting a mean of 15.8 years (SD = 2.7). The distribution of handedness categories was balanced by design: within each age cohort there were 16 consistent right-handed participants (LQ > 80) and 16 mixed/left-handed participants (LQ ≤ 80). The mean laterality quotient (LQ) for consistent right-handed participants was +92.5 (SD = 5.8), while for mixed/left-handed participants the mean LQ was +18.7 (SD = 46.0), indicating heterogeneity in the mixed/left group that included left-handed individuals and those with weaker right preference.

Descriptive memory performance

Table-style descriptive information is summarized in text. For the item recognition task, young adults produced a mean corrected recognition score of .63 (SD = .12), indicating a robust ability to discriminate studied from unstudied words. Older adults produced a mean corrected recognition score of .58 (SD = .14) on the item task. For the associative recognition task, young adults produced a mean corrected associative recognition score of .59 (SD = .15), and older adults produced a mean corrected associative recognition score of .50 (SD = .18). These descriptive means show the expected pattern in which older adults' associative recognition scores are, on average, lower than those of young adults and that the age-related gap appears somewhat larger for associative recognition than for item recognition.

Mixed ANOVA on corrected recognition scores

A 2 (Test Type: item, associative) × 2 (Age Group: young, older) × 2 (Handedness Group: consistent right, mixed/left) mixed ANOVA was conducted on corrected recognition scores. The main effect of Test Type was not significant, F(1,60) = 2.02, p = .161, partial η2 = .03, indicating that, across participants, associative recognition scores were not statistically different from item recognition scores when collapsing across groups. The main effect of Age Group showed a trend in the expected direction but did not reach conventional significance, F(1,60) = 3.55, p = .064, partial η2 = .06, reflecting modestly lower overall recognition performance for older adults relative to young adults. The main effect of Handedness Group was not significant, F(1,60) = 0.42, p = .521, partial η2 = .01, indicating no reliable overall advantage for mixed/left-handed participants relative to consistent right-handed participants.

Crucially, the Age × Test Type interaction, which tests the central prediction of the associative deficit hypothesis, showed a non-significant trend in the predicted direction, F(1,60) = 2.95, p = .091, partial η2 = .05. The pattern of means indicated a larger age difference for associative recognition (.09 difference between young and older adults) than for item recognition (.05 difference), but this differential was modest and did not reach statistical significance at the .05 level. The Age × Handedness interaction was non-significant, F(1,60) = 0.17, p = .683, partial η2 = .003, as was the Test Type × Handedness interaction, F(1,60) = 0.03, p = .868, partial η2 < .001. The three-way interaction among Test Type, Age Group, and Handedness Group was also non-significant, F(1,60) = 0.42, p = .520, partial η2 = .007.

Planned follow-up t-tests: associative and item task comparisons

Given the pattern that suggested a somewhat larger age gap for associative recognition, we conducted planned independent-samples t-tests comparing young and older adults separately for each test. For the item recognition task, the age difference in corrected recognition (.63 for young, .58 for older) was not significant, t(62) = 1.68, p = .098, Cohen’s d = 0.42, 95% CI for the mean difference = [-.01, .11]. For the associative recognition task, young adults outperformed older adults (.59 vs. .50), t(62) = 2.08, p = .042, Cohen’s d = 0.52, 95% CI for the mean difference = [.01, .17]. Thus, in the pairwise comparison the associative task difference reached conventional significance at p < .05, while the item task difference did not. However, because the formal Age × Test Type interaction in the mixed ANOVA did not reach significance, the evidence for a differential associative deficit across age groups must be interpreted with caution.

Handedness analyses and correlations

Handedness group comparisons on each task showed no significant differences. On the item recognition task, consistent right-handed participants had a mean corrected recognition score of .61 (SD = .13) and mixed/left-handed participants had a mean of .60 (SD = .13); this difference was not significant, t(62) = 0.24, p = .812, Cohen’s d = 0.06. On the associative recognition task, consistent right-handed participants had a mean of .55 (SD = .17) and mixed/left-handed participants had a mean of .54 (SD = .17); again, this difference was not significant, t(62) = 0.28, p = .782, Cohen’s d = 0.07.

Correlations between the continuous laterality quotient (LQ) and memory scores were calculated to examine linear relations. The LQ was not significantly correlated with item corrected recognition (r = -.08, p = .532) or with associative corrected recognition (r = -.12, p = .342) across the full sample. When correlations were computed within age groups, the correlations remained nonsignificant: in young adults, LQ with associative scores r = -.10, p = .582; in older adults, LQ with associative scores r = -.15, p = .420. Thus, there was no evidence of a linear relationship between the degree of right-handedness and episodic memory measures in either age cohort.

Supplementary signal detection analyses

As a supplementary analysis, we computed d′ values for both tasks and submitted these to the same ANOVA framework. The pattern of results paralleled that observed for corrected recognition scores. The Age × Test Type interaction on d′ showed a nonsignificant trend in the predicted direction, F(1,60) = 2.41, p = .126, partial η2 = .04. The main effect of Age on d′ approached but did not reach significance, F(1,60) = 3.21, p = .078, partial η2 = .05. Handedness effects on d′ again were negligible and nonsignificant.

Exploratory regressions controlling for education

Because older adults in the sample reported slightly higher mean years of education, we conducted exploratory regression analyses to determine whether controlling for education altered the pattern of age and test type effects. When education was entered as a covariate in models predicting associative corrected recognition, age group remained a non-significant predictor in the full model, β = -.19, t = -1.63, p = .108, and education was also not a significant predictor, β = .12, t = 1.03, p = .306. Similar patterns were observed for item recognition. Including education did not reveal any significant association between laterality quotient and memory scores.

Checks of assumptions and potential confounds

Residuals and normality diagnostics on the corrected recognition scores revealed no gross departures from normality, and Levene’s tests did not indicate significant heterogeneity of variance across age or handedness groups for the principal dependent variables. Response time data were examined for speed–accuracy trade-offs; no systematic trade-off was observed. Participants showed expected levels of attention during study and test phases, and there were no significant differences in the number of trials with missing responses across groups.

Summary of findings

In summary, descriptive means conformed to the ADH prediction with older adults showing lower associative recognition than young adults and with the age gap appearing numerically larger for associative than item recognition. However, the formal mixed-model test of the Age × Test Type interaction did not reach statistical significance, and the main effect of age was only marginal. No evidence was found that handedness, whether treated categorically or continuously, exerted a reliable main effect or interacted with age or test type to influence episodic memory performance in this sample.

Discussion

The present study tested the associative deficit hypothesis by comparing young and older adults on item and associative recognition tasks, and it examined whether handedness as assessed by the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory related to episodic memory performance. The principal pattern of descriptive results was in line with the associative deficit hypothesis: older adults performed more poorly than young adults on associative recognition, and the age-related decrement appeared somewhat larger for associative recognition than for item recognition. When analyzed in separate t-tests, the age difference on the associative task reached conventional significance whereas the age difference on the item task did not. Nonetheless, the formal mixed-design ANOVA testing the Age × Test Type interaction, which is the stringent test of a disproportionate associative deficit, did not reach statistical significance. Moreover, handedness exhibited no reliable association with memory performance either as a categorical grouping or as a continuous laterality quotient, and there was no evidence of an interaction between handedness and age or test type.

The mixed pattern of findings warrants careful interpretation. The descriptive and pairwise results are consistent with the general expectation that associative memory may be more vulnerable to age-related decline than item memory, and the observed associative difference between age groups in the pairwise test aligns with prior reports of an age-related associative deficit. However, the lack of a significant Age × Test Type interaction in the omnibus ANOVA cautions against strong conclusions that the associative deficit in this sample is robust and disproportionately large. The discrepancy between the pairwise t-test significance for the associative task and the nonsignificant interaction may reflect limited power to detect interaction effects, sampling variability, or a small true effect size that is detectable in some contrasts but not in the full interaction model. The partial eta squared estimates indicated small-to-moderate effect sizes for age and the interaction (on the order of .04 to .06), which suggests that the study had only modest power to detect these effects given the sample size. Thus, the data are better characterized as suggestive of an associative deficit phenomenon rather than providing unequivocal confirmation.

The lack of significant effects of handedness on episodic memory in the present study contrasts with some prior reports that mixed-handed individuals exhibit superior memory performance relative to strongly right-handed participants. There are several possible explanations for this discrepancy. First, methodological differences in handedness assessment and classification can substantially influence findings. In the present study balanced grouping was achieved by targeted recruitment, but the mixed/left group remained heterogeneous, including both left-handed participants and those with weaker right-hand preference. This heterogeneity may attenuate any relations that are specific to true left-handedness versus mixed-handedness. Second, handedness as measured by self-report inventories is an imperfect proxy for the neural lateralization that might underlie cognitive differences; functional brain lateralization is not perfectly aligned with the behavioral measure of hand preference. Therefore, null findings for handedness on behavioral memory tasks do not rule out the possibility that neural lateralization influences memory; they merely indicate that hand preference alone may be an insufficient marker. Third, the effect sizes for handedness-memory associations are reported in the literature as small, and thus the present study may not have had adequate power to detect subtle effects even with balanced grouping.

Another consideration concerns task sensitivity and encoding instructions. The associative deficit is more likely to emerge under conditions that require spontaneous relational encoding or that do not scaffold associative binding. In the current protocol we used intentional encoding instructions without mandating a specific relational strategy such as imagery-based binding. While this approach mirrors many naturalistic memory situations and some prior ADH studies, it may have introduced variability in encoding strategies across participants. Some older adults may have employed compensatory strategies that preserved associative performance, while others did not, resulting in increased within-group variance and reduced between-group effects. Future studies could manipulate encoding instructions to compare strategy-driven binding (e.g., instruct participants to form mental images linking pair members) versus spontaneous encoding, which would help clarify whether strategy use moderates age-related associative deficits.

In addition to encoding differences, the retention interval and stimulus characteristics can affect detectability of associative deficits. The present study employed relatively short delays with brief distractors between study and test, which could reduce the magnitude of age effects by minimizing opportunities for forgetting. Longer delays and interference manipulations tend to exacerbate age-related memory differences and might therefore amplify associative deficits. Similarly, the use of concrete, high-imageability words may facilitate associative encoding for some participants, potentially attenuating age differences. Using less imageable or more abstract materials might increase task difficulty and sensitivity to age-related binding deficits.

The present results also raise the possibility that the associative deficit is not a universal feature of aging but may vary with individual differences in cognitive reserve, health status, and other moderating factors. Older adults are a heterogeneous group, and some exhibit preserved memory performance into late life. In the current sample older participants reported, on average, slightly higher years of education than young participants, and education is often associated with cognitive reserve that can mitigate the impact of neurobiological aging on cognitive tasks. Our exploratory regression analyses controlling for education did not reveal a substantial mediating effect, but other facets of reserve—such as occupational complexity, engagement in cognitively stimulating activities, or baseline cognitive ability—were not fully assessed and could account for inter-individual variability in associative performance.

Neurobiological explanations for associative deficits often point to age-related changes in hippocampal and medial temporal lobe structures, which play central roles in binding elements of an episodic event. Structural and functional declines in these regions with age could disproportionately affect associative memory. Meanwhile, handedness-related differences in hemispheric specialization could theoretically influence the allocation of resources during encoding and retrieval. However, the present null results for handedness suggest that, at least at the behavioral level and with the present tasks, hand preference is not a robust predictor of episodic memory. This outcome encourages the use of more direct indices of brain lateralization, such as functional neuroimaging or behavioural paradigms that assess hemispheric processing more directly, to test whether lateralization moderates age-related associative deficits.

Several methodological limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size, while adequate for many standard comparisons, provided only modest power to detect interactions and small effects, particularly for handedness. Future studies would benefit from larger samples to provide more precise estimates of effect sizes and to afford sufficient power to detect interactions among age, test type, and handedness. Second, the heterogeneity within the mixed/left-handed group complicates interpretation of categorical handedness effects. A larger-scale sampling approach that actively recruits substantial numbers of strongly left-handed participants would allow separate analyses for left-handed versus mixed-handed participants and might uncover differential relations. Third, handedness was measured solely by self-report inventory; incorporating behavioral lateralization tasks or neuroimaging would provide a more complete assessment of lateralization and its relation to memory function. Fourth, the study employed a cross-sectional design, which conflates age differences with cohort effects and cannot address intra-individual change. Longitudinal designs would be preferable for drawing conclusions about aging processes per se.

Several lines of future research may address these limitations and refine our understanding of associative memory decline and the role of handedness. First, studies with larger, more diverse samples that include a broader range of handedness profiles would help clarify whether the null handedness findings replicate. Second, experiments that manipulate encoding strategies and delay intervals systematically can test whether associative deficits emerge under specific conditions that tax relational binding or retention. Third, combining behavioral assessments with neuroimaging could elucidate whether interindividual differences in hippocampal function or hemispheric engagement mediate age effects on associative memory and whether such neural markers relate to handedness. Fourth, assessing other individual difference variables such as cognitive reserve, processing speed, attentional control, and executive function might identify moderators that determine which older adults are most vulnerable to associative deficits. Fifth, pre-registered meta-analyses of handedness and memory studies could quantify the overall effect size and clarify conditions under which any effects are most likely to appear.

Theoretically, the study contributes to an accumulating body of evidence that supports the basic tenet that associative memory is susceptible to age-related decline but also highlights that empirical demonstrations of a disproportionate associative deficit are sensitive to methodological choices and sample characteristics. The failure to find a significant interaction in the omnibus test suggests that conclusions about the universality and magnitude of the associative deficit require careful qualification. The null handedness findings caution against over-reliance on simple behavioral proxies of lateralization when predicting complex cognitive outcomes like episodic memory. Instead, a more nuanced approach that integrates behavioral, neural, and demographic variables may be required to fully account for individual differences in memory aging.

In conclusion, the present study found descriptive evidence consistent with the associative deficit hypothesis in older adults, with significantly lower associative recognition in pairwise tests but no significant Age × Test Type interaction in the full model. No reliable effects of handedness on item or associative recognition emerged. These results suggest that while associative memory vulnerability in aging is a plausible phenomenon, its manifestation is contingent on task parameters, individual differences, and sample characteristics. Future research should employ larger samples, richer measures of lateralization and neural function, and manipulations of encoding and retention to better delineate when and why associative memory declines with age and whether behavioral markers like handedness can contribute meaningfully to predicting those declines.
