Introduction

Excessive alcohol consumption remains a significant public health concern in many high-income countries, impacting physical health, mental health, academic outcomes, and social functioning among young adults. Student populations have been identified repeatedly as a group in which risky drinking behaviors cluster, with social contexts, cultural norms, and developmental transitions contributing to elevated levels of consumption. Researchers have therefore sought to identify psychological and social predictors of alcohol use in student samples in order to inform prevention and intervention strategies. Psychological variables such as self-esteem have received attention because they are relatively stable traits that are related to a range of health behaviors. Low self-esteem has been linked conceptually and empirically to coping-motivated substance use, and the idea that people with lower self-regard may use alcohol as a means of mood regulation or social facilitation is intuitive. At the same time, other social variables such as perceived popularity, peer acceptance, and social status are related to drinking in student groups because alcohol use is often embedded in social rituals that govern inclusion and identity. Popularity in peer networks may increase exposure to drinking opportunities, social reinforcement for drinking, or normative beliefs that drinking is expected or rewarded.

The literature thus points to multiple, intersecting pathways that could connect self-esteem, social status, and alcohol consumption. One broad model frames alcohol use as a behavior that is jointly influenced by intrapersonal motives and interpersonal pressures. From this perspective, an individual's barrier or vulnerability to misuse may be partly a function of self-regulatory resources and self-appraisal, and partly a function of the social environments to which the individual is exposed. Empirical studies have reported correlations between lower self-esteem and higher alcohol consumption in various samples, particularly when coping motives are assessed as mediators. Other studies, however, have found that higher social status or popularity is associated with greater alcohol use, suggesting an alternate pathway in which social capital confers opportunities for and incentives to drink. These dual patterns in the literature raise a question about how self-esteem and popularity might interrelate in predicting alcohol consumption. If both variables matter, then examining them in isolation could produce misleading inferences.

A more nuanced possibility is that popularity may mediate or moderate any association between self-esteem and alcohol consumption. Mediation would imply that self-esteem affects alcohol use via its impact on social status or vice versa, whereas moderation would imply that the effect of self-esteem on alcohol varies as a function of popularity. Another possibility is statistical suppression, a phenomenon whereby the zero-order correlation between a predictor and an outcome is small or null but a unique association emerges when a correlated third variable is controlled. Suppression can occur when two predictors share variance that is unrelated to the criterion but their unique variances have opposite associations with the outcome. In applied research with intercorrelated psychosocial measures, suppression can lead to patterns that appear contradictory if only bivariate associations are considered.

Given these theoretical complexities, it is important to examine self-esteem, perceived popularity, and alcohol consumption together in the same study to clarify their joint relations. Doing so can help differentiate whether self-esteem has an independent effect on drinking, whether popularity accounts for the link between self-esteem and alcohol, or whether the apparent relationships are the product of statistical artifacts. Empirically disentangling these possibilities requires assessments that include reliable measures of self-esteem and perceived popularity, valid estimates of alcohol consumption, and analytical strategies capable of detecting mediation, moderation, and suppression effects.

Prior research that has modeled these variables simultaneously is limited, and findings have been mixed. Some studies that control for social variables report attenuated or non-significant associations between self-esteem and alcohol use, implying that social status may account for much of the association. Other work reports residual effects of self-esteem even after accounting for social factors, suggesting independent pathways. The inconsistency in prior findings could reflect real heterogeneity across samples, differences in measurement approaches, and differences in analytic attention to suppression or non-linear relations. Moreover, many studies focus on adolescents or early adolescents, whereas emerging adults in university settings may exhibit distinct patterns because of different social structures and drinking norms.

The practical significance of clarifying these relations is substantial. If self-esteem is a direct risk factor for excessive drinking, interventions that bolster self-worth, teach adaptive coping, and reduce harmful self-critical cognitions could be prioritized. If popularity is the primary driver, then interventions that address social norms, peer influence, and alternative pathways to social inclusion may be more effective. If suppression or complex multivariate patterns are common, then researchers and practitioners must interpret bivariate correlations cautiously and adopt multivariable approaches when designing prevention strategies.

The current study sought to examine the interrelations among self-esteem, perceived popularity, and weekly alcohol consumption in a sample of young adults recruited online. The study employed self-report measures for all three constructs and tested bivariate correlations, partial correlations, multiple regression models, and mediation models to probe the structure of associations. The primary goals were to (a) estimate the zero-order association between self-esteem and weekly alcohol consumption, (b) estimate the zero-order association between perceived popularity and weekly alcohol consumption, (c) test whether popularity mediates or moderates any association between self-esteem and alcohol, and (d) determine whether controlling for popularity reveals a unique association between self-esteem and alcohol that is not apparent in bivariate analyses, potentially indicating suppression.

Because prior literature has reported both directions of association and because the possibility of suppression arises in intercorrelated psychosocial constructs, the study took an exploratory stance, testing multiple plausible models rather than committing to a single a priori directional hypothesis. The recruitment approach and measures were designed to be consistent with common survey research practices in the field, with attention to reaching a broad sample of young adults who vary in student status, living arrangements, and drinking behavior. The study avoided clinical screening but collected basic demographic data to assess potential covariates such as age and gender.

This study was motivated in part by practical concerns about alcohol use in student communities. Excessive drinking in university settings is associated with immediate harms such as accidents, assault, academic failure, and long-term consequences including dependence and health problems. Identifying psychosocial correlates of drinking can inform campus health initiatives, targeted educational efforts, and peer-based interventions. Clarifying whether self-esteem per se is a marker for risky drinking, or whether its observed relationships are better explained by social status dynamics, can help refine the content of prevention messaging and intervention targets.

Methodological rigor in examining these relationships includes careful attention to the possibility of multicollinearity, suppression, and measurement error. The present study therefore included diagnostics for multicollinearity, examined distributions and potential non-linearities, and performed sensitivity analyses. Because the study is cross-sectional, causal inferences are limited; nevertheless, cross-sectional analyses can provide useful descriptive and associative evidence to guide future longitudinal and experimental work. Recognizing the limits of cross-sectional designs, the analyses emphasize careful interpretation of observed associations and generate hypotheses for follow-up research.

Another key consideration is the validity of self-report measures for alcohol consumption. Self-reports are widely used and can be accurate when anonymity and straightforward timeframe questions are provided, but they remain susceptible to under-reporting and recall biases. The present study used a weekly consumption metric intended to capture typical drinking patterns over the recent month, with clear instructions to minimize misunderstanding. Measures of perceived popularity were administered using established item formats that ask respondents to rate their sense of social standing relative to peers, which may capture subjective social experience rather than objective network centrality. Self-esteem was assessed with a brief global self-esteem scale capturing general self-worth rather than domain-specific esteem.

In sum, the study explored whether the commonly reported link between self-esteem and alcohol consumption holds when perceived popularity is taken into account, and whether adjusting for popularity reveals a suppressed or otherwise hidden relationship. The design, sample, and analytic plan were intended to provide a careful descriptive account of the joint relations among these constructs in a community sample of young adults and to suggest directions for improved future research if the findings suggested methodological artifacts or unanticipated complexities.

Methods

Participants and recruitment

Participants were recruited via an online recruitment platform and through social media postings that targeted young adults aged 18 to 30 years. Recruitment text described a short anonymous survey about social experiences and health behaviors and offered no monetary compensation, although a modest raffle incentive was made available. Respondents who clicked the study link were presented with an online informed consent form that explained the voluntary and anonymous nature of participation, the approximate time burden, and the inclusion criteria of age between 18 and 30 and ability to read English. The consent form also indicated that participants could skip any question and could withdraw at any time.

A total of 412 individuals initiated the survey. After screening for completion and attention checks, a final analytic sample of 378 participants was retained. Participants who failed an embedded attention item or who produced implausible response patterns (e.g., reporting extremely high weekly drinking that was inconsistent with follow-up items) were excluded prior to analysis. The demographic composition of the final sample included a range of living situations, student status, and self-reported gender. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 30 with a mean in the early twenties. The sample included individuals who self-identified as current students and non-students to capture some variability in drinking contexts.

Measures

Self-esteem

Global self-esteem was assessed using a brief five-item self-report inventory adapted from widely used global self-esteem measures. Items asked participants to indicate agreement with statements reflecting general self-worth, such as feeling competent, having self-respect, and overall satisfaction with oneself. Responses were given on a five-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Items were scored so that higher values indicated higher self-esteem. The five items were summed to create a composite score. Internal consistency for the composite scale in the sample was acceptable.

Perceived popularity

Perceived popularity was assessed using a short scale that asked participants to rate their standing and popularity within their peer group. Items included self-evaluations of how well-liked the participant perceived themselves to be, how often they were invited to social events, and how prominent they believed they were in their social circles. Responses were collected on a five-point scale and summed to create a composite perceived popularity score, with higher scores indicating greater subjective popularity. The composite demonstrated internal consistency consistent with brief social standing measures used in prior survey research.

Alcohol consumption

Weekly alcohol consumption was estimated through self-report using a drinks-per-week approach. Participants were asked to report the typical number of standard drinks they consumed on each day of a typical seven-day week over the past month. The survey provided a brief descriptor of what constitutes a standard drink to improve accuracy. Responses for each day were summed to generate a weekly drinks total, which served as the primary alcohol outcome variable. Because alcohol consumption distributions are often skewed, descriptive checks and transformations were planned and implemented as needed.

Covariates and demographic information

Participants also provided demographic information, including age, gender identity, student status (current student or not), and living arrangements (e.g., living with parents, in student accommodation, or alone). These covariates were considered for inclusion in sensitivity analyses because they often relate to drinking patterns and social experiences.

Procedure

The survey was administered online using a secure survey platform. After providing informed consent, participants completed demographic questions, followed by the self-esteem and perceived popularity scales. The placement of psychosocial measures prior to the alcohol questions was intended to reduce any priming effects that might arise from first considering one’s drinking. The alcohol questions were then presented with clear instructions and examples. Two brief attention check items were embedded to identify disengaged respondents. The entire survey took approximately 10 to 15 minutes to complete.

Ethics

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the investigators’ university departmental ethics committee. All participants were 18 years or older and provided informed consent. Anonymity was preserved by not collecting personally identifying information, and participants could skip any items or withdraw at any time without penalty. Resources for alcohol support services and student counseling were provided at the end of the survey.

Data preparation and analytic plan

Prior to analysis, data were screened for missingness, implausible values, and distributional properties. Participants with excessive missing data were excluded. For scale scores with minimal missingness, mean imputation for the scale was used when only one item was missing, consistent with common practice for brief scales. The distribution of weekly drinks was inspected; because alcohol consumption commonly exhibits positive skew, both the raw drinks-per-week variable and a log-transformed version were considered in analyses. Descriptive statistics and zero-order Pearson correlations were computed among self-esteem, perceived popularity, and weekly drinks.

Primary analyses included multiple linear regression models predicting weekly drinks from self-esteem and perceived popularity entered simultaneously to estimate unique associations. Variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance values were computed to assess multicollinearity. Partial correlations were calculated to examine the association between self-esteem and drinks controlling for perceived popularity, and vice versa. Mediation models were estimated to test whether perceived popularity statistically mediated the relation between self-esteem and alcohol consumption or whether self-esteem mediated the relationship between popularity and alcohol, using bootstrapped confidence intervals for indirect effects. Moderation analyses tested whether the association of self-esteem with alcohol varied as a function of perceived popularity by including an interaction term; continuous variables were mean-centered to reduce multicollinearity and to aid interpretation.

Because the analytic plan recognized the possibility of suppression effects, additional exploratory steps included calculating semipartial correlations to gauge the unique variance each predictor contributed to alcohol consumption after removing variance shared with the other predictor. Sensitivity analyses assessed whether results were robust to the use of the log-transformed drinks variable, to the inclusion of demographic covariates (age, gender, student status), and to the exclusion of outliers defined by standardized residuals. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and statistical significance was evaluated at the conventional alpha level of .05, while also considering effect size estimates and confidence intervals for interpretation.

Results

Sample characteristics and descriptive statistics

The final analytic sample included 378 participants after exclusions for incomplete responses or failed attention checks. The sample had a mean age of 22.1 years (SD = 2.6), with a range from 18 to 30 years. Approximately 62% of respondents identified as female, 36% as male, and 2% as non-binary or another gender identity. Fifty-five percent reported being current students, and the remainder were non-students or had completed their studies. Living arrangements varied, with about 35% living in student accommodation, 30% living with parents or family, and 35% living independently or with peers.

Descriptive statistics for the main study variables showed that the self-esteem composite had a mean in the upper half of the possible score range and acceptable variance. The perceived popularity score also exhibited moderate variance and a distribution that approximated normality. The weekly drinks variable had a mean indicative of light-to-moderate average consumption and exhibited positive skew, which is typical for alcohol consumption measures. A log transformation reduced skewness and improved approximation to normality for analyses sensitive to distributional assumptions. Internal consistency estimates for the self-esteem and perceived popularity composites were acceptable, with Cronbach’s alpha values in ranges considered adequate for brief scales.

Zero-order correlations

Zero-order Pearson correlations among the primary variables were computed as an initial step. The bivariate correlation between self-esteem and weekly drinks was small and not statistically significant, indicating no clear simple association between global self-esteem and average weekly alcohol consumption in this sample. In contrast, the bivariate correlation between perceived popularity and weekly drinks was positive and statistically significant, suggesting that individuals who reported higher subjective popularity also reported higher weekly alcohol consumption. Self-esteem and perceived popularity were positively correlated with one another, indicating that those who felt higher self-esteem also tended to perceive themselves as more popular. The pattern of these zero-order correlations set the stage for multivariable analyses that could reveal suppression or unique effects.

Multiple regression: self-esteem and perceived popularity predicting weekly drinks

A multiple linear regression model was estimated with weekly drinks as the dependent variable and self-esteem and perceived popularity entered simultaneously as predictors. In the full model, perceived popularity showed a significant positive unique association with weekly drinks, consistent with its positive zero-order correlation. Interestingly, self-esteem showed a small but statistically significant positive unique association with weekly drinks in the multivariable model, despite its non-significant zero-order correlation with drinks. This pattern is indicative of a suppression effect: when the shared variance between self-esteem and perceived popularity is partialed out, the unique variance in self-esteem is associated positively with alcohol consumption.

To probe the robustness of this finding, variance inflation factors were inspected. Both predictors had VIF values well within commonly accepted thresholds, suggesting that multicollinearity was not severe enough to compromise coefficient estimates. The semipartial correlations indicated that perceived popularity explained a greater proportion of unique variance in weekly drinks than did self-esteem, but the semipartial for self-esteem was non-trivial once perceived popularity was controlled. The overall model accounted for a modest proportion of variance in weekly drinks, which is consistent with prior studies where psychosocial predictors typically explain small-to-moderate amounts of variance in drinking behaviors.

Partial correlations and suppression testing

Partial correlations were computed to further clarify the relations. The partial correlation between self-esteem and weekly drinks controlling for perceived popularity was positive and statistically significant, while the zero-order correlation had been near zero. This corroborates the regression finding of a suppression effect. Conversely, the partial correlation between perceived popularity and weekly drinks controlling for self-esteem remained positive and slightly attenuated compared to its zero-order correlation. These patterns suggest that the observed null bivariate association of self-esteem with alcohol masks a positive unique association that becomes apparent after accounting for perceived popularity.

Mediation and moderation analyses

Given the correlated nature of self-esteem and perceived popularity, mediation models were tested to evaluate whether popularity might mediate any effect of self-esteem on weekly drinks, or vice versa. A model with perceived popularity as a mediator of self-esteem’s effect on weekly drinks produced an indirect effect that was positive and statistically significant, indicating that higher self-esteem was associated with higher perceived popularity, which in turn was associated with higher weekly drinks. However, the direct effect of self-esteem on weekly drinks after accounting for perceived popularity was also positive and statistically significant, consistent with partial mediation combined with suppression dynamics. The reverse mediation model in which self-esteem mediated the effect of perceived popularity on drinks indicated a substantial direct effect of perceived popularity on drinks and a smaller indirect effect through self-esteem. These mediation tests therefore suggested that the relationships are complex and not well-captured by a single simple mediation pathway.

A moderation analysis tested whether the association between self-esteem and weekly drinks varied as a function of perceived popularity by including the interaction term between mean-centered self-esteem and mean-centered perceived popularity. The interaction term was not statistically significant, indicating no evidence that perceived popularity moderated the self-esteem–drinks relationship. Thus, the data did not support a model in which self-esteem is only related to alcohol consumption at particular levels of perceived popularity.

Sensitivity analyses

Because alcohol consumption was positively skewed, analyses were repeated using a log-transformed weekly drinks variable to assess whether findings were robust to distributional assumptions. The pattern of results was consistent with the primary analyses: perceived popularity had a positive association with log-transformed weekly drinks, and self-esteem showed a positive unique association once popularity was controlled. Including demographic covariates such as age, gender, and student status in the regression model did not materially change the pattern of results, although age and student status were modestly related to drinking in expected directions in some models. When outliers with high standardized residuals were excluded, the suppression pattern persisted, suggesting that the effect was not driven by a few extreme cases.

Exploratory diagnostics and consideration of alternative explanations

Given the counterintuitive pattern of a non-significant zero-order correlation and a positive partial correlation for self-esteem, additional diagnostics were run. Examination of scatterplots and residuals did not reveal clear non-linear relations between self-esteem and drinking that would account for the unusual pattern. The correlation matrix showed that self-esteem and perceived popularity shared a moderate positive correlation, and both correlated in the same direction with weekly drinks when unique variance was considered. The overall pattern is consistent with classical statistical suppression, where the shared variance between predictors is unrelated to the criterion, and controlling for the correlated predictor reveals a unique effect.

Because suppression effects can sometimes indicate measurement issues, the internal consistency of the scales was revisited. Both self-esteem and perceived popularity composites had acceptable alphas, and item-level analyses did not point to any single problematic item driving the effects. Nonetheless, the possibility remains that perceived popularity items captured aspects of social behavior that overlap with drinking contexts, thereby accounting for some shared variance that masks the association of self-esteem with drinking at the zero-order level.

Summary of findings

In summary, the zero-order analyses showed no relation between self-esteem and weekly alcohol consumption but did show a positive relation between perceived popularity and weekly drinking. Multivariable analyses revealed that when perceived popularity was accounted for, self-esteem exhibited a positive unique association with weekly drinks, consistent with a suppression effect. Mediation and moderation tests did not provide evidence for simple mediation or moderation models that fully accounted for the pattern of results. Sensitivity analyses retained the core pattern across transformations, covariate inclusion, and outlier removal. These results point to a complex multivariate relationship among self-esteem, perceived popularity, and alcohol consumption in this sample.

Discussion

This study examined the relations among global self-esteem, perceived popularity, and weekly alcohol consumption in a community sample of young adults. The findings present a nuanced picture that has implications for how researchers interpret bivariate associations and how practitioners think about intervention targets. At the bivariate level, self-esteem bore no significant relation to weekly drinking, whereas perceived popularity correlated positively with alcohol consumption. When both predictors were entered into multivariable models, however, self-esteem displayed a positive unique association with drinking, a result that is indicative of statistical suppression. Perceived popularity remained a robust predictor of drinking, and mediation and moderation models did not fully explain away the suppression pattern. The core empirical contribution is therefore the identification of a suppression-like effect in which self-esteem’s zero-order null association with alcohol masks a positive unique association when overlapping variance with popularity is controlled.

Interpreting this pattern requires careful consideration of conceptual and statistical factors. One plausible interpretation is that the aspects of self-esteem that overlap with perceived popularity are not directly related to drinking, whereas the unique aspects of self-esteem that are orthogonal to popularity are positively related to drinking. That is, global self-esteem encompasses elements related to social acceptance and elements related to internal evaluative processes. For individuals whose self-esteem asserts itself primarily through social standing, drinking may be more a function of social context than of self-appraisal per se. Conversely, the variance in self-esteem that is not accounted for by perceived popularity may reflect trait characteristics such as assertiveness, social confidence, or sensation-seeking tendencies that could contribute to higher alcohol consumption independent of social status.

Another way to conceptualize the result is that perceived popularity may act as a suppressor variable by removing variance in self-esteem that is unrelated to drinking, thereby unveiling a positive relation between the remaining component of self-esteem and drinking. Statistically, suppression occurs when the predictor that is controlled shares variance with another predictor that is irrelevant or negatively related to the criterion. In this study, the shared variance between self-esteem and popularity appears to be orthogonal to drinking, while the unique variance in both predictors has a positive relation with drinking. This creates the paradoxical situation of a null zero-order correlation for self-esteem but a positive partial effect when popularity is included in the model.

It is important to emphasize that suppression effects often signal the need for theoretical refinement rather than being dismissed as mere statistical curiosities. The fact that perceived popularity has a positive zero-order association with drinking aligns with conceptual models that position alcohol use as a socially mediated behavior, particularly in student and young adult contexts where drinking can serve social bonding functions and status signaling. In contrast, the positive unique effect of self-esteem after controlling for popularity suggests that certain facets of self-regard may relate to drinking through mechanisms that are not purely social. For example, higher self-esteem may be related to greater engagement in exploratory social activities, risk-taking, or socialization patterns that lead to higher alcohol exposure even when popularity is held constant.

These interpretations remain speculative given the cross-sectional design. The data do not provide evidence for causal directionality among self-esteem, perceived popularity, and drinking. It is possible that alcohol consumption influences perceptions of popularity or self-esteem, or that unmeasured third variables such as sensation seeking, extraversion, or social anxiety drive associations among these constructs. Longitudinal and experimental designs will be necessary to establish temporal precedence and causal mechanisms.

Measurement considerations also bear on interpretation. The reliance on self-report for both predictors and the outcome introduces the possibility of shared-method variance, which can inflate associations among constructs but is less likely to explain why a suppression effect would occur. Nonetheless, self-reported perceived popularity captures subjective appraisals rather than objective network centrality; subjective popularity may be influenced by personality traits or momentary mood that also affect drinking behavior. Likewise, the self-esteem measure was a brief global scale that may not capture domain-specific self-evaluations that could be more closely linked to alcohol use, such as social self-efficacy or body image. Future studies should include multi-method measurements, such as peer-reported popularity, behavioral indicators of social status, and ecological momentary assessment of drinking episodes, to triangulate findings.

Another important implication of the findings is methodological: researchers should be cautious when interpreting zero-order correlations in the presence of intercorrelated predictors. The presence of suppression means that bivariate analyses can be misleading, and that multivariable approaches are essential when predictors covary. This is particularly relevant in psychosocial research where constructs are often conceptually overlapping. Failure to account for suppression can lead to erroneous conclusions about whether a psychological characteristic is unrelated to an outcome when in fact it has a unique relation that is masked by shared variance with other constructs.

From an applied perspective, the results suggest that interventions targeting excessive drinking in student populations need to consider both social-contextual factors and individual-level self-appraisals. Perceived popularity emerged as a clear correlate of higher drinking, indicating that social norms and status-based incentives likely play a major role in shaping drinking behavior. Campus-based prevention efforts that focus on modifying social norms, offering alternative social activities that do not center on alcohol, and addressing the social drivers of drinking may therefore be effective. At the same time, because aspects of self-esteem that are independent of popularity also related to drinking positively when isolated, it may be useful for interventions to assess the specific facets of self-esteem and related personality characteristics that might predispose individuals to higher consumption. For example, programs that address sensation-seeking tendencies or teach harm-reduction strategies tailored to socially active individuals might complement normative interventions.

The non-significant moderation effect in this study reduces the likelihood that self-esteem’s impact on drinking varies substantially at different levels of perceived popularity. Instead, the data indicate additive and complex relations in which both constructs contribute unique information. Nevertheless, moderation could still emerge in other samples or with more refined measures, and future research should continue to explore interaction effects, especially in samples with greater variability in social contexts or in longitudinal designs where dynamic interplay over time can be modeled.

Limitations of the current study warrant careful note. The cross-sectional design precludes causal claims and raises the possibility of reverse causation or bidirectional effects. The sample, although adequate in size for the planned analyses, was drawn via online convenience sampling and may not be representative of the broader student population. Self-report measures, particularly for alcohol consumption, are subject to recall bias and social desirability effects; while the survey attempted to mitigate these concerns with brief standard drink definitions and assurances of anonymity, objective measures would strengthen future research. The measures of self-esteem and perceived popularity were brief composites; while they demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, more comprehensive assessments could yield richer insights into which subcomponents of self-regard or social status are most relevant to drinking.

Several directions for future research follow naturally from the present findings. First, longitudinal designs could test whether changes in perceived popularity precede increases in drinking or whether escalation in drinking leads to changes in perceived popularity and self-esteem. Second, incorporating objective measures of social network position, such as sociometric methods or digital trace data, would enable the disentangling of subjective perceived popularity from objectively measured network centrality and could clarify whether popularity effects are driven by real social influence or perceived social reward. Third, experimental manipulations that alter perceived social standing or provide normative feedback could examine causal effects on drinking intentions and behavior. Fourth, studies that include personality measures such as extraversion, impulsivity, and sensation seeking could test whether these traits account for the unique variance in self-esteem related to drinking and whether they moderate suppression effects.

In addition, qualitative research could explore how individuals interpret their own popularity and self-esteem in relation to drinking, providing contextualized understanding of the motives and social meanings that underpin the statistical associations. Such mixed-method approaches could be particularly valuable in unpacking why the shared variance between self-esteem and popularity appears unrelated to drinking, while their unique variances are positively associated with consumption.

Another fruitful line of inquiry is to examine subgroup differences. The social dynamics of drinking may differ across cultural, gender, and situational subgroups. For instance, the relation between popularity and drinking might be particularly pronounced in contexts where alcohol is central to social rituals, whereas in other subcultures popularity might be signaled by behaviors unrelated to drinking. Investigating whether suppression effects replicate across diverse subpopulations would help to assess the generalizability of the current findings.

Finally, intervention research could use the current findings to inform multi-component approaches that address both social and intrapersonal drivers. Randomized trials that compare norm-based interventions to those that target self-esteem facets or personality-linked risk profiles could elucidate which strategies are most effective for different risk profiles. Such implementation-oriented work would bridge the gap between analytic insight and practical application.

In conclusion, the present study highlights the importance of considering intercorrelated psychosocial predictors jointly when investigating correlates of alcohol consumption. The identification of a suppression effect involving self-esteem and perceived popularity underscores that bivariate null findings can conceal substantive multivariable relations. Perceived popularity emerged as a relatively robust correlate of weekly drinking, consistent with the view that social status and norms are central to understanding alcohol use among young adults. At the same time, aspects of self-esteem that are distinct from popularity also related to drinking when isolated, indicating potential intrapersonal pathways to consumption that merit further investigation. Clarifying these complex relations through longitudinal, multi-method, and intervention research will be critical for developing targeted and effective strategies to reduce excessive drinking in student and young adult populations.
