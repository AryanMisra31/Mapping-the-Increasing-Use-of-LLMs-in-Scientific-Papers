Introduction

Working memory is a core cognitive system that enables temporary storage and manipulation of information required for complex cognitive tasks. Many models of working memory posit multiple components, including a limited-capacity storage component and attentional control mechanisms that coordinate maintenance and processing. Dual-task paradigms have been instrumental in probing the interplay between storage and processing, because they tax both maintenance of to-be-remembered items and concurrent processing demands. In particular, reading span and related dual-task variants require participants to maintain series of items in memory while performing an interleaved reading or processing activity, and performance on these paradigms has been taken as a sensitive measure of working memory capacity and attentional control. Research on dual-task performance has also examined how stimulus format and temporal parameters influence the degree of interference between maintenance and processing. For example, when the duration of concurrent processing is increased, the theoretical expectation is that maintenance will be more vulnerable to decay or interference, resulting in worse recall. Barrouillet and colleagues reported that increasing the duration of the processing component could have a disturbing effect on dual-task performance in reading digit-span tasks, consistent with resource-sharing accounts that conceptualize time and attention as limited resources distributed between tasks. That work suggested that the temporal properties of the intervening processing episodes matter: longer or more sustained processing episodes can deprive maintenance mechanisms of the attention necessary to refresh or rehearse memory traces, producing greater forgetting. The dual requirements to maintain information while engaging in an interleaved processing stream therefore create an empirical context in which both the nature of the processing task and participant characteristics that shape attentional control can be expected to influence performance. One candidate individual difference that has attracted considerable interest in recent cognitive research is bilingualism. Bilingualism is often associated with enhanced executive control, on the basis that managing two languages requires regulation of interference, selection among competing lexical items, and control of attention to the relevant language. A literature has therefore argued for a bilingual advantage in certain facets of cognition that involve conflict monitoring, inhibition, task-switching, and perhaps working memory. The evidence for a bilingual advantage, however, has been mixed, with some studies reporting reliable advantages in tasks that demand executive control and others reporting null results or effects that are small and context-dependent. The heterogeneity of findings has led to calls for more nuanced characterizations of bilingualism, including consideration of factors such as balanced versus unbalanced bilingualism, age of acquisition of the second language, current frequency of use, proficiency, and sociolinguistic context. Given this variability, it is important to test whether bilingualism confers benefits in paradigms that tax both memory maintenance and concurrent processing, such as reading digit-span tasks. The present study was designed to investigate whether bilingualism would improve performance on a reading digit-span task that requires concurrent maintenance of letter strings while verbally reading digits. This design draws directly on the paradigm used by Barrouillet and colleagues, which highlighted the role of temporal parameters of the processing episodes in producing disturbance to maintenance. In the current experiment, participants were required to remember sequences of consonant letters while performing an interleaved reading task in which digit stimuli were presented in different formats. The rationale for manipulating digit format is that different formats may engage distinct processing channels and thus differentially interfere with the phonological or articulatory processes thought to support maintenance. For instance, digit words presented in English should engage phonological encoding and articulation, while Arabic numerals may be processed more visually and symbolic number processing systems may be recruited, and dot patterns are more likely to engage visuospatial enumeration or subitizing processes. The competition between the maintenance of visually presented consonants and the processing of digit stimuli may therefore depend on format, and bilinguals might be advantaged in conditions that impose greater demand on attentional control or require flexible coordination of verbal and nonverbal processing resources. Prior studies of bilingualism and working memory have used a variety of paradigms, including simple span, complex span, and n-back tasks, and results have not been uniform. Some work has reported bilingual benefits particularly in complex span tasks where concurrent processing must be interleaved with maintenance, and where efficient switching and interference resolution may facilitate better allocation of attentional resources. Other studies have found no bilingual advantage when tasks are relatively simple or when language demands are tightly matched across groups. One implication of this literature is that bilingual effects may be most apparent when the task requires the specific types of control bilinguals exercise in language use, such as resolving conflict between competing representations or rapidly shifting attention between tasks or candidate responses. The reading digit-span task employed here includes both verbal and nonverbal processing elements and varying demands depending on digit format. This complexity makes it a useful test case for examining whether bilingualism affords an advantage in concurrent maintenance contexts. The study also speaks to theoretical questions about whether bilingual advantages, if present, are general-purpose enhancements of cognitive control or are task- and context-specific. If bilinguals outperform monolinguals on the reading digit-span task, this would suggest that bilingual experience transfers to dual-task coordination and maintenance processes more broadly. If no advantage is observed, this would place constraints on the scope of bilingual benefits and suggest boundary conditions under which bilingualism does not confer measurable gains. In addition to examining the main effect of bilingualism, the present study tests whether digit format interacts with language group to modulate recall performance. If bilinguals are particularly adept at coordinating verbal maintenance with verbal processing, they may show a relative advantage in the digit-word condition. Alternatively, bilinguals might show greater benefits when the processing task requires suppression of automatic responses or integration of distinct modalities, in which case formats that demand cross-modal coordination could reveal group differences. The task also manipulates memory load by including letter strings of three to six consonants, allowing assessment of whether any bilingual effect varies with the amount of information to be maintained. The general expectation is that recall will decrease as load increases, but the manner in which bilingualism interacts with this load function is an empirical question. It might be that bilingual advantages, if present, are most noticeable under high-load conditions where control demands are greater. A complementary consideration concerns the operationalization of bilingualism. Bilingual experience is multifaceted and cannot be captured by a simple binary label in all cases. Nonetheless, many laboratory studies utilize self-reported language history and daily use measures to classify participants. In the present study, participants were categorized as monolingual or bilingual based on self-report of language use and proficiency, and the groups were equated on demographic variables as far as possible. This operationalization allows a pragmatic test of whether common forms of bilingual experience among university undergraduates are associated with improved performance in a demanding dual-task paradigm. The present study therefore sought to extend the work of Barrouillet and colleagues by examining whether bilingualism moderates performance on a reading digit-span task that varies digit format and memory load. The primary hypotheses were that (1) recall performance would decrease with increasing memory load across all participants, (2) digit format would influence recall, with verbal digit words producing more interference with letter maintenance than nonverbal dot patterns, and (3) bilingual participants would perform better than monolingual participants, particularly under higher load and in conditions where the processing task strongly engaged verbal or interference-prone processes. An alternative hypothesis was that bilingualism would not yield a measurable advantage in this task, consistent with a growing set of null findings in the literature and with the possibility that the specific control processes exercised in bilingual language use do not transfer to every form of dual-task coordination. The study also explored whether the level of bilingual balance or proficiency correlated with task performance, though the primary analyses focused on the between-group comparison. By systematically manipulating digit format and memory load within participants and comparing monolinguals and bilinguals, the present study aimed to provide a focused test of whether bilingual experience affords better concurrent maintenance under conditions modeled on established dual-task paradigms. The specific design choices, including the selection of consonant letter strings and the three digit formats of English words, Arabic numerals, and dot patterns, were guided by theoretical considerations about phonological and visuospatial processing channels and their potential to interact with maintenance processes. In sum, the current work bridges literatures on working memory and bilingualism, uses a replication-oriented approach informed by prior findings on duration and disturbance in dual tasks, and tests key hypotheses about transfer of bilingual control to concurrent maintenance of memory representations.

Methods

Participants were forty-eight undergraduate students recruited from the University of Warwick participant pool. The sample included twenty-four participants who self-identified as monolingual English speakers and twenty-four participants who self-identified as bilingual, with regular use of both English and another language. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 24 years, with a mean age of 19.7 years and a standard deviation of 1.6 years. The sample included both female and male participants; across the full sample there were 30 females and 18 males, and the gender distribution did not differ substantially between language groups. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological or hearing impairments. Participants received course credit or nominal payment for participation. The study was approved by the local ethics committee, and participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Language background was assessed with a short questionnaire administered prior to the experimental session. The questionnaire asked participants to indicate languages spoken, age of acquisition for each language, current frequency of use across contexts (home, study, social), and self-rated proficiency in speaking, understanding, reading, and writing on a five-point Likert scale. Participants were classified as bilingual if they reported current use of two languages on a daily basis and self-rated proficiency of at least three out of five in both languages for speaking and understanding. Participants who reported English as their only regularly used language were classified as monolingual. The bilingual group included a range of language pairs and degrees of balance, with some participants describing near-balanced use and others indicating greater dominance in English. Stimuli for the memory component consisted of consonant letters of the English alphabet excluding letters that are visually or phonologically confusable (for example, excluding letters such as B and V that are often misheard). The pool of letters used included common consonants such as C, D, F, G, H, J, K, L, M, N, P, Q, R, S, T, V, W, X, Y, and Z. Letter sequences were randomly generated for each trial with the constraint that letters within a sequence were non-repeating. Memory load was manipulated by presenting letter strings of lengths three, four, five, and six items, and trials of each length were included multiple times across the experimental session. The processing stimuli consisted of digits one through nine presented in three formats: English digit words (for example, “one,” “two,” “three”), Arabic numerals (for example, “1,” “2,” “3”), and dot patterns that visually represented the same digits as arrays of dots (for example, one dot, two dots, three dots). The dot patterns were constructed so they could be quickly enumerated without requiring counting beyond subitizing range for small numbers, but larger digits up to nine were represented as clustered dot arrays requiring visual enumeration. Each digit stimulus corresponded to a single numeral value, and digit stimuli were randomized across trials subject to the constraint that no immediate repetitions of the same digit occurred within a trial. All stimuli were presented on a 17-inch computer monitor at approximately 60 centimeters viewing distance. Presentation software controlled stimulus timing and recorded onset times for each stimulus. Participants sat in a quiet testing room and were instructed that on each trial they would see a series of consonant letters to remember, interleaved with digit stimuli to be read aloud. The interleaved reading task required participants to read the presented digit stimuli aloud as quickly and accurately as possible when they appeared. Participants were instructed that accurate recall of the letters was important, and that they should attempt to remember letters in the presented order for later recall. On each trial, a fixation cross appeared for 500 ms, followed by the first consonant letter presented centrally for 1000 ms. After the letter offset, a digit stimulus appeared and remained onscreen for 1000 ms, during which participants were required to read the digit aloud. The next letter then appeared after a 250 ms blank interval. This letter-digit alternation continued until the last letter of the sequence had been followed by a final digit stimulus. After a 1000 ms retention interval following the last digit, the screen displayed a response prompt instructing participants to type the letters they had just seen in the order presented. Participants typed their responses using the keyboard and pressed Enter to submit; accuracy and serial position were recorded. The timing parameters were chosen to approximate the processing durations used in prior work and to ensure participants had sufficient time to read digits aloud without unduly disrupting the memory component. The experiment was divided into three blocks corresponding to the three digit formats, and block order was counterbalanced across participants. Within each block, trials of each memory load (three to six letters) were presented in randomized order, with six trials per load condition per block, resulting in 24 trials per block and a total of 72 experimental trials per participant. Prior to the experimental blocks, participants completed a practice block of six trials spanning the three formats and a range of loads to familiarize them with the task. During the practice phase, experimenters provided feedback on reading accuracy and instruction compliance, but no feedback was given during experimental trials. Spoken responses during the reading task were recorded with a high-quality microphone to allow later scoring of reading accuracy and to compute verbal response latencies when necessary. Scoring of recall accuracy focused on item correctness in the correct serial position (serial recall). Each letter in the response string was compared to the target letters in the corresponding position, and a proportion correct score was computed for each trial (number of letters in correct position divided by sequence length). These trial-level proportion correct scores were then averaged across trials to compute mean recall accuracy for each experimental condition (digit format × memory load) for each participant. Reading accuracy and latency data were also scored; reading errors were rare and were used to verify task compliance. Data cleaning procedures included inspection for trials with missing responses or responses produced outside expected timing windows; no participants were excluded from analyses for failure to follow instructions, and fewer than 1% of trials were removed because of technical issues or recording errors. The primary statistical analyses addressed the effects of language group, digit format, and memory load on recall accuracy. A mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted with language group (monolingual, bilingual) as a between-subjects factor and digit format (word, numeral, dots) and memory load (3, 4, 5, 6) as within-subjects factors. Sphericity assumptions were evaluated using Mauchly’s test, and Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied where necessary. In addition to frequentist ANOVAs, Bayesian analyses were conducted for the critical comparison of language group differences to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis. Effect sizes for significant effects were reported as partial eta squared for ANOVAs and Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons. Exploratory analyses examined correlations between self-rated bilingual proficiency measures and recall performance within the bilingual group, as well as potential differences between balanced and unbalanced bilinguals as indexed by self-reported language use. Statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software with an alpha threshold of .05 for inferential tests. Power considerations informed the sample size: with forty-eight participants, the study was powered to detect medium-sized between-group effects in mixed designs with conventional sensitivity, though smaller effects may not be reliably detected with the present sample size.

Results

Descriptive statistics for recall accuracy are presented first to characterize performance across conditions. Across the full sample, mean recall accuracy decreased as memory load increased from three to six letters, consistent with expected load effects. For load three, the grand mean proportion correct across formats and groups was 0.92 (SD = 0.06). For load four, the grand mean was 0.85 (SD = 0.08). For load five, the grand mean was 0.70 (SD = 0.12). For load six, the grand mean was 0.54 (SD = 0.15). These descriptive patterns indicate a monotonic decline in recall performance with increasing memory load. Means broken down by digit format showed some variation: in the digit-word condition the overall mean proportion correct across loads was 0.73 (SD = 0.14), in the Arabic numeral condition it was 0.74 (SD = 0.13), and in the dot pattern condition it was 0.71 (SD = 0.15). These means suggest modest differences in recall by digit format, with numerals showing slightly higher mean accuracy than words and dots showing slightly lower mean accuracy. When split by language group, monolingual participants had an overall mean recall accuracy across all conditions of 0.73 (SD = 0.13), and bilingual participants had an overall mean of 0.72 (SD = 0.14). The group means were therefore very similar, with only a 0.01 difference in the grand mean proportion correct. The primary mixed-design ANOVA tested effects of language group, digit format, and memory load on recall accuracy. The ANOVA revealed a highly significant main effect of memory load, F(3, 138) = 212.45, p < .001, partial eta squared = .82, confirming that recall accuracy declined as the number of letters to maintain increased. Mauchly’s test indicated that the sphericity assumption for the load factor was violated, χ2(5) = 23.17, p = .001, and Greenhouse-Geisser correction (ε = .76) was applied for the reported load effect. Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction indicated that each increase in load produced a reliable decrease in mean recall accuracy (all ps < .01), reflecting the stepwise decay in performance from three through six letters. There was a modest but statistically significant main effect of digit format, F(2, 92) = 4.21, p = .018, partial eta squared = .08. Post-hoc comparisons revealed that the numeral format produced slightly higher recall accuracy relative to the dot pattern format (mean difference = .03, p = .012), while the difference between word and numeral formats was not reliable (mean difference = -.01, p = .45), and the difference between word and dot formats approached significance (mean difference = .02, p = .08). These effects indicate that digit format exerted a small influence on recall, with numerals being marginally less disruptive than dot patterns overall. Crucially, the between-subjects main effect of language group was not significant, F(1, 46) = 0.12, p = .73, partial eta squared = .003, indicating no reliable difference in recall accuracy between monolingual and bilingual participants when averaged across formats and loads. In addition, the two-way interaction of language group by digit format was non-significant, F(2, 92) = 0.34, p = .71, partial eta squared = .007, and the interaction of language group by load was also non-significant, F(3, 138) = 0.96, p = .41, partial eta squared = .02. The three-way interaction among language group, digit format, and load likewise did not reach significance, F(6, 276) = 0.42, p = .87, partial eta squared = .009. These null interactions indicate that the pattern of load-related decline and format differences did not differ between monolingual and bilingual participants. Given the absence of a language group effect in the frequentist analyses, Bayesian t-tests were conducted to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis of no difference between monolinguals and bilinguals on overall recall accuracy. The Bayesian analysis comparing overall mean recall across groups yielded a Bayes factor BF01 = 4.35, indicating moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis over the alternative of a group difference. Bayes factors for comparisons within specific formats similarly favored the null, with BF01 values ranging from 3.1 to 5.2 depending on format, further supporting the interpretation that bilingualism did not confer measurable advantages in this task. Reading accuracy during the interleaved digit reading task was uniformly high across participants and formats, with mean reading accuracy above 95% in all conditions, indicating that participants complied with the processing task. Mean reading latencies for digit stimuli varied by format, with numerals producing the fastest average vocal response times (mean RT ≈ 420 ms), digit words producing intermediate RTs (mean ≈ 480 ms), and dot patterns producing longer RTs (mean ≈ 590 ms), consistent with the idea that dot patterns required more time for enumeration. These latency differences were statistically significant, F(2, 92) = 45.67, p < .001, partial eta squared = .50. However, there were no reliable differences between language groups in reading latency, F(1, 46) = 0.34, p = .56, and no interactions of language group with format in the latency data. Exploratory correlation analyses within the bilingual group examined relations between self-rated proficiency and recall performance. Pearson correlations between self-rated speaking proficiency and overall recall accuracy were small and non-significant (r = .08, p = .70), as were correlations between current frequency of language use and recall accuracy (r = -.06, p = .77). When bilingual participants were split into subgroups of those reporting balanced usage across languages (n = 10) and those reporting English dominance (n = 14), descriptive means suggested no clear advantage for balanced bilinguals in recall accuracy, but the subgroup sample sizes were small and differences were not statistically reliable. An additional analysis examined the possibility of a tradeoff between reading performance and recall accuracy by computing within-subject correlations between average digit reading latency and corresponding trial-level recall accuracy. These within-subject correlations were small and not consistently different from zero across participants, suggesting that slower reading responses on individual trials did not systematically predict better or worse recall on those same trials. Finally, checks for speed-accuracy tradeoffs at the group level revealed no evidence that bilinguals adopted a different strategy from monolinguals in prioritizing reading speed over recall accuracy. Taken together, the results demonstrate robust effects of memory load on recall and modest effects of digit format, while providing no evidence that bilingual participants outperformed monolingual participants on the reading digit-span dual-task.

Discussion

This study examined whether bilingualism confers an advantage in a reading digit-span dual-task that requires maintaining letter strings while verbally reading digit stimuli presented in different formats. The experimental design replicated key elements of prior dual-task work and incorporated format and load manipulations intended to probe phonological and visuospatial interference channels. Across forty-eight undergraduate participants, robust declines in recall accuracy were observed with increasing memory load, confirming the sensitivity of the task to the amount of information to be maintained. The manipulation of digit format produced small effects on recall, with Arabic numerals associated with marginally higher recall than dot patterns, and digit words producing intermediate levels of disruption. Crucially, bilingual and monolingual participants performed equivalently across the experimental conditions; no significant main effect of language group emerged and interactions involving language group were non-significant. Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence favoring the null hypothesis of no bilingual advantage in this task. These results indicate that, in the present sample and task configuration, bilingual experience did not produce measurable benefits for concurrent maintenance under interleaved reading demands. The observed load effect aligns with many models of working memory that predict capacity limitations and increased vulnerability to interference as memory set size rises. That effect replicates core features of the reading-span paradigm and supports the interpretation that the experimental manipulation successfully taxed maintenance mechanisms. The modest effect of digit format suggests that the nature of the processing task can differentially interfere with maintenance, which is consistent with theories positing multiple processing channels. The relatively better performance in the numeral condition compared to dot patterns could reflect differences in processing efficiency or the degree to which the processing task competes for the same representational resources as the memory letters. For instance, Arabic numerals may be processed more symbolically and may less strongly engage phonological rehearsal processes that overlap with letter maintenance, whereas dot patterns may recruit visuospatial enumeration resources that are less compatible with encoding serial letter sequences. These format effects, however, were small in magnitude and did not interact with bilingual status. The lack of a bilingual advantage in this paradigm contributes to a growing body of evidence suggesting that bilingual benefits are not universal and may be sensitive to task specifics, participant characteristics, and methodological choices. Several interpretations can explain why bilinguals did not outperform monolinguals here. One possibility is that the particular control processes exercised in typical bilingual language use do not translate into superior performance on every form of dual-task coordination. Bilingual advantages have been most reliably observed in tasks that specifically involve conflict resolution, inhibition of prepotent responses, and rapid switching between conflicting stimulus-response mappings. The reading digit-span task, while demanding in terms of dividing attention and maintaining serial order, may not have placed a premium on the exact executive control skills that bilingualism enhances. Maintaining serial letter sequences and reading simple digit stimuli may rely more on domain-specific rehearsal and encoding strategies rather than on inhibition or switching processes. Another possibility concerns the operationalization of bilingualism in the present study. Participants were classified as bilingual based on self-report of language use and proficiency, and the bilingual group was heterogeneous with respect to age of acquisition, balance of language use, and proficiency. If only balanced bilinguals or those with high proficiency in both languages exhibit cognitive benefits, pooling across a heterogeneous bilingual sample could obscure such effects. Exploratory comparisons between balanced and unbalanced bilinguals in the current sample did not reveal reliable differences, but such subgroup analyses were underpowered. Future studies should more precisely characterize bilingual experience using comprehensive language history inventories, objective proficiency measures, and measures of language switching frequency to better identify potential moderators of cognitive effects. Sample size is another consideration. Although the study was powered to detect medium-sized effects, smaller bilingual advantages, if present, might go undetected in a sample of forty-eight participants. Some prior studies that have reported bilingual benefits used larger samples or meta-analytic approaches to aggregate small effects. The Bayesian analyses in the present study suggested moderate evidence for the null, but these conclusions are contingent on sample size and prior assumptions. Larger samples with adequate statistical power to detect small effects are essential for clarifying whether bilingualism exerts subtle influences on dual-task maintenance performance. Task sensitivity and ecological validity also matter. The reading digit-span task used here employed relatively brief processing episodes and a straightforward reading response requirement. Barrouillet and colleagues emphasized that increasing the duration of the processing component can have a disturbing effect on maintenance. It is possible that bilingual advantages would be more apparent under conditions of more prolonged or more challenging processing demands that place sustained load on attentional refreshing mechanisms. In the present protocol, the digit stimuli were presented for 1000 ms with a brief inter-stimulus interval, which may not have produced sufficiently prolonged diversion of attention to reveal differences in the ability to allocate attentional refreshing. Manipulating processing duration, increasing processing difficulty, or requiring processing that produces greater cognitive conflict may be fruitful avenues for future research. Another avenue relates to the nature of the memory material and the overlap between memory and processing modalities. The current task required memorization of visually presented consonant letters, which can be rehearsed phonologically. If bilingualism primarily influences nonverbal executive control rather than phonological maintenance, a dual-task with visuospatial memory demands might show different patterns. Conversely, tasks that require simultaneous phonological processing in a language other than the dominant language could engage bilingual control mechanisms more strongly. Designing tasks that more directly mirror the control demands of bilingual language use, such as those involving language switching or suppression of an activated translation, could provide stronger tests of transfer. In addition, individual strategies in performing the task might moderate outcomes. Participants likely differed in their reliance on rehearsal, chunking, or other mnemonic techniques, and such strategy differences were not tightly controlled in the present study. If bilinguals and monolinguals adopt different strategies, this could mask group-level differences in raw accuracy. Collecting strategy reports or instructing participants to use or avoid specific strategies could shed light on the role of strategy use in bilingual effects. Methodological limitations should be acknowledged. The binary categorization of bilingualism via self-report is a coarse measure that fails to capture the nuanced dimensions of bilingual experience. Sociodemographic factors such as socioeconomic status and educational background, which were not comprehensively controlled here, can correlate with both bilingualism and cognitive outcomes and may confound group comparisons. Additionally, the timing parameters and the specific stimuli used in the experiment were chosen to approximate prior work, but different choices could produce different patterns of interference and might interact with bilingual status. Despite these limitations, the present findings have important theoretical implications. They suggest that bilingual experience alone does not guarantee enhanced performance on all tasks that combine memory maintenance and concurrent processing. Instead, bilingual advantages may be selective, emerging under particular task demands that more closely align with the control processes enhanced by bilingual language management. This selectivity underscores the need for more mechanistic theorizing about what aspects of bilingual experience lead to transfer and under what circumstances. Future research should therefore focus on delineating the boundary conditions for bilingual advantages with greater precision. Several concrete directions are recommended. First, larger-scale studies that sample a wide range of bilingual profiles and include objective proficiency and language-use measures are needed to assess whether particular bilingual subgroups drive any observed cognitive benefits. Second, parametric manipulations of processing duration and difficulty in dual-task paradigms should be undertaken to test whether sustained diversion of attention reveals group differences in attentional refreshing or maintenance strategies. Third, tasks that directly incorporate language control demands—such as bilingual language switching, bilingual Stroop variants, or tasks requiring suppression of translation equivalents—may be more likely to show transfer and should be included alongside conventional working memory measures. Fourth, multimodal measures such as eye-tracking and neurophysiological indices could illuminate whether bilinguals deploy attention differently during maintenance and processing even when overt performance is equivalent. Such measures could reveal subtler differences in strategy or resource allocation that behavioral measures alone do not capture. Finally, longitudinal and training designs that follow individuals during second-language acquisition could test whether changes in bilingual experience causally influence dual-task coordination and working memory processes. In conclusion, the present study replicated core features of the reading digit-span dual-task, showing robust load effects and small format effects, but did not find evidence that bilingual undergraduates outperform monolinguals on this task. These findings argue against a broad claim of generalized bilingual superiority in concurrent maintenance and emphasize the importance of task characteristics and bilingual heterogeneity in understanding cognitive outcomes associated with bilingualism. Future work that refines measurement of bilingual experience, increases sample sizes, and targets task demands more precisely should help clarify when and how bilingualism affects the coordination of memory and processing.
