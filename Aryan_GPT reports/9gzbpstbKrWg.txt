Introduction

The study of cognitive control and selective attention has long been central to cognitive psychology. One of the most enduring and widely cited demonstrations of processes that interfere with task performance is the Stroop phenomenon. The Stroop phenomenon, often referred to as the Stroop effect, reveals the cost that automatic processing can impose on goal-directed behavior. It illustrates that when two stimulus attributes compete for response selection, the more automatic attribute can slow or disrupt performance on the intended task. The classic demonstration involves naming the ink color of color words when the word meaning and ink color are either congruent or incongruent. In the congruent case, the word “red” printed in red ink typically yields fast responding, whereas in the incongruent case, the word “red” printed in blue ink typically yields slower responding. This difference in response time and error rate between congruent and incongruent trials is the hallmark of the Stroop effect. The effect has been replicated across decades of research and has been used to probe automaticity, attention, inhibition, and the architecture of lexical and color processing. The robustness of the Stroop effect has made it a standard tool in cognitive research and a benchmark for theories of attention and interference.

Beyond demonstrating the basic interference effect, researchers have used variations of the Stroop task to examine a range of cognitive and individual difference factors. For example, modifications of stimulus timing, task instructions, and response modality have illuminated the temporal dynamics of processing and the locus of interference. The short-term dynamics between stimulus onset and response selection have been modeled to show how automatic activation of word meaning and controlled color naming evolve and interact over time. In addition to manipulating task parameters, researchers have also examined how stable individual differences influence Stroop performance. Individual differences such as working memory capacity, age, bilingualism, and cognitive abilities have all been linked to variations in Stroop interference magnitude. These findings have motivated inquiry into whether personality-like cognitive styles might also shape the ease or difficulty with which people resolve competing stimulus information.

Cognitive style is a broad construct that refers to stable preferences in how individuals process information. One influential framework distinguishes between experiential and rational styles. The experiential style is characterized by a preference for intuitive, quick, affect-laden processing. The rational style is characterized by a preference for deliberative, analytical, and effortful processing. Measures purporting to assess these dimensions commonly present statements about thinking preferences and ask respondents to indicate their level of agreement. The rationale for examining cognitive style in relation to Stroop performance stems from the idea that experiential processing relies more heavily on automatic and associative processing, whereas rational processing relies more on controlled and reflective processing. If Stroop interference arises in part from automatic, habitual processes such as word reading, then an individual who habitually favors automatic, intuitive processing might be more susceptible to that interference. Conversely, an individual who favors analytic deliberation might more readily recruit controlled processes that override the automatic activation of word meaning and thus show reduced interference.

Despite the intuitive linkage, there has been relatively little systematic investigation of how these cognitive style preferences interact with classic measures of interference such as the Stroop task. A small number of studies have investigated related constructs, including impulsivity, need for cognition, and cognitive reflection, but the direct comparison of experiential and rational preferences as assessed by a standard self-report inventory and performance on the Stroop task remains understudied. This gap is striking given the theoretical clarity of the prediction: experiential thinkers should show larger Stroop interference due to a reliance on automatic processing routes, whereas rational thinkers should show smaller interference due to greater propensity to engage controlled processing. Testing this prediction would extend the explanatory reach of cognitive style constructs and would also inform theories of automaticity and control by clarifying whether relatively stable processing preferences generalize to basic attentional conflict tasks.

In addition to theoretical considerations, there are methodological reasons to revisit this question in a contemporary sample. Early demonstrations of the Stroop effect relied heavily on vocal response measures and relatively small numbers of trials per condition. Modern computerized tasks allow for precise measurement of reaction times, larger trial counts, and robust control of stimulus presentation. These features increase the reliability of the Stroop measure and hence its suitability for correlational work with self-report measures. Moreover, replication of classical effects in modern laboratories is valuable in its own right given concerns about robustness and reproducibility in psychological science. Therefore, a project that both replicates the classic Stroop effect in a contemporary sample and examines its relation to cognitive style would serve dual purposes: to reaffirm the basic interference phenomenon and to probe individual differences that may modulate that interference.

The present study was designed to achieve these aims. First, it sought to replicate the basic Stroop effect using a computerized color-naming task in which participants responded via keypress to the ink color of printed words. Second, it sought to examine the relationship between Stroop interference magnitude and self-reported experiential and rational cognitive styles as measured by a widely used inventory. The prediction was straightforward and theory-driven. It was predicted that reaction times would be slower and errors would be more frequent in the incongruent condition relative to the congruent condition, reflecting the classic Stroop interference. It was also predicted that higher scores on an experiential thinking scale would be associated with larger Stroop interference effects, whereas higher scores on a rational thinking scale would be associated with smaller interference effects. The predicted pattern of correlations would support the hypothesis that stable individual differences in processing preferences modulate susceptibility to automatic processing conflicts.

Beyond these core predictions, the study also aimed to explore secondary questions. One secondary question concerned the relationship between cognitive style and response accuracy alongside reaction times. It is possible that cognitive style affects speed and accuracy in different ways. For instance, experiential thinkers might be faster on congruent trials but more error-prone on incongruent trials, or rational thinkers might be slower overall but maintain higher accuracy in conflict conditions. A second exploratory question involved whether cognitive style would predict within-subject variability in reaction time, which could signal differences in attentional control or consistency of processing. Taken together, these secondary analyses were intended to provide a richer picture of how cognitive style relates to the dynamics of conflict resolution.

To ensure sufficient power to detect medium-sized correlations between cognitive style scores and Stroop interference, a reasonably sized sample was recruited. The study also incorporated standard data quality procedures, including practice trials, counterbalanced response mappings, and exclusion criteria for outlier reaction times and high error rates. These procedural details were implemented to maximize the internal validity of the measurements and to ensure that any observed correlations reflect stable individual differences rather than random noise or task confusion.

The anticipated implications of the study are both theoretical and practical. Theoretically, a finding that experiential processing preferences are linked to larger Stroop interference would suggest that stable cognitive styles influence basic attentional control in ways that align with dual-process models of cognition. Practically, understanding how cognitive styles influence susceptibility to interference could inform interventions or task designs that tailor cognitive demands to individual processing preferences, potentially improving performance in educational or occupational contexts where conflict resolution is important.

Finally, the study contributes to a broader agenda of integrating individual differences research with experimental cognitive approaches. By examining how trait-like preferences interact with a well-understood experimental manipulation, the research fosters cross-fertilization between descriptive personality research and process-oriented cognitive science. The present paper therefore reports the methods, results, and interpretation of a study designed to replicate the Stroop effect and to assess its relationship with rational and experiential cognitive styles, advancing a modest but important line of inquiry about the interplay between stable processing preferences and moment-to-moment cognitive control.

Methods

Participants were undergraduate students recruited from a university participant pool. The recruitment materials indicated that participants would complete a short computerized task followed by questionnaires and that the session would last approximately 30 minutes. Eighty participants completed the study session in return for course credit. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal color vision. Prior to analysis, four participants were excluded for failing to follow task instructions or for excessive errors or missing data, leaving a final sample of seventy-six participants. Exclusion criteria were determined in advance and included committing errors on more than 30% of trials in either condition or having more than 10% of trials with reaction times below 150 ms or above 3000 ms. The retained sample included both males and females and spanned a narrow age range typical of undergraduate samples, with mean age in the early twenties. Participants provided informed consent and were debriefed at the end of the session.

The experimental stimuli consisted of color words printed in one of four ink colors. The words were “RED,” “BLUE,” “GREEN,” and “YELLOW” presented in uppercase letters. Each word was displayed centrally on a neutral gray background. There were two primary trial types: congruent and incongruent. In congruent trials, the word meaning matched the ink color (e.g., “RED” printed in red). In incongruent trials, the word meaning did not match the ink color (e.g., “RED” printed in blue). Each participant completed 120 experimental trials, with 60 congruent and 60 incongruent trials. Trial order was randomized for each participant to minimize sequence effects. Prior to experimental trials, participants completed 12 practice trials that included both congruent and incongruent stimuli to acquaint them with the response mapping and task demands.

Participants responded using a standard computer keyboard. Four response keys were used and were mapped to the four ink colors. Keys D, F, J, and K were assigned to red, blue, green, and yellow respectively, and participants were instructed to press the key corresponding to the ink color as quickly and as accurately as possible. The response mapping was displayed on the screen throughout the task to reduce memory load. Stimulus presentation and response recording were controlled by experimental software that recorded reaction time (RT) from stimulus onset to keypress, and correctness of the response. The software also logged trial type, stimulus word, ink color, and whether the response was correct.

The trial structure was as follows. Each trial began with a central fixation cross displayed for 500 ms. The stimulus word then appeared and remained on the screen until a response was registered or for a maximum of 2000 ms. An inter-trial interval of 800 ms followed the response before the next fixation cross. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible and to prioritize both speed and accuracy. Feedback was not given during the experimental trials, but during the practice block participants received feedback about correctness to encourage learning of the response mapping.

Following the computerized Stroop task, participants completed a self-report cognitive style inventory administered on the same computer. The inventory assessed two dimensions: experiential thinking and rational thinking. The experiential scale comprised items that probed preference for intuition, affective decision-making, and reliance on gut feelings. The rational scale comprised items that assessed preference for deliberation, logic, and analytical consideration. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Scale scores were computed as the mean of the items comprising each subscale, yielding two composite scores for each participant. Higher scores reflected stronger endorsement of the respective cognitive style.

Data preprocessing included standard trimming of reaction times and computation of accuracy rates. Trials with reaction times below 150 ms were considered anticipatory and removed. Trials with reaction times above 3000 ms were considered outliers and removed. In addition, for each participant, reaction times more than 2.5 standard deviations above their condition mean were trimmed to reduce the influence of extreme values; this trimming removed a small proportion of trials (<2% overall). Mean reaction times for correct responses were computed separately for congruent and incongruent trials for each participant. Error rates were computed as the proportion of incorrect responses per condition. The primary dependent variable for the correlation and regression analyses was the Stroop interference score, defined as the difference between mean RT for incongruent trials and mean RT for congruent trials (RTincongruent - RTcongruent). Positive values indicated slower responses on incongruent trials and hence greater interference.

Statistical analyses began with descriptive summaries of reaction times and accuracy by condition. Paired-samples t-tests were used to test the predicted differences between congruent and incongruent conditions in both reaction time and accuracy. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for paired comparisons. Pearson correlation coefficients were used to examine associations between the Stroop interference score and the two cognitive style scores. In addition, multiple regression analyses were conducted to examine whether experiential and rational scores accounted for unique variance in Stroop interference when entered simultaneously. All statistical tests used an alpha level of .05 for significance. For transparency, the distributional properties of key variables were examined and, when necessary, analyses were supplemented by robust methods; however, primary results are reported from conventional parametric tests, which were appropriate given the reasonably symmetric distributions observed after trimming.

Complementary analyses explored potential confounds and moderators. These included examining whether age or gender were associated with Stroop interference or cognitive style scores and whether adjusting for these variables altered the main associations. Additional exploratory analyses examined within-subject variability in reaction time, operationalized as the standard deviation of reaction times within each condition, and whether cognitive style dimensions predicted this variability. The analytic plan was pre-registered prior to data collection to reduce risks of analytical flexibility; minor deviations from the pre-registered plan are noted in the Results section and were limited to exploratory follow-up analyses prompted by observed patterns in the data.

Ethical procedures were followed throughout the study. The university institutional review body approved the protocol. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were free to withdraw at any time without penalty. Data were anonymized for analysis, and individual responses to the cognitive style inventory were treated as confidential. The study adhered to standard ethical guidelines for human subjects research.

Results

Descriptive statistics for reaction times and accuracy are reported first. For the final sample of seventy-six participants, the mean reaction time on congruent trials was 519.8 ms with a standard deviation of 79.9 ms. The mean reaction time on incongruent trials was 650.1 ms with a standard deviation of 99.6 ms. The mean Stroop interference score, computed as RTincongruent minus RTcongruent, was 130.3 ms with a standard deviation of 92.4 ms. Inspection of the distribution of interference scores showed a roughly normal shape with no extreme skewness, consistent with effective trimming procedures. Accuracy rates were high overall, with mean proportion correct of .982 on congruent trials and .922 on incongruent trials. The difference in accuracy between conditions indicates elevated errors on incongruent trials.

A paired-samples t-test confirmed that reaction times were significantly slower in the incongruent condition relative to the congruent condition. The mean difference of 130.3 ms was statistically significant, t(75) = 12.58, p < .001, and corresponded to a large effect size, Cohen’s d = 1.44. This result replicates the classic Stroop effect in the present sample and indicates robust interference when word meaning and ink color are incongruent. A paired-samples t-test on accuracy rates also found a significant difference between congruent and incongruent trials, t(75) = 5.57, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.64, demonstrating that incongruent trials produced not only slower responses but also more errors.

Turning to the cognitive style measures, the mean experiential score was 3.12 (SD = 0.61) on the five-point scale, and the mean rational score was 3.48 (SD = 0.57). The two cognitive style scales were modestly negatively correlated, r = -0.21, p = .07, indicating some independence between the scales but also a slight tendency for higher experiential endorsement to be associated with lower rational endorsement. Neither age nor gender was significantly correlated with the Stroop interference score, experiential scores, or rational scores; adjusting for these demographic variables did not materially change the pattern of results reported below.

The primary correlational analyses examined associations between the Stroop interference score and the two cognitive style dimensions. Experiential scores were positively correlated with Stroop interference, r = .32, p = .004. This indicates that higher endorsement of experiential thinking was associated with greater slowing on incongruent trials relative to congruent trials. The correlation corresponds to a medium-sized effect in the context of individual differences research. In contrast, rational scores were not significantly correlated with Stroop interference, r = -0.05, p = .64, indicating no reliable linear association between rational thinking endorsement and the magnitude of interference.

To test whether experiential and rational scores accounted for unique variance in interference, a multiple regression was conducted with Stroop interference as the dependent variable and both cognitive style scores entered simultaneously as predictors. The overall model accounted for a significant proportion of variance, R^2 = .11, F(2,73) = 4.39, p = .016. Within the model, experiential thinking remained a significant positive predictor of interference, b = 41.9, SE = 14.2, t = 2.95, p = .004, indicating that a one-point higher experiential score predicted approximately a 42 ms greater interference score. Rational thinking was not a significant predictor in the model, b = -12.8, SE = 15.1, t = -0.85, p = .40, and its inclusion did not attenuate the experiential coefficient substantially.

Complementary analyses examined whether cognitive style related to accuracy rather than reaction time. Experiential scores correlated modestly with the increase in error rate from congruent to incongruent trials (computed as error rate incongruent minus error rate congruent), r = .21, p = .07, indicating a trend-level association in the same direction as the RT effects. Rational scores exhibited no reliable association with the accuracy difference, r = -.08, p = .53. These patterns suggest that experiential thinking may be linked to both slower and somewhat less accurate responding under conflict, although the accuracy association was weaker than the RT association.

Additional exploratory analyses examined within-condition reaction time variability. The standard deviation of reaction times within incongruent trials was positively correlated with experiential scores, r = .27, p = .018, indicating that higher experiential thinkers not only experienced greater mean interference but also displayed greater intra-individual variability in conflict trials. In contrast, variability within congruent trials showed no reliable association with experiential or rational scores. These findings hint that experiential thinkers may experience less stable attentional control in the face of conflict.

To probe the time-course of responding, median splits on experiential scores were used to generate high and low experiential subgroups. The high-experiential group exhibited a mean interference of 157.4 ms (SD = 95.1), whereas the low-experiential group exhibited a mean interference of 103.2 ms (SD = 84.3). An independent-samples t-test confirmed that the groups differed significantly on interference, t(74) = 2.52, p = .014, Cohen’s d = 0.58. This subgroup comparison provides a descriptive illustration of the correlational pattern and underscores the practical magnitude of the association.

Robustness checks were performed to ensure that the observed associations were not driven by a few extreme cases. Spearman rank correlations between experiential scores and Stroop interference yielded a similar coefficient, rho = .28, p = .012. Exclusion of participants with interference scores more than two standard deviations above the mean did not materially alter the correlation (r = .30, p = .009). These checks increase confidence that the correlation reflects a general tendency in the sample rather than outliers.

Finally, given the possibility that motivation or fatigue might confound the relationships, task order and response times across trial blocks were examined. Reaction time did not systematically increase over the course of the experiment, and block-level analyses revealed consistent congruency effects across the session. There was no sign that experiential scores were associated with differential time-on-task effects. Taken together, the results demonstrate a robust replication of the Stroop interference effect and a consistent positive association between experiential thinking and susceptibility to interference on the task.

Discussion

The present study achieved its primary goals by demonstrating a clear Stroop interference effect in a contemporary undergraduate sample and by identifying a meaningful relationship between cognitive style and susceptibility to interference. The basic replication is unambiguous: participants were substantially slower and less accurate on incongruent color-word trials relative to congruent trials. This replication provides reassurance about the robustness of the Stroop effect when assessed with modern computerized procedures and common keypress response mappings. The size of the interference in this study, with a mean difference of approximately 130 ms, is consistent with past reports of substantial and reliable costs associated with resolving conflict between automatic word reading and instructed color naming. These results confirm that Stroop interference remains an effective assay of how automatic and controlled processes interact during simple perceptual-linguistic tasks.

Beyond replicating the classical effect, the study offers insight into how stable cognitive preferences relate to performance on tasks that impose conflicting demands. Specifically, experiential cognitive style scores were positively associated with the magnitude of Stroop interference. Individuals reporting a greater tendency to rely on intuition and affective, fast processing exhibited larger increases in reaction time on incongruent trials compared to congruent trials. This pattern suggests that habitual reliance on automatic, associative processing pathways may increase susceptibility to interference when a more controlled response is required. In contrast, rational cognitive style did not reliably predict interference magnitude. Together, these findings align with dual-process conceptions of cognition that differentiate between automatic, intuitive processing and controlled, analytic processing.

The association between experiential style and Stroop interference is theoretically coherent. Stroop interference emerges in part from the automatic activation of word meaning, which competes with the task-relevant color naming. An individual who habitually favors automatic processing may experience stronger or more readily available activation of automatic responses, making it more difficult to suppress that activation in favor of the instructed response. Alternatively, experiential thinkers might allocate less cognitive control to tasks or might adopt strategies that prioritize speed or associative fluency, leading to greater susceptibility to automatic distractors. The correlation found here does not adjudicate among these mechanistic explanations, but it points to an interesting link between a trait-like preference and a basic cognitive control capacity.

The absence of a reliable association between rational thinking and interference is also informative. It suggests that endorsement of deliberative, analytic thinking does not automatically translate to superior performance on brief, low-level interference tasks, at least not as captured by self-report measures. Several reasons might explain the null association. First, rational thinking as self-reported may capture a general preference for analysis that does not directly correspond to moment-to-moment engagement of control in a simple perceptual task. Second, the Stroop task at the trial level primarily taxes rapid inhibition and selective attention, capacities that may not be strongly tied to the broader disposition to engage in deliberation. Third, ceiling effects in accuracy on congruent trials and generally efficient performance by most participants may limit the observed variance that rational style could explain.

The relationship between experiential scores and within-subject variability in reaction time on incongruent trials further refines the picture. Higher experiential thinkers showed greater RT variability on conflict trials, which may reflect fluctuations in attentional engagement or inconsistent recruitment of control processes. This finding complements the mean RT association and suggests that experiential style might be linked both to average levels of interference and to the temporal stability of conflict resolution performance. Fluctuating attention or inconsistent inhibitory control could result in both greater slowing on average and more variable trial-to-trial performance.

The results have implications for how individual differences in processing preferences are conceptualized and measured. Self-report inventories such as the experiential and rational scales capture global tendencies, and yet the experiential scores in this study related to behavior on a specific, brief laboratory task. This linkage suggests that stable preferences in processing style can manifest in basic cognitive operations. Practically, these findings hint at the potential utility of tailoring tasks or training to individuals’ processing preferences. For example, interventions that bolster deliberate control or that re-train automatic tendencies might differentially benefit those high in experiential preference who are more susceptible to interference in certain contexts.

Several methodological points warrant discussion. The study used a computerized keypress response mapping rather than vocal naming. While both response modalities have been used in Stroop research, the choice of manual responses confers advantages in timing precision and ease of response coding. It is possible that the relation between cognitive style and interference would differ under vocal response conditions, where lexical processes and articulatory planning might interact differently with individual differences. Future work could compare modalities to determine the generality of the observed associations.

Another methodological consideration concerns the self-report nature of the cognitive style measures. Self-report scales capture participants’ introspections about their typical preferences, but they may be subject to biases or limited self-awareness. Behavioral measures of cognitive reflection, impulsivity, or habitual response tendencies might provide converging evidence. Indeed, marrying self-report with behavioral assays could illuminate whether experiential styles are better captured by subjective endorsement or by behavioral signatures. The current study demonstrates a meaningful association using self-report, but complementary measurement strategies would strengthen causal inferences and deepen understanding.

The correlational design also limits causal interpretation. While it is plausible that experiential style predisposes individuals to greater interference, alternative explanations exist. For example, a third variable such as general executive function capacity, working memory, or processing speed could influence both cognitive style endorsements and Stroop performance. However, the observed pattern—in which experiential, but not rational, scores predicted interference—argues against a simple general-performance confound and instead suggests a more specific linkage. Nevertheless, future studies using longitudinal designs or experimental manipulations of processing mode (e.g., priming experiential versus rational processing) could more directly test causal relations.

The magnitude of the observed correlation between experiential scores and Stroop interference was modest to moderate. This magnitude is consistent with typical effect sizes in individual differences research and indicates that cognitive style is one of multiple factors that influence susceptibility to interference. Cognitive control is multifaceted, and factors such as working memory capacity, attentional focus, arousal, stress, and task familiarity are likely to contribute to performance. The experiential preference should therefore be viewed as a contributor to performance variance rather than as a sole determinant.

Several avenues for future research follow from these findings. One promising direction is to examine whether interventions that encourage analytic strategies reduce interference more among high-experiential individuals than among low-experiential individuals. Such training studies could test whether cognitive style moderates training effectiveness and whether styles are malleable over time. Another direction is to investigate the neural correlates of the interaction between cognitive style and conflict processing. Functional neuroimaging or electrophysiological measures during Stroop-like tasks could reveal whether experiential thinkers show different patterns of activation in regions implicated in conflict monitoring and control, such as the anterior cingulate cortex and prefrontal areas.

Cross-cultural and developmental extensions are also of interest. Cognitive style distributions and their behavioral manifestations may vary across cultures and across the lifespan. For example, older adults may show different patterns of association between style and interference due to age-related changes in cognitive control. Additionally, examining bilingual populations could yield insights, since bilingualism often modifies the control of competing linguistic representations and could interact with processing preferences.

There are limitations to acknowledge. The sample consisted of university students, which limits generalizability to broader or more diverse populations. The use of a single task and a single self-report measure constrains inferences about the generality of the findings. Moreover, the cross-sectional and correlational nature of the study precludes causal claims. Despite these limitations, the consistency of the findings across multiple analytic approaches and the robustness of the Stroop replication support the credibility of the core conclusions.

In conclusion, the present study replicated the Stroop effect and demonstrated that experiential cognitive style is positively associated with the magnitude of Stroop interference. These results provide evidence that stable processing preferences shape how individuals contend with basic attentional conflict and underscore the value of integrating individual differences into experimental cognitive paradigms. The findings open avenues for intervention research, neuroscientific investigation, and cross-population studies aimed at understanding how trait-like cognitive styles influence moment-to-moment control. By bridging trait-level constructs and laboratory measures, this work contributes to a more comprehensive account of the interplay between habitual cognitive tendencies and fundamental cognitive control processes.
