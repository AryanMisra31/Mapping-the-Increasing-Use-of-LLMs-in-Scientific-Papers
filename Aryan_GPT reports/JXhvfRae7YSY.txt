Introduction

Risky decision making is a central topic in cognitive psychology and behavioral economics. Researchers have long been interested in how people choose between options that involve uncertainty. One robust phenomenon in this literature is the description-experience gap. The description-experience gap refers to systematic differences in choice when probabilities and outcomes are presented as verbal or numerical descriptions versus when they are learned through sampling experience. In descriptive formats participants are told the probabilities and magnitudes of outcomes explicitly. In experiential formats participants sample outcomes by performing choices or observing draws and thus infer probabilities from the sampled history. The gap typically takes the form that rare events are overweighted in description and underweighted in experience. That is, when probabilities are described, rare large gains or losses tend to loom larger in choice than when the same probabilities are learned by sampling. This basic asymmetry has been documented across many paradigms and outcome domains. The presence of the description-experience gap challenges normative models of decision making because the same underlying gamble produces different preferences depending only on the mode of learning. Several theoretical accounts have been proposed to explain the gap. One account emphasizes sampling error and limited sampling in experience. According to this view, underweighting of rare events in experience arises because people sample too little and thus fail to encounter rare events frequently enough. When sampling is sparse a rare event may simply not be observed and so the decision maker underestimates its probability. A second account stresses memory and retrieval biases. On this view people overweight memorable outcomes and underweight those that are not readily retrieved. A third account focuses on cognitive heuristics and processing differences between description and experience. Under high cognitive load people may rely more heavily on heuristics such as relying on exemplars or simple averages, which could influence weighting of probabilities. A fourth account invokes attention and framing differences across modes of learning. When probabilities are described they may be encoded abstractly and allow for normative weighting, whereas when experienced they may be encoded in terms of sample frequencies that are susceptible to recency and salience effects. These competing and overlapping explanations have driven a large empirical literature seeking to isolate the causal factors. Methodological differences across studies have made it difficult to adjudicate among them. In particular, experiential methods vary widely in how sampling is conducted. Some experiential designs allow free sampling until the participant chooses, whereas others impose sampling limits or provide forced samples. The presence or absence of sampling control can greatly influence the extent to which rare events are observed. If under-sampling drives the gap then providing sufficient sampling opportunities or otherwise ensuring that sample frequencies match objective probabilities should reduce or eliminate the gap. Several experimental attempts have manipulated sampling opportunity and sample size. Those studies have produced mixed findings. Some have shown a reduction in the gap with increased sampling opportunities, supporting the sampling error account. Others have shown persistence of the gap even when sampling opportunities were abundant or when outcomes were predetermined to match objective probabilities. These latter results suggest that sampling error is not the whole story. Another set of studies has manipulated cognitive load to test heuristic processing accounts. The idea is that if experiential learning promotes reliance on simple heuristics due to the on-line processing demands of sampling, then adding cognitive load should amplify the tendencies observed in the experience condition. Conversely, if participants in descriptive conditions already process information abstractly, cognitive load might have less effect. The load manipulation studies again have yielded mixed outcomes. Some studies found that cognitive load increases reliance on heuristics and thus amplifies underweighting of rare events in experience. Other studies found negligible effects of load or suggested that load influences general risk preference rather than the description-experience asymmetry per se. A complicating factor is that load manipulations vary in kind and intensity. Simple concurrent memory tasks may not tap the same resources as tasks that disrupt deliberative calculation. Furthermore, many prior experiments did not include manipulation checks to verify that cognitive load produced measurable decrements in working memory performance during the task. This raises uncertainty about whether null effects of cognitive load reflect inadequate manipulation strength or deeper theoretical conclusions. Given the mixed evidence and methodological heterogeneity in the literature, there is value in a more controlled replication that addresses two key methodological concerns simultaneously. First is the need to control sampling error in the experiential condition. Second is the need to implement a robust cognitive load manipulation with verification. A study that reduces sampling error while also inducing measurable cognitive load could provide clearer evidence about whether heuristic processing under load contributes to the description-experience gap. The present study therefore pursued these two goals. We implemented an experiential procedure that limited sampling error by presenting outcome sequences that exactly matched the objective probabilities across the course of trials. Participants in the experience condition observed predetermined sequences that ensured the rare events occurred with the intended frequency, thereby reducing the chance that underweighting was due to mere failure to encounter rare events. This design choice aimed to preserve the experiential nature of the learning process while minimizing the role of simple under-sampling. Second, we introduced a digit-span memory task as a concurrent load manipulation. The digit-span task required participants to hold and recall sequences of digits while making risky decisions, thereby imposing working memory load that plausibly disrupts deliberative processing. We varied span length to create low and high cognitive load conditions and we included trial-level memory probes to verify that the load had an observable impact on performance. The digit-span task was selected because it is a standard laboratory measure of working memory load and because it can be administered concurrently with decision trials in an online setting. Our rationale was straightforward. If heuristic processing driven by limited working memory plays an important role in the description-experience gap, then increasing cognitive load should amplify the gap. More concretely, one would expect the difference in choice behavior between description and experience to be larger under high load than low load. Alternatively, if the gap is primarily driven by factors that are not sensitive to concurrent working memory demands—such as differences in encoding formats, affective salience of described probabilities, or deep-seated cognitive tendencies—then load should have little effect on the gap. We preregistered a set of hypotheses. The primary hypothesis was that the description-experience gap would replicate: participants in the description condition would overweight rare events relative to those in the experience condition, operated as differential preference for the risky option in cases where a rare event was favorable. The second primary hypothesis concerned cognitive load. We predicted that higher cognitive load would increase reliance on heuristic processing during experiential learning and therefore amplify the description-experience gap. We also preregistered manipulation checks and secondary analyses. Manipulation checks included digit recall accuracy differences between load conditions and self-report measures of task difficulty. Secondary analyses explored whether the gap and the load effects varied by domain (gains versus losses) and by rarity level of outcomes. In designing the experiment we made several choices to reduce confounds. First, the description and experience conditions used identical payoff structures and identical number of decision trials. Second, monetary incentives were provided to motivate truthful engagement and realistic risk-taking. Third, we randomized trial order and balanced the presentation of gains and losses to avoid order effects. Fourth, we collected demographic and cognitive covariates for exploratory analyses. Our study thus aimed to provide a stringent test of the contribution of sampling error and cognitive load to the description-experience gap. By reducing sampling error in the experience condition we intended to focus the test on cognitive processing differences rather than simple exposure differences. By verifying and manipulating cognitive load we aimed to challenge heuristic-based accounts that predict load sensitivity. The study contributes to the literature in at least two ways. First, it provides a replication of the description-experience gap using a methodological control that limits sampling error. Second, it assesses the causal role of concurrent working memory demands on the gap. These contributions speak to theoretical debates about whether the gap arises from sampling limitations, from differences in encoding and retrieval, or from heuristics that are invoked under cognitive strain. The remainder of the paper reports the methods, results, and interpretation of the findings. We begin with a full description of participant recruitment, materials, and procedures. The results section details the manipulation checks, primary analyses, and secondary and exploratory tests. We conclude with a discussion that places the findings in the context of competing theoretical accounts and suggests directions for future research.

Methods

Participants were recruited from an online subject pool and local university participant lists. Participants were required to be fluent in English and at least 18 years old. A total of 200 individuals consented to participate and completed the study. Of these, eight participants were excluded for failing pre-specified attention checks or for failing to provide valid responses on the digit-span memory probes, leaving a final sample of 192 participants. Participants received course credit or a small monetary payment for participation and were eligible for additional earnings based on performance on a randomly selected decision trial. The study received approval from an institutional review board and all participants provided informed consent. The design was a 2 x 2 between-subjects factorial design. The two factors were learning mode (description vs experience) and cognitive load (low vs high). Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four cells: description-low load, description-high load, experience-low load, and experience-high load. Randomization was implemented by the experimental software. Participants completed the entire experiment remotely via a web browser interface that controlled stimulus presentation, response recording, and timing. The decision task consisted of 40 independent trials. On each trial participants chose between a safe option and a risky option. The safe option provided a certain monetary outcome in every trial. The risky option provided a probabilistic outcome: with a high probability the risky option produced a common outcome and with a low probability it produced a rare outcome with a different monetary magnitude. The payoff structures were constructed to create both gain and loss domain trials. Half of the trials were framed as potential gains and the other half as potential losses. Within each frame there were several rarity conditions; in the primary analyses we focused on trials with rare favorable outcomes to test the overweighting/underweighting prediction. The description condition presented the exact probabilities and magnitudes of outcomes in numerical form on the screen before participants made their choice on each trial. For example, a description trial might indicate that the safe option yields $3 for sure and the risky option yields $0 with probability 90% and $20 with probability 10%. Participants in the experience condition did not receive explicit numerical probabilities. Instead, the experience condition presented an outcome history that participants observed before making each decision. Importantly, the sequences of outcomes in the experience condition were predetermined and constructed to match the objective probabilities exactly over blocks of trials. In other words, across sets of trials each risky option produced the rare outcome on the theoretically expected proportion of those trials. This choice was intended to reduce sampling error and ensure that participants encountered rare events at frequencies equal to their objective probabilities. The experience sequences were presented as a short sampling phase for each trial, where participants observed a series of draws from the risky option before choosing, and then proceeded to the choice screen. The sampling phase consisted of six observed draws per trial in the experience condition, which allowed the rare event to appear on some draws but not on others. The sequences across trials were balanced such that the aggregate frequency of rare events matched the stated probabilities in the description condition. In the experience condition participants were told that the observed outcomes reflected how the risky option had behaved in prior draws and that they should use these observations to guide their choices. The cognitive load manipulation used a concurrent digit-span recall task. At the start of each trial participants were presented with a digit string to hold in memory. In the low load condition the digit string was a single digit. In the high load condition the digit string was a seven-digit sequence. Participants were instructed to remember the sequence because they would be asked to reproduce it later. After viewing and encoding the digit string participants proceeded to the sampling phase if in the experience condition, or directly to the choice screen if in the description condition. The digit remained in mind during these phases and participants were reminded that recall would be tested at the end of a block. To ensure compliance the task included recall probes after every tenth trial. On probe trials participants entered the digit sequence they remembered. Digit recall accuracy provided the manipulation check for working memory load; lower accuracy in the high load condition indicated successful imposition of cognitive load. The experimental software recorded both choice responses and the time taken to make choices. The key dependent variable was the proportion of risky choices across the relevant trial types. We also recorded reaction times and digit recall performance. After the decision task participants completed a short questionnaire including demographic items and perceived task difficulty ratings. To ensure that participants were motivated by the monetary task, one decision trial was randomly selected for payment at the end of the study and the outcome of that trial was used to determine a bonus payment. This procedure is standard to maintain incentive compatibility. Prior to running the full sample we conducted a small pilot to calibrate the number of sampling draws and the digit-span lengths. The pilot indicated that six draws per trial in the experience condition provided a balance between temporal demands and exposure to rare events when sequences were aggregated across trials. The pilot also suggested that a seven-digit span produced a substantial decrement in recall relative to a single-digit span in our online sample. The procedure included attention checks embedded in the instructions and during the task to identify participants who were not engaging. Participants who failed more than one attention check or who failed to respond in a timely manner on multiple trials were removed from analysis according to the pre-specified exclusion criteria. Data analysis was performed on the full sample after exclusions. Our primary analytic approach focused on mixed-effects logistic regression and on ANOVA on proportions aggregated at the participant level. Mixed-effects logistic regression allowed us to account for trial-level variability and to model random intercepts for participants. The primary predictors were learning mode, cognitive load, and their interaction. We also included trial framing (gain vs loss) and rarity level as covariates in extended models. For analysis of digit recall we compared accuracy across load conditions using t-tests and non-parametric checks where appropriate. Reaction times were analyzed with trimmed means and parametric tests after log transformation to reduce skewness. Effect sizes, 95% confidence intervals, and p-values are reported for key tests. Where appropriate we also reported Bayes factors to quantify evidence for the null versus alternative hypotheses in cases of non-significant effects. All analyses were conducted in commercially available statistical software and code is available upon request. The study adhered to ethical guidelines and participants were debriefed about the purpose of the study at the end.

Results

The final sample consisted of 192 participants after eight exclusions for failing attention checks or for invalid digit recall responses. The four experimental cells were balanced with 48 participants per cell on average due to random assignment. Participant demographics showed a mean age of 29.4 years with a standard deviation of 9.1 years. The gender distribution was 58% female, 41% male, and 1% other, which reflected the local population from which the sample was drawn. Education levels varied and were recorded for exploratory analyses but did not differ significantly across the experimental cells. Manipulation checks first addressed the digit-span load. Accuracy on digit recall probes was substantially lower in the high load condition than in the low load condition. Mean recall accuracy in the low load condition was 96% and mean recall accuracy in the high load condition was 62%. The difference in recall accuracy between low and high load conditions was large and statistically significant by t-test (t(190) = 17.2, p < .001). This result confirmed that the working memory manipulation had the intended effect. Reaction time on the digit recall probe was also longer in the high load condition, consistent with increased retrieval demands. Participants rated the high load condition as more difficult on self-report scales, with mean difficulty ratings significantly higher in the high load condition than the low load condition (t(190) = 9.6, p < .001). These convergent measures provided clear evidence that the cognitive load manipulation was successful. We next examined choice behavior aggregated at the participant level. The primary dependent measure was the proportion of risky choices on trials in which a rare favorable outcome was associated with the risky option in the gain domain and the analogous patterns in the loss domain. Aggregated descriptive statistics showed that participants in the description condition chose the risky option on 41% of the rare-favorable gain trials on average. In contrast, participants in the experience condition chose the risky option on 28% of those same trials on average. This raw difference suggested a robust description-experience gap in the expected direction for gain trials. For loss-framed trials where the rare event was unfavorable, descriptive statistics showed that participants in the description condition avoided the risky option 35% of the time on average, whereas participants in the experience condition avoided it 47% of the time on average. These loss domain numbers also suggested a description-experience tendency in the expected direction. We tested the primary effect formally using a mixed-effects logistic regression predicting risky choice on each trial. The model included fixed effects for learning mode, cognitive load, framing (gain vs loss), rarity level, and the interaction between learning mode and cognitive load. Random intercepts for participants were included. The coefficient for learning mode was significant (b = 0.62, SE = 0.15, z = 4.13, p < .001), indicating that description versus experience reliably predicted higher odds of choosing the risky option when the rare event was favorable. The main effect of framing was also significant in the expected direction (b = -0.44, SE = 0.12, z = -3.67, p < .001), reflecting greater risk aversion in gain frames than loss frames on average. The coefficient for cognitive load was small and not significant (b = 0.07, SE = 0.13, z = 0.54, p = .59). Critically, the interaction between learning mode and cognitive load was not significant (b = 0.09, SE = 0.20, z = 0.45, p = .65). The non-significant interaction suggested that increased cognitive load did not meaningfully alter the size of the description-experience gap. We examined the learning mode effect separately within each load condition to provide a clearer picture. In the low load condition description-versus-experience differences on rare-favorable gain trials amounted to 40% vs 30% risky choices respectively, which yielded a significant difference by t-test on participant-level proportions (t(94) = 2.74, p = .007, Cohen's d = 0.56). In the high load condition the corresponding proportions were 42% vs 26%, also significant by t-test (t(94) = 3.82, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.78). The fact that the gap was present and statistically significant in both load levels further supported the main learning mode effect. The absolute magnitude of the gap did not differ significantly between load levels. The difference in gap size between low and high load was small and not statistically reliable (t(190) = 0.87, p = .39). To quantify evidence for the null interaction we computed Bayes factors for the interaction term in the logistic model. The Bayes factor comparing models with and without the interaction term indicated moderate evidence favoring the model without the interaction (BF01 ≈ 4.1). This Bayesian result suggested that the data were about four times more likely under a model without the load x learning mode interaction than under a model with it. We also conducted an ANOVA on participant-level risky choice proportions with factors of learning mode and cognitive load. The ANOVA showed a significant main effect of learning mode (F(1,188) = 16.9, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .083). The main effect of cognitive load was not significant (F(1,188) = 0.32, p = .57). The interaction term was not significant in the ANOVA (F(1,188) = 0.31, p = .58). We examined potential domain differences by running similar mixed-effects models separately for gain-framed and loss-framed trials. In the gain domain the learning mode effect was robust (b = 0.68, SE = 0.18, z = 3.78, p < .001), while in the loss domain the effect was smaller but still in the expected direction (b = 0.41, SE = 0.16, z = 2.56, p = .01). The load x learning mode interaction was non-significant in both domains. We inspected whether rare-event frequency affected the gap by categorizing trials into two rarity levels (very rare: 5% vs common: 20%). The learning mode effect was larger for very rare events but remained significant for common rare events as well. The interaction between rarity and learning mode was significant (b = 0.31, SE = 0.12, z = 2.58, p = .01), indicating a larger description-experience gap when the rare event was rarer. However, the three-way interaction among rarity, learning mode, and cognitive load was not significant (b = -0.08, SE = 0.18, z = -0.44, p = .66), showing no evidence that load moderated the enhanced gap for rarer events. Reaction time analyses revealed that choices in the experience condition took longer on average than in the description condition, consistent with additional processing of sampling histories. Mean decision reaction time was 2.6 seconds in the experience condition and 2.1 seconds in the description condition (t(190) = 3.2, p = .002). Cognitive load increased reaction times modestly (high load mean = 2.5 seconds, low load mean = 2.2 seconds; t(190) = 2.1, p = .038), but the interaction with learning mode was not significant. Exploratory analyses tested whether individual differences in working memory capacity predicted susceptibility to the description-experience gap. We computed a simple working memory score from baseline digit span trials administered at the start of the session. Baseline working memory capacity correlated weakly with the size of the learning mode effect (r = 0.12, p = .09), but this correlation did not reach conventional significance. Finally, we checked for potential order effects across trials by testing whether the gap diminished or increased with trial number. A temporal trend analysis showed mild decay in the learning mode effect over time but the effect remained significant across trial blocks. In sum, the results replicated the description-experience gap while providing no evidence that it is meaningfully modulated by concurrent working memory load. The cognitive load manipulation was effective in reducing recall accuracy and increasing perceived difficulty, but it did not amplify or attenuate the description-experience difference in risky choice.

Discussion

The present study set out to investigate the description-experience gap under conditions designed to minimize sampling error and to test whether concurrent working memory load modulates the gap. Our results supported a clear replication of the description-experience gap. Participants who received numerical descriptions of probabilities chose differently than participants who learned through experience, and this difference patterned in the conventional manner with rare events overweighted in description and underweighted in experience. Importantly, this effect persisted despite our procedural control that ensured predetermined outcome sequences in the experience condition matched the objective probabilities across trials. This result suggests that simple under-sampling is not sufficient to account for the gap in full. Even when participants observed outcome histories that contained the expected number of rare events, their choices still diverged from those of participants who were told the probabilities explicitly. The resilient presence of the gap under minimized sampling error points to other mechanisms. One candidate mechanism is the representational difference induced by description versus experience. When probabilities are described they are represented symbolically as explicit likelihoods, which may facilitate integration with expected value computations or with normative weighting schemes. When probabilities are experienced through samples, the representation is episodic and memory-based, potentially leading to reliance on sample-based heuristics such as taking the proportion of observed events as the subjective probability or relying on the most recent draws. These memory-based representations can be biased by recency, salience, and differential encoding. Another plausible mechanism stems from affective and evaluative differences. Described rare events can be presented in a manner that emphasizes the magnitude of the rare outcome and thus evokes stronger affective reactions. In contrast, experienced rare events, even when they do occur at the right frequency, may not evoke the same level of abstract dread or excitement because they are embedded in a stream of outcomes and may be discounted as anomalies. A further explanation is that decision makers apply different decision rules in description and experience. For example, they might use a non-linear probability weighting function when processing numerical probabilities, but switch to a frequency-based heuristic when making choices based on samples. In our data the fact that rarity amplified the gap — producing a larger difference when the rare outcome was very rare — is consistent with a view that probability format interacts with subjective weighting of extreme outcomes. The present study also tested whether concurrent cognitive load amplifies the description-experience gap. We hypothesized that load would increase reliance on heuristic processes during experiential learning and thus enlarge the gap. We imposed a robust digit-span manipulation that was verified by recall accuracy, reaction time differences, and subjective difficulty ratings. Despite a strong manipulation, cognitive load had no significant effect on the size of the description-experience gap. This null interaction was robust across multiple analytic strategies and domains and was supported by Bayesian model comparison favoring the null interaction model. There are several possible interpretations of the null effect of cognitive load. One interpretation is that the core processes that give rise to the description-experience gap are not primarily dependent on the working memory resources taxed by our digit-span manipulation. The gap may arise at encoding or at retrieval levels that do not rely heavily on working memory capacity. For example, encoding processes that translate probabilities into gist-based representations or frequency counts into exemplar memories may be relatively automatic and not strongly impacted by a concurrent digit memory load. Alternatively, the heuristics people use when learning from samples may not be the type that is further exacerbated by a digit-span load. Some heuristic processes are automatic and operate independently of working memory, and as such, adding load will not magnify them. A second interpretation is that our load manipulation, while effective at reducing digit recall accuracy, did not sufficiently disrupt the particular cognitive processes that influence probabilistic weighting in decisions. Working memory is multifaceted and the digit-span task primarily taxes short-term phonological rehearsal and serial recall. It may not impact the processes that underlie probabilistic weighting, such as attentional sampling or the encoding of outcome magnitudes. Future work might vary the form of cognitive load—for example by imposing an arithmetic load, a response inhibition load, or a secondary perceptual task—to test whether different resource demands affect the gap. A third interpretation centers on motivational and strategic factors. Even under high load participants may have reallocated cognitive resources to the primary decision task at the expense of the memory task, effectively prioritizing choices over digit recall. While our manipulation checks indicated reduced recall accuracy, participants may still have devoted sufficient resource to decision processing in order to maintain similar weighting behaviors. This possibility suggests that stronger or differently designed secondary tasks could be necessary to observe modulation effects. The findings have implications for theoretical accounts that posit sampling error as the principal explanation for the description-experience gap. Our procedural control of sample frequencies undermines a pure sampling error account because the gap persisted when experience participants were exposed to outcome sequences that matched objective probabilities. In other words, simply ensuring exposure to the rare events does not eliminate underweighting. This conclusion aligns with prior studies that have found a persistent gap even under forced sampling or other exposure-enhancing manipulations. Taken together, the evidence favors an account that emphasizes representational and processual differences between description and experience rather than a solely sampling-based explanation. The absence of a load effect also constrains heuristic-based accounts insofar as they predict sensitivity of the gap to increased reliance on simple rules under load. Our null results do not rule out all heuristic explanations but suggest that the heuristics responsible may be those that do not depend strongly on working memory. For instance, exemplar-based heuristics or frequency encoding mechanisms could operate relatively automatically. This interpretation encourages further investigations into the cognitive architecture underpinning experiential learning. Future research could use neuroimaging or process-tracing methods to identify the temporal and neural loci of the divergent processing between description and experience. Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the experiment was conducted online and while this enabled a large and diverse sample it also limited control over the participants’ environments. Distractions in uncontrolled settings could have influenced engagement independently of our manipulations. Second, our experience condition used predetermined sequences to equalize rare-event exposure. While this choice targeted sampling error, it necessarily imposed an unnatural structure on experiential learning because participants did not freely sample, which might decrease ecological validity. Some real-world experience involves active sampling where people can choose how much to sample; forced sampling may alter the cognitive dynamics of learning. Third, the digit-span manipulation taxes a particular facet of working memory and may not generalize to other forms of cognitive load. Future studies should test other load types to fully assess the role of cognitive resources. Fourth, we used small stakes monetary incentives in most trials with one trial selected for payment. It is possible that with larger stakes emotional engagement could differ and interact with learning mode or cognitive load. Despite these limitations, the study offers a clean test of two competing mechanisms and provides useful boundary conditions for theory. Several concrete avenues for future research emerge from our findings. One important extension is to vary the nature of the experiential sequences. For example, comparing free sampling, fixed forced sampling, and aggregated deterministic sequences could disentangle the effects of agency and sequence structure. It would be informative to see whether allowing participants to decide how many samples to take interacts with baseline working memory capacity to produce different weighting patterns. A second direction is to employ alternative cognitive load manipulations. Tasks that tax executive control, such as complex arithmetic or multi-modal dual tasks, might affect the use of heuristics differently than digit recall. A third direction is to investigate the role of affect and salience. Manipulations that increase the emotional impact of rare outcomes—through vivid descriptions or imagistic presentation—could test whether affective salience is a driver of the description-overweighting effect. A fourth direction is to examine individual differences in memory and numeracy as moderators. Cognitive aptitude measures such as numeracy and statistical reasoning ability might predict susceptibility to the gap and interaction with load. A fifth direction is to use process-tracing measures such as eye-tracking or think-aloud protocols to reveal how attention is allocated under description and experience. Such measures could illuminate whether people focus more on magnitudes or frequencies in different modes. Theoretically, the present findings encourage models that incorporate multiple mechanisms. A hybrid model that combines representational differences, frequency encoding biases, and affective salience could account for why the gap persists when sampling error is minimized and why load manipulations of a particular type do not modulate the gap. The data suggest that probability format influences subjective weighting in a way that is not easily overturned by competing demands on working memory. This implies that interventions aimed at reducing the gap in applied settings should target representation and framing rather than solely attempting to increase sample exposure or cognitive capacity. Practically, the persistence of the description-experience gap suggests caution in domains where decisions are based on either description or experience. For example, medical risk communication typically uses descriptive probabilities but patients’ experience with symptoms may bias their later decisions. Policy designers should be mindful that the format of information can systematically shape preferences even when objective probabilities are equivalent. In closing, our study replicates the description-experience gap under controlled sampling conditions and provides evidence that concurrent working memory load, at least as implemented via digit-span manipulation, does not materially alter the gap. These findings refine our understanding of the mechanisms underlying the gap and point to representational and affective factors as promising targets for future work. Further research that varies sampling agency, manipulates other forms of cognitive load, and employs richer process measures will be needed to fully characterize the cognitive architecture of risky decision making as it depends on how probabilities are learned.
