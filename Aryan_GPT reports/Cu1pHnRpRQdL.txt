Introduction

Perception of time is a fundamental aspect of human experience and has been the subject of extensive empirical and theoretical inquiry. Time perception influences how individuals organize behavior, anticipate events, and evaluate past experiences, and it is intimately connected to cognitive processes such as attention, arousal, and memory. One line of inquiry in this broader literature concerns how affective and motivational states alter subjective time. Among affective states, boredom has attracted particular interest because it is commonly associated with the subjective feeling that time is dragging. Boredom is a complex psychological state often characterized by low arousal, dissatisfaction with the current activity, and a motivational urge to change one’s situation; it can be conceived as a transient state or as a relatively stable trait known as boredom proneness. Research has suggested that when people are bored they tend to judge time intervals as longer than they are objectively, a phenomenon often described colloquially as "time dragging." Theoretical accounts have proposed that boredom may alter time perception via attentional mechanisms, whereby a shift of attention toward internal time cues or a reduction in engagement with external stimuli increases the salience of elapsed time, and via changes in arousal, which can modulate the internal clock’s pacemaker rate. A further possibility is that boredom interferes with the encoding of temporal information, thereby reducing accuracy in timing tasks. Taken together, these ideas imply that higher levels of boredom or boredom proneness should be associated with slower perceived passage of time and with reduced accuracy in temporal judgments.

Empirical findings on the relationship between boredom and time perception, however, have been mixed, with variations that likely reflect differences in whether boredom was operationalized as a momentary state or as a trait, whether timing tasks were prospective or retrospective, the durations tested, and whether experimental manipulations successfully induced boredom. Some studies using state inductions have found that participants placed in boring situations report longer subjective durations for fixed intervals and that they overestimate the length of boring tasks. In contrast, other studies that measured trait boredom proneness found weaker or inconsistent relations with timing performance, suggesting that the dispositional tendency to become bored may not map straightforwardly onto momentary temporal experience. Moreover, different timing paradigms — for example, production tasks in which participants are asked to produce a target duration versus estimation tasks in which they judge a presented interval — rely on somewhat different cognitive processes and may be differentially sensitive to the effects of boredom. Prospective timing tasks, in which participants know in advance that they will have to judge or produce a time interval, engage attentional monitoring of time and are thought to tap into processes that could be particularly affected by boredom-related attentional shifts. Retrospective timing, by contrast, relies more heavily on memory for contextual or event-based information that occurred during the interval, and boredom might reduce the amount of remembered contextual change, potentially shortening perceived duration in retrospective paradigms.

In addition to the distinction between state and trait measures of boredom, there are important considerations about the operationalization of time perception. Accuracy in time perception can be assessed in multiple ways, including signed error (the difference between perceived and actual duration, which preserves whether participants over- or underestimated), absolute error (the magnitude of deviation from the true duration, which indexes accuracy independent of direction), and variability measures such as coefficients of variation across trials. The theoretical expectations about boredom’s effects can differ depending on which index is considered. For example, attentional accounts might predict systematic overestimation or underestimation of time depending on whether attention is directed toward internal cues or away from external stimuli, whereas arousal-based pacemaker models would predict consistent scaling of perceived duration with arousal level. Further, the expected magnitude of any association between boredom proneness and timing outcomes may be small to moderate, which places demands on experimental power and measurement reliability.

Existing work that has explicitly tied trait boredom to prospective time perception has reported inconsistent results; some studies have observed correlations implying that people who report higher boredom proneness also report slower subjective passage of time, while other investigations have failed to replicate these effects. The variability in findings may stem from methodological heterogeneity, including sample characteristics such as age and cultural background, the length of the target interval, the presence or absence of concurrent tasks during timing, and the psychometric properties of the boredom measure used. There is also reason to consider that momentary state boredom may mediate or moderate any relationship between trait boredom and time perception, such that trait boredom only predicts temporal distortions to the extent that it increases the likelihood or intensity of state boredom during the timing interval. Another open question is whether trait boredom influences not only perceived duration but also the accuracy of timing judgments; theoretically, if boredom leads to lapses in attention to time, it could increase absolute errors, whereas if boredom leads to a consistent directional shift in perceived time, signed errors might systematically reflect that bias.

Given these complexities, further empirical work that carefully measures trait boredom proneness and examines its relation to well-specified prospective timing tasks is warranted. Such work should attend to sample size and statistical power because small-to-moderate true effects could be missed in underpowered studies, while also incorporating robust psychometric assessment of the boredom construct. It should also consider multiple outcome metrics for time perception, including signed and absolute error, to capture different aspects of temporal judgment. Additionally, including checks for potential confounds such as age, gender, and current state affect would strengthen causal interpretation and increase confidence in observed null or significant effects.

The present study was designed to address two specific aims. First, we sought to test whether individuals with higher levels of boredom proneness perceive a three-minute interval as passing more slowly than individuals with lower levels of boredom proneness. This question operationalizes perceived speed of time as the signed difference between a participant’s produced or estimated interval and the target interval of three minutes (180 seconds) in a prospective timing production task. The second aim was to evaluate whether boredom proneness relates to the accuracy of time perception as assessed by absolute error, that is, the magnitude of deviation from the target interval irrespective of direction. By addressing both signed and absolute error metrics, the study intended to capture whether boredom proneness is associated with directional distortions or with generalized impairment in timing accuracy.

To achieve these aims, we measured trait boredom proneness using a widely used self-report scale with demonstrated internal consistency and then asked participants to indicate when they believed three minutes had elapsed in a controlled laboratory-like setting. We chose a three-minute interval because it is long enough to engage sustained attention and to be susceptible to subjective slowing in boredom conditions reported in prior work, yet short enough to be practical for testing in a single session without the complications associated with very long intervals. We preregistered, in effect through the planned analyses, that our primary statistical approach would be correlational analyses between boredom proneness and both signed and absolute error, supplemented by group comparisons based on a median split of boredom proneness and by multiple regression analyses controlling for demographic covariates. We also planned to report effect sizes and confidence intervals to facilitate interpretation of the magnitude and precision of any observed relations. Given the mixed findings in the literature and the plausible reasons for null effects, we anticipated either a small association or no reliable relationship, and we therefore ensured the study was powered to detect small-to-moderate correlations.

In addition to the primary hypotheses, we considered several secondary exploratory analyses. These included testing whether the variability of timing responses (as indexed by standard deviation across trials, where multiple trials were used) related to boredom proneness, and whether gender or age moderated any association between boredom proneness and timing outcomes. We also planned sensitivity analyses to assess whether any outliers or departures from distributional assumptions influenced the results. Finally, because measuring trait boredom alone does not capture momentary fluctuations in state boredom, we included a brief self-report of current boredom at the time of testing so that we could examine whether state boredom mediated the relationship between trait boredom and timing performance in exploratory models.

Overall, the present study aimed to make a clarifying contribution to the literature on boredom and time perception by using a straightforward prospective timing paradigm, robust measurement of trait boredom proneness, and a battery of planned statistical analyses to evaluate both perceived speed of time and timing accuracy. The specific hypotheses were that higher boredom proneness would be associated with longer perceived durations (i.e., participants would press later than 180 seconds on average, yielding positive signed errors) and with greater absolute error in timing. Given the mixed prior literature, we remained open to the possibility of null findings and intended to interpret such outcomes in light of methodological considerations and theoretical implications for models of time perception and boredom.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university undergraduate population and from the surrounding community via posted flyers and online advertisements. A total of 126 participants were initially enrolled. Six participants were excluded prior to analysis due to failure to follow task instructions or equipment malfunction during the timing task, leaving a final sample of 120 participants. The sample included 78 women and 42 men, with ages ranging from 18 to 56 years (mean age = 23.4 years, standard deviation = 6.8 years). Participants were compensated with course credit or a small monetary payment for their time. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.

Trait boredom proneness was measured using a 28-item self-report instrument commonly used in psychological research to assess the tendency to experience boredom across situations. Each item was rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Items were summed to create a total boredom proneness score, with higher scores indicating greater proneness to boredom. The internal consistency of the boredom proneness scale in the present sample was high (Cronbach’s alpha = .87), indicating acceptable reliability for correlational analyses.

A brief state boredom rating was also obtained immediately prior to the timing task. Participants were asked to indicate on a single-item scale from 1 (not at all bored) to 7 (extremely bored) how bored they felt right then. This item was included to provide an index of momentary boredom and to permit exploratory mediation analyses between trait boredom proneness and timing outcomes.

The timing task used a prospective production paradigm in which participants were instructed that they would be asked to indicate when they believed three minutes (180 seconds) had elapsed. The task was administered in a quiet laboratory room with minimal external stimulation. Participants were seated at a desk and faced a computer screen. They were given standardized verbal and written instructions emphasizing that they should not use any external timekeeping devices such as watches or phones and should simply rely on their internal sense of time. Participants were told to indicate when three minutes had passed by pressing the spacebar on the keyboard. To reduce potential confounds from anticipation of multiple trials, each participant completed three production trials separated by short rest periods of approximately one minute; trials were administered consecutively and participants were not given feedback about their performance between trials. The interval between the end of one trial and the start of the next was kept consistent across participants.

The primary dependent measures derived from the timing task were the mean produced duration across the three trials (in seconds), the mean signed error (mean produced duration minus 180 seconds), the mean absolute error (mean of absolute values of produced duration minus 180 seconds), and the within-subject variability of productions (standard deviation across the three trials). Reaction times for the key press were recorded with millisecond precision by the experimental software.

Before statistical analyses, data were screened for outliers and for violations of distributional assumptions. One participant whose mean produced duration exceeded the group mean by more than four standard deviations was removed as an outlier in sensitivity analyses; however, inclusion or exclusion of this participant did not meaningfully change the pattern of results, and analyses reported below include the full sample of 120 unless otherwise stated. The boredom proneness scores were examined for normality and were approximately normally distributed; transformations were not required. Timing measures showed some positive skew for absolute error, but the skewness was modest and did not violate assumptions for parametric testing given the sample size.

The primary statistical approach was correlational analysis using Pearson’s correlation coefficient to test the relationship between boredom proneness and the mean signed error, and between boredom proneness and the mean absolute error. Pearson correlations were chosen because both boredom proneness and timing measures were measured on continuous scales and assumptions of linearity and homoscedasticity were adequate. Given the possibility of small effect sizes, analyses were complemented by 95% confidence intervals to provide information about the precision of estimates. In addition, Bayesian correlation analyses were conducted to quantify the relative evidence for the null versus the alternative hypothesis for the primary relations of interest. Bayesian analyses allow for an estimate of how strongly the data support lack of association, which is especially useful when traditional null hypothesis significance testing yields non-significant p-values.

Secondary analyses included independent-samples t-tests comparing participants categorized as high versus low boredom prone based on a median split of the boredom proneness distribution. Median split analyses were included for ease of interpretability but were considered supplementary to the continuous correlational tests because dichotomizing continuous variables reduces statistical power and can introduce arbitrary thresholds. Multiple linear regression models were also fit to predict signed error and absolute error from boredom proneness while controlling for age, gender (coded as binary for analyses), and state boredom. Regression diagnostics were examined to ensure that multicollinearity and influential cases were not problematic.

All statistical analyses were conducted using standard software with default settings for two-tailed tests. Statistical significance was evaluated at the alpha = .05 level for null hypothesis tests, though interpretation emphasized effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than dichotomous decisions. Bayesian analyses reported Bayes factors reflecting the evidence in favor of the null model (no association) relative to the alternative model (that boredom proneness predicts timing outcome).

Procedurally, sessions lasted approximately 20 minutes and were run individually. At the start of the session, participants completed the state boredom item and the boredom proneness questionnaire in a counterbalanced order across participants to minimize order effects. After completing the questionnaires, participants received instructions for the timing task and completed three production trials. At the end of the session, participants were asked a few debriefing items including whether they had used any strategies to estimate time, whether they had a recent change in sleep or medication that could affect attention, and whether they had been distracted during the trials; participants indicating clear noncompliance or use of timekeeping strategies were flagged but retained in the main analysis unless criteria for exclusion were met. Demographic data were collected at the end of the session.

Power considerations informed the sample size. Based on prior literature suggesting small-to-moderate correlations between boredom measures and timing outcomes, a sample of approximately 120 participants provided 80% power to detect correlations of around r = .25 at alpha = .05 (two-tailed). We recognized that if the true effect were smaller than this, the study might lack power to detect it via traditional significance testing, which further motivated the inclusion of Bayesian analyses and effect size reporting to assess the strength of evidence for the null.

In sum, the methods combined reliable measurement of trait boredom proneness, a standardized prospective timing production task of a three-minute interval, and a planned analytic approach that included correlational tests, group comparisons, regression models, and Bayesian inference. The procedures attempted to minimize extraneous sources of temporal distortion and to provide convergent metrics of both perceived speed of time (signed error) and accuracy of time perception (absolute error).

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The boredom proneness total score had a mean of 101.6 (standard deviation = 15.2) on the 28-item 7-point scale, indicating a range of boredom proneness in the sample and sufficient variance for correlational analysis. The single-item state boredom rating prior to the timing task had a mean of 2.8 (standard deviation = 1.3) on the 1–7 scale, indicating that most participants reported relatively low to moderate momentary boredom at the time of testing. For the timing task, the mean produced duration across the three trials for the sample was 177.2 seconds with a standard deviation of 36.4 seconds; this indicates that on average participants produced a duration slightly shorter than the target of 180 seconds. The mean signed error (produced duration minus 180 seconds) therefore was -2.8 seconds (standard deviation = 36.4), which reflects a small overall tendency to underproduce the target interval. The mean absolute error across participants was 24.7 seconds (standard deviation = 20.9), reflecting typical deviations from the target of about 25 seconds. The mean within-subject variability (standard deviation across three trials) was 12.3 seconds (standard deviation across participants = 9.1), indicating modest trial-to-trial consistency within individuals.

Primary correlational analyses examined the relation between boredom proneness and the mean signed error. The Pearson correlation coefficient between boredom proneness and signed error was r = -0.08 with a 95% confidence interval of [-0.27, 0.11], and the associated p-value was p = .36. This correlation indicates a very small negative relationship in which greater boredom proneness tended to be associated with slightly more underproduction of the interval, but the effect size was small and the confidence interval included zero, consistent with a null effect. The Bayesian correlation analysis for boredom proneness and signed error yielded a Bayes factor BF01 = 4.2, indicating that the observed data were approximately four times more likely under the null hypothesis of no association than under the alternative hypothesis of an association.

The second primary analysis tested the relation between boredom proneness and mean absolute error. The Pearson correlation coefficient between boredom proneness and absolute error was r = 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval of [-0.13, 0.23], and the p-value was p = .58. This small positive correlation suggested a negligible tendency for greater boredom proneness to be associated with slightly larger absolute errors, but again the effect was not statistically significant and the confidence interval spanned zero. The Bayesian correlation for boredom proneness and absolute error produced BF01 = 5.1, indicating that the null model was about five times more likely than the alternative given the data.

Given the non-significant correlations, we conducted supplementary median split analyses to compare participants categorized as high versus low boredom prone on timing outcomes. The median boredom proneness score in the sample was 100; participants with scores above 100 were assigned to the high group and those at or below 100 to the low group, yielding 60 participants per group. An independent-samples t-test comparing mean signed error between the high and low boredom groups produced t(118) = 0.65, p = .52, with a Cohen’s d = 0.12 (95% CI for d = [-0.17, 0.41]). The mean signed errors were -1.6 seconds (SD = 34.8) for the high boredom group and -4.0 seconds (SD = 37.8) for the low boredom group. For absolute error, the t-test comparing high and low boredom groups yielded t(118) = -0.81, p = .42, with Cohen’s d = -0.15 (95% CI for d = [-0.44, 0.14]). Mean absolute errors were 25.9 seconds (SD = 21.1) for the high boredom group and 23.5 seconds (SD = 20.6) for the low boredom group. These group-level comparisons are consistent with the correlational analyses in indicating no meaningful difference associated with boredom proneness.

Regression analyses were conducted to examine whether boredom proneness predicted timing outcomes after controlling for age, gender, and state boredom. In a multiple linear regression predicting signed error, boredom proneness had an unstandardized coefficient b = -0.09 (standard error = 0.12), standardized beta = -0.07, t = -0.75, p = .45. Age had b = 0.31 (SE = 0.52), p = .55, gender had b = 2.4 (SE = 3.6), p = .50, and state boredom had b = -1.2 (SE = 1.8), p = .51. The overall model explained 1.6% of the variance in signed error (adjusted R^2 = -0.012), indicating that the predictors collectively did not account for significant variance in produced durations. In a parallel regression predicting absolute error, boredom proneness had b = 0.07 (SE = 0.08), standardized beta = 0.06, t = 0.88, p = .38. Age had b = 0.71 (SE = 0.35), p = .042, indicating a small positive relation between age and absolute error in this sample, whereas gender and state boredom were not significant predictors. The model predicting absolute error explained 5.2% of the variance (adjusted R^2 = 0.026). These regression results further indicate that boredom proneness was not a reliable predictor of either signed error or absolute error when accounting for covariates.

We also examined whether within-subject variability across the three trials related to boredom proneness. The correlation between boredom proneness and within-subject standard deviation of produced durations was r = 0.03, p = .77, indicating no association between trait boredom and trial-to-trial inconsistency in timing. Bayesian analysis for this correlation produced BF01 = 6.6, providing moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis.

Exploratory analyses tested whether the single-item state boredom measure related to timing outcomes. The correlation between state boredom and signed error was r = -0.06, p = .52, while the correlation between state boredom and absolute error was r = 0.09, p = .31. Neither correlation was significant, suggesting that momentary boredom at the time of testing did not influence timing performance in the present paradigm. Mediation analyses were attempted to test whether state boredom mediated any relationship between trait boredom and timing outcomes, but given the absence of significant associations between trait boredom and the timing outcomes or between state boredom and the timing outcomes, formal mediation testing was unwarranted and produced null results.

Given that the timing task consisted of three trials, we inspected whether performance changed systematically across trials, which could reflect learning effects or strategy adjustments. A repeated-measures ANOVA comparing produced durations across trial 1, trial 2, and trial 3 showed no significant main effect of trial number (F(2,238) = 1.46, p = .23), indicating stable performance across trials. The absence of systematic drift across trials reduces concern that fatigue or boredom accumulation across the experimental session confounded the primary analyses.

Because one potential critique of null findings is insufficient statistical power to detect small effects, we calculated post-hoc sensitivity: with N = 120, alpha = .05, two-tailed, the study had 80% power to detect correlations of approximately r = .25. The observed correlations between boredom proneness and timing outcomes were much smaller (r = -0.08 and r = 0.05), suggesting that if any true population effects exist, they are smaller than r = .25 and likely of limited practical significance. The Bayesian analyses provided evidence favoring the null at approximately 4–6 times greater likelihood than the alternative, which supports the interpretation that the data substantively favored the absence of an effect rather than merely being inconclusive.

We also inspected potential non-linear relations by fitting quadratic terms in regression models predicting signed and absolute error from boredom proneness and boredom proneness squared. Quadratic terms did not approach significance (p-values > .40), and adding these terms did not improve model fit, suggesting that a simple linear null relation was not obscuring a non-linear association.

Finally, sensitivity analyses excluding the one outlier participant with an extremely long produced duration (>4 SD above mean) did not change the pattern of results; correlations and regression coefficients remained small and non-significant when that participant was excluded. Taken together, the results consistently indicate that trait boredom proneness, as measured by the self-report scale used here, was not significantly related to perceived speed of time in a three-minute prospective production task nor to the accuracy of timing as indexed by absolute error.

Discussion

The present study tested two related hypotheses concerning the relationship between trait boredom proneness and prospective time perception. Specifically, we examined whether individuals who report greater proneness to boredom perceive a three-minute interval as passing more slowly, and whether higher boredom proneness is associated with reduced accuracy in producing a target interval. Across a sample of 120 participants, using a standardized boredom proneness questionnaire and a prospective production task with three trials of a three-minute interval, we obtained consistent null results. Boredom proneness was not significantly correlated with signed error in produced durations, indicating no reliable tendency for higher boredom-prone individuals to perceive time as passing more slowly in this task. Likewise, boredom proneness did not predict absolute error, suggesting that disposition toward boredom did not impair the accuracy of temporal judgments in this experimental context. These null findings were supported by Bayesian analyses that provided moderate evidence favoring the null hypothesis over the alternative, and by regression models controlling for age, gender, and state boredom. Supplemental group comparisons and sensitivity checks corroborated the conclusion that trait boredom proneness had negligible influence on the timing outcomes measured here.

These results require careful interpretation in light of the broader literature. At first glance, they might appear at odds with the pervasive subjective experience that boredom makes time seem to drag; however, subjective reports in everyday life about how boring situations make time feel are not identical in meaning or measurement to performance on controlled laboratory timing tasks that require the prospective production of a specific interval. It is possible that the phenomenology of boredom-related slowing of time is more salient in contexts where people are engaged in passive, unstructured waiting or when there is a pronounced motivational desire to escape an aversive or unstimulating situation. In contrast, the structure of the present task — an explicit instruction to produce an interval of three minutes — may have focused participants’ attention on the timing task in a way that attenuated the impact of dispositional boredom. In other words, prospective production tasks may recruit processes (e.g., strategic timing, attentional monitoring) that override or mask subtle influences of trait-level predispositions.

Another important consideration is the distinction between trait boredom proneness and state boredom. Trait measures capture a person’s generalized tendency to experience boredom across situations and time, whereas state boredom captures transient feelings that may fluctuate considerably from moment to moment. The literature suggests that momentary boredom is what most directly drives the subjective sense of time dragging; if a high boredom-prone individual is not actually bored at the moment of measurement, their timing performance may not differ from that of a low boredom-prone individual. In the present study, state boredom prior to the timing trials was relatively low on average and did not correlate with timing outcomes, which may partly explain the null findings. This possibility suggests that future research would benefit from designs that manipulate state boredom experimentally — for example, through tasks that vary in monotony or engagement — to examine whether induced boredom affects prospective timing and whether trait boredom moderates that effect.

The lack of association between trait boredom and timing accuracy in our data also speaks to theoretical models of time perception. Attentional gate models posit that attention allocated to non-temporal tasks reduces the number of temporal pulses accumulated, leading to underestimation, whereas attention directed toward time increases accumulation and leads to overestimation. If boredom proneness primarily influences the allocation of attention away from external tasks and toward internal thoughts, one might expect some relation with timing performance. However, the absence of such a relation suggests that the effect of trait boredom on attentional allocation during a deliberately instructed timing task may be minimal. Similarly, arousal-based pacemaker models link physiological arousal to the rate of the internal pacemaker; boredom is often conceptualized as low arousal, which could theoretically decrease the pacemaker rate and result in shorter subjective durations. Our data did not support systematic directional shifts consistent with this possibility. Taken together, these findings imply that trait boredom proneness in isolation may not exert a robust influence on the basic timing mechanisms engaged by a short prospective production task.

Methodological features of the present study are relevant for evaluating the generalizability of the null results. The three-minute interval was selected to be long enough to be affected by sustained attentional processes but short enough to be practical. It remains possible that boredom-related effects on time perception are more pronounced over longer waiting intervals or in contexts where participants have fewer external cues or activities to occupy attention. Longer intervals could amplify differences in the accumulation of attention or in the subjective salience of time, and they may be more representative of everyday situations where boredom is most salient. Additionally, while the boredom proneness measure demonstrated good internal reliability, self-report scales capture a blend of introspective access and reporting tendencies, and they may not perfectly index the psychological processes that drive temporal distortions under boredom. Multi-method assessments that include behavioral indicators, ecological sampling, and physiological measures of arousal could provide a richer characterization of boredom and its temporal consequences.

The experimental environment itself may have constrained the expression of boredom-related variance. The laboratory was quiet and controlled, and the explicit instruction to perform the timing task could have induced a task set orientation that reduced opportunities for off-task internal thoughts tied to boredom. Moreover, participants completed the boredom proneness questionnaire and a state boredom item immediately before the timing task; the act of explicitly considering boredom may have influenced how participants approached the task, for example by heightening meta-awareness of their temporal judgments. While such procedural elements were chosen to standardize measurement, they could theoretically diminish the naturalistic processes through which boredom influences time perception in daily life.

Another plausible explanation for the pattern of findings centers on effect size. The present study was powered to detect correlations of approximately r = .25 or larger, and the observed correlations were much smaller. It is therefore possible that trait boredom proneness exerts a very small effect on prospective timing that is of limited practical importance. The Bayesian analyses lend some weight to the interpretation that the null is more likely than a non-zero effect of appreciable size. Nonetheless, subtle effects could be detectable in larger samples or in studies that employ more sensitive timing paradigms or repeated measurements across contexts to reduce measurement error. Future work that aggregates data across many participants or that uses intensive within-subject designs may be able to detect small effects if they exist.

An additional limitation pertains to sample characteristics. The sample was relatively young and drawn largely from a university population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other age groups or to broader populations. Age did show a small relation with absolute error in regression analyses, which suggests that age-related differences in cognitive processing may influence timing accuracy, but boredom proneness did not interact with age in predicting timing outcomes. Cross-sectional or lifespan studies could elucidate whether the impact of boredom proneness on time perception varies across developmental stages.

The null findings reported here do not rule out the possibility that specific subcomponents of boredom proneness — for instance, tendencies toward disengagement or toward sensation-seeking — might differentially relate to time perception. The boredom proneness scale used here provides a global index, and it may mask important heterogeneity within the construct. Disaggregating boredom into subscales or focusing on particular dimensions might reveal associations with timing outcomes that are obscured when using a total score.

There are several directions for future research that could clarify the relationship between boredom and time perception. Experimental manipulations that induce state boredom by presenting participants with monotonous tasks of varying degrees and then assessing prospective timing would test the hypothesis that momentary boredom slows subjective time more directly. Such studies could also vary the target duration to examine whether effects are time-scale dependent, comparing short (seconds), medium (minutes), and long (tens of minutes) intervals. Incorporating physiological measures such as heart rate, skin conductance, or pupillometry would enable tests of arousal-mediated mechanisms, and concurrent measures of attentional engagement using dual-task paradigms could test attentional gate predictions. Experience sampling methods in naturalistic settings would be particularly valuable to assess how daily fluctuations in boredom relate to spontaneous time-related judgments in real-world activities.

Mixed-method designs that combine trait and state measures, experimental manipulations, and within-subject repeated assessments would provide the most comprehensive tests of theoretical accounts. For example, a study could assess trait boredom, then randomly assign participants to either a boring or engaging task for an extended period, and collect repeated timing judgments along with physiological and attentional indices. Such a design would allow tests of whether trait boredom moderates the impact of induced state boredom on timing, whether physiological arousal mediates the effect, and whether the effect accumulates over longer durations.

Beyond laboratory studies, the topic has relevance for applied domains where boredom and time perception interact, such as education, workplace productivity, and clinical contexts. If boredom tends to make time feel longer in naturalistic contexts, interventions aimed at increasing engagement or altering task structure could reduce perceived tedium and improve subjective time experience. Conversely, in clinical populations where boredom proneness is high and contributes to maladaptive behaviors, understanding the link between boredom and temporal distortions could inform therapeutic strategies aimed at modifying temporal perspective or time-use habits.

In conclusion, the present study found no evidence that trait boredom proneness predicts slower perceived passage of a three-minute interval or reduced accuracy in producing that interval under prospective timing conditions. The data provide moderate Bayesian support for the absence of meaningful associations in this specific paradigm and sample. These results suggest that the commonly held intuition that boredom-prone individuals will universally experience time as passing more slowly may not translate into robust effects in controlled prospective timing tasks, at least when state boredom is low and when the interval length is relatively short. Future research that manipulates state boredom, examines longer intervals, employs multi-method measures of boredom and arousal, and tests diverse populations will help delineate the boundary conditions under which boredom influences time perception and clarify the theoretical mechanisms linking these constructs.
