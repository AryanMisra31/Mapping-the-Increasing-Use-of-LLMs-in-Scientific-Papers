Introduction

Sound symbolism describes non-arbitrary associations between aspects of sound and aspects of meaning. This general phenomenon challenges the traditional linguistic assumption that the relationship between form and meaning is arbitrary and instead suggests that certain phonetic properties systematically evoke particular semantic content. One of the most widely discussed and empirically robust manifestations of sound symbolism is the bouba/kiki effect, in which people reliably match particular nonsense words to particular shapes. In the canonical demonstration, rounded shapes are consistently matched with words such as "bouba" while jagged, angular shapes are consistently matched with words such as "kiki." The bouba/kiki effect has been documented across many populations and experimental conditions and has been invoked as evidence for cross-modal correspondences between auditory and visual features. The origins and mechanisms that give rise to such mappings remain a matter of active theoretical debate, and understanding the sensory and cognitive contributions to the phenomenon is important for clarifying both standard models of language and broader principles of perception.

Early work by Köhler introduced the idea that phonetic qualities of speech sounds might be linked to particular semantic impressions, and more recent articulations by Ramachandran and Hubbard popularized the bouba/kiki paradigm as a clear empirical example. These foundational contributions established that adults in industrialized societies, and even young children in some studies, choose a consistent mapping between simple pseudowords and simple shapes. The stability of the effect across languages and cultures has been explored to varying degrees, with some studies reporting robust cross-cultural consistency and others noting modulation by language-specific phonotactic and phonological features. The bouba/kiki effect therefore sits at the intersection of psycholinguistics, perception, and cognitive anthropology, as it raises questions about innate biases, perceptual heuristics, and language-mediated learning.

Alongside the bouba/kiki effect, researchers have described related forms of sound symbolism in which specific phonetic features convey notions of magnitude or size. For example, words containing low back vowels and sonorant consonants are often perceived as referring to larger objects, whereas words with high front vowels or voiceless consonants may be perceived as referring to smaller objects. This magnitude-sound symbolism taps into a different conceptual dimension than roundedness or angularity; whereas bouba/kiki concerns contour and shape, magnitude symbolism concerns scalar properties such as size and weight. The delineation between these two forms of sound symbolism raises important questions about whether they share the same underlying cognitive and sensory bases or whether they reflect distinct mappings that can be differentially prioritized when multiple cues are present.

Another important strand of research has examined the sensory modalities through which bouba/kiki mappings are elicited. The classic bouba/kiki task is multimodal in that participants often hear a pseudoword while viewing a shape. However, subsequent studies have attempted to parse the contributions of auditory and visual channels more precisely by presenting words in a visual (orthographic) form, in an auditory form, or audiovisually synchronized. Some evidence suggests that visual presentation of the orthographic string alone can be sufficient to elicit bouba/kiki mappings, indicating that the visual appearance of the letters and their spatial properties might themselves cue particular shape associations. Other work has emphasized the primacy of auditory acoustic features, arguing that articulatory and acoustic properties of the speech signal drive the effect. This literature therefore leaves open the question of whether the bouba/kiki effect is primarily auditory, primarily visual, or genuinely cross-modal with roughly equal contributions of each modality.

Closely related to the modality question is the question of cognitive awareness and introspective access. Participants vary in whether they report that they are explicitly aware of making a systematic mapping between sound and shape when performing a bouba/kiki task. Some participants report no explicit strategy and claim to be guessing, whereas others report that they can hear a roundedness or sharpness to certain nonsense words. The degree of awareness might influence performance in two opposing ways. On the one hand, explicit awareness could amplify the effect because participants who notice the mapping might apply it more consistently. On the other hand, explicit awareness could permit strategic responding that reduces spontaneous cross-modal mapping, for instance if participants attempt to second-guess experimenter expectations. Empirical characterization of the role of awareness is therefore crucial for interpreting the bouba/kiki effect as an implicit perceptual bias versus an explicit learned association.

Despite the substantial literature, two specific empirical questions remain incompletely resolved. The first is whether the bouba/kiki effect is equivalently elicited by visual versus auditory presentation of pseudowords, and if there are subtle differences when the two modalities are combined in congruent or incongruent ways. The second question is how bouba/kiki mappings compare in influence to other sound-symbolic mappings, particularly magnitude-based symbolism, when participants are presented with competing cues. If sound-symbolic systems are organized hierarchically or interactively, then one form of symbolism might dominate when both are present; alternatively, the mappings might be independent and combine additively or even conflict.

The present report addresses these two questions in two experiments. The first experiment was designed to provide a fine-grained analysis of modality effects on bouba/kiki mappings. Specifically, we compared auditory-only, visual-only (orthographic), and congruent audiovisual presentations of bouba-like and kiki-like pseudowords to determine whether modality modulates the strength or speed of the mapping to rounded and angular shapes. We also included incongruent audiovisual trials in which the auditory and visual forms signaled different pseudoword types to test whether audiovisual conflict produces interference or whether one modality tends to dominate in such cases. Additionally, we collected a brief post-task questionnaire to assess participants’ explicit awareness of sound-shape correspondences in order to test whether awareness modulated mapping strength or modality effects.

The second experiment was designed to evaluate the relative authority of bouba/kiki symbolism versus magnitude-sound symbolism when the two are placed in direct competition. In this study, participants performed a forced-choice task in which each trial presented two novel objects that differed either in shape (rounded versus angular), size (small versus large), or both, and participants were asked to select which object matched a given pseudoword. Crucially, we used pseudowords that systematically varied on both bouba/kiki-related acoustic features and magnitude-related acoustic features, allowing us to create trials in which the bouba/kiki cue and the magnitude cue were congruent, incongruent, or neutral relative to each other. This design permitted a test of which form of symbolism would guide choice when cues conflict. We again measured reaction times and collected awareness ratings after the task to determine the influence of conscious knowledge.

Our approach builds on existing literature by combining modality manipulations with competitive cue designs, enabling an assessment of both sensory contributions and cue prioritization. If the bouba/kiki effect is predominantly auditory, we expected stronger mappings in auditory-presented trials and slower or weaker mappings in visual-only presentations. If the effect is truly cross-modal, we expected comparable performance across modalities and possibly an additive benefit for congruent audiovisual presentation. In Experiment 2, if bouba/kiki mappings represent a more salient or fundamental correspondence, we expected bouba/kiki cues to dominate magnitude cues when the two were in opposition. Conversely, if magnitude iconicity is more perceptually salient, then magnitude cues should assert greater influence.

The present experiments are thus designed to clarify the sensory bases and hierarchical relations among types of sound symbolism. By assessing modality-specific performance, audiovisual congruency effects, and competitive cue interactions within the same conceptual framework, we aimed to provide a more mechanistic understanding of how listeners integrate phonetic cues to derive semantic inferences. These findings have potential implications for theories of language origins, multimodal perception, and the design of communication systems that exploit non-arbitrary form-meaning mappings.

Beyond theoretical interest, understanding the modality and hierarchy of sound-symbolic cues has applied relevance. For instance, designers of brand names, user interfaces, and educational materials may capitalize on different sound-symbolic principles to convey shape or size with minimal explicit instruction. Clarifying whether auditory, orthographic, or audiovisual cues are most effective can inform best practices in naming and visual design. Moreover, mapping the influence of awareness on these processes informs whether interventions that make such correspondences explicit are likely to enhance or diminish their utility.

In the sections that follow, we present two experiments. The Introduction has outlined the conceptual background, clarified the empirical gaps we intended to address, and articulated specific expectations. The Methods and Results of each experiment are described in detail below, followed by a Discussion that integrates the findings, considers theoretical implications, acknowledges limitations, and suggests directions for future work.

Methods

Experiment 1

Participants. Eighty adult volunteers participated in Experiment 1. Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from an online platform that included a mix of community members and students. Inclusion criteria required normal or corrected-to-normal vision and self-reported normal hearing. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 45 years. Gender distribution was approximately balanced. Participants were compensated with course credit or a modest monetary payment. All participants provided informed consent in accordance with institutional review board procedures. No participant reported prior participation in bouba/kiki experiments, and none reported language impairments.

Stimuli. Stimuli consisted of visual shapes and pseudowords. The visual shapes included four rounded shapes and four angular shapes, each drawn as simple black outlines on a white background. Rounded shapes had smooth continuous curves and lack of sharp points, whereas angular shapes contained multiple sharp vertices. For pseudowords, we constructed a set of eight bouba-like words and eight kiki-like words. Bouba-like words included bilabial and sonorant consonants (e.g., /b/, /m/) combined with low back or rounded vowels (e.g., /u/, /o/), such as "bouba," "momo," "boba" and novel variants. Kiki-like words included voiceless plosives and velar consonants (e.g., /k/, /t/) combined with high front vowels (e.g., /i/, /e/), such as "kiki," "tete," "kete" and novel variants. All pseudowords were two to three syllables long and were controlled for syllable count and approximate duration.

Each pseudoword was recorded by a female native English speaker in a sound-attenuated booth at a constant speaking rate. Audio files were normalized for peak amplitude and trimmed to remove silence onset and offset. Orthographic forms of the pseudowords were also presented in a simple sans-serif font at a consistent size. For audiovisual trials, the orthographic text was displayed simultaneously with the audio playback.

Design. The experiment employed a within-subjects design in which modality (auditory-only, visual-only, congruent audiovisual, incongruent audiovisual) was manipulated across trials. Each participant completed 96 trials in total. In auditory-only trials, participants heard a pseudoword and viewed two shapes side-by-side (one rounded and one angular) and were asked to choose which shape matched the pseudoword. In visual-only trials, participants saw the orthographic form of the pseudoword instead of hearing it. In congruent audiovisual trials, the audio and orthographic forms corresponded to the same pseudoword, and in incongruent audiovisual trials the audio and orthographic forms corresponded to different pseudowords of opposite bouba/kiki type (e.g., auditory "kiki" paired with orthographic "bouba"). The side of the screen (left/right) on which rounded and angular shapes appeared was counterbalanced across trials.

Procedure. Participants completed the task on a computer in a quiet testing room or via an online platform with instructions to use headphones. Each trial began with a fixation cross for 500 ms, followed by stimulus presentation according to modality condition. For auditory-only trials, the fixation cross was followed by audio playback while the two shapes remained on the screen. For visual-only trials, the orthographic pseudoword was displayed centrally above the shapes for the same duration as audio trials. In audiovisual trials, both audio and text were presented simultaneously. Participants indicated their choice by pressing one of two keys corresponding to the left or right shape. No feedback was provided. Reaction time was recorded from stimulus onset to key press. Trials were presented in pseudorandom order and were preceded by 12 practice trials.

Awareness assessment. After completing the experimental trials, participants filled out a short questionnaire assessing their awareness of sound-shape correspondences. Questions included whether they noticed any systematic relationship between words and shapes, whether they used any strategy, and whether they had prior knowledge of the bouba/kiki effect. Participants were also asked to rate on a scale from 1 to 7 how confident they were in their choices overall.

Analysis plan. The primary dependent variable was the proportion of trials on which participants selected the congruent mapping (e.g., selecting the rounded shape for a bouba-like word). Secondary measures included reaction time and confidence ratings. We analyzed congruency proportions using repeated-measures ANOVA with modality as the within-subjects factor and paired t-tests for planned contrasts. Reaction times were analyzed after trimming outliers beyond three standard deviations from each participant's mean. We also examined the effect of awareness by splitting participants into aware and unaware groups based on their self-reported questionnaire responses and conducting mixed ANOVAs. Logistic mixed-effects models with random intercepts for subjects and items were used as complementary analyses to account for trial-by-trial variability.

Experiment 2

Participants. One hundred and twenty adult volunteers participated in Experiment 2. None had participated in Experiment 1. The sample included students and community members recruited through the same channels as Experiment 1. Demographic and inclusion criteria matched those of Experiment 1. Participants provided informed consent and received compensation.

Stimuli. The stimulus set for Experiment 2 included three orthogonal dimensions: shape (rounded vs angular), size (small vs large), and pseudoword type. We constructed four classes of pseudowords by systematically manipulating phonetic properties associated with bouba/kiki and magnitude symbolism. Bouba-like pseudowords used bilabial consonants and rounded vowels (e.g., "bouba" variants), whereas kiki-like pseudowords used velar/voiceless consonants and high front vowels (e.g., "kiki" variants). Magnitude-large pseudowords employed low back vowels and sonorant consonants (e.g., "baka," "boma"), while magnitude-small pseudowords used high front vowels and voiceless consonants (e.g., "piti," "kiki-like" small variants). Using these building blocks we created pseudowords that were congruent for both bouba and magnitude cues, incongruent (bouba-cue combined with small-sounding vowels), and neutral.

Objects on each trial were simple rendered silhouettes that varied independently in shape and size. Small objects were scaled to occupy approximately one quarter of the area of large objects. Rounded and angular shapes were drawn in the same manner as Experiment 1. This allowed trials to pit the bouba/kiki cue against a magnitude cue by presenting two objects that differed both in shape and size.

Design. The experiment used a within-subjects design with trial types categorized as congruent (shape and size both align with the pseudoword’s cues), bouba-dominant conflict (bouba cue favors one object while magnitude cue favors the other), magnitude-dominant conflict (magnitude cue favors one object while bouba cue favors the other), and neutral trials. Each participant completed 120 trials balanced across these trial types. On each trial, a pseudoword was presented auditorily and the participant selected which of two objects best matched the word. The side assignment and trial order were randomized.

Procedure. The procedure mirrored Experiment 1 for trial timing and response collection. Participants were instructed to select the object they thought best matched the spoken pseudoword. No mention was made of sound-symbolic principles prior to the task. After the main task participants completed the same awareness questionnaire and also answered specific questions asking whether they favored shape, size, or other cues when making their choices.

Analysis plan. The primary outcome was the proportion of trials on which participants selected the object consistent with bouba/kiki mapping versus the object consistent with magnitude mapping in conflict trials. We used chi-square tests and logistic regression to test whether selection frequencies differed from chance. Reaction times and confidence ratings were analyzed similarly to Experiment 1. We also examined whether self-reported awareness predicted preferential selection of one cue type over the other by conducting subgroup analyses and including awareness as a covariate in mixed-effects models.

Results

Experiment 1

Overall mapping performance. Participants demonstrated a robust bouba/kiki mapping across modalities. Averaged across all modality conditions, participants selected the congruent shape (e.g., rounded for bouba-like words) on 76.3% of trials (SD = 11.8). A one-sample t-test comparing this proportion to chance (.50) indicated a strong effect, t(79) = 21.51, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 2.41, indicating high reliability of the basic mapping in our sample.

Modality comparisons. We examined whether modality influenced the proportion of congruent mappings. Mean congruent selection rates were 77.9% (SD = 13.0) in the auditory-only condition, 74.8% (SD = 14.1) in the visual-only (orthographic) condition, 81.4% (SD = 12.2) in the congruent audiovisual condition, and 63.2% (SD = 15.8) in the incongruent audiovisual condition. A repeated-measures ANOVA with modality as the within-subject factor yielded a significant main effect of modality, F(3,237) = 36.42, p < .001, ηp2 = .32. Planned paired t-tests revealed that congruent audiovisual trials produced a higher congruent mapping rate than auditory-only trials, t(79) = 2.64, p = .01, Cohen’s d = 0.29, and higher than visual-only trials, t(79) = 3.72, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.42. Visual-only and auditory-only did not differ significantly from each other, t(79) = 1.21, p = .23, Cohen’s d = 0.14. Incongruent audiovisual trials produced significantly lower congruent selections than all other modalities, all ps < .001.

Interpretation of modality findings. The lack of a significant difference between auditory-only and visual-only conditions suggests that bouba/kiki mappings are similarly elicited by auditory and orthographic presentations of pseudowords. The modest advantage for congruent audiovisual presentation over single-modality presentations suggests an additive benefit when both cues align. Incongruent audiovisual presentations reduced congruent mappings, indicating that cross-modal conflict interferes with mapping consistency.

Reaction time results. Reaction times were analyzed after removing trials with responses faster than 200 ms or slower than 3000 ms and trimming outliers beyond three standard deviations for each participant. Mean reaction times were 893 ms (SD = 210) in the auditory-only condition, 912 ms (SD = 225) in the visual-only condition, 834 ms (SD = 190) in the congruent audiovisual condition, and 1045 ms (SD = 265) in the incongruent audiovisual condition. A repeated-measures ANOVA showed a significant effect of modality on reaction time, F(3,237) = 29.16, p < .001, ηp2 = .27. Pairwise comparisons indicated that congruent audiovisual trials were significantly faster than both auditory-only and visual-only trials (both ps < .01). Incongruent audiovisual trials were significantly slower than all other modalities (all ps < .001). There was no significant difference between auditory-only and visual-only reaction times, p = .42.

Awareness assessment. Based on questionnaire responses, 17 participants (21.3%) reported explicit awareness of a systematic relation between the words and shapes or said they used a strategy based on perceived roundedness or sharpness. We categorized these as the aware group, and the remaining 63 participants formed the unaware group. The aware group exhibited a slightly higher overall congruent mapping rate (81.6%, SD = 9.7) than the unaware group (74.6%, SD = 12.4). A mixed ANOVA with modality as a within-subject factor and awareness group as a between-subjects factor revealed a main effect of awareness, F(1,78) = 5.12, p = .026, ηp2 = .062, and a main effect of modality, F(3,234) = 36.14, p < .001. The modality × awareness interaction was not significant, F(3,234) = 1.48, p = .22. This pattern indicates that although explicit awareness modestly amplified congruent mapping rates, the relative modality effects—equivalence of auditory-only and visual-only and boost for congruent audiovisual presentation—held across both groups.

Logistic mixed-effects models. To model trial-level responses while accounting for item and subject variability, we fit logistic mixed-effects models predicting congruent choice from modality with random intercepts for subjects and items. The model confirmed that incongruent audiovisual trials significantly decreased the odds of a congruent choice relative to auditory-only trials (OR = 0.39, 95% CI [0.31, 0.49], p < .001). Congruent audiovisual trials increased the odds of congruent choice relative to auditory-only trials (OR = 1.22, 95% CI [1.05, 1.42], p = .01), whereas visual-only trials did not differ significantly from auditory-only trials (OR = 0.92, 95% CI [0.79, 1.07], p = .27). Including awareness as an interaction term did not materially alter these estimates.

Summary of Experiment 1. Overall, Experiment 1 demonstrated a robust bouba/kiki mapping elicited by both auditory and orthographic presentations of pseudowords. Audiovisual congruency produced a measurable facilitation in both accuracy and reaction time, whereas audiovisual incongruency produced interference. There was no evidence for a dominant modality between auditory and visual orthographic presentation, and explicit awareness modestly increased congruent responding but did not alter modality effects.

Experiment 2

Overall performance. Performance across all trial types in Experiment 2 revealed that participants tended to favor bouba/kiki-consistent choices when both cues were present, but the balance differed depending on trial type. Aggregated across participants and trials, the proportion of bouba-consistent choices was 61.9% (SD = 14.7) when cues were congruent and 67.5% (SD = 16.1) on conflict trials where bouba and magnitude cues were opposed. Overall performance was significantly above chance on congruent trials, t(119) = 15.14, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.38.

Conflict trials. The core analysis for Experiment 2 concerned conflict trials in which bouba/kiki cues favored one object and magnitude cues favored the other. In the bouba-dominant conflict condition, where the pseudoword contained strong bouba-like phonetic markers but magnitude markers favored the alternative object, participants chose the bouba-consistent object on 62.8% of such trials (SD = 17.2). In magnitude-dominant conflict trials, where magnitude cues were strong but bouba cues favored the other object, participants chose the magnitude-consistent object on only 38.1% of trials (SD = 16.9), meaning they chose the bouba-consistent object on 61.9% of those trials. Aggregating across conflict trials, participants selected the bouba-consistent option on 64.5% of trials, which was significantly above chance, χ2(1, N = 1440) = 202.84, p < .001. This indicates that when bouba/kiki and magnitude cues conflicted, participants were more likely to follow bouba/kiki cues.

Statistical modeling of conflict outcomes. A logistic mixed-effects model predicting selection of the bouba-consistent object on conflict trials included fixed effects for cue strength (strong vs weak for each cue) and random intercepts for subject and item. The model yielded a significant positive effect of bouba cue strength (β = 1.12, SE = 0.14, z = 8.00, p < .001), indicating higher odds of selecting the bouba-consistent object as bouba cues strengthened. By contrast, the magnitude cue strength had a negative coefficient when bouba cues were present (β = −0.42, SE = 0.13, z = −3.23, p = .001), indicating that stronger magnitude cues slightly pulled choices away from bouba-consistent selections but did not override them. The odds ratio for bouba cue strength was approximately 3.07, suggesting that strong bouba cues more than tripled the odds of a bouba-consistent choice relative to weak bouba cues.

Reaction times and confidence. Mean reaction times on conflict trials were significantly slower (M = 1098 ms, SD = 245) than on congruent non-conflict trials (M = 854 ms, SD = 196), t(119) = 14.62, p < .001, indicating that conflict induced decisional difficulty. Confidence ratings on a 1-to-7 scale were lower on conflict trials (M = 3.9, SD = 1.2) than on congruent trials (M = 5.4, SD = 1.1), t(119) = 17.34, p < .001, reflecting increased uncertainty when cues opposed each other.

Awareness and cue selection. Participants were divided based on post-task awareness responses into aware (N = 26, 21.7%) and unaware groups (N = 94, 78.3%). The aware group selected the bouba-consistent object on conflict trials at a slightly higher rate (69.1%, SD = 15.3) than the unaware group (63.1%, SD = 16.4). A mixed ANOVA with awareness as a between-subjects factor and trial type as within-subjects factor revealed a main effect of trial type, F(2,236) = 153.24, p < .001, and a main effect of awareness, F(1,118) = 6.18, p = .015, but no significant trial type × awareness interaction, F(2,236) = 1.51, p = .22. Thus, explicit awareness modestly increased reliance on bouba cues but did not fundamentally alter the dominance pattern.

Comparisons between bouba/kiki and magnitude effects. To quantify the relative influence of the two cue types, we computed logistic regression coefficients from models that included both cue predictors. Averaged across all trials, the standardized coefficient for bouba cues (β = 0.94, SE = 0.11, p < .001) was substantially larger than that for magnitude cues (β = 0.31, SE = 0.09, p = .002). The contrast between coefficients was significant by a Wald test, z = 3.85, p < .001, indicating that bouba/kiki cues had greater authority in guiding object-word matching decisions than magnitude-sound cues in our stimulus set and task.

Exploratory analyses. We conducted exploratory analyses examining whether particular phonetic features (e.g., consonant type vs vowel height) drove the dominance of bouba cues. When consonant type (bilabial vs velar/voiceless) and vowel quality (front vs back, high vs low) were entered simultaneously into the model, consonant type exhibited a larger coefficient (β = 0.78, SE = 0.12, p < .001) than vowel quality (β = 0.27, SE = 0.10, p = .006), suggesting that consonant-related articulatory features contributed more strongly to bouba/kiki choices than vowel-related magnitude cues in our stimuli.

Summary of Experiment 2. In conditions where bouba/kiki and magnitude cues conflicted, participants predominantly followed bouba/kiki cues. Reaction times and confidence ratings indicated increased difficulty during conflict trials. Explicit awareness modestly increased bouba-consistent responding but did not change the overall pattern of bouba dominance. Exploratory analyses implicated consonant features as primary drivers of the bouba/kiki effect in the present stimuli.

Discussion

The two experiments reported here investigated sensory modality contributions to the bouba/kiki effect and the relative authority of bouba/kiki symbolism versus magnitude-sound symbolism. Experiment 1 demonstrated that both auditory and orthographic presentations of pseudowords elicit robust bouba/kiki mappings, and that congruent audiovisual presentation offered an additive facilitation while incongruent audiovisual presentation produced interference. Experiment 2 showed that when bouba/kiki and magnitude cues were pitted against one another, participants tended to favor bouba/kiki-consistent choices, indicating that bouba/kiki symbolism exerted stronger influence than magnitude-based sound symbolism in our task. Explicit awareness of the mappings had modest effects, tending to increase the consistency of bouba/kiki choices but not altering the modalities’ relative contributions or the dominance of bouba cues over magnitude cues. Together the experiments suggest that the bouba/kiki effect is a robust cross-modal correspondence that can be elicited via multiple sensory channels and that, at least under typical experimental conditions, bouba/kiki mappings carry greater behavioral authority than magnitude-sound correspondences.

The modality findings of Experiment 1 clarify an important empirical point about the sensory bases of bouba/kiki mappings. The absence of a significant difference between auditory-only and visual-only (orthographic) presentations suggests that the effect is not strictly tied to the acoustic signal. Instead, the effect can be driven by the visual form of the word as rendered in orthography. This is consistent with the notion that multiple visual cues—such as the curvature of letter shapes, the spatial distribution of strokes, or the gestalt of orthographic patterns—can instantiate associations with rounded versus jagged shapes. Such visual contributions may operate via learned letter-shape correspondences, or via more general perceptual mapping processes that associate visual curvature in the word form with curvature in the referent.

At the same time, auditory presentation produced equivalent mapping strength, and congruent audiovisual presentation provided an additive benefit, which is indicative of multisensory integration. The faster reaction times and higher congruent mapping rates in congruent audiovisual trials indicate that when both auditory and visual cues align, they jointly facilitate categorical mapping. Conversely, audiovisual incongruence substantially decreased congruent mapping rates and slowed responses, showing that cross-modal conflict increases processing difficulty. This pattern fits well with general principles of multisensory perception whereby congruent signals are integrated to improve performance while incongruent signals produce interference and require resolution.

The finding that orthographic presentation elicits bouba/kiki mappings raises interesting theoretical possibilities. One is that some aspects of orthography evoke phonological or articulatory representations automatically, and these representations in turn activate the same mapping tendencies that auditory presentation does. Another possibility is that orthographic features themselves are mapped directly to visual shape features in a way that parallels the phonetic mapping, without necessitating full phonological mediation. It is likely that both routes operate to varying extents, and future research manipulating orthographic transparency and letter shape properties could help disentangle these pathways.

Experiment 2’s demonstration of bouba/kiki dominance over magnitude-sound symbolism suggests that not all sound-symbolic mappings are equally weighted by perceivers when forced to choose between competing cues. In our stimuli, bouba cues—especially consonant articulatory properties—had a stronger influence on object-word matching than magnitude cues derived largely from vowel properties. This asymmetry could reflect the relative salience of abrupt, percussive consonantal sounds in signaling contour-related properties, whereas vowel quality may be more gradational and thus less decisive in binary forced-choice tasks. Our exploratory analyses supported this interpretation by showing a larger contribution of consonant type relative to vowel quality.

An alternative explanation for bouba dominance is that shape-related mappings are more conceptually coherent with the visual task employed. In the experimental design, trials often contrasted a rounded shape with an angular one, which may have made shape cues more salient or diagnostic than size cues for mapping to a single spoken pseudoword. In real-world language use, magnitude cues may be more relevant in contexts where size is explicitly tracked or when meanings involve comparisons on a scalar dimension. Therefore, task framing and stimulus salience likely influence the observed hierarchy of cue authority.

The role of awareness in both experiments provides useful nuance. While participants who reported explicit awareness of the mapping tended to show slightly stronger bouba-consistent responding, the majority of participants were unaware and still exhibited robust mapping patterns. This indicates that bouba/kiki correspondences can operate implicitly, and that explicit knowledge is not a prerequisite for their expression. The modest effect of awareness suggests that awareness may amplify an underlying statistical or perceptual mapping but does not create it. This aligns with accounts that posit a mixture of implicit perceptual biases and explicit cognitive strategies contributing to sound-symbolic behavior.

Taken together, the present findings inform theoretical debates about the nature of sound symbolism. The fact that bouba/kiki mappings are elicited across sensory modalities and can dominate other types of sound-meaning associations supports the idea that certain cross-modal correspondences are fundamental to human perception. Such correspondences may arise from shared structural features of the sensory systems, from constraints of articulatory-acoustic relations, or from embodied sensorimotor experiences linking the production of certain sounds with the perception of particular shapes. The greater weight of bouba cues observed here suggests a privileged link between particular phonetic articulations—especially bilabial and sonorant consonants—and visual contour properties.

These data also carry implications for theories of language evolution and acquisition. If certain sound-shape mappings are inherently easier to extract or more robust across sensory presentations, they may play a role in early word learning by providing bootstrapping cues that link vocalizations to referents. The cross-modal robustness of bouba/kiki could therefore facilitate early form-meaning mappings when infants encounter synchronous auditory and visual information. Moreover, the dominance of bouba cues suggests that some iconic mappings might be preferentially used in languages to label shape-related properties, while magnitude-related mappings may be less stable or more context-dependent.

There are several limitations to the present work that should be acknowledged. First, stimulus construction choices necessarily constrain the generality of the results. Our pseudowords and objects were designed to contrast specific phonetic and visual features, and different choices—such as using a broader range of languages, consonantal inventories, or more ecologically valid object images—could yield different patterns. Second, the participant samples were drawn primarily from a single cultural and linguistic background, and cross-cultural replication would be valuable to determine whether bouba dominance generalizes across languages with different phonological systems. Third, the tasks were binary forced-choice paradigms that emphasize categorical decisions; continuous rating tasks or free-naming paradigms might reveal more nuanced interactions between cue types.

Future work could extend the present findings in several directions. One important avenue is to test the bouba versus magnitude cue competition in richer communicative contexts, such as interactive naming games or referential communication tasks, to determine how these cues are used in dynamic language use. Another direction is developmental: investigating how infants and children weigh bouba and magnitude cues during word learning would clarify their potential role in language acquisition. Additionally, neuroimaging and electrophysiological approaches could illuminate the neural correlates of cross-modal integration and conflict resolution observed in audiovisual incongruent trials, shedding light on the temporal dynamics and cortical loci of sound-symbolic mapping.

Studies that manipulate orthographic properties systematically—varying letter shapes, font curvature, or script type—would also be informative for disentangling whether orthographic elicitation of bouba/kiki relies on phonological mediation or on direct visual-visual mapping. Cross-linguistic studies that include languages with different phonotactic constraints and vowel inventories could test whether the relative authority of bouba versus magnitude cues is universal or language-specific. Finally, computational modeling of multisensory cue integration in sound symbolism could formalize how different sources of information are weighted and combined, and could generate quantitative predictions that future empirical work can test.

In conclusion, the present experiments contribute to a clearer picture of how sound-symbolic correspondences operate across sensory modalities and how different types of symbolism interact when they provide conflicting information. The bouba/kiki effect emerges as a robust, multimodal mapping that can be driven by auditory and orthographic cues and that often supersedes magnitude-based acoustic cues in guiding referential choices. This hierarchy and robustness have implications for models of perception, language development, and the use of iconicity in human communication. The findings encourage further exploration of the sensory, cognitive, and cultural factors that shape non-arbitrary form-meaning mappings and highlight the value of integrating multimodal experimental designs when probing the foundations of sound symbolism.
