Introduction

Research on the neural bases of emotion has consistently emphasized that the human brain exhibits hemispheric asymmetries in how affective information is processed. One strand of this literature has argued that the right hemisphere plays a dominant role in processing facial expressions and other nonverbal cues to emotion. This right hemisphere hypothesis has been supported by a variety of methods including lesion studies, neuroimaging, and behavioral paradigms. Another stream of research has focused on lateralized frontal activity in relation to affective style, proposing that approach-related positive affect is associated with greater left frontal activity whereas withdrawal-related negative affect is associated with greater right frontal activity. These two bodies of work together suggest that different aspects of emotional processing and affective disposition may show distinct patterns of hemispheric lateralization. Despite the progress made, a number of empirical inconsistencies remain in how dispositional variables such as optimism relate to lateralized processing of emotional stimuli. Some studies have reported relationships between optimistic temperament and indices of left-hemisphere activity, whereas others have shown robust right-hemisphere advantages for perceptual processing of facial expressions. The coexistence of these findings raises the possibility that trait-level affective orientations may modulate, attenuate, or even reverse expected hemispheric biases for specific tasks, such as the processing of chimeric faces. The chimeric face paradigm is a widely used behavioral assay for assessing hemispheric dominance for emotional face processing. In this task, participants view composite faces in which the left and right visual hemifields contain different affective expressions, and they report which half of the face appears more expressive. Because information presented to the left visual field projects primarily to the right hemisphere and information presented to the right visual field projects primarily to the left hemisphere, a consistent bias toward the left half of chimeric faces is taken as behavioral evidence for right hemisphere specialization. Early influential work using chimeric faces demonstrated a reliable left-side bias, interpreted as indicative of right-hemisphere dominance in processing emotional facial expressions. These findings have been replicated in several samples and have contributed to the standing view that the right hemisphere is critically involved in the perception of facial emotion. At the same time, physiological and electrophysiological studies have documented an association between optimistic disposition and relatively greater activity in left-sided cortical regions. This pattern has been interpreted within approaches that link dispositional optimism and approach-related affect with enhanced left frontal activation. Taken together, these literatures create a theoretical tension. On the one hand, perceptual tasks such as chimeric face judgments suggest that emotional expressions are predominantly processed in the right hemisphere. On the other hand, dispositional optimism appears to be associated with left-sided activation patterns, potentially reflecting an influence of trait-level affect on the lateralization of emotion-related processes. Reconciling these perspectives requires direct empirical investigation of whether optimism modulates hemispheric asymmetries in perceptual tasks involving facial expressions. A plausible hypothesis is that individuals with higher levels of optimism may show reduced right-hemisphere dominance when processing facial expressions. This could occur because optimistic individuals may engage left-lateralized approach systems more strongly, thereby offsetting or diminishing the typical right-hemisphere advantage observed in the chimeric face task. If true, optimism would be associated with a lower laterality index on the chimeric face task, reflecting a movement toward more bilateral processing or a relative shift toward left-sided processing. Conversely, it is also possible that optimism relates to hemispheric lateralization in a manner that does not alter perceptual asymmetries for facial expression. In that case, right-hemisphere biases observed in chimeric face tasks would remain stable across levels of optimism, and optimism would instead manifest in other physiological or cognitive domains. Prior empirical efforts have not reached consensus on which of these alternatives is more likely. For example, the classical study by Levy and colleagues reported that processing of facial expressions principally took place in the right hemisphere, as evidenced by robust left hemiface preferences in chimeric face judgments. In contrast, subsequent work by Hecht and others indicated that dispositional optimism was linked to physiological indices of left-hemisphere activity. The divergent results from these studies underscore the need for direct tests of the relationship between optimism and hemispheric lateralization in facial expression processing. The present study therefore set out to examine whether higher optimism levels are associated with reduced hemispheric lateralization during a chimeric face task. To address this question, we administered a standard chimeric face task to a reasonably large sample of healthy participants and measured dispositional optimism using a validated self-report instrument. We focused on a homogeneous participant group to reduce potential confounds arising from sex differences and handedness, both of which have been reported to influence lateralization patterns. Specifically, the present sample consisted of female, right-handed undergraduates and graduate students recruited at a single university. This choice of sample was made to increase the internal validity of the study and to facilitate comparisons with prior experiments that often used similar convenience samples. The primary prediction was twofold. First, consistent with the right hemisphere hypothesis and prior chimeric face findings, we expected a group-level bias in favor of the left half of the face, indicating right hemisphere dominance for processing facial expressions. Second, we predicted that optimism would be negatively correlated with the laterality score derived from the chimeric face task, such that higher optimism would correspond to less right-hemisphere dominance. These predictions were intended to test whether dispositional affective style measurably modulates perceptual asymmetries for emotional faces. To ensure robust measurement, the chimeric face stimuli were prepared in a standardized manner and counterbalanced across emotional categories and visual field presentations. The optimism measure was selected for its demonstrated psychometric properties and ease of administration in group testing. Handedness was confirmed with a standardized inventory to ensure lateralization effects were not confounded by mixed-handedness. Additional procedural controls included randomization of trial order and instructions designed to encourage intuitive rather than analytic judgments about the expressive intensity of each half-face. The analytic strategy involved testing the group-level laterality bias against a null hypothesis of no bias and computing the correlation between the optimism scores and individual laterality indices. We also planned exploratory analyses to examine whether any subcomponents of the optimism measure or demographic variables showed associations with lateralization. The present design thus provides a direct and focused test of the hypothesis that optimism attenuates right-hemisphere dominance for facial expression processing. If a negative relationship were found, it would provide behavioral evidence linking dispositional optimism to shifts in the hemispheric balance of emotional perception. Alternatively, a null result would indicate that optimism does not substantially influence perceptual hemispheric asymmetries for faces, at least within the constraints of the current task and sample. The study therefore has the potential to contribute to theoretical debates about the scope and limits of affect-related lateralization effects. By using a well-established perceptual paradigm and standard self-report measures, the study aims to bridge the gap between physiological findings related to optimism and behavioral measures of hemispheric specialization. The next sections describe the methods, present the results, and discuss the implications of the findings in the context of the larger literature on emotion and brain lateralization.

Methods

Participants were recruited from the University of Warwick student population. A total of 94 female participants took part in the study. All participants reported being right-handed. Participants were either undergraduate or graduate students. The sample size was chosen to provide reasonable power to detect a moderate relationship between optimism and laterality. All participants provided informed consent before taking part in the study. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee. Participants were compensated with course credit or a small monetary payment. Testing took place in a quiet laboratory room with controlled lighting. Stimuli for the chimeric face task were created from standardized facial expression images. Images included prototypical happy and neutral expressions that were combined to form chimeric composites. For each identity, one half of the face showed a happy expression and the other half showed a neutral expression. Left–right combinations were counterbalanced across trials so that each participant saw an equal number of faces in which the left half was expressive and faces in which the right half was expressive. Stimuli were presented on a computer monitor with participants seated approximately 60 cm from the screen. On each trial, a chimeric face appeared in the center of the screen. Participants were instructed to indicate which half of the face looked more emotionally expressive. Responses were made by pressing one of two labeled keys on a button box. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible and to rely on their immediate impressions rather than engaging in deliberate analytic comparison. Before the experimental trials, participants completed a short set of practice trials to become familiar with the task. The experimental session consisted of 60 chimeric face trials. Trial order was randomized for each participant to avoid systematic order effects. Each stimulus was displayed until the participant responded, and an intertrial interval of 1000 ms was used between trials. The main dependent variable from the chimeric face task was the laterality score. For each participant, the laterality score was computed as the proportion of trials on which the left half of the face was chosen minus the proportion of trials on which the right half of the face was chosen. This laterality score consequently ranged from -1 to +1, with positive values indicating a bias toward the left half of the face and, by inference, right hemisphere dominance. Negative values indicate a bias toward the right half and, by inference, left hemisphere dominance. A score of zero would indicate no bias in either direction. Dispositional optimism was measured using a widely used self-report questionnaire that assesses generalized positive expectancies. Participants completed this optimism questionnaire after finishing the chimeric face task to avoid any potential priming effects of self-reflection on the perceptual judgments. The optimism instrument yields a total score as well as subscale scores in some versions; for the present study the total score was used as the primary measure in analyses. The optimism questionnaire was scored according to standard procedures, with higher scores indicating greater dispositional optimism. In addition to the main measures, basic demographic information was collected, including age and academic level. Handedness was confirmed using a short handedness inventory administered at the start of the session to verify right-handedness. Data were entered into a spreadsheet and inspected for missing values and anomalies. No participant had incomplete data for the primary measures. Data distributions were visually inspected with histograms and boxplots to evaluate normality and the presence of outliers in both the laterality scores and optimism scores. Statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. The primary planned analyses were a one-sample t-test assessing whether the group mean laterality score differed from zero and a Pearson correlation assessing the relationship between laterality score and optimism score. The one-sample t-test was chosen because the hypothesis of a right-hemisphere bias predicts a mean laterality score greater than zero. The Pearson correlation was used to test the prediction that higher optimism would be associated with lower laterality scores. In addition to these primary tests, supplementary analyses were conducted. These included computing a 95% confidence interval for the mean laterality score and for the correlation coefficient, calculating effect sizes such as Cohen’s d for the one-sample test and r for the correlation, and conducting nonparametric equivalents when distributional assumptions warranted. We also inspected scatterplots for any nonlinear relationships between optimism and laterality scores. Given the possibility that mood on the testing day could influence perceptual judgments, we included a single-item measure of current mood as a covariate in exploratory regression analyses. The mood item asked participants to rate their current affective state on a simple scale immediately after the main task. This additional covariate was examined to assess whether state mood accounted for any variance in lateralization beyond dispositional optimism. All analyses were two-tailed unless otherwise specified. Alpha was set at 0.05 for statistical significance. However, because the directional prediction concerning the mean laterality score could be justified by prior literature, we also report one-tailed statistics for that specific test as supplementary information. Effect sizes and confidence intervals are emphasized alongside p-values to provide a fuller picture of the data. Finally, given that the focal hypothesis involved a null effect for the relation between optimism and lateralization if no association were found, exploratory Bayes factor analyses were performed to quantify evidence for the null versus the alternative hypothesis. This was intended to offer interpretive leverage beyond p-values in the case of non-significant results. Procedures for computing Bayes factors followed recommended defaults for tests of correlations within standard analysis packages.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The sample consisted of 94 right-handed female participants. The mean age of participants was 21.8 years with a standard deviation of 3.4 years. The mean optimism score in the sample was 16.4 with a standard deviation of 3.7. The distribution of optimism scores approximated normality on visual inspection, with no extreme skew or kurtosis. The laterality scores from the chimeric face task ranged from -0.40 to +0.47, indicating individual variability in bias direction and magnitude. The mean laterality score across participants was 0.01 with a standard deviation of 0.14. The histogram for laterality scores revealed a distribution clustered around zero with no strong shift toward positive values. The one-sample t-test examining whether the mean laterality score differed from zero yielded t(93) = 0.65, p = 0.515. The 95% confidence interval for the mean laterality score was [-0.02, 0.04]. The effect size for the group-level laterality bias, Cohen’s d, was 0.07, reflecting a very small and non-significant effect. When the one-tailed test that favored a positive mean laterality score was computed as supplementary information, the result remained non-significant at p = 0.258. Thus, contrary to the classical findings of a left hemiface bias, the current sample did not show a statistically reliable group-level right-hemisphere advantage on the chimeric face task. The primary test of the relationship between optimism and hemispheric lateralization was conducted using Pearson’s correlation. The correlation between optimism score and laterality score was r = -0.07, p = 0.494. The 95% confidence interval for this correlation ranged from -0.24 to 0.11, indicating that moderate-to-large associations could be ruled out with reasonable confidence. The corresponding coefficient of determination indicated that optimism accounted for less than one percent of the variance in laterality scores. Visual inspection of the scatterplot did not reveal any apparent nonlinear pattern or outliers exerting undue influence on the correlation. A robust correlation estimator produced a similar result, further indicating that the null association was not driven by a few extreme cases. Nonparametric Spearman correlation analysis also returned a non-significant association between optimism and laterality (rho = -0.06, p = 0.531). Given the concern that state mood might impact perceptual judgments, we conducted an exploratory partial correlation controlling for the single-item mood rating. The partial correlation between optimism and laterality controlling for mood was r = -0.05, p = 0.603, indicating that controlling for current mood did not change the null finding. We also conducted a simple linear regression predicting laterality score from optimism score. The regression coefficient for optimism was β = -0.03, t(92) = -0.69, p = 0.494. The model explained less than 1% of the variance in laterality score, and the overall regression was not significant. To provide additional inferential leverage, Bayes factors were computed for the correlation of interest. Using default priors appropriate for correlation tests, the Bayes factor in favor of the null hypothesis over the alternative (BF01) was approximately 3.2. This value indicates that the observed data were about three times more likely under the null hypothesis of no association than under the alternative of a true correlation. For the group-level one-sample test of laterality equal to zero, the Bayes factor slightly favored the null as well, with BF01 around 2.8. These Bayes factor values suggest moderate evidence for the absence of effects. To examine whether any subgroups showed different patterns, we conducted median splits on optimism scores and compared laterality means between higher and lower optimism groups. The high-optimism group had a mean laterality score of 0.02 (SD = 0.13) and the low-optimism group had a mean laterality score of 0.00 (SD = 0.15). An independent-samples t-test comparing these groups was non-significant, t(92) = 0.42, p = 0.677. Effect sizes for this comparison were small and negligible. We also inspected whether age or educational level correlated with laterality; age correlated r = 0.04 with laterality, p = 0.697, and educational level (undergraduate vs graduate) showed no significant differences in laterality scores. In addition, we evaluated the distribution of individual-level biases. Approximately 51% of participants selected the left half of the face more often than the right half, and 49% showed the reverse pattern, a nearly symmetrical split that aligns with the group mean near zero. This balance suggests that any individual biases were distributed around the null rather than clustering in the leftward direction as would be expected under a robust right-hemisphere dominance. Reaction time data from the chimeric face task were also examined. Mean response times did not significantly differ as a function of whether the left or right half was chosen, and response times did not correlate with optimism scores. These results suggest that decision speed did not confound the primary findings. Finally, exploratory analyses examining potential nonlinear or threshold effects of optimism on laterality were performed, including quadratic models and piecewise regressions. None of these models yielded significant associations or meaningful improvements in model fit. Taken together, the results show no significant group-level right-hemisphere bias in the chimeric face task and no evidence that dispositional optimism relates to hemispheric lateralization in this perceptual domain. The associated Bayes factors provided moderate evidence favoring the null over alternatives. The pattern of null findings was robust across parametric and nonparametric tests, median-split comparisons, and models controlling for mood and demographic covariates.

Discussion

The present study tested whether dispositional optimism is associated with hemispheric lateralization in the perceptual processing of facial expressions. Specifically, the research addressed two linked hypotheses. The first hypothesis was that the chimeric face task would reveal a group-level bias favoring the left half of faces, consistent with right-hemisphere dominance. The second hypothesis was that higher optimism would be associated with less right-hemisphere dominance, as reflected in a negative correlation between optimism scores and laterality indices. The empirical findings did not support either hypothesis. At the group level, the mean laterality score was close to zero and failed to differ significantly from no bias. In addition, there was no significant relationship between optimism and laterality, and Bayes factor analyses offered moderate support for the null. These results therefore do not support the view that dispositional optimism attenuates lateralized perceptual biases for facial emotion as measured by the chimeric face task. The absence of a group-level left-side bias is unexpected in light of classical chimeric face findings that documented a robust left hemiface advantage. Several methodological factors may help to account for this discrepancy. First, sample characteristics differed from those in some prior studies; the present sample consisted exclusively of female right-handed university students, which may differ in meaningful ways from mixed or community samples. Sex, age, and cultural factors can affect emotional expressiveness and perceptual strategies, and a homogeneous sample may reduce the variance that contributes to detecting group-level biases. Second, differences in stimulus construction or task instructions may have influenced the processing strategies employed by participants. Even subtle variations in how chimeric faces are created, how expressions are morphed, or how the task is framed can shift participants away from rapid, automatic perceptual judgments toward more analytic appraisal. If participants adopted analytic strategies, the sensitivity of the task to reveal hemispheric asymmetries could have been reduced. Third, modern testing contexts and participants’ prior exposure to psychological tasks could affect how individuals approach such experiments. Participants who have familiarity with psychological paradigms may adopt response styles that differ from those in earlier generations of studies. Fourth, the reliability of behavioral measures of lateralization can be modest, and individual variability in attentional focus, fatigue, or momentary mood states could have added noise to the laterality index, thereby reducing the detectability of a group-level effect. Importantly, the null relationship between optimism and lateralization must be interpreted in light of the moderate Bayes factors that favored the null. These Bayes factors suggest that the current data provide some evidence against the existence of a meaningful association of the magnitude that our study was powered to detect. However, moderate evidence is not definitive proof of the absence of any effect. It remains possible that optimism exerts subtle influences on neural processing that were not captured by the specific behavioral metric used here. For instance, dispositional optimism may be more directly linked to frontal cortical activation patterns than to perceptual asymmetries in occipitotemporal regions that subserve facial perception. If optimism primarily modulates higher-order regulatory or motivational circuits rather than low-level perceptual mechanisms, then behavioral lateralization measures like the chimeric face task may not reflect those dispositional influences. Another possibility is that optimism interacts with task demands; optimism may influence lateralization only under conditions that engage approach-related motivation or when faces are ambiguous or socially relevant in a way that pertains to the perceiver’s goals. The current task used relatively simple happy-versus-neutral chimeras and may not have tapped the socio-motivational contingencies under which optimism effects would emerge. Furthermore, optimism is a complex construct that may encompass multiple component processes, including expectancy, affect regulation, and cognitive reappraisal tendencies. It is plausible that only specific subcomponents or interacting traits are associated with lateralized processing rather than the broad optimism score used here. Future research could parse optimism into its constituent elements and examine more targeted hypotheses about which aspects of optimism, if any, relate to hemispheric bias. Methodological avenues for future work include employing neurophysiological or neuroimaging measures concurrently with behavioral tasks. Techniques such as EEG lateralized readiness potentials, frontal alpha asymmetry metrics, or functional MRI could provide more direct indices of hemispheric engagement and might reveal optimism-related patterns that behavioral choices fail to capture. For example, optimism may relate to left-sided frontal activation during emotion anticipation or approach, while perceptual asymmetries in posterior regions remain stable. Simultaneous measurement would clarify whether dispositional optimism influences early perceptual encoding, later evaluative stages, or predominantly frontal regulatory processes. Another promising direction is to manipulate task framing to explicitly engage approach-related motivations or to present faces in contexts that evoke evaluative concerns relevant to optimism. For instance, framing tasks in terms of social reward, positive outcomes, or future-oriented judgments may amplify the relevance of optimistic disposition and thereby expose lateralization differences that are otherwise latent. Developmental and longitudinal designs could also illuminate whether optimism and hemispheric lateralization co-develop or influence each other across time. Longitudinal tracking from adolescence into young adulthood could determine whether emerging optimism relates to shifts in lateralized processing as neural systems mature. The use of more diverse samples is another key consideration. The present sample was limited to right-handed female students, which constrains generalizability. Including males, mixed-handed individuals, and participants from a range of ages and cultural backgrounds would increase external validity and allow for tests of potential moderators. For example, sex differences in hemispheric specialization and emotional processing might moderate any optimism-laterality link, and cultural norms around emotional expression could shape perceptual biases. Analytical improvements could also be valuable. Increasing the number of trials in the chimeric face task could enhance the reliability of the laterality index. Employing hierarchical modeling to separate within-subject measurement error from between-subject variability could yield more precise estimates of individual differences in lateralization. Additionally, pre-registration of hypotheses and analysis plans would reduce the risk of analytic flexibility and improve the interpretability of null findings in this domain. The theoretical implications of the current null findings merit careful consideration. On one hand, the results challenge a simplistic mapping between dispositional optimism and a reduction in right-hemisphere dominance for facial expression perception. This suggests that the link between optimism and hemisphere-specific processing is more nuanced than previously assumed. On the other hand, the data do not falsify the broader view that optimism is associated with left-sided frontal activity in other contexts; rather, they indicate that such associations may not generalize across all aspects of emotion processing. Thus, the current findings contribute to refining theoretical models by highlighting that lateralization effects are task-dependent and that trait-level dispositions may differentially impact distinct neural components of emotion processing. In conclusion, the present study did not replicate classical right-hemisphere dominance in chimeric face processing and found no association between dispositional optimism and hemispheric lateralization on this task. The data provide moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis for the specific relations tested. Future research should incorporate multimodal measurement, diverse samples, and task variations that more directly engage motivational and regulatory processes linked to optimism. Such efforts will clarify whether optimism influences hemispheric lateralization under particular conditions or whether its primary neural correlates lie outside the perceptual systems measured by chimeric face paradigms. Overall, the current findings invite more nuanced theoretical accounts that distinguish among different neural substrates of affect, consider task specificity, and recognize the potential limitations of behavioral lateralization measures for capturing dispositional influences on brain function.
