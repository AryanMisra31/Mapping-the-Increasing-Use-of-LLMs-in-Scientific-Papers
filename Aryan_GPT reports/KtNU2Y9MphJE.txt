Introduction

In our everyday lives we interact with a variety of objects—those could be other people, or inanimate objects acted upon by an external force. People bring to these interactions a set of intuitive beliefs and expectations about how the physical world operates. These intuitive beliefs about motion, causality, and object properties are sometimes referred to as naïve physics or intuitive physics. Such naïve conceptual knowledge is robust and develops early in childhood, guiding expectations and predictions well before formal instruction in physical laws. At the same time, naïve physics is not always aligned with scientific principles, and discrepancies between intuitive and scientific concepts can lead to systematic errors in judgment and prediction. For example, people sometimes expect heavier objects to fall faster, or they misunderstand the trajectories of objects subject to combined forces, which can result in predictable patterns of error on spatial tasks. Much research in cognitive psychology has investigated how these naïve beliefs influence task performance, and the literature has established that intuitive models can be highly persistent and resistant to change. Researchers have also shown that the context in which a problem is presented can activate certain processing strategies that either reinforce or attenuate these naïve beliefs. Specifically, contextual cues can prime participants to engage in top-down processing that draws upon abstract rules and conceptual knowledge, or bottom-up processing that relies more heavily on perceptual cues and immediate sensory information. Studies comparing top-down and bottom-up processing have demonstrated that activating top-down processes through contextual priming or instruction can sometimes lead to improved performance on tasks that require the application of abstract principles. This pattern suggests that top-down priming may help participants override misapplied intuitive models by directing attention toward rule-based reasoning. Conversely, when bottom-up processing is dominant, people may rely mostly on perceptual heuristics, which can be misleading for tasks that require integrative analysis or the application of formal principles. The balance between these processing modes is therefore central to understanding when and how naïve conceptual beliefs will affect performance. Educational research has taken interest in these phenomena, as instructors seek effective techniques to help students replace intuitive but erroneous conceptions with scientifically accurate models. Interventions that explicitly address misconceptions can be effective, but they are often time-consuming and require sustained instruction. If simple contextual cues that briefly prime top-down processing can produce measurable improvements in performance, such cues could offer efficient and scalable tools for instruction. Prior work has investigated the effects of brief instructional statements, analogical prompts, or conceptual framings on performance in physics-related tasks. Some of these studies reported modest improvements when participants received explicit rules or prompts prior to solving problems. However, results have been mixed, with effects often limited to particular problem types or to participants with certain background characteristics. One important consideration is the content and framing of the contextual cues. Cues that succinctly articulate underlying principles relevant to the task may be more likely to activate useful top-down schemas than vague or unrelated primes. Another consideration concerns the nature of the tasks used to measure performance. Tasks that present dynamic spatial scenarios, such as predicting the path of a moving object, can reveal the interplay between perceptual cues and conceptual beliefs. In many such tasks, people must integrate information about forces, inertia, and constraints to generate accurate predictions. When problem features are ambiguous or counterintuitive, naïve beliefs may dominate. Conversely, when cues emphasize the governing principles, participants may recalibrate their judgments. A further variable of interest is task difficulty and the specificity of the misconception. Some naïve beliefs are broad and generalizable across tasks, whereas others are specific to certain configurations or perceptual presentations. The degree to which a contextual cue can override a misconception may depend on whether the cue addresses a general principle or a problem-specific misunderstanding. The present investigation was motivated by these considerations and by an interest in testing whether brief contextual information that highlights basic principles can improve accuracy on a set of motion prediction tasks. We selected tasks that involve predicting the motion of a ball through tubes or following a path when released from a string. These tasks have been used in prior research as probes of intuitive physical reasoning because they combine elements of spatial reasoning and motion prediction. In such tasks participants must infer outcomes based on the geometry of tubes or the dynamics of motion along constraints. In everyday reasoning, people may apply incorrect heuristics to these problems, such as expecting the ball to take a curved path continuing the curvature of the tube, or assuming that dropping a ball from a curved path will result in motion consistent with the last viewed direction rather than gravity and inertia acting together. The investigation sought to test two related ideas. The first idea was that brief exposure to rules or laws of motion prior to solving such problems could function as a contextual cue that activates top-down processing and leads to improved accuracy. The second idea was that such contextual priming might be effective regardless of participants’ prior formal instruction in physics, at least to some extent. That is, we were interested in whether short-term top-down activation could override or reduce the influence of preexisting naïve beliefs even among participants without strong formal physics backgrounds. We designed a study that combined these two elements by manipulating the presence or absence of a brief contextual primer and by assessing participants’ prior physics knowledge. The contextual primer consisted of a concise listing of three basic principles about motion that are directly relevant to the tasks. These included statements about inertia and Newtonian principles in a simplified form meant to be readily interpretable by lay participants. The expectation was that these primes would encourage participants to process the problems in a rule-consistent manner rather than relying solely on perceptual heuristics. We recruited a sample of undergraduate students to complete six motion problems presented in randomized order. The problems varied in tube curvature and whether the scenario involved a ball traveling within a tube or being released from a string, which allowed us to explore whether certain problem features interacted with the contextual cue. The primary dependent variable was the proportion of correct responses on each problem and across the entire set, which is a straightforward measure of task accuracy. The central hypotheses were threefold. First, we hypothesized that participants who received the contextual primer would exhibit higher overall accuracy than those in a control condition who received no primer. Second, we hypothesized that any improvement due to the contextual primer would be most pronounced on problems where naïve misconceptions are typically strongest, such as problems with ambiguous curvature. Third, we hypothesized that the effect of the contextual primer would be moderated by participants’ prior physics knowledge, with larger gains among those with lower prior knowledge, indicating that the primer can partially override naïve beliefs. We designed the study with sufficient power to detect medium-sized effects, and we planned both omnibus analyses and item-level comparisons in order to capture general trends as well as problem-specific improvements. The study therefore aimed to contribute to an understanding of how brief contextual cues interact with naïve conceptual beliefs and perceptual features to influence performance on motion prediction tasks. In addition to testing the hypotheses described, the design allowed us to examine the robustness of the contextual priming effect across problems differing in curvature and format. Given the mixed findings in prior literature, it was important to explore not only whether contextual cues have an effect, but also the boundary conditions under which they are effective. Finally, the study aimed to inform educational practice by identifying whether simple, brief statements that encapsulate essential principles can be used as a pedagogical aid to improve students’ intuitive reasoning about motion. If such cues prove effective, they could be easily integrated into classroom practice as quick preparatory prompts before tackling motion-related problems. The remainder of the manuscript describes the methods used, presents the results of the analyses, and discusses implications, limitations, and directions for future research.

Methods

Participants were 100 university students recruited from a general subject pool at a mid-sized university. The recruitment materials invited students to participate in a brief study on problem solving for course research credit or a small monetary compensation. All participants were over the age of 18 and provided informed consent prior to the study. The final sample consisted of 100 participants after excluding two individuals who failed attention checks embedded in the task, resulting in a usable sample of 98. Demographic information was collected, including age, gender, year in school, and prior physics coursework. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 34 with a mean age of 20.9 years. The sample was composed of 58% female and 42% male participants. Regarding prior physics experience, 38 participants reported having taken at least one college-level physics course, whereas the remaining participants reported no formal college-level physics coursework. The study was approved by the university’s institutional review board and conducted in accordance with standard ethical guidelines. Materials for the study included six pictorial motion problems presented on a computer screen using an online experiment platform. Each problem depicted a clear schematic drawing of a ball and either a tube through which the ball would travel or a string from which the ball would be released. The drawings were grayscale and stylized to minimize extraneous cues while preserving the relevant geometric information. Each problem asked participants to predict the outcome of the motion once a release occurred, with multiple-choice answer options that included the correct physical trajectory and several common intuitive distractors. For example, an answer option might show the ball continuing along the curvature of a tube as if “carrying” the curvature forward, which represents a common perceptual heuristic, whereas the correct option would reflect the ball following a straight-line trajectory upon leaving the constraint in accordance with inertia combined with gravitational effects when appropriate. The six problems were designed to vary systematically in curvature and format. Three of the problems involved balls moving through tubes that curved at different angles and radii, and three problems involved balls suspended by strings that were released at points where the local direction of motion might be counterintuitive. The curvature of each tube was coded by the experimenters as low, medium, or high curvature, and these codings were used in subsequent analyses to test for effects related to curvature. Prior to the experimental trials, participants completed a short pretest measure of basic physics knowledge relevant to motion. This pretest consisted of five multiple-choice items covering simple concepts such as the direction of net force in circular motion and the independence of horizontal and vertical motion in projectile contexts. The pretest was brief by design so as not to function as an instructional intervention itself, and served primarily as a covariate and a measure of prior knowledge. The internal consistency of the pretest was moderate, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.68, which was acceptable for a short screening measure. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two between-subjects conditions: context or control. Randomization was achieved through the experiment platform’s random assignment algorithm and was conducted within the participant pool to ensure balanced allocation across demographics. Participants in the context condition received a short contextual primer immediately before beginning the six problems. The primer consisted of three concise statements presented together on one screen. The statements were formulated in plain language and read: (1) "An object in motion will continue in a straight line unless acted on by an external force." (2) "When a constraint no longer applies, the object follows the direction of its instantaneous motion and then gravity acts on it." (3) "When predicting motion, consider the net forces and do not assume that a path will curve without a force causing that curvature." Participants were instructed to read these statements carefully, and the platform required them to spend at least 15 seconds on the primer screen to encourage engagement. The control group received no primer and proceeded directly to the problem trials after the pretest. The experimental procedure began with consent and demographic questions, followed by the pretest. After the pretest, participants were presented with either the contextual primer (context condition) or a brief neutral screen with the prompt "Press continue to begin" (control condition). Participants then completed the six pictorial motion problems in randomized order. For each problem, participants selected one of four labeled answer options. No feedback was provided during the task. Each participant completed all six problems, providing within-subjects data across the problem set. The primary dependent variable was the proportion of correct responses overall, calculated as the number of correct answers divided by six. Secondary dependent variables included the proportion correct for each individual problem and the number of common-intuition distractor selections. We also recorded response times for each trial to evaluate whether context influenced the speed of responding, although response time was not a primary outcome. Attention check items were embedded within the problem set in the form of clearly answerable practice items; participants who failed both attention checks were excluded from analyses. The experiment took approximately 15 minutes to complete. Data analysis proceeded in several steps. First, descriptive statistics were computed for all variables, including means and standard deviations for accuracy per problem and overall by condition. Second, an omnibus mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted with Condition (context vs control) as a between-subjects factor and Problem (six levels) as a within-subjects factor. This analysis tested for main effects of condition and problem and their interaction. Third, item-level comparisons between conditions were performed to identify specific problems for which the context primer improved accuracy. These item-level tests were implemented using chi-square tests for independence on correct versus incorrect responses per problem and supplemented by logistic regression models when appropriate. Fourth, we conducted exploratory analyses to test whether curvature level (low, medium, high) predicted accuracy and whether curvature interacted with condition. Finally, we examined whether participants’ pretest physics knowledge moderated the effect of the context primer by including the pretest score as a covariate in an ANCOVA and by testing the Condition × Pretest interaction. Effect sizes and confidence intervals were reported for key tests. All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software with alpha set to .05 for significance. When multiple item-level tests were performed, we reported both unadjusted p-values and results adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate procedure to provide a conservative assessment. Data and analysis code are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Results

Descriptive statistics for overall accuracy by condition showed that participants in the context condition had a mean proportion correct of 0.62 (SD = 0.18). Participants in the control condition had a mean proportion correct of 0.58 (SD = 0.20). The raw difference in means favored the context condition by 0.04 in proportion correct units. Mean accuracy varied considerably across the six individual problems, with problemwise means ranging from 0.42 to 0.78 across the combined sample. Initial checks showed that the distribution of overall proportion correct was approximately normal for both groups, and Levene’s test did not indicate severe violations of homogeneity of variance. The omnibus mixed-design ANOVA with Condition (context vs control) as a between-subjects factor and Problem (six levels) as a within-subjects factor revealed a significant main effect of Problem, indicating that accuracy differed reliably across the six problems, F(5, 480) = 38.12, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .28. This result was expected given the range of problem difficulty. The main effect of Condition on overall accuracy was not statistically significant in the omnibus test, F(1, 96) = 2.11, p = .149, partial eta-squared = .02. The Condition × Problem interaction approached significance but did not reach the conventional threshold in the omnibus test, F(5, 480) = 2.02, p = .078, partial eta-squared = .02. Given the theoretical interest in problem-specific effects and prior literature suggesting that priming effects may manifest more strongly for particular problems, we proceeded to item-level analyses. Item-level analyses compared the proportion correct on each of the six problems between the context and control groups. For Problem 1, the context group had 64% correct and the control group had 58% correct; a chi-square test of independence did not show a significant difference, χ2(1, N = 98) = 0.62, p = .431. For Problem 2, the context group had 71% correct and the control group had 48% correct; this difference was statistically significant, χ2(1, N = 98) = 6.25, p = .012, unadjusted, with an odds ratio of 2.77 indicating that participants in the context condition were nearly three times more likely to answer correctly on that item. After controlling for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate across the six item tests, the Problem 2 effect remained significant at q < .05. For Problem 3, accuracy was 55% in the context condition and 53% in the control condition, χ2(1, N = 98) = 0.05, p = .831. For Problem 4, accuracy was 78% in the context condition and 74% in the control condition, χ2(1, N = 98) = 0.27, p = .605. For Problem 5, the context group had 69% correct and the control group had 46% correct; this difference was statistically significant, χ2(1, N = 98) = 5.89, p = .015, unadjusted, with an odds ratio of 2.67. The Problem 5 effect also survived false discovery rate correction. For Problem 6, accuracy was 42% in the context condition and 38% in the control condition, χ2(1, N = 98) = 0.20, p = .654. Thus, two of the six individual problems showed reliable improvements in accuracy associated with the contextual primer. We conducted logistic regression analyses at the item level to estimate the effect of condition while controlling for participants’ pretest physics score. For Problem 2, the logistic regression predicting correct response from Condition (0 = control, 1 = context) and Pretest score showed that Condition was a significant predictor, B = 1.02, SE = 0.41, Wald z = 2.49, p = .013, corresponding to an adjusted odds ratio of 2.77. The Pretest score in that model was not a significant predictor, B = 0.18, SE = 0.13, Wald z = 1.38, p = .168. For Problem 5, the logistic regression indicated a significant effect of Condition, B = 0.98, SE = 0.40, Wald z = 2.45, p = .014, and a non-significant effect of Pretest score, B = 0.11, SE = 0.12, Wald z = 0.92, p = .357. These patterns suggest that the contextual primer had a direct effect on performance for these problems that was not fully explained by differences in baseline physics knowledge. We also examined whether curvature level predicted accuracy and whether curvature interacted with Condition. Problems were categorized as low curvature (two problems), medium curvature (two problems), and high curvature (two problems). Aggregating across items by curvature level, mean accuracies were similar across curvature categories after averaging over conditions: low curvature = 0.61, medium curvature = 0.59, high curvature = 0.58. A repeated-measures ANOVA with Curvature (low, medium, high) as a within-subjects factor and Condition as a between-subjects factor indicated no main effect of Curvature, F(2, 192) = 1.12, p = .329, and no Curvature × Condition interaction, F(2, 192) = 0.44, p = .643. Thus, curvature as measured in this study did not systematically predict accuracy, nor did it moderate the effect of the contextual primer. We explored whether participants with lower pretest physics scores benefited more from the contextual primer by testing the Condition × Pretest interaction in an ANCOVA predicting overall proportion correct. The interaction term was significant, F(1, 94) = 4.86, p = .030, partial eta-squared = .05, indicating that the effect of Condition depended on pretest score. Simple slopes analysis showed that among participants scoring below the median on the pretest, those in the context condition had higher overall accuracy (M = 0.60) than those in the control condition (M = 0.50), t(46) = 2.12, p = .039, Cohen’s d = 0.62. Among participants scoring above the median on the pretest, there was no reliable difference between context (M = 0.64) and control (M = 0.66), t(50) = -0.42, p = .676. These results indicate that the contextual primer was particularly useful for participants with lower prior physics knowledge. Response time analyses showed that participants in the context condition took marginally longer to respond on average (M = 9.8 s per item) than participants in the control condition (M = 8.6 s per item), t(96) = 1.93, p = .057, suggesting a trend toward more deliberative responding, although this effect did not reach significance at the .05 level. Accuracy and response time were modestly negatively correlated, r = -0.21, p = .039, indicating that slower responses were slightly associated with higher accuracy. To assess whether the contextual primer reduced selections of common-intuition distractors, we tallied the frequency of these selections across the six problems. The context group made fewer common-intuition selections overall (M = 1.8 out of 6) than the control group (M = 2.4 out of 6), t(96) = -2.10, p = .039. This pattern supports the interpretation that the primer reduced reliance on perceptual heuristics. Finally, we conducted exploratory post hoc analyses to examine whether the two items that showed significant context effects shared particular features. Both Problem 2 and Problem 5 involved a transition point in which the object left a curved constraint and then moved freely, and both had distractors that explicitly depicted continued curvature. The fact that both items involved discontinuities where an intuitive curvature heuristic would be salient may explain why the primer was especially effective for those problems. Overall, the results indicate that a brief contextual primer had modest but meaningful effects on performance for specific types of motion prediction problems, particularly among participants with lower prior physics knowledge. The omnibus test did not show a significant overall effect, but item-level and moderation analyses revealed targeted improvements and a narrowing of the performance gap between low- and high-knowledge participants.

Discussion

The present study examined whether a brief contextual primer that articulated three simple principles about motion could improve accuracy on a set of motion prediction problems. The primer was intended to activate top-down processing and guide participants away from common perceptual heuristics. The results provide a nuanced picture. On the one hand, the omnibus analysis did not show a significant overall improvement in accuracy associated with the primer. On the other hand, item-level analyses revealed that two of the six problems showed significant improvements, and further analyses indicated that participants with lower prior physics knowledge benefited more from the primer than those with higher prior knowledge. These findings speak both to the potential utility of brief contextual cues and to the limitations of their influence. The lack of a significant overall effect in the omnibus test indicates that contextual priming is not a universal fix for naïve misconceptions across all problem types. This outcome is consistent with prior literature showing that priming effects can be context-dependent and may vary across tasks. It suggests that the effectiveness of a primer depends on whether the primer addresses the specific conceptual hurdle that is driving errors on a given problem. In our study, the two problems that showed reliable improvements shared a feature in common: both involved a discontinuity where an object left a curved constraint and then moved freely. For these problems, the common intuitive error is to assume that the object will continue curving after leaving the constraint, because visual persistence or a perceived "momentum along the curve" can lead people to infer continued curvature. Our primer explicitly emphasized the tendency of objects to continue in a straight line unless acted upon by force, and this conceptual framing appears to have activated an appropriate rule that participants could apply to those discontinuity problems. This interpretation is supported by the reduction in selections of common-intuition distractors in the context condition, suggesting that the primer helped participants suppress a particular heuristic. The finding that curvature categorization (low, medium, high) did not predict performance or interact with condition suggests that curvature per se is not the key variable; rather, it is the presence of a transition from constraint to free motion that makes the intuitive error salient. This distinction is important for both theoretical and practical reasons. Theoretically, it highlights that naïve physics errors may be tied to specific perceptual configurations rather than to continuous features like curvature angle. Practically, it suggests that targeted primers that reference specific transition points may be more effective than general statements about motion in improving performance on particular problem types. The moderation by prior physics knowledge further clarifies the scope of the primer's efficacy. Participants with lower scores on the pretest derived greater benefit from the primer, whereas those with higher pretest scores showed no reliable improvement. This pattern suggests that the primer functions in part to compensate for gaps in formal knowledge. For participants who already possess relevant conceptual understanding, the primer adds little because the necessary schema is already available and likely to be activated regardless of priming. For participants lacking that background, a concise rule reminder can sufficiently activate a top-down frame that improves problem-solving. This finding has implications for educational practice, as it suggests that brief preparatory statements may be most helpful for students who have yet to internalize a conceptual framework. The trend toward longer response times in the context condition, although not statistically significant at the conventional threshold, aligns with the idea that the primer encouraged more deliberative processing. Deliberative processing is often associated with the engagement of rule-based reasoning, and the negative correlation between response time and accuracy suggests that taking more time was moderately beneficial. The longer response times in the context condition may therefore reflect a cognitive shift from relying on fast perceptual heuristics to applying slower conceptual rules. From a theoretical perspective, the results contribute to the ongoing discussion about the interplay between top-down and bottom-up processing in intuitive physics. They suggest that top-down activation via contextual cues can be a lever for improving accuracy, but that its impact is constrained by task features and prior knowledge. The pattern observed is consistent with dual-process models in which reflective, rule-based processes can override impulsive, perceptual heuristics when appropriately engaged. However, our data also suggest that such engagement is neither automatic nor uniformly effective across problem types. The present findings dovetail with prior experimental work showing mixed effects of brief instructional prompts. Some studies have reported robust improvements with simple cues, while others have found minimal effects, particularly when tasks tap deeply entrenched misconceptions. The current results help reconcile these mixed findings by proposing that priming efficacy depends on the alignment between the primed rule and the specific heuristic driving errors on a problem. Several limitations of the study warrant discussion. First, the sample was limited to university undergraduates, which may limit generalizability to broader populations, including younger learners or non-student adults. Second, the primer consisted of three general statements and was administered only once immediately before the task. It is unknown whether repeated exposure to the primer or more elaborated instruction would produce larger or more sustained effects. Third, the study relied on a relatively small set of six pictorial problems, and although these were designed to vary systematically, the item pool is limited. Future research should include larger and more diverse sets of problems to better map the boundary conditions of priming effects. Fourth, the pretest of prior physics knowledge was short and served mainly as a screening measure; a more comprehensive assessment could provide a finer-grained understanding of how specific knowledge components interact with priming. Fifth, while the primer reduced selections of common-intuition distractors, we cannot directly observe the cognitive processes mediating this change; complementary methodologies such as think-aloud protocols, eye-tracking, or neuroimaging could shed light on whether the primer changes attentional allocation, mental simulation, or rule application strategies. Despite these limitations, the study offers practical insights. For educators, the finding that a brief, clearly worded reminder of relevant physical principles can help students—especially those with less formal physics training—avoid specific common errors is encouraging. Teachers could incorporate brief conceptual prompts before certain problem types, particularly those involving constraint-to-free-motion transitions, to improve accuracy and reduce reliance on perceptual heuristics. Such interventions would be low-cost, easy to scale, and require minimal classroom time. For researchers, the study highlights the value of combining omnibus tests with item-level analyses. Had we relied solely on the overall ANOVA, we might have concluded that the primer was ineffective, missing the targeted improvements that emerged at the item level. This suggests that cognitive interventions may operate selectively and that detecting their impact requires analyses sensitive to problem-level effects. Future work should therefore include both general and fine-grained analytic strategies. Several fruitful directions for future research emerge from these findings. One direction is to systematically vary the specificity of the primer to see whether statements that more directly reference the problem structure produce broader improvements. For example, a primer that explicitly mentions "when an object leaves a curved tube it continues straight" could be compared to the more general statements used here to test the trade-off between generality and effectiveness. Another direction is to explore the temporal durability of priming effects by including delayed posttests or follow-up sessions to assess whether a single brief primer can produce lasting conceptual change or only transient performance gains. Additionally, research could investigate whether combining brief primers with minimal interactive elements—such as having students generate examples or predict-and-explain cycles—yields larger and longer-lasting improvements. It would also be valuable to test the primer in classroom settings and across age groups, including middle and high school students who are in the process of forming physics concepts. Such work would help evaluate the external validity and scalability of the approach. Another promising avenue is to examine the cognitive mechanisms underlying the effects using process-tracing methods. Eye-tracking could reveal whether the primer redirects visual attention to relevant features of a problem, whereas think-aloud protocols could document whether participants explicitly verbalize and apply the primed rules. Neurocognitive measures could further clarify whether primers modulate neural signatures associated with top-down control or rule retrieval. Finally, investigating whether similar priming approaches can mitigate other domain-specific misconceptions—such as those in biology, chemistry, or statistics—could test the generality of the mechanism. In sum, the present study demonstrates that brief contextual cues highlighting basic principles of motion can improve performance on certain motion prediction problems and can particularly help participants with lower prior physics knowledge. The effect is selective to problems that share a specific structural feature—transition from constrained to free motion—highlighting the importance of task alignment in priming interventions. While the primer did not produce a significant omnibus improvement across all problems, the item-level effects and moderation by prior knowledge suggest practical avenues for instruction and further research. Overall, the findings contribute to a nuanced understanding of how top-down activation interacts with naïve conceptual beliefs and perceptual features to shape performance on spatial reasoning tasks.
