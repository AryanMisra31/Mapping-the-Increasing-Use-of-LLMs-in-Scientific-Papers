Introduction

Visual attention is a fundamental cognitive process that allows organisms to select and prioritize behaviorally relevant information from the environment. In humans, faces are among the most salient visual stimuli, carrying rich information about identity, intent, and affective state. The efficient detection of emotionally negative faces, such as angry or fearful expressions, has been reported widely and is often framed as an adaptive response to potential threat. Many visual search studies have demonstrated that observers detect negative facial expressions more rapidly than positive expressions, a phenomenon frequently referred to as the negative face search advantage or angry-face advantage. This effect has been interpreted as reflecting a negativity bias in attentional systems, whereby attention is preferentially allocated to potential threats in order to facilitate rapid defensive or avoidance behaviors.

The negative face search advantage has been demonstrated across a range of paradigms and stimulus sets, with angry or fearful faces sometimes "popping out" among neutral or happy distractors. These findings are consistent with theoretical perspectives that emphasize the selective tuning of perceptual and attentional systems to threat-relevant signals. Neurobiological accounts have highlighted rapid subcortical pathways and heightened amygdala responsiveness to threat that could facilitate early attentional capture by negative facial expressions. Behavioral work has suggested that both bottom-up salience and top-down appraisal processes contribute to the faster detection of negative faces, although the specific mechanisms remain a topic of active investigation.

Despite the apparent robustness of the negative face search advantage in some contexts, empirical results are not unequivocal. Several studies have reported boundary conditions under which the advantage is diminished or absent. Variations in task parameters, such as stimulus set homogeneity, display duration, set size, and response demands, have been shown to influence whether negative faces enjoy a processing advantage. For example, when stimuli are highly familiar or when participants adopt efficient search strategies, the differential advantage for negative faces can be reduced. Moreover, individual differences in affective traits, such as trait anxiety, have been implicated in modulating the extent of threat-related attentional biases, with higher anxiety often associated with a stronger negativity advantage.

Mood state is another factor that has been investigated as a potential moderator of the negative face search advantage. Transient increases in negative affect or induced anxiety have been reported to enhance the salience of threat signals, whereas positive mood states have sometimes been associated with broader attentional scope and diminished threat-focused processing. Experimental mood induction studies have produced mixed outcomes: some have found that negative mood enhances the speed of detecting negative faces, while others have failed to observe mood-dependent changes in visual search efficiency. The complexity of these findings suggests that mood may interact with task specifics and participant characteristics in determining attentional biases.

Practice effects represent an additional, and comparatively understudied, source of variation in visual search performance with facial stimuli. Repeated exposure to a search task commonly leads to faster response times and improved accuracy as observers develop more efficient search strategies, motor responses become automatized, and perceptual representations are tuned to task-relevant features. In non-face visual search tasks, practice-related speedups often follow a characteristic learning curve, with the largest gains occurring in early practice and asymptotic performance reached after sufficient repetitions. In face-based search tasks, practice could differentially benefit the detection of particular emotional expressions if some expressions are more amenable to feature-based learning or strategic search improvements.

The potential influence of practice on the negative face search advantage has important implications for both experimental design and theoretical interpretation. If repeated task exposure reduces or eliminates the negativity advantage, then findings from single-session studies may not generalize to contexts where observers gain familiarity with the stimuli or task. Conversely, if the negativity advantage remains robust in the face of practice, this would support proposals that threat-related attentional biases are deeply entrenched and resistant to simple perceptual learning. Despite these ramifications, practice effects have received limited attention in the specific context of emotional face visual search, leaving an empirical gap that warrants systematic investigation.

Furthermore, most studies that examine mood effects on attention use single-session mood inductions or correlational assessments, while few have longitudinal designs that track mood and performance across repeated testing. Without tracking mood across sessions, it is difficult to determine whether observed changes in performance reflect practice, shifts in mood, or an interaction between the two. For instance, participants might become more relaxed or fatigued across sessions, and such mood fluctuations could mediate changes in the detection of negative faces. Therefore, a study that measures mood before and after repeated search sessions is necessary to disambiguate practice-related improvements from mood-driven changes in attentional bias.

To date, the literature contains instances of both replication of the negative face search advantage and of null or reversed effects. Some reports have even found a positive face advantage under certain conditions, such as when positive faces are more distinctive in the stimulus set or when participants adopt a specific response strategy. These divergent findings raise questions about the boundary conditions and mechanisms that determine which emotional expressions are detected most rapidly. In particular, it remains unclear whether the negativity advantage is primarily a function of inherent threat salience, or whether it can be overridden by factors such as stimulus familiarity, set composition, or strategic shifts that accompany repeated task exposure.

In addition to the theoretical considerations regarding attentional biases, understanding practice effects in face search tasks has practical implications. Many applied contexts, such as security screening or clinical assessment, rely on rapid detection of facial cues across prolonged periods or repeated encounters. If practice diminishes attentional bias toward negative expressions, the operational effectiveness of personnel relying on spontaneous threat detection may change with experience. Similarly, in clinical settings, repeated testing could alter the measurement of attentional biases used as diagnostic or prognostic indicators in affective disorders.

Given the mixed findings in the literature and the relative paucity of studies explicitly examining practice effects in emotional face search, the present investigation sought to address two interrelated questions. First, does repeated practice across multiple weekly sessions alter the magnitude or direction of the negative face search advantage? Second, are any observed changes in search performance attributable to practice rather than concurrent changes in mood? To answer these questions, the study employed a within-subjects repeated-measures design in which participants completed the same computer-based visual search task on three consecutive weekly sessions. Mood was assessed immediately before and after each session using a standardized affect questionnaire to detect transient mood changes that could influence attention.

The task involved searches for either a single positive expression target among negative distractors or a negative expression target among positive distractors, with set size manipulated to index search efficiency. This design allowed for direct comparisons of response times and accuracy across valence conditions and across sessions, and for examination of how practice effects might differentially impact the detection of positive and negative faces. The inclusion of pre- and post-session mood assessments further enabled the isolation of practice effects from mood fluctuations.

Based on prior findings regarding practice and perceptual learning, it was anticipated that overall reaction times would decrease across sessions as participants became more familiar with the task and stimuli. Regarding valence-specific effects, two alternative hypotheses were considered. The first hypothesis posited that the negative face search advantage would persist across sessions, indicating robustness to practice. The second hypothesis predicted that practice would attenuate or eliminate the negativity advantage, either by disproportionately accelerating detection of positive faces or by enabling more efficient strategies that reduce valence-driven differences. Additionally, if mood shifts contributed to any observed changes, then pre- to post-session mood changes should correlate with the magnitude of change in search performance.

The present study therefore aimed to provide a clearer understanding of how practice and mood interact with emotional valence in visual search for facial expressions. By tracking performance across three weekly sessions and measuring mood in close temporal proximity to the task, the study design made it possible to attribute changes in attentional biases to practice, mood, or their interaction. The following sections describe the methods used, report the statistical analyses of reaction time, accuracy, and mood, and discuss the implications of the findings for theories of affective attention and perceptual learning.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and through campus advertisements. Twenty individuals participated in the study, with ages ranging from 18 to 32 years. The sample comprised 12 female and 8 male participants. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. Participants received either course credit or monetary compensation for their involvement. The study protocol was approved by the university ethics committee and participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

The experimental design was a fully within-subjects repeated-measures design with three factors: Session (three levels: Session 1, Session 2, Session 3), Target Valence (two levels: positive target among negative distractors vs negative target among positive distractors), and Set Size (three levels: small, medium, large). Session corresponded to three weekly visits separated by approximately seven days. The Target Valence factor determined whether the target face in a trial displayed a positive expression (happy) while distractors displayed a negative expression (angry) or vice versa. Set Size manipulated the number of faces in a display and included three levels: 4, 8, and 12 items. The 3 x 2 x 3 factorial design allowed for tests of main effects of practice (session), valence, and set size, as well as their interactions.

Stimuli consisted of grayscale photographs of faces selected from a standardized face database. A pool of 60 unique identities was used, with each identity photographed displaying three facial expressions: happy, angry, and neutral. For the purposes of the search task, only happy and angry expressions were used as target and distractor faces, while neutral faces were excluded to maintain a clear valence contrast. Stimuli were resized to subtend approximately 2.5 degrees of visual angle at the viewing distance used in the laboratory and were presented against a mid-gray background. Faces were arranged on an invisible grid within a circular search area to avoid overlapping stimuli and to equate eccentricity across set sizes as much as possible.

Each session consisted of 360 trials divided into 6 blocks of 60 trials each. Trials were evenly distributed across the Target Valence and Set Size conditions, and target-present and target-absent trials were balanced within blocks. On target-present trials, precisely one face displayed the target expression and the remaining faces displayed the opposite-valence distractor expression. Target location was randomized across trials to avoid location-based strategies. Participants indicated whether a target was present or absent by pressing one of two keys on a standard keyboard with their dominant hand. Key-response mapping was counterbalanced across participants but held constant within a participant across sessions.

Before the first experimental session, participants completed a short training phase consisting of 20 practice trials to familiarize them with the task and response mapping. The same practice procedure was provided at the start of Sessions 2 and 3, but with a reduced number of practice trials (10) to re-acclimate participants without inducing additional learning effects. Each trial began with a central fixation cross presented for 500 ms, followed by the search display which remained on screen until response or until 3000 ms elapsed. Inter-trial intervals were jittered between 500 and 800 ms. Feedback on accuracy was provided during the practice trials only and not during the experimental trials.

Mood was measured using a standardized positive and negative affect schedule administered immediately before the start of the practice trials and again immediately following completion of the final block in each session. The affect measure yielded separate scores for positive affect (PA) and negative affect (NA), and these scores were analyzed to detect any systematic changes in mood as a function of session or task engagement. Participants completed the pre-task mood assessment in approximately two minutes and the post-task assessment in a similar timeframe.

Behavioral measures included reaction time (RT) on correct trials and accuracy (percent correct) for each condition. Trials with incorrect responses were excluded from reaction time analyses but were included in accuracy analyses. Reaction times faster than 200 ms or slower than 3000 ms were excluded as anticipatory or excessively slow responses. Additionally, RTs that were more than 2.5 standard deviations from a participant's condition mean were treated as outliers and removed. The data cleaning procedure resulted in the exclusion of approximately 3.8% of trials overall, with comparable exclusion rates across sessions and conditions.

The primary statistical approach was a three-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Session (3 levels) x Target Valence (2 levels) x Set Size (3 levels) on mean RTs for correct responses. Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied when sphericity assumptions were violated. Significant omnibus effects were followed by planned pairwise comparisons using paired-samples t-tests with Bonferroni correction to control for multiple comparisons. Accuracy data were analyzed analogously with repeated-measures ANOVA. To address the question of whether mood changes were related to performance changes, pre- to post-session differences in PA and NA were tested using paired t-tests for each session, and correlations between mood change scores and RT change scores across sessions were computed using Pearson correlation coefficients.

All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Effect sizes for ANOVA effects were reported as partial eta squared (ηp2), and for t-tests as Cohen's d. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < .05 for primary analyses. Where appropriate, 95% confidence intervals for mean differences were reported to provide information about the precision of estimates.

Participants were instructed to maintain consistent sleep and caffeine consumption habits on testing days to minimize extraneous variability. They were also asked to refrain from consuming alcohol for 24 hours prior to each session. The same laboratory testing room and the same computer display were used for all sessions to reduce environmental variability. The experimenter running the sessions remained the same across sessions whenever feasible to maintain procedural consistency.

Debriefing occurred at the end of the third session. Participants were thanked for their participation, provided with a brief explanation of the study's aims, and given contact information for the research team. No adverse effects were reported. The study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.

Results

The reaction time data were first summarized across conditions after the data cleaning procedures described above. Mean RTs and standard deviations were calculated for each participant in each Session x Target Valence x Set Size cell. Group-level descriptive statistics indicated a general pattern of faster responses in later sessions, as well as faster responses for positive targets relative to negative targets. Set size effects were evident, with larger set sizes associated with longer RTs on average.

A 3 (Session: 1, 2, 3) x 2 (Target Valence: positive, negative) x 3 (Set Size: 4, 8, 12) repeated-measures ANOVA was performed on mean RTs for correct trials. The ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Session, F(2,38) = 42.17, p < .001, ηp2 = .69, indicating that reaction times decreased across sessions. Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction showed that mean RT in Session 1 (M = 842 ms, SD = 131) was significantly slower than in Session 2 (M = 733 ms, SD = 117), t(19) = 6.21, p < .001, d = 1.39, 95% CI [77, 131]. Session 1 RT was also significantly slower than Session 3 (M = 698 ms, SD = 104), t(19) = 8.04, p < .001, d = 1.80, 95% CI [111, 171]. Session 2 RT was significantly slower than Session 3 as well, t(19) = 2.84, p = .010, d = .64, 95% CI [12, 58], indicating continued improvement across sessions.

The ANOVA also revealed a significant main effect of Set Size, F(2,38) = 215.43, p < .001, ηp2 = .92, with mean RTs increasing as set size increased. Pairwise comparisons confirmed that RTs at set size 4 (M = 642 ms, SD = 95) were faster than at set size 8 (M = 778 ms, SD = 116), t(19) = 9.35, p < .001, d = 2.09, and set size 8 was faster than set size 12 (M = 853 ms, SD = 139), t(19) = 5.21, p < .001, d = 1.17. These results are consistent with typical visual search load effects.

Contrary to many prior reports of a negative face search advantage, the main effect of Target Valence was significant in the opposite direction. The ANOVA showed a main effect of Target Valence, F(1,19) = 12.09, p = .002, ηp2 = .39, with positive targets (happy faces) detected faster than negative targets (angry faces) on average. Across sessions and set sizes, mean RT for positive targets was M = 748 ms (SD = 109) and for negative targets was M = 786 ms (SD = 126). A paired-samples t-test collapsing across session and set size confirmed that the difference was significant, t(19) = 3.48, p = .002, d = .78, 95% CI [18, 62].

To examine whether the valence effect varied across sessions or set sizes, the Session x Target Valence interaction and the Target Valence x Set Size interaction were examined. The Session x Target Valence interaction was not significant, F(2,38) = 1.03, p = .366, ηp2 = .05, indicating that the positive-target advantage was present in all three sessions and did not significantly change with practice. Inspection of means showed that positive targets were faster in Session 1 (positive M = 816 ms, negative M = 868 ms), Session 2 (positive M = 705 ms, negative M = 761 ms), and Session 3 (positive M = 622 ms, negative M = 774 ms), with consistent differences favoring positive targets. The Target Valence x Set Size interaction was also not significant, F(2,38) = 1.87, p = .168, ηp2 = .09, suggesting that the positive advantage did not depend on display size.

The three-way interaction among Session, Target Valence, and Set Size was examined and found to be non-significant, F(4,76) = 0.68, p = .606, ηp2 = .04. This indicated that practice-related RT reductions and set size effects generalized across valence conditions without a differential pattern that depended jointly on session and set size. To probe practice effects in more detail, separate Session x Set Size repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted within each valence condition. For positive targets, the Session main effect was significant, F(2,38) = 56.12, p < .001, ηp2 = .75, and the Set Size main effect was significant, F(2,38) = 142.31, p < .001, ηp2 = .88. For negative targets, both Session, F(2,38) = 32.84, p < .001, ηp2 = .63, and Set Size, F(2,38) = 110.92, p < .001, ηp2 = .85, were significant. These within-valence analyses confirmed that practice effects occurred for both positive and negative targets.

Accuracy data were analyzed to assess speed-accuracy trade-offs. A 3 x 2 x 3 repeated-measures ANOVA on percent correct revealed a significant main effect of Session, F(2,38) = 6.43, p = .004, ηp2 = .25, indicating modest improvements in accuracy across sessions. Mean accuracy increased from Session 1 (M = 89.5%, SD = 6.2) to Session 2 (M = 91.7%, SD = 5.4) and Session 3 (M = 92.8%, SD = 4.9). The main effect of Set Size on accuracy was also significant, F(2,38) = 38.24, p < .001, ηp2 = .67, with accuracy decreasing as set size increased. The main effect of Target Valence on accuracy was not significant, F(1,19) = 2.13, p = .162, ηp2 = .10, suggesting that the faster RTs for positive targets were not accompanied by lower accuracy. There were no significant interactions involving Target Valence in the accuracy analysis.

Mood was assessed using the positive and negative affect scales administered immediately before and after the task in each session. Paired-samples t-tests comparing pre- and post-task PA and NA for each session showed no significant changes in mood. For Session 1, PA pre- to post-change was non-significant, t(19) = 0.92, p = .367, and NA change was non-significant, t(19) = 1.12, p = .277. For Session 2, PA change was non-significant, t(19) = 0.63, p = .536, and NA change was non-significant, t(19) = 0.98, p = .341. For Session 3, PA change was non-significant, t(19) = 0.45, p = .658, and NA change was non-significant, t(19) = 1.04, p = .309. These results indicate that completing the task did not systematically alter self-reported mood within sessions.

To evaluate whether individual differences in mood change were related to practice effects in RT, Pearson correlations were computed between session-to-session RT reductions and corresponding changes in PA and NA. The correlation between the RT reduction from Session 1 to Session 3 and the change in NA across the same interval was r = .08, p = .737, and the correlation with change in PA was r = -.12, p = .610. No significant correlations were observed for the Session 1 to Session 2 RT change either. These correlational analyses provided no evidence that mood change accounted for the observed practice-related improvements in RT.

Given the unexpected direction of the valence effect, exploratory analyses were conducted to examine whether baseline affective traits might explain the positive-target advantage. Trait measures were not systematically collected as part of the main protocol, but retrospective self-reports of typical mood and anxiety were available for a subset of participants. Correlations between these retrospective self-reports and average valence RT differences were non-significant in the available subset (N = 12), suggesting that baseline trait affect did not clearly account for the positive advantage in this sample. However, the limited availability of trait data precluded firm conclusions.

Finally, an analysis of RT slopes as a function of set size was performed to quantify search efficiency. For each participant, RT was regressed on set size separately for each session and valence condition. Median slopes across participants were steeper in Session 1 (positive target slope M = 52 ms/item; negative target slope M = 58 ms/item) and became shallower across sessions (Session 3: positive target slope M = 36 ms/item; negative target slope M = 40 ms/item). The decrease in slope magnitude across sessions was significant for both valences, indicating improved search efficiency with practice. These slope analyses corroborate the main ANOVA findings that practice facilitated more efficient search across set sizes and contexts.

In summary, the results showed robust practice effects reflected in decreasing RTs and improving accuracy across three weekly sessions. Set size effects were strong and consistent with standard visual search paradigms. Contrary to many prior reports, a positive-target advantage emerged with faster detection of happy faces relative to angry faces across sessions. Mood assessments before and after the task did not show systematic changes and were not correlated with RT improvements, suggesting that practice rather than mood change drove the observed performance gains.

Discussion

The current study set out to examine whether practice across multiple weekly sessions would alter the negative face search advantage commonly reported in visual attention literature, and whether any changes in search performance could be attributed to concurrent shifts in mood. By employing a repeated-measures design with three weekly sessions and measuring mood immediately before and after each session, the study provided a test of the relative contributions of practice and mood to emotional face detection. The principal findings were twofold. First, robust practice effects were evident: reaction times decreased substantially across sessions and search efficiency, as indexed by RT slopes with set size, improved for both positive and negative targets. Second, contrary to many prior reports of a negative face search advantage, participants in this study detected positive faces faster than negative faces across all sessions, and this valence effect did not vary significantly with practice. Finally, mood, as assessed by pre- and post-session positive and negative affect measures, did not change systematically and was not correlated with practice-related RT improvements.

The presence of strong practice effects aligns with a considerable body of work demonstrating learning and adaptation in visual search tasks. Repetition typically leads to faster responses and greater accuracy as observers refine perceptual discriminations, streamline decision processes, and automatize motor responses. In the present data, the largest RT improvements occurred between Session 1 and Session 2, with continued albeit smaller gains into Session 3. The reduction in RT slopes across set sizes also indicates that participants became more efficient at scanning or rejecting distractors, possibly through improved feature-based search strategies or better allocation of attentional resources. These improvements were observed for both positive and negative targets, suggesting that the practice-related mechanisms were general rather than valence-specific.

The finding that positive faces were detected faster than negative faces across all sessions is noteworthy and invites careful interpretation. A negative face search advantage has often been framed as a robust attentional bias reflecting evolutionary pressures to rapidly detect threat. However, our results demonstrate that valence effects can vary and, under certain conditions, a positive-face advantage may emerge. Several factors may account for this pattern. One possibility is that the specific stimuli used in the study yielded happier faces that were more perceptually salient or distinctive relative to angry faces. Differences in the visual features that characterize happy expressions—such as exposed teeth, which increase local contrast—could have made positive faces stand out more among angry distractors. Even though stimuli were drawn from a standardized database and efforts were made to equate them, idiosyncratic differences can produce systematic biases.

Another plausible account concerns task demands and response strategies. Because the task required speeded identification of a target face among distractors, participants may have adopted an efficient template-matching strategy that privileged features diagnostic of happy expressions. If the diagnostic features for happy faces were simpler or more consistent across identities than those for angry faces in our stimulus set, template-based detection would yield faster RTs for positive targets. Practice could have amplified such strategic biases by allowing participants to refine target templates that favored the more easily identifiable valence.

Participant characteristics also warrant consideration. The sample comprised young adult university students who may not exhibit strong attentional sensitivity to threat in the same way that clinically anxious or high-trait-anxiety individuals do. Prior studies reporting robust negativity advantages often either specifically sampled or reported effects moderated by trait anxiety or stress levels. In samples with low baseline anxiety, threat-related attention biases might be attenuated or absent. Although we did not collect systematic trait-anxiety measures for all participants, the absence of a negativity advantage may reflect the characteristics of a non-clinical, low-anxiety cohort.

The absence of systematic mood changes before and after task performance suggests that the practice effects cannot be attributed to affective shifts induced by repeated testing. PANAS scores remained stable across sessions and within sessions, and mood change scores did not correlate with RT improvements. These findings strengthen the conclusion that the observed performance changes were driven primarily by practice and learning rather than emotional reactivity to the task environment. This conclusion is particularly important given the hypothesis that mood modulates attentional allocation to threat; our data suggest that, in the present protocol, mood was not the operative factor.

It is also relevant to consider methodological differences between this study and prior demonstrations of a negative face search advantage. Many previous studies used single-session designs, relied on different stimulus sets, or manipulated additional variables such as gaze direction, contextual cues, or brief exposure durations that emphasize early attentional capture. In contrast, our repeated-session approach may have promoted the development of strategic, top-down processes that mitigate bottom-up threat biases. Practice can shift the balance of attentional control from stimulus-driven capture toward goal-directed search, especially as participants learn which features reliably signal the target. Such a shift could diminish a threat-driven negativity advantage and even reverse it under certain conditions.

Despite the clear practice effects and valence findings, several limitations of the study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size of twenty participants, while adequate for within-subjects detection of medium-to-large effects, limits the precision of estimates for smaller effects and precludes strong inferences about individual differences. A larger sample would provide greater power to detect subtle valence-by-session interactions or to examine moderating influences of trait affect. Second, although mood was assessed using a widely used self-report instrument, self-report measures are subject to demand characteristics and may not capture subtle physiological changes in affect that could influence attention. Future research would benefit from including objective measures such as skin conductance or heart rate variability to complement self-reports.

A further limitation relates to stimulus selection. While standardized, the particular subset of faces chosen for this study may have contained systematic differences in expression intensity or salience that favored happy faces. Future work should include explicit controls or manipulations of expression intensity and distinctiveness, or employ morphing techniques to equate low-level visual features across valence categories. Additionally, the exclusion of neutral distractors and the use of exclusively happy and angry faces created a stark valence contrast that may not generalize to more ecologically valid multi-valence contexts.

Another consideration is the potential influence of response mapping and motor learning. Although response-key mapping was counterbalanced across participants, motor practice could have contributed to RT reductions independently of attentional improvements. However, the observed decrease in RT slopes across set sizes suggests that improvements were not solely motoric, as motor improvements would likely produce uniform RT reductions independent of set size. The specific pattern of slope decreases supports the interpretation that search efficiency improved.

The implications of these findings are multifaceted. The robust practice effects indicate that attentional biases observed in one-off sessions may not be stable traits but can be altered by even modest amounts of experience with the task. For researchers investigating affective attention, this underscores the importance of considering practice and exposure when designing studies and interpreting cross-sectional findings. For applied domains, such as security screening, the results suggest that training could modulate the salience of different facial expressions and, consequently, influence detection performance. Whether such training leads to more accurate threat detection or inadvertently reduces sensitivity to subtle negative cues is an important question for future applied research.

Future research directions are numerous. One promising avenue is to manipulate mood experimentally while holding practice constant, or vice versa, to directly test the causal contributions of each factor. For example, mood induction procedures could be administered prior to the first session and the same procedure repeated in later sessions to examine persistent mood influences on practice trajectories. Another approach would be to use eye-tracking measures to determine how practice alters overt attentional deployment, such as changes in fixation patterns, dwell times on targets versus distractors, and the sequence of item inspections. Eye-tracking would elucidate whether practice leads to faster target detection through improved scanning strategies or through faster processing of fixated items.

It would also be informative to investigate whether targeted training protocols can selectively enhance detection of negative expressions in populations where threat detection is critical. Such training could be applied to security personnel or to clinical populations with threat-processing biases to determine whether practice can recalibrate attentional priorities in desired ways. Conversely, exploring whether excessive practice reduces sensitivity to negative cues in ways that compromise performance would be valuable for understanding trade-offs.

Longer-term longitudinal studies examining the persistence of practice effects beyond three weekly sessions would clarify whether improvements plateau, consolidate, or revert with extended intervals between sessions. Investigating retention over weeks or months would inform whether practice leads to durable changes in attention. Additionally, studies that incorporate neuroimaging or electrophysiological measures could reveal whether practice-related behavioral changes correspond to alterations in neural responses to emotional faces, such as attenuation of amygdala responses or changes in visual cortical tuning.

Finally, replication studies with larger and more diverse samples are essential to determine the generalizability of the positive-face advantage observed here. Cross-cultural research could examine whether the valence advantage varies with cultural norms surrounding expression and attention to emotional cues. Inclusion of clinical samples with elevated trait anxiety or depression would test whether practice interacts with affective disposition to shape attentional biases differently across populations.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that practice across three weekly sessions produces substantial improvements in visual search for facial expressions, enhancing speed and efficiency for both positive and negative targets. Contrary to expectations based on much of the prior literature, positive faces were detected faster than negative faces across sessions, and mood measures did not change in ways that could account for performance shifts. These findings suggest that practice can alter the balance of attentional priorities in emotional face search and that valence advantages are not immutable. The results emphasize the need to account for practice and experience when studying affective attention and point to several fruitful directions for future research to disentangle the complex interplay among practice, mood, and emotional salience in guiding visual attention.
