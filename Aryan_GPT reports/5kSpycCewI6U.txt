Introduction

Human memory for language is both robust and selective, and the types of information that are preserved from discourse vary markedly depending on processing conditions and the nature of the material. Some aspects of linguistic input, such as the gist or semantic content of a passage, are readily retained across different encoding contexts. Other aspects, particularly surface-level structural features of sentences such as specific word order or voice, tend to be more fragile and are often lost in the course of retention. Research in cognitive psychology has long sought to characterize which features of utterances are encoded and later recoverable, and how task demands and encoding modes influence the preservation of different types of information. Early and influential experimental work provided evidence that memory for sentence form decays more quickly than memory for sentence meaning, with participants often failing to recognize literal changes in syntactic or surface structure while preserving the propositional content. This pattern has been taken to indicate that comprehension processes abstract away from surface form to represent a more enduring semantic representation. At the same time, however, more nuanced findings have complicated this simple dichotomy by showing that under certain conditions, structural information can be preserved and retrieved. The existence of boundary conditions for retention of form versus content has led to a body of research investigating how modality, attention, and production during encoding might influence what aspects of sentences are remembered.

One of the classic demonstrations relevant to this question comes from the work by Sachs, who reported intriguing modality-dependent effects in recognition of voice changes. In the reported studies, participants who read material silently were able to recognize changes in voice—such as shifts from active to passive constructions—when later asked to judge whether a test sentence matched an earlier one. By contrast, participants who heard the same material spoken by an experimenter showed relatively poor recognition of such voice changes. These findings suggested that silent reading, unlike listening, may leave behind a trace of the surface form that includes voice or syntactic realization, perhaps because readers generate an internal vocal representation or phonological code when decoding text. Sachs’ work therefore raised the possibility that encoding mode affects the extent to which structural features such as voice are retained, and it highlighted the importance of considering presentation modality when investigating memory for sentence form.

Subsequent research has attempted to reconcile the apparent tension between the general tendency to retain meaning over form and the specific finding that silent reading can support recognition of voice changes. In some studies, silent reading has been associated with greater encoding of orthographic or phonological form, which may facilitate later detection of surface alterations. In other investigations, however, no consistent advantage for silent reading over listening has been observed with respect to structural memory, suggesting that task demands and the nature of the manipulations matter. Research has also explored whether active engagement with the text, for example via producing the material aloud during encoding, might enhance memory for different types of information. The production effect literature reports that producing verbal material, such as reading it aloud, often increases memory relative to silent reading for items presented either visually or auditorily. This production advantage has been attributed to the distinctiveness of produced items, enhanced attention during production, or multimodal encoding involving both auditory and articulatory systems. However, the production effect is not uniformly observed across all paradigms and materials, and its influence on memory for sentence-level structural features has not been extensively explored.

Given these varied lines of evidence, it is plausible that different reading forms—silent reading, reading aloud, or mixed production—could differentially affect the retention of semantic versus structural information. Reading aloud may increase memory for item-specific features by creating an additional auditory trace, while silent reading might favor retention of syntactic form through internal phonological coding. Conversely, the act of producing material aloud might also lead readers to focus on semantic content rather than on subtle syntactic differences, depending on task goals and attentional allocation. Thus, there remains uncertainty about whether and how reading form determines the degree to which voice and semantic content are preserved.

Another important variable that may influence recognition of sentence alterations is the nature of the change itself. Semantic changes alter the propositional content of a sentence and therefore should be directly detectable when participants are sensitive to meaning. Voice changes, by contrast, alter syntactic realization without necessarily altering core semantic relations and may therefore be less salient to participants who prioritize meaning. If semantic changes are more accessible to memory than voice changes, participants should be more accurate in detecting semantic manipulations than in detecting voice manipulations. This expectation aligns with the traditional view that memory for gist is more durable than memory for surface form.

Furthermore, the base grammatical voice of a sentence—whether it is originally presented in active or passive voice—could influence detection of subsequent manipulations. Passive constructions often emphasize different participants and may be less natural or less frequent in certain populations, which could affect encoding and retrieval. If passive sentences are less typical, they might stand out during encoding and be more memorable in terms of both content and form. Alternatively, if passive constructions are more syntactically complex, they might be processed less deeply, reducing memory for both content and structure. Because of these competing possibilities, it is important to directly examine whether base voice affects recognition of identical sentences, semantic manipulations, and voice manipulations.

In addition to these theoretical considerations, practical aspects of experimental design matter. For example, the immediacy of the recognition test, the presence or absence of filler tasks between encoding and test, and the nature of the instructions can all influence whether participants rely on surface-level traces versus semantic representations. Immediate testing with no filler tasks might favor retention of surface features that are otherwise transient, whereas a delay or intervening task might selectively diminish surface-level traces. Similarly, explicit instructions to attend to form versus content may shift participants’ encoding strategies and thereby the type of information they later retrieve. Studies that fail to control or report these parameters provide results that are difficult to compare directly.

Taken together, the literature motivates a study that systematically manipulates reading form at encoding while assessing memory for identical sentences, semantic changes, and voice changes, with base voice varied orthogonally. Such a design allows examination of (a) whether production during reading enhances recognition overall or selectively for certain change types, (b) whether voice changes are generally less detectable than semantic changes, and (c) whether the original voice of the sentence moderates detection performance. A mixed design with reading form as a between-subjects variable and sentence type and base voice as within-subjects variables enables testing of these hypotheses while avoiding potential carryover of production strategies across conditions for individual participants.

The present study therefore examines how reading form—silent reading, reading aloud, or a mixed encoding condition combining both forms—affects recognition memory for sentences that are either identical to material in a just-presented passage, contain a semantic alteration, or contain a voice change. The study further examines whether the base voice (active or passive) of the original sentence influences recognition performance. Participants read a series of passages in one of three encoding conditions and then judged single sentences as identical or different, providing explanations for differences when detected. The procedural choice to elicit open-ended explanations allows investigation not only of binary accuracy but also of the nature of participants’ awareness of the detected differences, such as whether they describe semantic shifts or syntactic voice changes.

Based on prior literature, I hypothesized that semantic changes would be more readily detected than voice changes across reading forms, reflecting the greater durability and salience of propositional content. I also hypothesized that reading aloud would produce a recognition advantage relative to silent reading, consistent with production effect accounts, but that this effect might interact with sentence type such that production particularly enhances detection of semantic changes through enhanced encoding of item-specific detail. Finally, I considered the possibility that base voice could moderate detection, with passive base sentences potentially yielding different recognition patterns than active base sentences, though I did not specify a strong directional prediction for this effect given competing theoretical considerations. The study therefore tests these hypotheses while also exploring the qualitative content of participants’ explanations for detected differences as an indicator of what aspects of the sentences are represented at retrieval.

Methods

Participants were 96 undergraduate students recruited from a university participant pool. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 32 years, with a mean age of 20.7 years. The sample included both male and female participants, and participants reported English proficiency sufficient for participation in the experiment. Participants received course credit for their participation. The sample size of 96 was selected to provide reasonable power to detect medium-sized effects in a mixed-design analysis, and to allow approximately equal numbers of participants in the three between-subjects encoding conditions. Thirty-two participants were assigned to each reading form condition: silent reading, reading aloud, and mixed production.

Materials consisted of 24 brief expository passages, each one to two short paragraphs in length. Passages were constructed to be neutral and to contain a target base sentence embedded naturally in the text. Target sentences were simple transitive constructions that could be presented in either active or passive voice without altering the semantic relations. For each base sentence, three test sentences were created: an identical sentence that matched the base sentence word-for-word, a semantic manipulation that altered a key content word (for example changing the direct object to a semantically different but plausible referent), and a voice manipulation that preserved the propositional content but changed the grammatical voice from active to passive or vice versa. The manipulations were designed so that the semantic change produced a clear change in meaning, whereas the voice change preserved meaning but altered syntactic form. Half of the target sentences in the passages were originally written in active voice, and half in passive voice, yielding a balanced design for base voice.

Test sentences appeared individually on a computer screen immediately following each passage. Each passage was followed by a single test sentence drawn from one of the three test types. Across participants, the assignment of passages to test type and to whether the base voice was active or passive was counterbalanced so that each passage served in each test condition an equal number of times. Likewise, in the mixed production condition the assignment of which passages were read aloud and which were read silently was counterbalanced across participants such that each passage occurred in each combination of encoding form and test type across the group.

The experimental design was a mixed factorial design with reading form as a between-subjects factor with three levels (silent, aloud, mixed) and with sentence type (identical, semantic change, voice change) and base voice (active, passive) as within-subjects factors. Each participant read 24 passages and made 24 judgments. In the silent and aloud conditions each participant read all passages in the assigned mode, whereas participants in the mixed condition read 12 passages aloud and 12 silently; the mixed condition therefore allowed assessment of production effects within participants while maintaining comparability to the strictly silent and strictly aloud groups.

The procedure took place in individual testing rooms. Participants were told that they would read a series of short passages and then would see a single sentence and had to judge whether the sentence was identical to something they had just read. Participants were asked to make a “same” or “different” decision for each test sentence by pressing one of two keys on the keyboard and then to type a brief explanation if they judged the sentence to be different. The instructions emphasized accuracy rather than speed. No explicit instructions were provided to focus on form or content; participants were simply told to read naturally as they would normally do. In the aloud condition participants were instructed to read each passage aloud at a comfortable pace and were recorded with a microphone to ensure compliance; in the silent condition participants read silently to themselves; in the mixed condition participants alternated between reading aloud and reading silently according to on-screen instructions that specified whether the upcoming passage should be read aloud or silently.

Passages were presented one at a time on a computer screen in the same font and at a comfortable size. Participants controlled the pace of reading using a key press to proceed to the test sentence. There was no filler task or delay between reading and test; the test sentence appeared immediately after the participant finished reading and indicated readiness by pressing the key. The immediate nature of the test was chosen to examine recognition under conditions that maximize the availability of surface-level traces. Participants completed the 24 trials in a single session lasting approximately 25 to 35 minutes.

Responses were recorded for both the binary same/different judgments and for the typed explanations provided on trials judged as different. The primary dependent variable was proportion correct in the binary same/different task, scored as a hit for correctly identifying a changed sentence as different or a correct rejection for labeling an identical sentence as same. For the binary task, false alarms (labeling an identical sentence as different) and misses (failing to label a different sentence as different) were also examined. Explanations typed by participants on “different” trials were coded for whether they correctly identified the type of change (semantic versus voice) and for the specificity of the explanation. Two independent raters, blind to experimental condition, coded all explanations according to a pre-defined coding scheme. Inter-rater agreement was calculated and any disagreements were resolved by discussion to reach consensus.

Statistical analyses focused on mixed ANOVA models examining the effects of reading form (between-subjects), sentence type (within-subjects), and base voice (within-subjects) on accuracy. Planned pairwise comparisons were used to follow up significant main effects or interactions, with adjustments for multiple comparisons when appropriate. Additionally, analyses examined the content of participant explanations by comparing the proportion of correctly identified change types across conditions using repeated-measures tests. Response latencies for the same/different decision were also examined as an auxiliary measure of processing difficulty. All tests used an alpha level of .05, and effect sizes were reported in terms of partial eta-squared for ANOVA and Cohen’s d for pairwise contrasts.

To ensure data quality, trials with response times exceeding five seconds were examined for outliers, and trials in which participants left the explanation field blank on trials they labeled as different were flagged and inspected. Data from participants who failed to follow instructions in the aloud condition (for example by not reading aloud) were excluded from the primary analyses; this exclusion affected two participants whose data were replaced by additional recruits to maintain group sizes. All remaining data were included in the reported analyses.

Results

Descriptive statistics indicated variability in performance across sentence types and reading forms. Mean accuracy across all participants and conditions was 0.74 with a standard deviation of 0.12. Mean accuracy for identical sentences was 0.85 (SD = 0.10), for semantic changes was 0.79 (SD = 0.13), and for voice changes was 0.56 (SD = 0.18). The large difference between voice change detection and detection of the other two types was visually apparent in the summary data and suggested a strong main effect of sentence type. Across reading form groups, mean accuracy for the silent group was 0.75 (SD = 0.11), for the aloud group was 0.73 (SD = 0.13), and for the mixed group was 0.74 (SD = 0.12), suggesting little overall difference by encoding mode.

A 3 (reading form: silent, aloud, mixed) x 3 (sentence type: identical, semantic, voice) x 2 (base voice: active, passive) mixed ANOVA on accuracy revealed a significant main effect of sentence type, F(2,186) = 164.32, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .638. Pairwise comparisons using Bonferroni adjustment showed that accuracy for voice changes was significantly lower than accuracy for both identical sentences, t(95) = 16.75, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.71, and semantic changes, t(95) = 15.02, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.53. Accuracy for identical sentences was slightly higher than for semantic changes and the difference was significant, t(95) = 3.61, p = .001, Cohen’s d = 0.37. Thus, detection of voice changes was markedly worse than detection of semantic changes or labeling of identical sentences as same.

The main effect of reading form was not significant, F(2,93) = 0.34, p = .713, partial eta-squared = .007. Post hoc comparisons confirmed that there were no reliable differences between the silent, aloud, and mixed groups in overall accuracy. The reading form x sentence type interaction was also non-significant, F(4,186) = 1.02, p = .401, partial eta-squared = .021, indicating that the substantial disadvantage in detecting voice changes was consistent across reading conditions. The main effect of base voice (active vs passive) was not significant, F(1,93) = 1.46, p = .230, partial eta-squared = .015, and there were no significant interactions involving base voice and reading form or sentence type, all Fs < 1.40, all ps > .20. These results indicate that neither reading form nor the original grammatical voice meaningfully altered the pattern of sentence detection performance; instead, the type of change determined accuracy.

To examine whether differences in response tendencies (bias) contributed to the observed pattern, signal detection measures were calculated for each participant and condition. Sensitivity (d') was substantially lower for voice change detection (mean d' = 0.92, SD = 0.48) than for semantic detection (mean d' = 1.79, SD = 0.62) and identical sentence recognition (mean d' = 2.23, SD = 0.51 when treated as target vs lure in a conventional signal detection formulation). A mixed ANOVA on d' reproduced the strong main effect of sentence type, F(2,186) = 79.04, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .460, and again showed no main effect of reading form, F(2,93) = 0.21, p = .809. The response bias measure (criterion c) did not differ reliably across reading conditions, indicating that participants’ willingness to label sentences as different was similar regardless of encoding mode.

Analysis of the typed explanations provided further insight into the types of information participants used when they judged a sentence to be different. On trials with semantic changes that were correctly identified as different, participants provided an explanation correctly describing the semantic shift on 84% of those trials. On trials with voice changes that were correctly labeled as different, only 31% of explanations explicitly mentioned a change in voice or syntactic realization; many other explanations either described a perceived change in emphasis or used vague language that did not clearly point to voice. For identical sentences incorrectly labeled as different (false alarms), explanations often indicated participants' uncertainty or an attention lapse rather than any specific perceived difference. A repeated-measures analysis comparing the proportion of correctly identified change types in explanations found a large advantage for semantic over voice changes, F(1,95) = 132.16, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .582. This pattern held across reading form groups, and there was no significant interaction between reading form and explanation accuracy, F(2,93) = 0.47, p = .628.

Response time analyses showed that participants were slower to judge voice change trials (mean RT = 2,840 ms, SD = 740 ms) than semantic change trials (mean RT = 2,260 ms, SD = 620 ms) and identical trials (mean RT = 2,000 ms, SD = 540 ms). A repeated-measures ANOVA on response times confirmed a main effect of sentence type, F(2,186) = 54.18, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .368. There was no main effect of reading form on response times, F(2,93) = 1.12, p = .333, and no interaction between reading form and sentence type, F(4,186) = 0.85, p = .499. Longer response times for voice change trials are consistent with greater difficulty in detecting syntactic alterations relative to semantic ones.

An ancillary analysis examined whether individual differences in reading proficiency or working memory, as measured by brief self-report items about reading habits and a short digit span task administered at the end of the session, related to performance on voice change detection. Correlations were modest and non-significant after correction for multiple comparisons; the strongest relationship was between digit span and voice change detection accuracy, r = .18, p = .076, which did not reach statistical significance. This suggests that the difficulty in detecting voice changes was not strongly driven by individual differences in basic verbal working memory in this sample.

Given the lack of any effect of reading form on either accuracy or response times, planned exploratory analyses examined whether the mixed production group, in which participants read some passages aloud and some silently, showed any within-subject advantage for passages read aloud versus silently. A paired-samples comparison within the mixed group revealed no reliable difference in accuracy for passages read aloud (mean = 0.75) versus read silently (mean = 0.74), t(31) = 0.58, p = .565. Likewise, explanation accuracy and response times did not differ between aloud and silent trials within the mixed group. Overall, the converging patterns from accuracy, detection sensitivity, explanation content, and response time analyses point to a robust difficulty in recognizing voice changes relative to semantic changes or identical sentences, and they indicate that reading form in this paradigm did not modulate this pattern.

Finally, trial-level error patterns were inspected to ensure that lower voice detection was not simply due to a subset of passages being particularly problematic. Voice change trials showed lower accuracy across nearly all passages, with few exceptions, indicating that the difficulty generalized across materials. Similarly, the frequency of false alarms on identical trials did not vary systematically by passage or by reading form, supporting the conclusion that the principal effect was driven by the inherent detectability of change types rather than by item-specific idiosyncrasies.

Discussion

The present study investigated how reading form during encoding interacts with sentence-level changes to influence recognition memory for discourse material. Specifically, the study compared silent reading, reading aloud, and a mixed production condition and assessed recognition of test sentences that were identical to an earlier sentence, contained a semantic alteration, or contained a voice change. Additionally, the study varied whether the original sentence was presented in active or passive voice. The results provided a clear pattern: reading form had no measurable effect on recognition performance, whereas the type of change had a large effect, with voice changes detected significantly less often than semantic changes or identical sentences. Base voice had no reliable effect, and participants’ explanations indicated much greater awareness of semantic than of voice changes. Together, these findings speak to the relative fragility of structural information compared with semantic content and raise questions about the conditions under which production during reading enhances memory for form.

One of the central findings is the robust disadvantage in detecting voice changes across encoding conditions. Voice manipulations, which preserved meaning but altered syntactic realization, produced substantially lower accuracy, lower detection sensitivity, and longer response times than semantic manipulations. Participants’ open-ended explanations further confirmed that when they did identify a difference, they were far more likely to explicitly note a semantic change than a voice change. This pattern aligns with the long-standing observation that memory for gist is more durable and accessible than memory for surface features. It suggests that when readers process discourse, semantic relations and propositional content tend to be the primary basis for later recognition, whereas syntactic realizations such as active versus passive voice may not be encoded in a way that supports reliable later discrimination.

The failure to find any advantage of reading aloud over silent reading is notable given prior literature documenting production effects in a variety of paradigms. In the present study, neither the between-subjects comparison of silent, aloud, and mixed groups nor the within-subjects comparison within the mixed group revealed reliable differences in accuracy, sensitivity, response times, or explanation quality. These null results indicate that producing the material aloud did not confer a detectable benefit to recognition of the kinds of sentence features assessed here. Several factors may account for the absence of a production effect in this study. First, the production effect is often strongest for item-level memory tasks such as free recall of single words or lists, and its influence on sentence-level recognition may be attenuated because sentence processing already engages multiple encoding systems and because comprehension of sentences may prioritize semantic integration over verbatim representation. Second, the immediate testing procedure may have favored retention of surface traces that are equally available following both silent and aloud reading, thereby reducing the potential advantage of added auditory or articulatory encoding. Third, the task emphasized accuracy rather than speed and did not explicitly direct participants to attend to surface form, and so any distinctiveness conferred by producing material aloud may have been insufficient to shift encoding strategies toward preservation of syntactic form.

The present findings diverge from those reported by Sachs, who observed that silent readers could recognize voice changes whereas listeners could not, suggesting modality-specific encoding of structural features. A critical difference between Sachs’ work and the present study is the comparison of modalities: Sachs compared reading silently to auditory presentation, whereas the present study compared silent reading to producing aloud. The act of reading aloud is different from hearing speech in that the former involves self-generated auditory and motoric traces that may align with internal speech representations in silent reading, but may also introduce different attentional dynamics. It is possible that silent reading uniquely supports the generation of an internal phonological trace that preserves syntactic form, whereas producing aloud may shift attention to prosodic realization or semantic emphasis rather than to the specific syntactic label of voice. Another possibility is that the character of the test in Sachs’ studies favored detection strategies that are differentially facilitated by silent reading versus listening, and that these strategies were not invoked in the current paradigm.

The lack of a base voice effect in the present study also merits discussion. Whether the original sentence was active or passive did not reliably influence detection of changes. This suggests that the syntactic status of the base sentence—at least in terms of active versus passive realization—did not render the sentence more or less memorable in ways that altered the detection of subsequent manipulations. Although passive constructions can be less frequent and potentially more distinctive, their distinctiveness may not translate into better form memory unless participants attend specifically to syntactic form. The null effect of base voice therefore supports the idea that participants were not encoding voice in a robust or consistently retrievable manner, regardless of the baseline realization.

The explanation data provide an important complement to the binary accuracy results by revealing the qualitative nature of participants’ retrieval. High rates of correct explicit identification of semantic changes indicate that participants often retained and could articulate the nature of those changes. By contrast, the low rate of explicit identification of voice changes—even on trials that participants correctly flagged as different—suggests that some participants registered a subtle sense that something was different without being able to specify that the difference was a syntactic voice change. This pattern is consistent with partial or implicit detection of surface differences that may not be linked to explicit syntactic awareness.

The absence of a reading form effect raises questions about the robustness and boundary conditions of the production effect in sentence-memory tasks. One possibility is that producing material aloud enhances memory for item-level acoustic or phonological features that are not critical for detection of semantic or syntactic manipulations in sentences. Another possibility is that any potential advantage of producing aloud is offset by increased cognitive load associated with articulatory planning, which could reduce resources available for encoding syntactic form. Alternatively, readers may adopt different strategies depending on whether they are producing aloud or reading silently, and these strategies may produce similar overall outcomes in terms of the types of information retained.

The design choices in the present study also constrain the conclusions that can be drawn. The immediate presentation of test sentences following reading was selected to maximize the availability of surface traces, and yet voice changes remained difficult to detect. It is possible that a delayed test or inclusion of a distractor task would selectively degrade voice detection further or might reveal production advantages that emerge over longer retention intervals. Adding filler tasks between encoding and test would help determine whether the lack of a reading form effect is specific to immediate retrieval conditions. Likewise, direct comparisons with an auditory listening condition would be necessary to evaluate the modality-specific predictions that motivated the comparison to Sachs.

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged. The sample consisted of university students, which may limit generalizability to broader populations with differing reading habits or language backgrounds. The passages and manipulations, although carefully constructed and counterbalanced, were still a constrained set, and it remains possible that certain linguistic contexts or more salient syntactic shifts might yield different results. The absence of a writing production condition also limits the scope of production-related inferences; adding a condition in which participants write passages by hand or type them could help to determine whether production effects depend on motoric versus auditory encoding channels.

Future research could pursue multiple extensions to address these issues. A direct replication that includes an auditory listening condition alongside silent and aloud reading would enable a more comprehensive test of modality effects on structural memory and would directly test Sachs’ modality-based account. Inclusion of a writing condition would provide information about whether production effects are tied to vocalization or more generally to active production. Introducing filler tasks of varying duration and cognitive load would test the durability of surface-level traces and whether production confers benefits at longer retention intervals. Eye-tracking during reading could reveal whether attention allocation differs across reading forms, which could in turn explain differences in encoding of syntactic form. Neurophysiological measures such as EEG could be used to detect implicit sensitivity to syntactic changes that does not reach conscious report, providing a complementary index of whether voice changes are processed differently from semantic changes at early stages.

It would also be valuable to examine individual differences more thoroughly, including differences in reading strategies, language experience, and working memory capacity, to identify subpopulations for whom structural encoding during reading is more robust. For example, individuals who habitually subvocalize during silent reading might be more likely to retain voice information. Training studies that encourage readers to attend specifically to syntactic form could test whether strategic guidance can enhance explicit recognition of voice changes.

The current results have theoretical implications for models of language comprehension and memory. They reinforce the idea that comprehension processes prioritize semantic representation and that surface-level syntactic details are not automatically preserved in a retrievable form. At the same time, the failure to observe an effect of reading form cautions against broad generalizations that production during encoding uniformly enhances memory for all types of linguistic information. The findings suggest that the influence of production is likely to be constrained by the level of linguistic representation that is task-relevant and by the nature of the retrieval demands.

In sum, the study demonstrates that voice changes are notably more difficult to detect than semantic changes or identical sentences following reading, and that reading aloud does not reliably enhance recognition of either semantic or structural sentence features in an immediate test paradigm. These results underscore the relative fragility of structural memory for voice and highlight the need for future work to delineate the boundary conditions under which production and modality influence the preservation of sentence form. By systematically varying modality, production mode, delay, and instruction, future studies can further clarify how and when the surface features of language are retained and accessible to explicit awareness.
