Introduction

Working memory is a central construct in cognitive psychology that has been characterized as a set of limited-capacity systems that temporarily hold and manipulate information for ongoing cognitive tasks. Historically, models of working memory have distinguished between modality-specific temporary stores and domain-general control processes, and this distinction has guided much experimental work on short-term recall. One influential instantiation of this view is the multicomponent model, which posits separate subsystems such as a phonological store and a visuo-spatial sketchpad, together with a central executive that coordinates processing. The phonological subsystem, often termed the phonological loop, is thought to specialize in the temporary storage and rehearsal of speech-based information and to be particularly sensitive to interference from articulatory activity. A large body of research has used dual-task procedures to probe the function of the phonological loop by introducing articulatory suppression during encoding and retention, thereby limiting the use of covert speech-based rehearsal and revealing the contribution of the phonological system to verbal short-term memory performance. Articulatory suppression paradigms typically require participants to repeat an irrelevant verbal token aloud while attempting to maintain a list of verbal items; this manipulation reliably reduces serial recall of phonologically similar lists and attenuates certain modality effects, consistent with the idea that rehearsal supports maintenance in the phonological loop.

Beyond articulatory suppression, other forms of interference have been used to test modality-specific stores and their boundaries. Tasks that load visuo-spatial processing, such as visual tracking or pattern-copying tasks, aim to disrupt the visuo-spatial sketchpad and thus selectively impair memory for visually coded material. Evidence from such visual suppression procedures has often supported modality-specificity, showing that visual tasks reduce memory for visually presented items more than for auditorily presented items. The mapping between suppression task and stimulus modality therefore serves as a useful test of whether participants rely on modality-specific temporary stores or on more flexible, cross-modal strategies. If modality-specific stores are used, a suppression task that targets the same modality as the to-be-remembered stimuli should produce larger impairments than a mismatching suppression task.

The literature also documents a modality effect in short-term memory: auditorily presented verbal items are often recalled better than visually presented items when recall is immediate and when phonological similarity is controlled. This auditory superiority has been taken as evidence that auditory inputs gain more robust access to the phonological store, perhaps because auditory presentation automatically invokes phonological encoding. Researchers have therefore examined how articulatory suppression interacts with the modality of presentation. In many classic results, articulatory suppression reduces or eliminates the modality advantage for visually presented lists, because suppression prevents conversion of visually presented letters into a phonological code and thus equalizes performance across presentation modalities. However, the degree to which these effects generalize across different tasks and stimulus types is not uniform, and some studies have reported nuanced interactions that suggest partial overlap and cross-modal engagement between the phonological and visuo-spatial systems.

A commonly used paradigm for investigating these processes is the letter recall task, in which participants attempt to maintain and serially recall short lists of consonants that are chosen to be phonologically dissimilar, thereby minimizing confounds related to phonological similarity and focusing on surface-level rehearsal processes. Landry and Bartling used a variant of this paradigm to demonstrate the effects of articulatory suppression on recall of visually presented consonants. In their study, participants performed an articulatory suppression task while viewing lists of phonologically dissimilar consonants and then attempted serial recall. The key result reported by Landry and Bartling was a robust decline in recall performance under articulatory suppression relative to control, which was interpreted as evidence that even phonologically dissimilar items rely on articulatory rehearsal to be maintained in the phonological loop when presented visually. The finding contributed to the view that visual presentation often triggers subvocal rehearsal, and that preventing such rehearsal impairs short-term retention even for items that do not share phonological properties.

Despite the important contribution of Landry and Bartling’s findings to our understanding of verbal working memory, the evidence leaves open several important questions. First, the dependence of visual presentation on articulatory rehearsal may be task-dependent and may not generalize across all types of suppression. If visual presentation primarily engages the visuo-spatial sketchpad and only secondarily engages the phonological loop when participants choose a covert rehearsal strategy, then a suppression task that disrupts the visuo-spatial system rather than the articulatory system might produce a larger impairment for visually presented lists. Conversely, auditory presentation may more directly and automatically engage the phonological store and thus be selectively vulnerable to articulatory suppression. Thus, to fully map the dependency of recall on modality and rehearsal processes, studies need to include suppression tasks that selectively target different subsystems and to manipulate the modality of presentation directly.

Second, the question of modality matching versus mismatch remains theoretically important. If the working memory system contains specialized subsystems that are relatively encapsulated, then suppression that targets the same modality as the incoming information should be particularly detrimental. That is, articulatory suppression should impair auditory encoding more than visual encoding, while a visual suppression task should impair visual encoding more than auditory encoding. However, if working memory is more integrated and flexible, participants may recruit alternative strategies when a primary route is blocked, such that suppression mismatches would not produce large decrements. In such a flexible account, mismatch conditions might show little difference from control because participants switch to other available codes or strategies that are not targeted by the suppression manipulation. Empirically differentiating these possibilities requires an experimental design that systematically manipulates both suppression type and stimulus presentation modality within the same participants.

Third, previous research has rarely combined a visual suppression condition with auditory presentation in a within-subject factorial design alongside articulatory suppression and a no-suppression control. Including a well-matched visual suppression task and an auditory presentation condition allows for a more comprehensive test of modularity and cross-modal compensation in working memory. It also clarifies whether the reduction in recall observed under articulatory suppression in earlier visual-presentation studies reflects a modality-general reliance on rehearsal or a task-specific tendency for participants to convert visual input into a phonological format. Without testing both suppression types and both presentation modalities in the same design, inferences about the locus of interference remain tentative.

Building on these considerations, the present study expanded Landry and Bartling’s paradigm by introducing two new manipulations. First, in addition to replicating the articulatory suppression versus control contrast used by Landry and Bartling with visually presented consonants, the current study included a visual suppression condition intended to selectively load the visuo-spatial sketchpad. Second, the study introduced auditory presentation of the same phonologically dissimilar consonants as a second level of the stimulus presentation factor. By crossing suppression type (articulatory, visual, and no suppression) with presentation modality (visual and auditory) in a within-subjects design, the study directly tested modality-specific interference effects and the extent to which suppression disrupts memory when the suppression task and stimulus modality are matched versus mismatched.

Based on theoretical accounts emphasizing modality-specific stores and rehearsal mechanisms, the primary hypothesis was that suppression effects would be modality-specific. Specifically, articulatory suppression was predicted to impair recall more strongly when stimuli were presented auditorily than when they were presented visually, because auditory input should enter the phonological loop more directly and thus be more reliant on articulatory rehearsal. Conversely, visual suppression was predicted to impair recall for visually presented stimuli more than for auditorily presented stimuli. In contrast, under a resource-flexibility perspective, suppression should lead to general impairment irrespective of presentation modality, or mismatch conditions should show decrements comparable to matched conditions because participants might rely on the same rehearsal strategy across modalities. Testing these competing hypotheses required sensitive, within-subject measurement of recall accuracy across all six conditions that result from the full cross of suppression type and presentation modality.

A secondary, but important, aim of the study was to evaluate whether the original findings of Landry and Bartling would replicate once such additional suppression and modality manipulations were included. Replication in a broader factorial design can reveal boundary conditions for the effects and clarify whether articulatory suppression reliably reduces performance for visually presented phonologically dissimilar consonants or whether such effects are contingent on the absence of alternative suppression demands. Moreover, the inclusion of both suppression types in a within-subjects design reduces between-subject variability and increases sensitivity to interaction effects, thereby improving the capacity to detect subtle modality-by-suppression interactions.

Methodological details were therefore selected to be comparable to prior work while ensuring that the visual suppression task loaded visuo-spatial processes rather than articulatory mechanisms. The consonant lists were constructed to be phonologically dissimilar according to established phonological similarity metrics, minimizing confounds due to within-list similarity that might differentially interact with suppression. The articulatory suppression regimen required continuous vocalization at a steady pace during encoding and retention intervals, in line with standard procedures that prevent subvocal rehearsal. The visual suppression task required continuous visual tracking and response to occasional target changes, designed to occupy visual attention and visuospatial processing resources without imposing an auditory or verbal load.

The present study therefore offers a stringent test of modality-specificity in working memory by systematically comparing the effects of articulatory and visual suppression under both visual and auditory presentation of phonologically dissimilar consonants. The main questions were whether suppression impairments would be specific to matched modality conditions, whether mismatch conditions would resemble control performance, and whether the previous reports of articulatory suppression effects with visual stimuli would replicate in a design that includes visual suppression and auditory presentation. Demonstrating modality-specific impairments would provide strong evidence for functional specialization within working memory, whereas more global or non-specific impairments would suggest greater flexibility or interdependence among temporary storage systems. The following Method section describes participants, materials, design, and procedures in detail.

Methods

Participants were 48 undergraduate volunteers recruited from an introductory psychology participant pool at a large university. All participants were native speakers of English, reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing, and had no history of neurological or language disorders. The sample size of 48 was chosen to provide adequate power for detecting medium-sized within-subject effects in a 3 × 2 repeated-measures design while remaining feasible for laboratory testing. Participants received either course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board and all participants provided informed consent before beginning the experiment.

Materials consisted of sets of seven phonologically dissimilar consonants selected based on established phonological similarity norms and pilot testing to ensure that items were not easily confusable. The pool of consonants included items whose spoken names and articulatory properties were judged to be distinct across the set; pilot data indicated that serial recall of these lists was reliably above chance in the absence of suppression. For the visual presentation condition, consonants were presented in uppercase letters on a computer monitor one at a time in the center of the screen. Each letter remained on the screen for 1000 ms, followed by a 500 ms interstimulus interval during which the screen was blank. For the auditory presentation condition, the same consonants were presented as spoken tokens via high-quality audio recordings that matched the visual timing: each token lasted approximately 1000 ms with a 500 ms silent interval between tokens. The auditory recordings were produced by a native speaker at a natural speaking rate and normalized for amplitude across tokens.

Three suppression conditions were used. In the articulatory suppression condition, participants were instructed to vocalize the sequence "the, the, the…" aloud at a steady rate of approximately two utterances per second, beginning one second before the onset of the first stimulus and continuing until after the recall period for that trial. Experimenters trained participants on the pacing during practice trials and monitored compliance. The choice of the syllable "the" was made because it is non-lexical in function relative to the target consonants and commonly used in articulatory suppression paradigms. In the visual suppression condition, participants performed a continuous visual detection task that required them to attend to a peripheral stream of colored shapes presented on the screen while the consonant list was presented centrally. Specifically, a small square appeared in the upper-left quadrant and changed color at a rate of approximately two changes per second; participants were instructed to press a designated key whenever the square turned red. This visual detection task required sustained visuo-spatial attention and frequent motor responses but did not impose a verbal load. In the control condition, no concurrent suppression task was required; participants remained silent and fixated the center of the screen during list presentation.

The experiment employed a fully within-subjects design with suppression type (articulatory, visual, control) and presentation modality (visual, auditory) as repeated factors. Each participant completed all six conditions in a counterbalanced order using a Latin square to control for sequence effects. Within each condition, participants completed eight trials, yielding a total of 48 experimental trials per participant. Trials were grouped into blocks by suppression type and presentation modality pairs to minimize task switching costs; brief rest breaks were provided between blocks. Prior to the experimental blocks, participants completed a practice session that included two trials of each suppression condition and modality pairing to familiarize them with the tasks and to ensure compliance with suppression instructions.

On each trial, participants were shown or heard a sequence of seven consonants and were instructed to recall the sequence immediately in the correct serial order at the end of the presentation. For recall, participants used a paper response sheet with seven blank positions and wrote the letters in order. Written recall was used to minimize the need for overt verbalization that could interfere with suppression tasks. In the articulatory suppression condition, participants were permitted to continue vocalizing until the experimenter indicated that recall was complete. In the visual suppression condition, the peripheral color-change stream continued during the recall period and participants were allowed to make responses as needed. The experimenter recorded accuracy by scoring the written responses after each trial.

Scoring and dependent measures: The primary dependent variable was proportion correct in correct serial position across the seven-item lists. A response was scored as correct only if the letter in a given serial position matched the target letter for that position. Proportions were computed for each participant in each of the six conditions by averaging across the eight trials in that condition. Secondary analyses also considered a more lenient scoring criterion (correct letter in any position) to probe order-specific effects, but the main analyses focused on strict serial-position accuracy because the theoretical interest concerned the maintenance of sequential order in a modality-specific rehearsal system.

Statistical analyses were conducted using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with suppression type and presentation modality as within-subject factors. When significant interactions were observed, follow-up simple-effects tests and pairwise comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction to control Type I error. Effect sizes for ANOVA effects were reported as partial eta squared, and Cohen’s d was reported for paired comparisons. In addition, exploratory analyses tested for order or practice effects by including block order as a covariate and by examining first-half versus second-half performance across the experiment.

Compliance checks were included to ensure participants followed suppression instructions. Articulatory suppression compliance was monitored by the experimenter and, in ambiguous cases, by inspecting audio recordings taken during some practice trials. Visual suppression compliance was monitored by recording responses to red squares and checking for reaction times and hit rates that indicated task engagement. Trials in which participants clearly failed to comply (e.g., stopped vocalizing for an extended period during articulatory suppression or missed more than 75% of target detections in visual suppression) were flagged and a small number of such trials were excluded from participant-level averages. Participants with excessive noncompliance (defined as more than 15% of trials excluded) would have been removed from analysis, but no participants met that exclusion criterion in the present sample.

The procedure was implemented in a quiet laboratory testing room. Each experimental session lasted approximately 45 minutes, including consent, instructions, practice, the main experimental blocks, and debriefing. Participants were debriefed at the end of the session about the purposes of the study and were thanked for their participation. The data were anonymized and prepared for analysis following the pre-specified plan described above.

Results

Preliminary data checks indicated that the dataset was complete for the 48 participants after exclusions for noncompliance were applied, and no participants exceeded the pre-specified exclusion threshold. Descriptive statistics for the primary dependent variable, proportion correct in correct serial position, were computed for each of the six suppression-by-modality conditions. For visual presentation, the mean proportion correct in the control condition was 0.72 (SD = 0.11), in the articulatory suppression condition was 0.71 (SD = 0.12), and in the visual suppression condition was 0.58 (SD = 0.13). For auditory presentation, the mean proportion correct in the control condition was 0.75 (SD = 0.10), in the articulatory suppression condition was 0.60 (SD = 0.14), and in the visual suppression condition was 0.73 (SD = 0.11). These descriptive differences suggested a pattern in which articulatory suppression selectively impaired auditory presentation and visual suppression selectively impaired visual presentation, whereas control performance remained relatively high in both modalities.

A 3 (suppression: control, articulatory, visual) × 2 (presentation: visual, auditory) repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to formally test these effects. The main effect of suppression type was significant, F(2,94) = 18.24, p < .001, partial ηp² = .279, indicating that performance varied across the three suppression conditions when averaged across modalities. The main effect of presentation modality was not significant, F(1,47) = 2.01, p = .162, partial ηp² = .041, suggesting that, on average, auditory presentation did not differ reliably from visual presentation collapsed across suppression types. Most critically, the interaction between suppression type and presentation modality was significant, F(2,94) = 24.52, p < .001, partial ηp² = .343, indicating that the effect of suppression depended on whether stimuli were presented visually or auditorily.

To unpack the interaction, simple main effects and paired comparisons were conducted. For auditory presentation, a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA across suppression conditions revealed a significant effect, F(2,94) = 21.76, p < .001, partial ηp² = .317. Paired comparisons showed that articulatory suppression (M = 0.60, SD = 0.14) produced significantly lower performance than the auditory control condition (M = 0.75, SD = 0.10), t(47) = 6.07, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.88. Visual suppression (M = 0.73, SD = 0.11) did not differ significantly from the auditory control condition, t(47) = 1.15, p = .255, Cohen’s d = 0.17, indicating that visual suppression did not substantially impair recall of auditorily presented consonants. Articulatory suppression also produced lower performance than visual suppression in the auditory modality, t(47) = 5.43, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.78.

For visual presentation, a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA across suppression conditions also revealed a significant effect, F(2,94) = 12.88, p < .001, partial ηp² = .215. Paired comparisons showed that visual suppression (M = 0.58, SD = 0.13) produced significantly lower performance than the visual control condition (M = 0.72, SD = 0.11), t(47) = 5.02, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.72. Articulatory suppression (M = 0.71, SD = 0.12) did not differ significantly from the visual control condition, t(47) = 0.92, p = .361, Cohen’s d = 0.13, indicating that articulatory suppression did not reduce recall of visually presented consonants relative to control. Visual suppression also produced lower performance than articulatory suppression in the visual modality, t(47) = 4.52, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.65.

Complementary comparisons tested cross-modality differences for each suppression type. For the control condition, auditory presentation (M = 0.75, SD = 0.10) did not differ significantly from visual presentation (M = 0.72, SD = 0.11), t(47) = 1.77, p = .083, Cohen’s d = 0.26, consistent with the non-significant main effect of modality. For articulatory suppression, auditory presentation (M = 0.60, SD = 0.14) was significantly lower than visual presentation (M = 0.71, SD = 0.12), t(47) = 4.82, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.70, reflecting the strong modality-specific impairment under articulatory load. For visual suppression, auditory presentation (M = 0.73, SD = 0.11) was significantly higher than visual presentation (M = 0.58, SD = 0.13), t(47) = 5.16, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.74, again reflecting modality specificity.

Additional exploratory analyses checked for order effects and potential practice improvements across the experiment. A mixed ANOVA including block order as an additional covariate showed no significant interactions involving block order and the primary factors, and first-half versus second-half performance comparisons revealed only a small and non-significant increase in overall accuracy across the session, t(47) = 1.41, p = .165. Compliance measures indicated that participants adhered to suppression instructions: average hit rates in the visual suppression condition were 85% (SD = 9%), and experimenter-coded compliance for articulatory suppression during practice was uniformly high. A small number of individual trials (less than 2% of trials across the dataset) were removed for noncompliance, but these removals did not affect the pattern of results when included.

To assess whether the pattern of effects depended on strict serial-position scoring, the analyses were repeated using the lenient scoring criterion (correct letter in any position). The interaction between suppression type and modality remained significant with lenient scoring, F(2,94) = 18.67, p < .001, partial ηp² = .284, and the modality-matched suppression effects persisted, although overall proportions were higher under the lenient scoring as expected. This convergence across scoring methods suggests that the observed modality-specific suppression effects reflect genuine differences in maintenance and not solely differential susceptibility of order retention.

In sum, the results indicate that suppression effects were modality-specific: articulatory suppression selectively impaired recall when stimuli were presented auditorily but not when presented visually, and visual suppression selectively impaired recall when stimuli were presented visually but not when presented auditorily. Control performance was high in both modalities and did not differ reliably. These findings therefore indicate that interference effects depend critically on the match between the suppression task and the modality through which the to-be-remembered information is presented.

Discussion

The current study examined how modality-specific suppression affects short-term recall of phonologically dissimilar consonants by crossing three suppression conditions (articulatory suppression, visual suppression, and no suppression) with two presentation modalities (visual and auditory) in a within-subject design. The key finding was a robust interaction between suppression type and presentation modality: articulatory suppression selectively impaired recall when items were presented auditorily, whereas visual suppression selectively impaired recall when items were presented visually. In contrast, mismatch conditions—articulatory suppression with visual presentation and visual suppression with auditory presentation—yielded performance levels that were comparable to the no-suppression control condition. This pattern is inconsistent with a simple general-resource account in which any secondary task would indiscriminately reduce recall performance, and instead supports the idea that temporary maintenance depends on modality-specific subsystems that are preferentially recruited according to the input modality.

These results have several theoretical implications. First, they strengthen the case for modality-specific components within working memory as proposed by multicomponent models. The selective impairment of auditory presentation under articulatory suppression suggests that auditory stimuli gain privileged access to a phonological maintenance mechanism that relies on articulatory rehearsal. When that rehearsal is blocked, retention suffers significantly. Conversely, the selective impairment of visual presentation under a visuo-spatial suppression task indicates that visual encoding and maintenance utilize visuo-spatial resources that can be disrupted by a concurrent visual tracking/detection task. The asymmetry of effects across modalities and suppression tasks reflects a functional specialization that aligns with classical distinctions between the phonological loop and the visuo-spatial sketchpad.

Second, the finding that mismatch suppression conditions did not differ from control suggests that when a suppression task targets a different modality than the incoming information, participants can maintain performance by relying on the modality-matched subsystem that remains intact. For example, when visual stimuli were presented under articulatory suppression, participants did not show the decline in recall observed in earlier studies such as Landry and Bartling’s that reported an articulatory suppression effect for visually presented consonants. This discrepancy can be understood in terms of task demands and strategy flexibility: in the absence of a competing visual suppression task, participants in Landry and Bartling’s paradigm may have spontaneously recoded visual items into phonological form and relied on subvocal rehearsal, thereby becoming vulnerable to articulatory suppression. However, when the visuo-spatial sketchpad was directly taxed in the present study, participants could not rely exclusively on visual maintenance and instead may have had to engage alternative non-articulatory strategies; critically, the present pattern suggests that when the primary matching subsystem is available, performance remains robust.

Third, the present findings refine interpretations of the role of articulatory suppression in visual recall tasks. Whereas some earlier work interpreted the effect of articulatory suppression on visually presented lists as evidence that visual items are obligatorily recoded into a phonological format for maintenance, the current data indicate that such recoding may be strategic rather than mandatory. In the presence of a competing visual suppression task, participants appeared to continue to use non-verbal visual maintenance strategies, and articulatory suppression no longer reduced performance. Thus, whether visual items are maintained phonologically may depend on task context and on the availability of alternative maintenance routes.

The results also contribute to understanding modality-specific resilience and compensation. The lack of general interference under mismatch conditions implies that the working memory system can compensate for a blocked route by employing the appropriate subsystem for the modality of input. This flexibility suggests a hybrid view whereby temporary maintenance comprises specialized subsystems that are preferentially, but not exclusively, engaged depending on input modality and participant strategy. When a subsystem is unavailable or overloaded, participants may attempt to switch to other coding formats, but such compensatory strategies appear to be most effective when they map onto the intact subsystem; mismatch suppression that targets a different subsystem leaves the matching subsystem free to operate and therefore yields little impairment.

Several methodological aspects of the study bear discussion. First, the within-subjects design increased sensitivity to detect interactions and minimized between-subject variability. Counterbalancing of condition order and the inclusion of practice trials helped reduce carryover and learning effects. Compliance checks confirmed that participants generally followed suppression instructions. Second, the use of phonologically dissimilar consonants reduced confounding influences of phonological similarity, allowing a clearer test of rehearsal-dependent maintenance. Third, using both strict serial scoring and more lenient scoring rules produced convergent patterns, suggesting that the observed effects are not an artifact of scoring method but reflect true differences in maintenance and retrieval processes.

The study also raises important questions and suggests directions for future research. One avenue is to examine the temporal dynamics of strategy selection: when participants encounter a suppression mismatch or block, how quickly and under what conditions do they switch from one maintenance code to another? Real-time measures such as verbal reports or secondary-task probes administered at different points in encoding and retention could illuminate how and when recoding occurs. Another direction is to vary the difficulty and resource demands of suppression tasks systematically to map thresholds at which participants are forced to abandon one code in favor of another. For instance, increasing the difficulty of the visual suppression task might reduce the ability to employ visual maintenance even further and thereby reveal conditions under which visual items are more likely to be recoded phonologically.

A related question concerns individual differences in strategy preferences and cognitive style. Some participants may habitually prefer phonological rehearsal even for visual stimuli, whereas others may rely more on visual imagery. Future work could relate predispositions in strategy to differential susceptibility to modality-matched and mismatched suppression, and could explore whether training or instruction modifies these tendencies. Such individual differences might also interact with working memory capacity; a capacity-limited central executive might constrain the ability to switch strategies effectively under dual-task demands.

Neuroimaging and neurostimulation methods could provide converging evidence regarding the neural substrates of the modality-specific suppression effects observed here. If articulatory suppression selectively disrupts phonological loop processes, then interference should be associated with reduced activation in brain regions implicated in speech rehearsal and phonological processing. Conversely, visual suppression should modulate activity in regions associated with visuospatial attention and imagery. Combining behavioral suppression paradigms with functional MRI or transcranial magnetic stimulation could help establish causal links between suppression tasks, modality-specific neural systems, and observed performance decrements.

The present study has limitations that temper the conclusions. Although the visual suppression task was designed to load visuo-spatial resources without imposing articulatory demands, it inevitably included motor components (key presses) and attentional demands that could have secondary effects. Future studies could refine visual suppression tasks to isolate specific visuo-spatial processes more cleanly, perhaps by using eye-tracking to require continuous gaze patterns that tax spatial maintenance without overt motor responses. In addition, the stimuli were limited to phonologically dissimilar consonants; extending the paradigm to include words, digits, or phonologically similar lists could reveal whether modality-specific suppression generalizes across different types of verbal material.

Another limitation is that the study assessed immediate serial recall only; longer retention intervals and complex span tasks that require concurrent processing would provide a more comprehensive view of how modality-specific suppression affects working memory under everyday cognitive demands. It would also be informative to examine whether suppression effects emerge differently across serial positions; exploratory analyses in the present study suggested that modality-specific impairments were distributed across positions rather than confined to particular serial positions, but more fine-grained position-by-position modeling could clarify whether rehearsal supports retention differentially across a list.

In closing, the present findings provide compelling support for modality-specific interference in working memory and suggest that the mapping between suppression type and presentation modality determines whether recall is impaired. The data indicate that articulatory suppression impairs auditory-based maintenance while visual suppression impairs visually based maintenance, and that mismatch conditions often leave performance relatively intact. These results reconcile some inconsistencies in the prior literature by emphasizing the role of task context and strategy in determining whether visual input is maintained phonologically. Overall, the study advances our understanding of the functional architecture of working memory and highlights the importance of considering modality matching when interpreting dual-task interference effects. Future work that probes the mechanisms of strategy selection, individual differences, and neural underpinnings will help to further elucidate how modality-specific subsystems contribute to the flexible maintenance of information in short-term memory.
