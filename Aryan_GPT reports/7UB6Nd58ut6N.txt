Introduction

Decision making under risk is a central topic in psychological science and behavioral economics because it illuminates how people reason about uncertainty and how they respond to low-probability, high-impact events. A key distinction that has emerged in the literature concerns choices that are based on verbatim descriptions of options and probabilities versus choices that are based on experienced outcomes experienced through sampling or feedback. When people make decisions from description, they are given explicit statements of the probabilities and outcomes that characterize each option. When people make decisions from experience, they learn about the options by sampling outcomes or by receiving feedback after making repeated choices. This description-experience distinction captures an important real-world contrast because people often must decide on the basis of statistical summaries in some settings and on the basis of direct encounter and feedback in others. A robust pattern that has been reported in multiple studies is that the weighting of rare events differs between description-based and experience-based choices. Specifically, rare events tend to be overweighted when people make choices from description, while the same rare events tend to be underweighted when people make choices from experience. This reversal has been described as the description-experience gap and is often cited to explain discrepancies between prospect theory predictions and behavior observed in learning or sequential sampling contexts.

The description-experience gap has been interpreted in a number of complementary ways. One account emphasizes sampling error and finite sampling in experience-based tasks. When people sample from an outcome distribution a limited number of times, rare events may simply not appear in their limited sample, leading to systematic underweighting of those events. Another account emphasizes memory and retrieval: rare outcomes that do occur during experience may be remembered but are down-weighted in subsequent decision making due to their infrequent occurrence or due to poor generalization across contexts. A further mechanistic account focuses on information format and cognitive access. Explicit descriptions present probabilities in symbolic form that can be weighted and integrated relatively directly, whereas experiences require individuals to infer and estimate probabilities from exemplars, a process that can lead to different subjective weights. Emotional and attentional processes have also been invoked, with some arguing that vivid or salient outcomes encountered in experience can sometimes be overweighted if they are highly affective, whereas unemotional rare outcomes in experience may be overlooked.

Empirical tests of the description-experience gap have usually contrasted decision-making tasks in which probabilities are explicitly presented with tasks in which participants sample from a computerized distribution of outcomes. The original demonstrations used computer-based sampling paradigms in which sampling events were generated by pseudo-random number draws or simulated lotteries. Those studies reported fairly consistent evidence for overweighting of rare events in description and underweighting in experience. The robustness of this pattern has important implications for models of risky choice, learning, and public policy, because it suggests that people’s subjective probability weighting is context-dependent and sensitive to how information is obtained.

Despite the apparent robustness of the description-experience gap in many published studies, there has been ongoing debate about boundary conditions and explanatory mechanisms. Some investigators have observed that the gap is attenuated or even eliminated under certain sampling regimes, such as when participants are allowed to draw very large samples, when they are instructed to explore thoroughly, or when sampling costs are manipulated. Other work has noted that the properties of the rare event itself—its valence, magnitude, and psychological salience—moderate the effect, and that decisions about gains versus losses can show divergent patterns. There is also debate about whether description and experience necessarily involve different cognitive processes or whether differences can be reduced to differences in the information available to the decision maker.

One methodological dimension that has received relatively less attention is the modality by which experience is instantiated. Most prior studies have used computerized draws or simulated lotteries to provide experiential feedback. Computerized draws have many advantages for experimental control, but they may differ in subtle ways from encounters with physical stimuli. For example, sampling from an opaque bag of counters or from a mechanical spinner may evoke different attentional or motivational states than sampling from a computer interface. Physical tokens and tangible sampling procedures may make outcomes more concrete or may alter the extent to which participants perceive the underlying generative process as stable or stochastic. If modality matters, then the choice of physical versus computerized sampling could contribute to variability in the observed description-experience gap across studies.

Related to the question of modality, individual differences are likely to shape how people respond to description versus experience. One individual-difference variable that is theoretically relevant to risk taking and probability perceptions is dispositional optimism. Optimism reflects a generalized expectation that positive outcomes are more likely and negative outcomes are less likely. Optimistic individuals might be less sensitive to low-probability negative outcomes because their prior beliefs favor favorable outcomes, or they might overemphasize rare positive outcomes when those outcomes are beneficial. Conversely, optimism might lead to greater risk taking when the rare event is positive, or it might produce risk aversion if optimism is tied to inflated beliefs about the efficacy of safe options. The precise relationship between optimism and risky choice in description and experience contexts has not been conclusively established.

Few studies have directly examined the interaction between optimism and the description-experience distinction. A small number of investigations have correlated trait measures such as optimism with choice patterns in both description and experience paradigms. These studies have yielded mixed results, with some reporting weak relationships and others reporting none. The inconsistency leaves open the possibility that optimism either does not systematically influence the description-experience gap or that its influence is subtle and contingent on contextual factors such as the framing of outcomes, the domain of risk, or the nature of the sampling process.

Replication and extension are essential for clarifying the generality of the description-experience gap and for identifying boundary conditions. Replication studies can test whether previously reported effects persist when critical aspects of the procedure are modified. One natural manipulation involves the mode of sampling in the experience condition. If the description-experience gap is robust to variations in sampling modality, then replacing computerized draws with physical sampling should yield the same pattern of overweighting in description and underweighting in experience. If modality matters, then a physical sampling procedure might attenuate or eliminate the gap.

This study was designed to test the replicability of the description-experience gap as reported in earlier work, while introducing a methodological modification: experience was generated using physical counters inside opaque bags rather than using computerized random draws. The description condition was constructed to be as similar as possible to prior description-based tasks, with explicit probabilities and outcomes presented in text form. The key outcome of interest was whether participants in the description condition would overweight rare events relative to the objective probabilities, while participants in the experience condition would underweight rare events. A secondary aim was to explore whether dispositional optimism related to individuals’ likelihood of choosing risky options in either condition.

The present design also sought to address some methodological considerations that arise in experience-based studies. Specifically, the bag-and-counter method was chosen to provide tangible, real-world sampling that could be transparently understood by participants but that would still obscure the exact composition of the outcome distribution. Bags contained fixed numbers of outcome tokens that were not visible to participants, and sampling drew tokens without replacement for each sampling episode. The choice to use counters aimed to create a clearer mapping between sampling and physical events, in contrast to computer-generated feedback that can sometimes be perceived as abstract or detached. It was conceivable that physical sampling could lead to more accurate representation of rare-event probabilities or to different patterns of salience for rare outcomes.

The study employed a between-subjects design in which participants were randomly assigned to either the description or experience condition. Each participant completed a sequence of binary choices between paired options that differed in their outcome distributions, including options with rare high-payoff or rare low-payoff outcomes. Choices were structured so that a clear pattern of overweighting versus underweighting of rare events would manifest as higher or lower propensities to choose the option that relied on the rare event. To capture trait optimism, participants completed a standardized optimism questionnaire after the decision task.

Based on previous reports, the pre-registered primary hypotheses were that participants in the description condition would be more likely to choose options that disproportionately reward rare events in scenarios where overweighting of rare events would be adaptive, whereas participants in the experience condition would be less likely to do so. A secondary hypothesis was that dispositional optimism would correlate with increased propensity to choose risky options, potentially differing by condition. The study was powered to detect medium-sized effects between groups, and multiple analytic strategies were planned, including proportion-based comparisons, logistic mixed-effects regression to account for repeated measures, and correlational analyses between optimism scores and aggregate risky choice behavior.

The present work contributes to ongoing debates about the nature and boundaries of the description-experience gap by testing whether the effect generalizes to physical sampling modalities and by assessing whether optimism moderates risk-taking tendencies across information formats. If the description-experience gap is robust to modality, the predictions derived from earlier findings should be replicated with physical sampling. If the gap is attenuated or eliminated, the results will point to previously underappreciated contextual factors that shape probability weighting. In either case, the findings will inform theories of decision making by clarifying the interplay between sampling mode, individual differences, and risk-sensitive choices. Given the practical relevance of understanding how people process rare events in different contexts, resolving the role of sampling modality and optimism has implications for domains ranging from health communication to financial decision making.

Methods

Participants were 120 adult volunteers recruited from a university participant pool and from the surrounding community. The sample size was chosen to match the scale of prior laboratory studies of the description-experience effect and to provide adequate power to detect medium-sized group differences. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 62 years, with a mean age of 26.8 years and a standard deviation of 8.2 years. The sample comprised 68 females and 52 males. Participants reported a variety of educational backgrounds, with most being current students or holding college degrees. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were compensated with course credit or a modest payment for their time.

The study used a between-subjects design with random assignment to one of two conditions: a description condition or an experience condition. Randomization was implemented via a pre-generated allocation list that the experimenter used to assign the next participant to a condition. The experimenter was not blind to condition during data collection, but instructions and materials were standardized to minimize experimenter effects. Ethical approval for the study protocol was obtained from an institutional review board prior to data collection.

Materials included a set of 16 binary choice problems that were modeled on problems commonly used in the literature to test description-experience differences. Each problem presented two options: a safer option that yielded a moderate but certain or probable outcome, and a riskier option whose expected value was similar but whose payoff structure hinged on a rare event. Eight of the problems were framed as gains and eight as losses to cover different domains of valence. Within each domain, four problems featured a rare high-reward event for one option, and four featured a rare low-loss event. The objective probabilities for the rare events were 0.10 in the critical cases and 0.90 for the complementary outcomes. Payoff magnitudes were set so that the expected values of the two options were closely matched, thereby making probability weighting the key determinant of choice preferences.

In the description condition, each choice problem was presented on paper and on a computer screen, and participants were provided with explicit statements of probabilities and outcomes. For example, a description problem would state that Option A yields $3 with probability 1.0 (a certain outcome) and Option B yields $30 with probability 0.10 and $0 with probability 0.90. These descriptions were accompanied by simple frequency-equivalent statements and visual aids to ensure comprehension. Participants in the description condition made 16 sequential choices and were informed that one of their choices would be randomly selected and paid out at the end of the experiment based on the stated probabilities.

In the experience condition, the same 16 choice problems were presented, but objective probabilities were not described explicitly. Instead, experience was instantiated using physical counters in opaque bags. For each problem, a bag contained 10 counters for the gamble with a 0.10 rare-event probability: one counter representing the rare outcome and nine representing the common outcome. Similarly, bags representing other distributions were composed to match the intended probabilities. Participants were allowed to sample from the appropriate bag a limited number of times prior to choosing between the two options for that problem. Sampling was conducted without replacement until participants indicated they had sampled enough, or until a maximum of 10 samples had been drawn for that problem. After sampling, participants were required to make a single choice for that problem, and one problem was randomly selected for payment based on the actual tokens drawn in the chosen option’s bag if physical sampling indicated that outcome, or the bag was used to execute the payoff if necessary.

Procedure began with standardized instructions explaining the general format of the experiment. Participants were told that they would face a sequence of choice problems and that their choices could affect their final payoff. They were informed about the payment procedure and about how sampling would work in the experience condition. In the experience condition, participants were shown an opaque bag and counters as an example and were allowed to handle practice counters to become comfortable with the sampling procedure. The practice phase included two practice problems to familiarize participants with the interface and the sampling mechanism. In the description condition, participants also completed two practice problems to ensure comprehension of the descriptive format. After practice, participants completed the 16 experimental trials in a fixed randomized order. Trial order was randomized for each participant to control for order effects.

After completing the 16 choice trials, participants completed a short questionnaire battery that included the Life Orientation Test–Revised (LOT-R) as a measure of dispositional optimism. The LOT-R contains 10 items, including filler items, and yields a composite score that ranges in higher values for greater optimism. The scale has been widely used and exhibits moderate reliability in diverse samples. In addition to the optimism measure, participants completed a brief manipulation check that asked them to estimate the probability of the rare event in a randomly selected problem and to report how many samples they drew in experience trials. They also provided demographic information.

All sessions were conducted individually in a quiet laboratory room. The experimenter followed a script to ensure consistency across participants and conditions. The experimenter recorded the number of samples drawn, choice responses, response times for choices, and the actual outcomes that occurred or were simulated for the payment stage. At the end of the session, participants were debriefed about the purposes of the study and were paid according to the predetermined payment scheme.

Planned analyses focused on testing the primary hypothesis that the propensity to choose the risky, rare-event–sensitive option would differ between description and experience conditions. The primary dependent variable was the proportion of trials on which the participant chose the option that paid off primarily through the rare event. Group-level comparisons were computed using independent-samples t-tests on these proportions as well as logistic mixed-effects models that treated individual choices as binary outcomes nested within participants with random intercepts. Effect sizes were estimated using Cohen’s d for t-tests and odds ratios for logistic models. Secondary analyses examined correlations between optimism scores and the aggregate risky choice proportion for each condition, using Pearson correlation coefficients. Additional exploratory tests assessed whether sampling intensity (number of samples drawn) predicted choice patterns in the experience condition and whether reaction times differed across conditions. Bayesian analyses were also planned to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis when appropriate.

Data screening procedures were implemented prior to analysis. Response patterns that indicated noncompliance or random responding (e.g., always choosing the same option across all trials regardless of problem type) were flagged for inspection. A small number of participants were pre-defined to be excluded if they failed comprehension checks or if they reported not understanding the task during debriefing. No participants were excluded for medical or psychotropic conditions. All analyses were conducted using established statistical software and the logistic mixed-effects models used maximum likelihood estimation with standard error corrections.

The study was not preregistered, but analytic decisions were made prior to inspecting the data and the primary contrasts and correlational analyses reported here were specified in writing before data collection began. The sample size of 120 participants with two groups of 60 was selected to provide approximately 80% power to detect medium-sized differences (Cohen’s d around 0.5) in the primary outcome at an alpha level of 0.05. This power calculation assumed moderate within-subject consistency and that the proportion-based measure would have reasonable variance under the chosen trial structure.

Results

Data from all 120 participants were available for analysis after initial screening. No participants met the pre-specified exclusion criteria for failing comprehension checks or for reporting that they had not understood the tasks. Data were inspected for missing values, and none were found for the primary variables. Demographic characteristics were roughly balanced across the two conditions due to random assignment; mean age in the description condition was 26.4 years and in the experience condition was 27.2 years. The proportion of female participants was 57% in the description condition and 56% in the experience condition. Educational attainment did not differ significantly between groups.

Descriptive statistics for the primary outcome, the proportion of trials on which participants chose the rare-event–sensitive risky option, are presented first. In the description condition (n = 60), the mean proportion of risky choices across the 16 trials was 0.44 with a standard deviation of 0.18. In the experience condition (n = 60), the mean proportion of risky choices was 0.41 with a standard deviation of 0.19. These raw means suggested a small difference in the direction of more risky choices in the description condition, but the difference was modest in magnitude.

An independent-samples t-test comparing the mean proportions of risky choices between description and experience conditions revealed no statistically significant difference. The t-statistic was 0.62 with 118 degrees of freedom, yielding a two-tailed p-value of 0.54. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference ranged from -0.06 to 0.12, indicating that the true difference between groups could plausibly be small in either direction. Cohen’s d for the comparison was 0.11, which constitutes a very small effect size by conventional benchmarks.

Because choice data are binary and repeated within participants, a logistic mixed-effects model was fitted as a complementary analysis. The model predicted choice of the risky option on each trial as a function of condition (experience vs description) with a random intercept for participant. The fixed effect of condition was non-significant, with an estimated log-odds coefficient of -0.10 (standard error = 0.18), corresponding to an odds ratio of approximately 0.90. The Wald z-statistic for the effect of condition was -0.56 with a p-value of 0.57. Random intercept variance indicated modest between-subject variability in baseline propensity to choose the risky option.

Given the null results in frequentist tests, Bayesian t-tests were conducted to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis. For the primary comparison of risky-choice proportions between conditions, the Bayes factor in favor of the null over the alternative (BF01) was estimated at 4.6 under default priors, indicating moderate evidence supporting the null hypothesis of no difference. This Bayesian outcome suggests that the data were more likely under the null model than under a model that posits a non-zero difference between conditions.

Analyses were also conducted separately for gain-framed and loss-framed trials. In the description condition, the mean risky-choice proportion for gain trials was 0.48 and for loss trials was 0.40. In the experience condition, the corresponding proportions were 0.46 for gains and 0.36 for losses. Paired contrasts within conditions indicated that the gain-versus-loss difference was modest and not statistically significant in either condition. A three-way mixed-effects model including valence (gain vs loss), condition, and their interaction found no significant interaction between condition and valence.

The primary secondary analysis tested the relationship between dispositional optimism and risky-choice propensity. Optimism scores (LOT-R composite) ranged from 6 to 22 with a mean of 14.9 and a standard deviation of 3.8. Pearson correlations between optimism and the overall proportion of risky choices were calculated separately by condition. In the description condition, the correlation between optimism and risky-choice proportion was r = 0.05 with a p-value of 0.68, indicating no meaningful association. In the experience condition, the correlation was r = 0.03 with a p-value of 0.82. These correlations were small and non-significant, suggesting that dispositional optimism did not predict choice behavior in the present task.

To further quantify the lack of association between optimism and risky choices, Bayesian correlation analyses were performed. The Bayes factors for the correlations in both conditions favored the null, with BF01 values of approximately 5.0 for the description condition and 6.2 for the experience condition, indicating moderate evidence for the absence of a relationship.

Manipulation checks in the experience condition indicated that participants sampled a mean of 4.2 counters per problem with a standard deviation of 2.1, and sampling frequency varied across participants. The distribution of sample counts showed a mode at three samples. Participants’ post-task estimates of the rare-event probability for a randomly selected problem averaged 0.16, which exceeded the nominal objective probability of 0.10. This pattern suggests that on average participants overestimated the likelihood of the rare event in their subjective probability judgments, though this did not translate into consistent differences in choice behavior between conditions.

An exploratory analysis tested whether sampling intensity in the experience condition predicted the propensity to choose the risky option. A correlation between average number of samples and overall risky-choice proportion in the experience group was r = -0.12 with a p-value of 0.35, indicating no significant association. A regression that included both optimism and sampling intensity as predictors of risky choice also failed to produce significant coefficients for either predictor.

Response time data were examined to see whether choices took longer in one condition than the other. Mean decision time from problem presentation to choice was 7.6 seconds in the description condition and 8.1 seconds in the experience condition. This difference was not statistically significant, t(118) = 0.84, p = 0.40. Reaction time variability did not differ between groups.

Given the potential for individual trials to differ in their sensitivity to the description-experience manipulation, item-level analyses were conducted. Item-level logistic regressions indicated that certain problems elicited higher risky-choice rates than others, but there was no consistent pattern in which items exhibited a reliable condition effect. Inspection of problem-level data did not reveal a subset of problems for which the description-experience gap emerged strongly.

Robustness checks included excluding participants with extreme choice patterns (e.g., participants who chose the risky option on fewer than 2 or more than 14 trials), and re-running the primary t-test. The excluded set comprised seven participants and the t-test on the reduced dataset remained non-significant, t(111) = 0.53, p = 0.60. Similarly, repeating analyses with non-parametric tests produced consistent null findings.

Finally, the payment outcomes from the randomly selected trials were examined. The frequency of participants receiving a rare-event payoff in the experience condition was approximately 11% of the paid trials, which is close to the intended 10% probability and indicates that the physical bags produced outcomes in line with the objective probabilities across the aggregate of paid trials. There were no reports of procedural irregularities or equipment failures that could account for the null effects. Overall, the analyses provided no evidence of a description-experience gap in the present study and no support for the hypothesis that optimism predicts risky choice in either condition.

Discussion

The present study set out to replicate and extend previous findings that rare events are overweighted in description-based choices and underweighted in experience-based choices. We conducted a between-subjects experiment in which 120 participants were randomly assigned to a description condition that provided explicit probabilities or to an experience condition in which participants sampled outcomes from opaque bags containing physical counters. In contrast to prior reports, our data did not show a significant difference in the propensity to choose rare-event–sensitive risky options between the description and experience conditions. Across multiple analytic approaches, including proportion comparisons, logistic mixed-effects models, and Bayesian analyses, the evidence favored the null hypothesis of no condition difference. Additionally, dispositional optimism did not correlate with risky-choice propensity in either condition. These findings raise important considerations about the boundary conditions of the description-experience gap and about methodological factors that influence the generality of earlier findings.

One straightforward interpretation of the null findings is that the description-experience gap is not a universal phenomenon and that it can be attenuated or eliminated depending on methodological particulars. Our study introduced a key modification relative to much of the prior work by replacing computerized random draws with physical sampling from bags containing counters. It is plausible that this change in modality altered participants’ experience of sampling in ways that reduced the tendency to underweight rare events in the experience condition. If physical sampling provided a more concrete or memorable instance of a rare outcome when it occurred, participants might have been more likely to incorporate the rare event into their subjective probability estimates, thereby reducing the discrepancy between description and experience conditions.

Another possibility is that the sampling regimen in our experience condition differed in effective exposure relative to earlier studies, thereby mitigating the underweighting effect. Participants in our study sampled a modest number of draws per problem on average, and some participants drew more samples than others. Prior work suggests that the number of samples can influence whether rare events are omitted from individuals’ mental models. In settings where participants draw very few samples, rare events may fail to appear and thus be underweighted. Conversely, if participants draw enough samples to witness rare events occasionally, their representation of probability may be more accurate. Our average sampling of about 4.2 draws per problem might have been sufficient, for many participants, to yield occasional exposure to rare outcomes over the course of the task, thereby blunting the underweighting that occurs with sparser sampling.

The pattern we observed in subjective probability estimates further complicates simple interpretations. Participants in the experience condition reported post-task probability estimates for a sampled problem that averaged above the objective 0.10 rate. This subjective overestimation of rare-event likelihood contrasts with the canonical description-experience pattern and suggests that physical sampling may lead to richer or biased probability estimates that do not align with underweighting. One plausible mechanism is that the concreteness of the counters and the tangibility of the sampling process increased the salience of each rare outcome instance and thus produced an availability-like effect that biased subjective estimates upward. If so, physical sampling may produce different cognitive processes than computerized sampling: where computerized draws are seen as abstract and prone to omission, physical counters may be anchored in experience and enhanced in memory.

A separate line of explanation focuses on task instructions and payoff implementation. In the description condition, participants were told that one choice would be randomly selected and paid according to the described probabilities, a framing that may encourage participants to treat the probabilities as definitive but somewhat abstract. In contrast, in the experience condition participants could see and manipulate tokens and had direct evidence of the stochastic structure. This discrepancy may have changed participants’ mental models of the tasks in ways that are not straightforwardly reducible to mere format differences. For example, participants in the experience condition might have inferred that the bag composition was fixed and thus that future draws would reflect the actual proportions, leading to a belief in stability that influenced choice differently than predicted by simple sampling error models.

The lack of a relationship between dispositional optimism and risky-choice propensity was somewhat surprising given theoretical reasons to expect optimism to influence risk perception. Optimistic individuals could plausibly display attenuated sensitivity to low-probability negative events or a greater attraction to options that hold potential for large positive outcomes. The null correlations in both conditions may indicate that trait optimism does not have a robust effect on this class of binary gambles or that the LOT-R captures an aspect of expectation that does not map onto momentary risk-taking in laboratory tasks. It may also be that optimism interacts with other variables, such as perceived control or domain-specific beliefs, and a simple bivariate correlation is insufficient to reveal its influence.

Several methodological limitations of the current study must be acknowledged. First, although the bag-and-counter method was intended to create a tangible sampling experience, it diverged from computerized sampling in multiple respects, including sampling without replacement and the physical handling of tokens. Sampling without replacement subtly changes the dependent structure of draws and could affect beliefs about independence and probability in ways that computerized draws with replacement do not. Second, the number of samples allowed per problem was constrained by pragmatic considerations, and this may have produced heterogeneous sampling behavior that introduced noise into the critical comparisons. Third, the between-subjects design has advantages for isolating format effects, but it may be less sensitive than a within-subjects design that directly compares description and experience for the same individuals under counterbalanced conditions. Fourth, the participant sample, drawn from a university context and local community, may not generalize to broader populations, and culture or education may moderate description-experience effects.

Beyond methodological caveats, the null pattern we observed contributes to the substantive debate over the generality of the description-experience gap. Our findings suggest that the effect may be contingent on how experience is instantiated and that physical modality is a non-trivial factor. This observation aligns with a broader perspective that cognitive illusions and biases often depend on context and implementation details. It also suggests that theoretical models of decision making that aim to account for the description-experience gap need to incorporate parameters that capture modality and sampling procedures rather than treating the difference as a single switch between “description” and “experience.”

Future work should address the modality question more systematically. A direct within-subjects comparison in which participants experience all three modalities—explicit description, computerized sampling, and physical sampling—would permit stronger inferences about modality effects and individual susceptibility. Such designs could be complemented by variations in sampling protocols, including sampling with replacement versus without replacement, fixed versus self-directed sampling budgets, and varying maximum numbers of samples. More granular measures of subjective probability estimates during the task, rather than only post-task, would clarify how exposure translates into beliefs in real time.

Another promising avenue is to examine memory processes that underlie experiential learning. If the physicality of samples enhances encoding or vividness of rare events, measures of memory accessibility and vividness could be incorporated to test whether these cognitive mediators explain the attenuation of the gap. Psychophysiological measures, such as skin conductance or pupil dilation, could assess whether emotional arousal to rare outcomes differs by modality and whether that arousal predicts subsequent choices.

Robustness to different outcome magnitudes and valence domains is also important. The present study included both gains and losses but did not find differential effects by valence. Future studies could systematically vary magnitudes to test whether modality interacts with the incentive scale, since larger stakes might accentuate differences between description and experience. Relatedly, investigating real-world contexts—such as medical decision making, insurance choices, or consumer decisions—could test external validity and practical consequences of modality effects.

Large-scale replication efforts that pool data across laboratories using coordinated protocols would be valuable in resolving inconsistencies in the literature. Pre-registered multi-lab designs could test the same problems across diverse samples and manipulate modality orthogonally to other task features. Such collaborative approaches would increase power to detect small but reliable effects and would help identify moderators that explain when and why the description-experience gap emerges.

The present study also highlights potential theoretical refinements. Models that explain the description-experience gap purely in terms of sampling error may need to be extended to incorporate modality-dependent encoding fidelity and belief updating processes. Bayesian models of learning that explicitly model sampling processes and memory decay could be adapted to include modality parameters that alter the likelihood of encoding or the salience weight assigned to rare observations. Prospect-theory–style probability weighting functions might be allowed to vary with information format in formal model comparisons.

Finally, the null relationship between optimism and risky choice suggests that trait-level predictors of probability weighting may be more selective than assumed. Future research could examine interactions between optimism and situational factors such as perceived control, feedback contingency, or framing. It could also test whether optimism predicts choices in more consequential or emotionally charged decisions where dispositional expectancies may have a stronger pull.

In conclusion, the current replication attempt using physical counters inside opaque bags did not reproduce the description-experience gap reported in earlier studies that used computerized sampling. The absence of a significant difference between description and experience conditions and the lack of association between optimism and risky choice point to the importance of modality and procedural detail in eliciting probability-weighting phenomena. These results do not invalidate the original findings, but they do underscore that the description-experience distinction is not a monolithic effect that will necessarily appear under all implementations. Rather, it is sensitive to how experience is created and to the cognitive processes engaged by different sampling modalities. Resolving these complexities will require systematic investigations that manipulate modality, sampling structure, and individual differences within unified frameworks and that leverage larger, multi-site samples to obtain stable estimates of effect sizes and moderators.
