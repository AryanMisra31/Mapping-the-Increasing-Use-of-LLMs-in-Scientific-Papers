Introduction

Faces are among the most frequent and informative visual stimuli that humans encounter, and they provide a rapid source of information about identity, intent, and emotional state. From the earliest stages of development, infants preferentially attend to faces, and this early tuning to faces persists across the lifespan. The visual system appears to be specialized for extracting socially relevant information from faces, and a large body of work has examined the perceptual and cognitive mechanisms that support face processing. One line of research has focused on how emotional expressions on faces influence attention and perception, and it has been repeatedly reported that the valence of a facial expression can modulate the speed and accuracy with which faces are detected and recognized. Theoretical perspectives often posit that negative or threatening facial expressions should be prioritized by attention because they signal potential danger in the environment and therefore may have greater adaptive significance.

A robust empirical phenomenon that has been offered in support of the prioritization of threatening signals is the so-called negative face search advantage, whereby observers detect negative-valenced faces (for example, angry or fearful faces) more quickly among neutral distractors than they detect positive-valenced faces (for example, happy faces). This negative advantage has been interpreted in evolutionary terms as reflecting an attentional bias toward threat, enabling rapid detection and appropriate response. Empirical demonstrations of the negative face search advantage have used a variety of paradigms, including visual search tasks in which a target face with an emotional expression is embedded among neutral faces, and observers are asked to find the target as quickly as possible. In several such studies, negative targets yielded faster reaction times and sometimes shallower search slopes than positive targets, consistent with the claim that negative expressions capture attention more effectively.

At the same time, not all studies have found a clear negative advantage, and the literature contains numerous inconsistencies. Some investigations have reported null or even reversed effects, in which positive faces were detected as quickly as, or more quickly than, negative faces. Methodological variations across studies have been proposed as a reason for these discrepancies. For example, differences in stimulus properties such as the intensity of expressions, the identity and variability of distractor faces, the use of schematic versus photographic faces, the proportion of target-present versus target-absent trials, and the range of display sizes examined are all factors that can materially affect the presence or magnitude of a negative search advantage. In addition, task-related factors such as block structure (e.g., blocked targets by valence versus intermixed targets), instructions emphasizing speed versus accuracy, and the presence or absence of time pressure can also change observed patterns.

Beyond methodological variability, individual differences in observers’ mood and affective state have been proposed to influence the processing of emotionally valenced stimuli. The mood congruence hypothesis posits that observers will process information congruent with their current affective state more effectively; for instance, individuals in a more negative mood are more likely to notice and remember negative stimuli, whereas those in a more positive mood show enhanced processing of positive stimuli. Mood congruence effects have been extensively documented in memory research, and there is also evidence that mood states can bias attention such that stimuli matching mood valence are attended to preferentially in visual tasks. In the context of visual search for facial expressions, mood congruence would predict that participants in a relatively negative affective state should detect negative facial targets more quickly than positive targets, while participants in a relatively positive affective state should show the reverse pattern.

Researchers have operationalized mood and affect in a variety of ways when testing these ideas. Trait and state measures of affect, mood induction procedures, and clinical samples characterized by persistent negative affect (for example, depression or anxiety disorders) have all been used to examine how affective state modulates attentional prioritization. Some studies that employ direct mood induction find that induced negative mood increases attention to negative stimuli, but other studies do not find strong or consistent mood congruence effects in real-time visual search tasks. Part of the inconsistency may come from the temporal stability and intensity of mood manipulations; brief inductions may not produce the same attentional biases as more enduring mood dispositions, and self-report measures of momentary affective state can vary in sensitivity.

A related consideration is that the interaction between stimulus-driven salience (e.g., the inherent threat-signaling value of negative faces) and observer-driven factors (e.g., current mood) may be complex. It may be that highly salient negative faces automatically capture attention independently of mood, whereas subtler expressions or lower-intensity negative signals are more susceptible to modulation by mood. Similarly, task constraints and expectations might modulate the degree to which mood engages attentional systems: when targets are blocked by valence, observers may strategically allocate attention differently than when valence is unpredictable, and strategy differences could mask or amplify mood congruence effects.

The visual search literature has also examined the role of display size—how many items appear in the search display—on the efficiency of search. Classic findings show that search times typically increase as a function of display size, and the slope of the RT-by-display-size function is taken as an index of search efficiency. If a particular type of target is processed in a more parallel or pop-out-like manner, the slope will be shallower compared to targets that require more serial attention. In face search paradigms, display size manipulations have therefore been used to probe whether emotional targets benefit from enhanced parallel processing. Findings in this area have again been mixed; some work suggests negative faces are searched more efficiently and produce shallower slopes, while other work finds equivalent slopes for positive and negative targets.

Given the mixed evidence for both the negative face search advantage and mood congruence effects, a careful empirical test that combines controlled visual search manipulations with direct measurement of participants’ affective state is valuable. Such an experiment can help adjudicate whether mood exerts a modulatory influence on the putative negative advantage and whether the negative advantage itself robustly emerges under tightly controlled conditions. Importantly, using a blocked design in which a target valence is constant within blocks but varies across blocks is one methodological choice that can help reveal strategic allocation effects: if observers adopt a search template tuned to the block-specific target valence, this may either enhance congruent processing or reveal inherent valence-driven differences in search efficiency.

The present study was designed to examine the interplay between target valence (positive versus negative), display size, and participants’ affective state in a visual search task using facial stimuli. Participants completed a standardized positive and negative affect schedule to quantify their current affective state and then performed a visual search task in which they indicated the presence of either a positive or a negative target face embedded among neutral distractors. Target valence was blocked, and display sizes varied across trials. The primary dependent measure was reaction time on target-present trials, and accuracy was recorded as a secondary outcome to ensure that speed differences were not explained by speed-accuracy tradeoffs.

We derived specific hypotheses from theoretical considerations. First, based on the negative face search advantage literature, we hypothesized that participants would detect negative targets faster than positive targets overall, and that negative targets would show shallower search slopes as a function of display size. Second, based on mood congruence theories, we hypothesized that participants with higher reported negative affect would show a relative advantage for negative targets, and participants with higher positive affect would show a relative advantage for positive targets. In other words, we expected an interaction such that affect would modulate the effect of target valence on reaction times. Third, we hypothesized that display size would influence reaction times in the typical manner, producing longer RTs for larger displays, and that any valence advantage might be more apparent at larger display sizes where serial search processes are more engaged.

The study was thus set up to test whether mood-congruence processes and the negative face search advantage operate independently or interactively in guiding visual attention to emotional faces. By measuring natural variability in affect and using a blocked target-valence design with multiple display sizes, the experiment sought to provide a rigorous test of these theoretical claims. If mood does modulate visual search in this paradigm, it would support accounts emphasizing top-down influences on attentional selection. Conversely, if mood has little or no effect and a negative advantage does emerge, this would bolster claims that threat-related stimuli receive priority regardless of current affective state. Finally, if neither effect is observed, the study would suggest that previously reported effects may be highly context-dependent, subject to methodological specifics, or influenced by other unmeasured factors. This rationale motivated the present investigation.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from the local community. In total, 72 individuals completed the study and provided usable data; four additional volunteers were tested but excluded prior to analysis because of technical failure or noncompliance with task instructions. Participants were adults between the ages of 18 and 45 years, with a mean age of 22.8 years and a standard deviation of 4.6 years. The sample comprised 46 women and 26 men; participants self-reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological disorders. All participants provided informed consent and were compensated with course credit or a small monetary honorarium.

A widely used self-report measure of current affect was administered to assess each participant’s positive and negative affective states. Participants completed the positive and negative affect schedule (PANAS) immediately before beginning the visual search task. The PANAS yields two scores for each participant, one reflecting positive affect (PA) and one reflecting negative affect (NA), each computed as the sum of ratings across a set of mood-descriptive adjectives. Participants rated their affect "right now" using the standard instructions of the PANAS, providing a snapshot of state affect. The PA and NA scores were used as continuous covariates in analyses designed to test for mood congruence effects; for supplementary analyses, the sample was also split at the median into higher and lower PA and NA subgroups.

Stimuli consisted of grayscale photographs of adult faces displaying neutral, happy, and angry expressions. Facial stimuli were drawn from a standard set of face images that contains multiple identities with validated emotional expressions; identities were balanced for gender, and images were normalized for size and luminance to reduce low-level visual confounds. Each face was presented on a uniform background and subtended approximately 2 degrees of visual angle when viewed at a typical monitor distance. Distractor faces always displayed a neutral expression; target faces displayed either a happy expression (positive target trials) or an angry expression (negative target trials). Stimulus selection ensured that positive and negative target faces were comparable in overall intensity based on the standard set’s normative ratings.

The visual search task employed a within-subjects design with two factors: target valence (positive target block, negative target block) and display size (4, 8, or 12 faces). Each participant completed two target-valence blocks; block order was counterbalanced across participants so that half of the sample encountered the positive-target block first and the other half began with the negative-target block. Within each block, trials varied randomly in display size. Each display contained exactly one target face among neutral distractors on target-present trials. Only target-present trials were analyzed for reaction time comparisons, as the interest was in the speed of detecting the target when present; target-absent trials were included in the task to encourage participants to perform the task accurately and to reduce predictability, but they were excluded from the primary RT analyses.

Participants received instructions to respond as quickly and accurately as possible by pressing one of two keys to indicate whether the target was present or absent. Trials began with a fixation cross presented for 500 ms, followed by the search display which remained on-screen until the participant made a response or until 3000 ms elapsed. Intertrial intervals varied randomly between 800 and 1200 ms. Each block contained 180 trials, for a total of 360 trials per participant. Within each block, the proportion of trials at each display size was balanced, and targets were equally likely to appear at any screen location. A brief practice phase preceded the main experiment to familiarize participants with the task and response mapping.

Reaction time data were collected on target-present trials only and were subjected to standard preprocessing. Trials with incorrect responses were removed from the RT analyses. Additionally, trials with response times below 200 ms or above 3000 ms were excluded as anticipatory or inattentive responses, respectively. For each participant, RTs exceeding 3 standard deviations from that participant’s mean were also trimmed to reduce the influence of outliers. The proportion of trials removed by these criteria was small (mean = 3.1% of target-present trials), and no participant lost more than 12% of trials due to trimming. Accuracy was calculated separately as the percentage of correct responses on target-present and target-absent trials.

The analytic approach used repeated-measures ANOVA to test the effects of target valence and display size on reaction times, with PA and NA scores entered as continuous covariates to test for mood congruence interactions. Because the PA and NA scores are orthogonal in conception, both were retained in the same models. Primary statistical tests examined main effects of valence and display size, the valence × display size interaction, and three-way interactions involving affect measures. Where appropriate, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied to degrees of freedom for tests that violated sphericity, although the three-level display size factor generally met sphericity assumptions. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta squared for ANOVA effects and Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons. Secondary analyses computed search efficiency slopes by conducting participant-level linear regressions of mean RT as a function of display size separately for positive and negative target blocks; slopes (ms/item) and intercepts (ms) were then compared across valence conditions using paired-samples t-tests.

To assess whether the absence of mood effects might be due to insufficient variability in affect scores, supplementary analyses examined the distribution of PA and NA scores and tested mood interactions after dichotomizing the sample by median split into high and low PA and high and low NA groups. Additional exploratory analyses tested whether block order (positive-block-first vs negative-block-first) influenced overall performance or interacted with valence effects, as strategic factors could be sensitive to order. All reported p-values are two-tailed, and α was set at .05 for primary hypotheses. Data processing and statistical analyses were performed using standard statistical software.

Results

Preliminary inspection of the PANAS data revealed that PA scores ranged from 12 to 34 with a mean of 22.1 and a standard deviation of 4.6, while NA scores ranged from 10 to 28 with a mean of 13.9 and a standard deviation of 3.7. The distributions were not severely skewed, and both PA and NA showed sufficient variability to support correlational and covariate analyses. PA and NA scores were modestly negatively correlated in this sample (r = -0.21), indicating some inverse relation but not redundancy. The median split for PA yielded a low-PA group (n = 36) and a high-PA group (n = 36), and an analogous split for NA produced low-NA and high-NA subgroups of equal size.

Accuracy on the visual search task was high overall, indicating that participants generally performed the task as instructed. Mean accuracy on target-present trials was 94.7% (SD = 3.8), and mean accuracy on target-absent trials was 92.3% (SD = 4.6). There was no reliable main effect of target valence on accuracy; mean accuracy for positive-target blocks was 94.9% and for negative-target blocks was 94.5%, a non-significant difference (t(71) = 0.84, p = .40). Accuracy did decrease modestly with larger display sizes, as expected, but this did not interact with target valence. These accuracy patterns suggest that any reaction time differences are not easily explained by speed-accuracy trade-offs.

After trimming for outliers and erroneous responses, the mean reaction time across all target-present trials was 723 ms with a between-subject standard deviation of 118 ms. Raw mean RTs for each condition provided an initial descriptive picture: for display size 4, mean RTs were 613 ms for positive targets and 639 ms for negative targets; for display size 8, mean RTs were 716 ms for positive and 753 ms for negative; and for display size 12, mean RTs were 835 ms for positive and 887 ms for negative targets. These descriptive values suggest that RTs increased with display size and that positive targets were processed more quickly than negative targets across display sizes.

A 2 (target valence: positive, negative) × 3 (display size: 4, 8, 12) repeated-measures ANOVA on mean RTs revealed a significant main effect of target valence, F(1,71) = 5.87, p = .018, partial η2 = .076, indicating that participants were faster to detect positive targets (M = 721 ms, SD = 105) than negative targets (M = 760 ms, SD = 119) overall. The main effect of display size was highly significant, F(2,142) = 112.38, p < .001, partial η2 = .613, with RTs reliably increasing as display size increased. The valence × display size interaction was not statistically significant, F(2,142) = 1.42, p = .246, partial η2 = .020, suggesting that the relative advantage for positive targets was present across display sizes rather than being specific to a particular set size.

Because the negative face search advantage literature often emphasizes search efficiency differences, we conducted participant-level regressions of RT on display size for each valence block to estimate search slopes. The mean search slope for positive targets was 21.5 ms per item (SD = 9.4), whereas the mean slope for negative targets was 28.9 ms per item (SD = 12.1). A paired-samples t-test comparing slopes revealed a significant difference, t(71) = 3.01, p = .003, Cohen’s d = 0.35, such that negative targets yielded steeper slopes and therefore less efficient search than positive targets. These slope results corroborate the main-effect finding showing faster detection of positive targets and suggest that positive targets were processed more efficiently across increasing display sizes.

We next examined whether affective state as measured by the PANAS influenced search performance or interacted with target valence. To do so, PA and NA scores were entered as continuous covariates in the repeated-measures ANOVA. Neither PA nor NA produced significant main effects on RTs, and neither covariate interacted significantly with target valence. Specifically, the target valence × PA interaction was non-significant, F(1,69) = 0.43, p = .514, and the target valence × NA interaction was also non-significant, F(1,69) = 0.21, p = .648. No three-way interactions that included display size and either PA or NA reached significance. These null effects suggest that within the present sample and task, individual differences in self-reported positive or negative affect did not modulate the relative speed of detecting positive versus negative facial targets.

Supplementary analyses used median-split groupings to test for potential non-linear effects of mood that might not be captured by linear covariate analyses. Participants in the high-PA group were compared to those in the low-PA group in terms of the valence effect (RT difference between positive and negative targets). The interaction of valence by PA group did not reach significance, F(1,70) = 0.67, p = .416. Similarly, splitting by NA produced no significant valence × NA group interaction, F(1,70) = 0.82, p = .369. These additional tests provide converging evidence that mood congruence effects did not emerge in this paradigm.

To probe whether block order might have influenced the valence effect, we tested the target valence × block order interaction. Block order did not significantly interact with target valence on RTs, F(1,70) = 1.14, p = .289, and there was no evidence that starting with positive or negative targets led to different patterns of search efficiency. We also assessed whether PA or NA related to overall RT variability or to accuracy; these correlations were small and non-significant (all |r| < .12, all ps > .30). Thus, the mood measures did not predict task performance in any robust way.

Given the main effect favoring positive targets, we explored whether any low-level visual differences might account for the pattern by examining RT variance across target identities. Analyses showed that the positive advantage was consistent across multiple identities and both genders of stimuli, and it was not driven by a small subset of faces. Therefore, stimulus-level idiosyncrasies are unlikely to explain the overall valence effect. Overall, the pattern of results runs counter to the classic negative face search advantage and fails to provide support for mood congruence biases facilitating search in the current task.

Discussion

The present study combined a visual search paradigm using emotional facial expressions with measurement of participants’ current affective state to investigate two related questions about attentional prioritization. First, we tested whether a negative face search advantage would emerge in a blocked target-valence design with multiple display sizes. Second, we examined whether mood congruence biases would modulate search such that participants with greater negative affect would detect negative targets more quickly and those with greater positive affect would detect positive targets more quickly. The data produced two clear findings. Participants were faster overall to detect positive targets than negative targets, and search slopes indicated more efficient search for positive targets; conversely, participants’ self-reported positive and negative affect scores failed to modulate the valence effects, providing no evidence for mood congruence in this protocol. These outcomes bear directly on theoretical accounts that posit a privileged status for negative, threat-signaling faces and on models that emphasize top-down mood-based biases in attentional selection.

The observation that positive facial targets were detected faster and with shallower search slopes than negative targets in this task stands in contrast to the negative face search advantage that has been emphasized in some prior work. Several potential explanations for this reversal are worth considering. First, the specific stimuli used in any visual search task can powerfully shape outcomes. Although we selected target faces from a standardized set and controlled for overall intensity and low-level features, subtle differences in salience or distinctiveness of happy versus angry expressions could have influenced detectability. Happy faces are often characterized by a prominent smile and exposed teeth, features that may produce greater local contrast or a more salient configuration that stands out from neutral distractors. Even when normative intensity ratings are comparable across valence, perceptual salience and its interaction with the particular neutral distractors in the display can yield asymmetries favoring positive targets.

Second, the blocked design in which target valence is constant within blocks may have encouraged strategic deployment of search templates that facilitated positive target detection. When participants know what to look for across an entire block, they may develop an efficient search routine that is more effective for targets with certain visual configurations. If positive targets produce a more easily specified template—for example, the presence of raised cheeks and an upward-curving mouth—participants might be able to instantiate a more precise attentional template for positive targets than for negative targets, yielding greater efficiency. By contrast, angry faces may share more features with neutral distractors (such as furrowed brows that are present to varying degrees even in neutral expressions), making template-based search less effective.

Third, task context and instructions could modulate valence effects. The present task emphasized both speed and accuracy, and the high overall accuracy suggests participants adopted a careful approach. Under these conditions, the presence of unique low-level features in positive faces may have provided a robust cue that allowed quicker confirmation of target presence without sacrificing accuracy. Previous demonstrations of a negative advantage sometimes relied on different proportions of target-present and target-absent trials, time pressure, or other procedural elements that might accentuate threat-driven attentional capture. It is plausible that the negative advantage is more likely to emerge under conditions that heighten the salience or urgency associated with threat detection, whereas in balanced laboratory tasks positive features may sometimes produce superior detectability.

The failure to observe mood congruence effects in the present study also warrants careful interpretation. Mood congruence in attention is a theoretically attractive idea, and several studies have reported that negative mood increases attention to negative stimuli. However, evidence across studies is mixed, and the current null results add to the literature suggesting that mood congruence effects are not universal or may be contingent on specific methodological factors. One reason for the null findings may be that the PANAS, while reliable for measuring state affect, captures participants’ introspective reports rather than the moment-to-moment, automatic affective biases that might be most relevant to rapid visual search. Self-reported affect may not fully match the affective processes that influence early visual selection. In addition, the range of mood variation in our non-clinical sample was fairly constrained: although there was adequate variability, extreme negative affect that might produce sizable attentional biases was uncommon. Clinical samples or targeted mood induction procedures might therefore be necessary to reveal robust mood-congruence effects on search.

Another possibility is that the strong bottom-up salience of the target stimuli overwhelms subtle top-down mood effects. If the attentional system’s initial orienting is dominated by perceptual salience and configural features that differ across expressions, then mood may not be able to modulate early detection. In other words, mood congruence may exert influence more strongly on later stages of processing, such as sustained attention or memory consolidation, rather than on the early perceptual processes tapped by simple visual search measures. Furthermore, the blocked design could have reduced the opportunity for mood to bias attentional set. When participants adopt a consistent template across a block, that template may dominate selection regardless of incidental mood variations.

It is also instructive to consider that mood congruence effects may be non-linear or contingent on interactive factors not captured in the present analyses. For instance, mood may interact with arousal, trait anxiety, or motivational states, and without measuring these additional constructs we cannot rule out more complex relationships. Moreover, mood congruence could be more evident when mood is experimentally induced immediately prior to task performance rather than measured as a naturally occurring state. Experimental inductions can create more pronounced and temporally aligned mood shifts, potentially producing more reliable biases in attention.

The present findings have implications for theoretical claims about a universal negative advantage in face detection. They suggest that the prioritization of negative expressions is not an invariant property of the visual system but may be modulated by stimulus features, task design, and perhaps by motivational or strategic factors. If negative faces were universally privileged because of evolutionary pressures, one might expect a robust advantage across a wide range of tasks and contexts. The current data argue for a more nuanced view in which threat-related prioritization can occur but is context-dependent and can be overridden under certain laboratory conditions that favor the detection of positive features.

There are several clear limitations of the present study that should be acknowledged and that point to directions for future research. First, although the PANAS is well-validated, the reliance on a single self-report instrument to index affect means we may not have captured all relevant aspects of mood that influence attention. Incorporating physiological measures of arousal, momentary mood inductions, or ecological momentary assessment of affect could provide additional sensitivity. Second, the choice to analyze only target-present trials for the primary RT analyses is defensible for isolating detection speed, but inclusion of target-absent trials and analysis of false alarms could provide complementary insights into decision criteria and search termination processes. Third, the controlled laboratory setting and the use of standardized face stimuli limit generalizability; real-world social contexts and dynamic facial expressions may engage different attentional mechanisms.

Future research could address these issues in several ways. One valuable approach would be to use eye-tracking to measure gaze allocation and the time course of attentional orienting; eye movements can reveal whether mood affects initial orienting or later dwell times on emotional faces. Another approach would be to use psychophysiological measures such as pupil dilation or event-related potentials to index arousal and early sensory processing that might be sensitive to mood and valence. Experimental mood inductions, including both positive and negative manipulations, could be used to test causal effects of mood on search efficiency and to examine whether induced mood produces stronger or different patterns than naturalistic self-reports. Additionally, manipulating the perceptual salience of positive and negative targets (for example, by equating contrast or spatial frequency) could help disentangle whether valence effects are driven by emotion per se or by associated low-level features.

Studying clinical populations or individuals with elevated trait negative affect could also be informative, as these groups may exhibit more pronounced mood-congruent attentional biases. Finally, expanding stimulus sets to include a greater diversity of facial expressions, cultural backgrounds, and display complexities, including dynamic or multimodal cues, would help determine the boundary conditions for valence-driven attentional prioritization.

In conclusion, the present research found that positive facial targets were detected more rapidly and with greater search efficiency than negative targets in a blocked visual search task with neutral distractors, and that individual differences in self-reported positive and negative affect did not reliably modulate these effects. These findings challenge the notion of a universal negative face search advantage and suggest that mood congruence effects on early visual search are not robust in all contexts. Together, the results underscore the importance of considering stimulus properties, task design, and the measurement of affect when drawing conclusions about how emotional expressions guide attention. Further work employing complementary methodologies and broader manipulations of affect and stimulus salience will be necessary to delineate the precise conditions under which valence and mood shape the allocation of visual attention.
