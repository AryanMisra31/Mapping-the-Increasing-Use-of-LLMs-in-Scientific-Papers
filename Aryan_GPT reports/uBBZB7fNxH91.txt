Introduction

Human memory is a reconstructive process that is inherently susceptible to distortions introduced after an event. The accuracy of memories for observed events is of central importance in a range of applied contexts, and nowhere is this more apparent than in the domain of eyewitness testimony. Courts, law-enforcement practices, and social judgments rely heavily on reports provided by observers, and errors in memory can lead to serious consequences for individuals and society. Over decades of research, psychologists have documented a variety of factors that influence the fidelity of memory reports, including encoding conditions, retention interval, stress, attention, and post-event information. One of the most robust findings within this literature is that memory for witnessed events can be altered by misleading information presented after the event. This phenomenon, often referred to as the misinformation effect or post-event suggestibility, has been demonstrated in many contexts and with many manipulations.

A classic demonstration of the influence of wording on memory comes from research that asked participants to estimate speeds of vehicles after watching a short film of an automobile collision. These studies showed that a subtle change in the verb used in a question about the speed of the cars, such as replacing the word "hit" with "smashed," led to systematic differences in participants' speed estimates. Moreover, the stronger verb not only inflated speed estimates but also increased the probability that participants would falsely remember seeing broken glass when none had been present in the film. These findings are frequently cited to illustrate how a simple linguistic manipulation in a question can alter both quantitative and qualitative aspects of memory.

Several accounts have been proposed to explain how leading questions exert their effects. One account suggests that the wording of a question introduces post-event information that becomes incorporated into a revised memory representation, effectively changing what the observer believes occurred. This is sometimes conceptualized as a form of source monitoring error, in which participants misattribute the origin of a remembered detail to the original event rather than to the post-event suggestion. Another account emphasizes that certain words provide retrieval cues that bias the reconstruction process at the time of recall without necessarily altering the stored memory trace. According to this view, the question wording primes a particular interpretation and thereby shifts the retrieval pathways that are used to reconstruct the memory. A complementary perspective points to social and pragmatic processes: participants may interpret the experimenter's wording as informative and adjust their answers to align with perceived social expectations or norms.

In parallel with work on suggestibility, researchers have investigated metacognitive variables such as confidence and how they relate to memory report and susceptibility to influence. Confidence is often treated both as an indicator of subjective conviction about a memory and as a variable that can shape subsequent judgments and behaviors. In some research contexts, confidence has been found to correlate imperfectly with accuracy, and high confidence is not always a reliable indicator of correct memory. Nonetheless, confidence can influence how individuals weigh new information, how they evaluate competing sources, and how resistant they are to suggestion. For instance, people who feel more confident about their abilities or the reliability of their perceptions may be less prone to accept external information that conflicts with their internal impressions.

Experimental manipulations of confidence have used a range of methods. Some studies manipulate confidence via feedback about performance on a task, providing positive or negative comments that either bolster or undermine self-assessment. Other work has manipulated confidence through training, performance pressure, social comparison, or explicit instructions about the accuracy of prior decisions. Across these manipulations, feedback that signals success tends to increase self-reported confidence and can alter subsequent task engagement and decision style. For memory tasks specifically, feedback has been shown to influence not only subjective confidence ratings but also subsequent willingness to revise recollections in the face of conflicting information.

The intersection of confidence and suggestibility raises several theoretically important questions. If confidence serves as a metacognitive resource that supports source monitoring and skepticism toward external inputs, then increasing confidence might be expected to reduce susceptibility to leading questions. Conversely, if confidence simply amplifies the subjective conviction associated with a constructed narrative, higher confidence could render individuals more willing to assert a memory that has been influenced by external cues. These opposing possibilities make the direction of the effect an empirical question. Understanding whether confidence modulates susceptibility has practical implications for legal settings, where witnesses may receive feedback from investigators, attorneys, or media, and where confidence can shape perceptions of credibility despite sometimes weak relations with accuracy.

Prior empirical work on the relationship between confidence and suggestibility is mixed. Some studies suggest that individuals with higher baseline confidence are less likely to change their responses after exposure to misinformation, while other studies indicate no systematic relationship or even increased suggestibility among high-confidence individuals under certain conditions. A complicating factor is that confidence itself is not a unitary construct: momentary confidence about a particular judgment may differ from trait-like self-efficacy, and manipulations that temporarily alter confidence may not have the same effects as stable differences across individuals. Moreover, methodological differences across studies, such as the timing of the manipulation, the nature of the feedback, and the type of memory test, make it difficult to draw strong generalizations.

Given these mixed findings and the theoretical importance of clarifying the role of confidence, the present study sought to investigate whether experimentally induced confidence would influence the magnitude of the effect of leading questions on memory for an observed event. Specifically, we adopted a well-established experimental paradigm in which participants watch a short film depicting a car collision and later estimate the speed of the vehicles. In keeping with the classic design, the leading question manipulation was implemented by varying the verb used to describe the collision in a critical question, with the expectation that a stronger verb would lead to higher speed estimates.

To manipulate confidence, participants completed a short series of practice trials before viewing the critical video. During these practice trials, participants received either positive feedback indicating good performance or negative feedback indicating poor performance, thereby inducing higher or lower confidence, respectively. This feedback-based approach is grounded in prior work demonstrating that external performance evaluations reliably alter self-perceived competence and task-specific confidence. A manipulation check consisting of self-reported confidence ratings was included to assess the efficacy of the feedback procedure.

The study employed a factorial design crossing the confidence manipulation (high vs. low) with the leading question verb (strong vs. weak). The central hypothesis was twofold. First, we hypothesized a main effect of question wording such that participants exposed to the stronger verb would provide higher speed estimates than those exposed to the weaker verb, replicating the classic effect. Second, we hypothesized that the confidence manipulation would moderate this effect, with the expected direction being that participants in the high-confidence condition would be less influenced by the leading verb, thereby showing a smaller difference in speed estimates across verb conditions compared to participants in the low-confidence condition. This directional hypothesis was premised on the idea that heightened confidence would bolster individuals' reliance on their own perceptual impressions and promote resistance to externally introduced cues.

In addition to testing these primary hypotheses, we planned exploratory analyses to examine the relation between self-reported confidence and speed estimates within each condition, as well as checks for potential covariates such as prior driving experience, attention to the video, and demographic variables. We also examined whether the confidence manipulation had any main effect on overall speed estimates independent of the verb manipulation. The study aimed to provide empirical clarity about whether a brief, feedback-based manipulation of confidence can alter the well-established influence of leading questions on memory reports.

By choosing a replication of a classic paradigm, the study sought to situate the confidence question within a well-understood empirical context and thereby provide clear insight into boundary conditions of the misinformation effect. If the confidence manipulation reduced suggestibility, this would imply that metacognitive states play a causal role in moderating the uptake of post-event information. Conversely, if confidence had no effect, this would suggest that some forms of suggestibility are robust to fluctuations in momentary confidence induced by feedback. Either outcome would have implications for theories of memory, source monitoring, and the application of findings to forensic practice. The present investigation therefore addresses a theoretically meaningful gap in the literature and offers an opportunity to refine our understanding of how metacognitive variables interact with social and linguistic influences on memory.

Methods

Participants

Sixty adult participants were recruited from a university subject pool and the surrounding community. Recruitment materials invited individuals aged 18 to 45 to participate in a study of perception and memory for a short film. Interested individuals were screened for normal or corrected-to-normal vision and for fluency in English, and anyone reporting vision deficiencies that could not be corrected or insufficient English was excluded. Participants received course credit or a modest monetary stipend for participation. The final sample included 60 participants after initial screening, and no additional participants were excluded for failing attention checks or other pre-established exclusion criteria.

Design

The experiment employed a 2 x 2 between-subjects factorial design. The first factor was confidence induction, with two levels: high-confidence induction and low-confidence induction. The second factor was the leading question verb, with two levels: strong verb ("smashed") and weak verb ("hit"). Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four cells of the design, yielding approximately 15 participants per cell. Random assignment was achieved using pre-generated randomization lists balanced across time blocks of data collection.

Materials

The primary stimulus was a short, 5-second video clip depicting a collision between two cars at an intersection. The clip was selected because it provided clear visual information about the motion of the vehicles and there was no visible broken glass or dramatic debris depicted. The video was presented on a computer monitor in a quiet laboratory room, with participants seated at a comfortable viewing distance. Sound was not a central feature of the clip and was not necessary for the dependent measures.

For the confidence induction, three brief practice trials were prepared. Each practice trial involved viewing a different short video clip followed by an objective question about a detail of the clip, such as the color of an object or the position of a car. These practice trials were designed to be of comparable difficulty and to provide an opportunity for participants to receive feedback. For participants assigned to the high-confidence condition, the feedback messages were framed positively and indicated that the participant had performed well and that their judgments were accurate. For participants assigned to the low-confidence condition, the feedback messages were framed negatively and indicated that their performance was poor and that they should be cautious about their judgments. Care was taken to keep the wording of feedback standardized in length and format across conditions, differing primarily in valence.

A brief questionnaire was constructed to capture the dependent measures and manipulation checks. The key dependent variable was an open-ended speed estimate in miles per hour (mph), elicited after the critical question about the collision. The leading question varied by condition: for the strong verb condition participants were asked, "About how fast were the cars going when they smashed into each other?" For the weak verb condition participants were asked, "About how fast were the cars going when they hit each other?" Participants were instructed to provide their best estimate in numeric form. In addition to the speed estimate, participants completed a manipulation-check item asking how confident they felt about their judgments on the practice trials and about their perception of the critical video, using a 7-point Likert scale anchored with "Not at all confident" and "Extremely confident." Additional items assessed attention to the video, prior experience with driving (including years of driving and self-rated driving experience), and demographic information such as age and gender.

Procedure

Participants provided informed consent and were told that the study examined perception and memory. The experimenter explained that the session would include a few practice trials followed by a main task. Participants were randomly assigned to either the high- or low-confidence feedback condition and to either the "smashed" or "hit" leading question condition. Participants first completed the three practice trials. On each practice trial they viewed a brief clip and answered a simple objective question. After each practice trial, participants received feedback that varied by condition. In the high-confidence condition, feedback emphasized accuracy and competence, using statements such as "Good work — your answers were highly accurate. You seem to perceive details reliably." In the low-confidence condition, feedback emphasized error and uncertainty, using statements such as "Your answers showed notable errors. You may want to be cautious about your judgments." The feedback was delivered in written form on the computer screen for a fixed interval to ensure that participants had time to read it.

Following the practice trials, participants completed a manipulation-check item rating how confident they felt about their performance on the practice trials. This provided a direct assessment of whether the feedback influenced self-reported confidence. Participants were then told they would view the main video. The critical collision clip was presented once without interruption. Immediately after viewing the clip, participants were asked the leading question appropriate to their assigned verb condition and were asked to provide a numeric estimate of the cars' speed in mph. Participants then completed the remaining questionnaire items, including the second confidence item rating their confidence about their perception of the critical video, attention checks, and demographic questions.

At the conclusion of the session, participants were debriefed. The debriefing explained the purpose of the feedback manipulation and the rationale for the leading question, and care was taken to ensure participants understood the deceptive elements of the feedback. Participants were given the opportunity to ask questions and were provided with contact information for follow-up.

Measures and scoring

The primary dependent measure was the speed estimate in mph. Responses were entered into a spreadsheet and checked for outliers and implausible values. A small number of extreme responses were identified as transcription errors and were corrected by re-checking the participant's original response sheet. No responses were removed based on predetermined criteria. Self-reported confidence scores from the manipulation-check item were treated as continuous variables on a 1 to 7 scale. Attention-check items were used to identify any participants who had failed to attend to the main clip; no participant failed the attention checks.

Data analysis plan

All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. The primary analysis was a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with confidence induction (high vs. low) and leading verb (smashed vs. hit) as between-subjects factors and the speed estimate as the dependent variable. The ANOVA tested for main effects of verb and confidence, as well as their interaction. In addition to the omnibus ANOVA, planned pairwise comparisons were conducted to examine whether the verb effect (smashed vs. hit) was present within each confidence condition. For the manipulation check, independent-samples t-tests compared self-reported confidence between the high- and low-confidence groups. Assumption checks, including tests for homogeneity of variance and inspection of residuals for normality, were performed. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared for ANOVA results and Cohen's d for t-tests. Alpha was set at .05 for all inferential tests. Exploratory correlations between self-reported confidence and speed estimates were computed as Pearson correlation coefficients.

Ethical considerations

The protocol was reviewed and approved by the relevant institutional ethics board. The feedback manipulation involved mild deception about performance feedback; participants were fully debriefed about this at the end of the session and were offered the option to have their data withdrawn if they expressed discomfort. No adverse events occurred, and participants reported no distress during debriefing.

Results

Preliminary data checks and descriptive statistics

Data from all 60 participants were included in the analyses. The sample was balanced across conditions with 15 participants assigned to each of the four cells of the 2 x 2 design. Descriptive statistics for the primary dependent variable, speed estimate in mph, were computed for each cell. For the smashed/ high-confidence cell, the mean speed estimate was 45.0 mph with a standard deviation of 9.8. For the smashed/ low-confidence cell, the mean was 42.0 mph with a standard deviation of 12.0. For the hit/ high-confidence cell, the mean was 35.2 mph with a standard deviation of 8.8. For the hit/ low-confidence cell, the mean was 33.5 mph with a standard deviation of 11.2. The overall pattern suggested a robust effect of the verb manipulation, with smashed conditions yielding higher speed estimates than hit conditions, and relatively modest differences across confidence conditions.

Manipulation check

The manipulation check compared self-reported confidence about the practice trials between the high- and low-confidence induction groups. Participants in the high-confidence induction condition reported higher confidence (mean = 5.6, SD = 0.9) than did participants in the low-confidence induction condition (mean = 3.2, SD = 1.1). An independent-samples t-test indicated that this difference was statistically significant, t(58) = 10.22, p < .001, Cohen's d = 2.63. The manipulation thus achieved its proximal goal of altering participants' self-reported confidence regarding the practice task.

Assumption checks

Prior to conducting the primary ANOVA, the data were examined for violations of assumptions. Levene's test for equality of variances across the four cells was non-significant, F(3,56) = 1.02, p = .39, indicating acceptable homogeneity of variance. Inspection of the residuals and Q-Q plots suggested approximate normality of the error terms, with no extreme skew or kurtosis. Given these results, the planned ANOVA was deemed appropriate.

Primary ANOVA

A two-way between-subjects ANOVA was conducted with leading verb (smashed vs. hit) and confidence induction (high vs. low) as factors and speed estimate in mph as the dependent variable. There was a significant main effect of leading verb, F(1,56) = 46.12, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .45, indicating that participants asked with the verb "smashed" provided higher speed estimates than those asked with the verb "hit." The main effect of confidence induction was not significant, F(1,56) = 1.73, p = .19, partial eta-squared = .03, suggesting that the feedback-based confidence manipulation did not produce a reliable main effect on speed judgments across verbs. The interaction between leading verb and confidence induction was not statistically significant, F(1,56) = 2.86, p = .096, partial eta-squared = .05, though the p-value indicated a trend-level effect.

Planned comparisons

Planned pairwise comparisons examined the verb effect within each confidence condition. In the high-confidence group, the difference between smashed (M = 45.0, SD = 9.8) and hit (M = 35.2, SD = 8.8) was significant, t(28) = 3.60, p = .001, Cohen's d = 1.30. In the low-confidence group, the difference between smashed (M = 42.0, SD = 12.0) and hit (M = 33.5, SD = 11.2) was also significant, t(28) = 2.78, p = .009, Cohen's d = 1.04. These comparisons confirm that the classic verb effect was present within both levels of the confidence induction.

Interaction trend

Although the interaction term in the ANOVA did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance, the means suggested a directionality consistent with the hypothesis that confidence might moderate susceptibility. The numerical difference between smashed and hit conditions appeared slightly larger in the high-confidence group (difference = 9.8 mph) than in the low-confidence group (difference = 8.5 mph), rather than smaller as hypothesized. This pattern indicates that if there were any moderation by confidence, it did not operate in the predicted direction in the aggregated means, and the observed trend was not statistically reliable.

Exploratory correlations

Exploratory analyses examined the relation between participants' self-reported confidence about the critical video and their speed estimates. Across the entire sample, the correlation between confidence about the critical video and speed estimate was small and not statistically significant, r = .12, p = .35. When examined within the verb conditions separately, the correlations remained non-significant for both smashed, r = .08, p = .72, and hit, r = .14, p = .49, conditions. These results suggest that momentary confidence about the critical viewing episode was not strongly associated with quantitative speed estimates.

Additional checks

Additional checks explored whether years of driving experience or self-rated driving skill were associated with speed estimates. Correlations between years of driving and speed estimates were negligible, r = .05, p = .72, and correlations between self-rated driving skill and speed estimates were also small, r = -.07, p = .61. There was no evidence that demographic variables such as age or gender systematically influenced the primary outcomes.

Power considerations

An a priori power calculation was not performed prior to data collection; however, post hoc considerations indicate that with 60 participants and an observed medium-to-large main effect of the verb manipulation, the design was adequately powered to detect the principal replication effect. Detection of a reliable interaction effect of modest size would have required a larger sample, and the present sample size limits the precision of estimates of moderation effects.

Summary of primary findings

In summary, the study replicated the classic effect of leading question wording on speed estimates: participants exposed to the stronger verb estimated higher speeds than those exposed to the weaker verb. The feedback-based confidence induction effectively altered self-reported confidence on a manipulation check, but it did not produce a significant main effect on speed estimates nor a significant interaction with the verb manipulation. There was a non-significant trend in the interaction term, but the direction of the observed mean differences did not support the hypothesized moderating role of increased confidence in reducing suggestibility.

Discussion

The present study set out to examine whether a brief, feedback-based manipulation of confidence would moderate the influence of a leading question on participants' recollected estimates of a witnessed event. Using a well-established experimental paradigm, we sought to replicate the classic effect in which the verb used to describe a collision influences subsequent speed estimates, and to test whether induced differences in confidence would change the magnitude of this effect. The findings can be summarized succinctly. First, the verbal framing manipulation replicated robustly: participants asked with the stronger verb provided substantially higher speed estimates. Second, the feedback manipulation accomplished its immediate objective by producing a reliable difference in self-reported confidence during the manipulation check. Third, despite the successful induction of confidence, the manipulation failed to produce a statistically significant effect on participants' speed estimates and did not interact significantly with the verb manipulation to alter susceptibility to the leading question. There was a trend-level interaction that did not reach conventional significance, but the numerical pattern did not align with the hypothesized protective effect of high confidence.

These results carry several implications for theoretical accounts of suggestibility and the role of metacognitive states. The replication of the verb effect confirms that subtle linguistic cues in questions can reliably influence memory reports in laboratory settings. This continues to support the notion that either memory representations are malleable to post-event information, or that retrieval processes are susceptible to priming by linguistic inputs. The fact that the feedback manipulation altered participants' reported confidence but not their susceptibility to the leading question is informative. It suggests that momentary, externally induced confidence in one's judgments may not necessarily serve as a buffer against post-event linguistic influences, at least within the parameters of the present design.

There are several non-mutually exclusive reasons why induced confidence may have failed to moderate suggestibility. One possibility is that the manipulations targeted different cognitive levels. The feedback manipulation concerned participants' general confidence in their perceptual judgments on practice trials, which may not have transferred to the specific perceptual encoding or source monitoring processes engaged during the critical video. Confidence is context-sensitive, and an alteration in self-efficacy regarding performance on practice tasks may not change the retrieval dynamics or the source-monitoring criteria applied when confronted with a leading question about a specific event. A second possibility is that the strength and immediacy of the linguistic manipulation overshadowed any subtle differences in metacognitive state. Leading verbs in the classic paradigm produce strong and consistent effects, and a short feedback intervention may be insufficient to counteract such powerful cues.

Another consideration concerns the temporal dynamics of the manipulations. The confidence induction occurred prior to the main event and was measured via self-report immediately after the practice trials, whereas susceptibility to a leading question involves processes at the retrieval stage after the event. It is possible that confidence manipulations applied contemporaneously with retrieval, or feedback given after encoding but before retrieval, might exert different effects. This raises the broader point that metacognitive interventions may have to be timed strategically to influence specific memory processes.

The present findings are also informative in light of prior mixed results on the relation between confidence and suggestibility. Some prior work has reported reduced susceptibility among higher-confidence participants, and other work has reported no relation or even increased suggestibility. The present study contributes to the view that the relation is not uniform and may depend heavily on the nature and timing of the confidence manipulation, the type of memory test, and the strength of the misinformation. It suggests that temporary increases in task-specific confidence produced by brief positive feedback do not necessarily translate into reduced acceptance of leading post-event information.

Methodological aspects of the study warrant further reflection. The manipulation check indicated a large effect of feedback on self-reported confidence, which supports the internal validity of the induction procedure. Nevertheless, the transfer of this induced state to the specific memory task may have been limited. It is also possible that the confidence manipulation affected other variables, such as response conservatism or willingness to provide extreme numeric values, in ways that were not captured by the measures used. Another methodological consideration is statistical power for detecting interaction effects. Although the sample size was adequate to replicate the main verb effect, detecting interactions of small to medium size would require greater numbers of participants. The interaction term in the present analysis approached but did not achieve significance, and the observed partial eta-squared suggests that a moderate sample-size increase could clarify whether a true moderation exists.

The pattern of means provided an unexpected aspect: the verb effect appeared numerically larger in the high-confidence group than in the low-confidence group, contrary to the hypothesized protective effect of high confidence. While this pattern was not statistically reliable, it raises intriguing theoretical possibilities. One speculative interpretation is that heightened confidence may, in some instances, lead participants to commit more strongly to an interpretation cued by an experimenter, thereby amplifying the influence of leading wording. If a participant feels confident in their judgments, they may be more decisive when reconstructing the event and may adopt the interviewer-provided framing as part of an authoritative narrative. This would align with a social-pragmatic account in which people interpret questions as informative and rely on them to fill in gaps, particularly when they are confident in their evaluative capacities. Alternatively, the numerical pattern may be idiosyncratic and should not be overinterpreted in the absence of a significant interaction.

The present results have implications for applied contexts such as legal interviewing. The findings suggest that brief positive feedback or encouragement intended to boost witness confidence may not reliably reduce suggestibility to leading questions. In some cases, such feedback might even unintentionally increase the incorporation of post-event information if the witness interprets the interviewer as an authoritative source. These possibilities underscore the importance of careful interviewer training and of minimizing leading language. They also highlight that interventions aimed at bolstering witness confidence should be empirically evaluated for potential side effects on susceptibility to misinformation.

There are additional limitations that should be acknowledged. The study used a laboratory sample with relatively homogeneous demographic characteristics and a short retention interval between viewing and recall. Real-world eyewitness situations often involve longer delays, higher stakes, emotional arousal, and more complex scenes, all factors that could interact with confidence differently. The present study also relied on a single type of confidence manipulation — feedback about performance on practice trials — and a single form of leading question. Future research should test whether alternative manipulations, such as social comparisons, training interventions, or physiological manipulations that alter arousal and perceived competence, yield different effects on suggestibility.

Future research directions are numerous. One fruitful avenue is to explore whether timing matters: does boosting confidence at retrieval, rather than before encoding, alter susceptibility? In other words, would offering positive affirmations or corroborating evidence immediately before an interview change how a witness evaluates an experimenter's question? Another avenue is to examine individual differences in trait-level confidence or metacognitive monitoring ability. For example, individuals high in metacognitive insight may be better at detecting conflicts between their memory and external suggestions and thus less likely to accept misleading information. Neurocognitive measures could also be informative, such as assessing whether brain indices of conflict monitoring or source monitoring predict susceptibility and interact with induced confidence.

Experimental manipulations could also be made more potent or ecologically valid. For instance, providing feedback that is ostensibly from a recognized expert or authority might have stronger carryover effects than anonymous on-screen messages. Alternatively, longitudinal designs could examine whether sustained changes in confidence through training or feedback influence susceptibility over longer periods and across multiple events. It would also be useful to test these interactions in contexts that vary the emotional intensity of the witnessed event, as emotion can both strengthen memory and influence metacognitive judgments.

Methodologically, future studies should aim for larger sample sizes to provide adequate power for detecting interactions and to allow for the testing of potential moderators such as gender, age, or cognitive ability. The use of pre-registered designs and replication across laboratories would help build a more reliable evidence base regarding the interplay between confidence and suggestibility. Mixed-methods approaches, including qualitative debriefings, could shed light on how participants interpret feedback and leading questions, providing insight into the psychological processes at play.

In terms of theoretical development, the dissociation between self-reported confidence and susceptibility found here suggests that models of memory suggestibility need to integrate meta-cognitive dimensions more precisely. The current findings imply that subjective confidence, at least as manipulated by brief feedback, may be orthogonal to some mechanisms of misinformation uptake. Therefore, theoretical frameworks should specify the conditions under which confidence is likely to influence source monitoring and retrieval dynamics, and when it is unlikely to do so. This could involve delineating the cognitive steps involved in reconciling internal memory traces with external cues, and identifying where metacognitive checks occur in that process.

To conclude, the study replicated a well-known effect of leading language on eyewitness-style speed estimates and demonstrated that a brief feedback-based confidence manipulation can alter self-reported confidence without necessarily changing susceptibility to misleading post-event questions. The absence of a clear moderating effect of induced confidence indicates that the relation between metacognitive states and suggestibility is complex and context-dependent. Future work that varies the nature, timing, and strength of confidence manipulations, employs larger and more diverse samples, and examines additional outcome measures will be valuable in elucidating the boundary conditions and mechanisms through which confidence may shape the uptake of post-event information. Such research has both theoretical significance for memory science and practical importance for forensic applications where the reliability of witness reports is critical.
