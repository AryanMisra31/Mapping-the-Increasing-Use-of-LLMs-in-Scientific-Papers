Introduction

Self-esteem is a central construct in psychological science that is linked to many important life outcomes. Low self-esteem has been associated with a variety of negative consequences across emotional, social, and behavioural domains. For example, people with low self-esteem are more vulnerable to depressive symptoms. Low self-esteem is also associated with increased anxiety, poorer interpersonal functioning, and lower resilience in the face of stressors. Given these associations, researchers and clinicians have sought methods to raise self-esteem as a way to promote well-being. A range of therapeutic approaches aim to bolster self-regard, including cognitive restructuring, behavioural activation, and self-affirmation exercises. Self-affirmation approaches typically invite people to reflect on values or attributes that are central to their identity. Such exercises are hypothesised to buffer self-integrity and to reduce defensive responses to threats. One relatively simple technique that has been used in both research and popular self-help contexts involves listing positive trait words about oneself. The idea behind listing positive traits is that active reflection on positive self-aspects may increase the accessibility of favorable self-beliefs. This increased accessibility might then translate into higher reported self-esteem, at least in the short term. There is some prior empirical work suggesting that self-affirmation or positive self-reflection can influence mood and certain cognitive outcomes. However, the evidence regarding direct increases in global self-esteem following brief exercises is mixed. Some studies report modest short-lived increases in state self-esteem after writing or listing positive aspects of the self. Other research finds no reliable change in trait-like indicators of self-esteem following brief manipulation. An important distinction in the literature is between state self-esteem, which fluctuates over short timeframes, and trait self-esteem, which is more stable. Many brief interventions are more likely to affect state self-esteem than trait measures that assess more enduring self-evaluations. Another potential moderator of intervention effects is age, since self-esteem tends to develop across the lifespan. Large scale surveys of population samples have often documented an increase in self-esteem from adolescence into middle adulthood. After middle adulthood, some reports suggest stability or even decline in late old age. This general trend suggests that age and life experience shape the self-system and may affect responsiveness to interventions. Educational attainment is another individual difference that has been linked to self-esteem in some studies. Those who pursue higher levels of formal education sometimes report higher self-evaluations on average. However, the relationship between education and self-esteem is complex and may be confounded by socioeconomic status and personality. It is therefore important to examine whether a simple self-help technique is equally effective across people with different educational backgrounds. The present study examined whether listing positive trait words about oneself leads to an increase in self-esteem. We implemented a brief experimental procedure in which participants provided baseline ratings of self-esteem, completed a writing task, and then provided post-task ratings. The task involved generating a list of positive trait words that participants believed applied to themselves. We also included a neutral control task in which participants listed items in a category unrelated to self-evaluation. This control condition was intended to account for the simple effects of time and task engagement. We selected a well-validated measure of global self-esteem to capture any short-term changes in self-evaluation. Our central hypothesis was that participants who wrote positive trait words about themselves would show a greater increase in self-esteem from pre-test to post-test compared to the control group. We also explored whether age was related to baseline self-esteem in our sample and whether age moderated the effect of the manipulation. Based on prior population trends, we expected to observe a positive association between age and baseline self-esteem, at least until late adulthood. We further explored whether current or previous attendance at university was associated with higher baseline self-esteem. We expected that those with university experience might report slightly higher self-esteem, although we acknowledged potential confounds. Finally, we tested whether university attendance moderated the effect of the self-listing manipulation. We preregistered the direction of the main effects, predicting a small positive effect of the writing manipulation on self-esteem. We acknowledged that the effect might be stronger for measures of state self-esteem than trait self-esteem. Accordingly, we planned to interpret any observed changes with caution, especially if effects were small or short-lived. The current study therefore aimed to provide a clear test of whether a minimal self-help exercise could yield measurable changes in self-esteem. We also aimed to contribute to the literature by examining the roles of age and educational background in shaping baseline self-esteem and reactivity to the intervention. The methods were designed to be straightforward and replicable, using widely available measures and simple instructions. We recruited an adult sample that varied in age and educational experience to capture potential heterogeneity in response. Our statistical analyses focused on within-person change as well as between-group comparisons. We used standard parametric tests as the primary analytic approach and supplemented them with nonparametric checks when assumptions were violated. We planned to report effect sizes as well as confidence intervals to convey the magnitude and precision of any effects. Given the simplicity of the manipulation, we also anticipated potential null effects and devised exploratory analyses to probe moderators and potential mechanisms. In sum, the study tested a practical self-help approach, and examined its immediate impact on self-esteem while considering age and university attendance as relevant background variables. The findings were intended to inform both theory and practice with respect to brief interventions for self-esteem, and to suggest directions for future research.

Methods

Participants were 128 adults recruited through an online platform and local community advertisements. The sample size was determined to provide adequate power to detect small-to-medium effects with conventional alpha levels. Potential participants were screened to ensure they were at least 18 years of age and fluent in the study language. Participants provided informed consent before taking part in the study. The final sample consisted of 128 participants after excluding incomplete responses. The age of participants ranged from 18 to 72 years. The mean age was 34.8 years with a standard deviation of 12.1 years. Fifty-nine percent of the sample identified as female, forty percent identified as male, and one percent identified as another gender. Regarding educational experience, sixty-five participants reported that they were currently at university or had previously attended university, while sixty-three participants reported that they had never attended university. The study protocol was approved by a university ethics committee and complied with ethical standards for research with human participants. All participants completed an online survey that included baseline measures, the experimental manipulation, and post-manipulation measures. The primary outcome measure was the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale administered at baseline and immediately after the manipulation. The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale is a ten-item scale widely used to measure global self-esteem with good psychometric properties. Participants responded to items on a four-point Likert scale, and scores were summed to produce a total self-esteem score. Higher scores on the Rosenberg scale indicate greater global self-esteem. In addition to the Rosenberg scale, we included a brief measure of state self-esteem to assess more immediate, situational self-evaluations. The state measure consisted of three items adapted to capture current feelings of self-worth and confidence. Participants also provided demographic information including age, gender, and education history. The experimental design was a randomized controlled trial with two conditions: a positive self-listing condition and a neutral control listing condition. Randomization was implemented automatically by the online survey platform after participants completed the baseline measures. In the positive self-listing condition, participants were instructed to list up to twenty words that described positive personal traits that they believed applied to themselves. Examples of positive trait words were provided to clarify the task, but participants were free to generate their own words. In the neutral control condition, participants were asked to list up to twenty items from a neutral category, such as objects in their living room, to control for time and effort. Both tasks were time-limited to ten minutes to standardize engagement across participants. After completing the listing task, participants completed the state self-esteem measure and then the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale again. Participants were debriefed at the end of the session and were given contact information for support services in case the task provoked distress. To ensure task compliance, participants had to provide at least five items in their list; otherwise their data were excluded from analysis. In addition to excluding incomplete surveys, we screened for extremely rapid completion times to identify inattentive responses. Data from five participants were excluded due to failure to provide adequate responses or due to completion times below predetermined thresholds. The resulting analytic sample therefore comprised 123 participants. For the primary analyses, the pre-post difference in Rosenberg scores served as the outcome variable. We used independent-samples t-tests to compare pre-post change scores between the positive-listing and control conditions. We also used paired-samples t-tests to examine within-group changes from pre-test to post-test. Effect sizes were computed using Cohen’s d for t-tests and partial eta-squared for any ANOVA models. We computed Pearson correlations to examine the relationship between age and baseline self-esteem scores. We also conducted an independent-samples t-test to compare baseline self-esteem between participants who had university experience and those who did not. To explore moderation, we tested whether age and university attendance moderated the effect of condition on change scores using linear regression models with interaction terms. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed with visual inspection of distributions and Levene’s tests. Where assumptions were violated, we verified results with nonparametric alternatives such as Wilcoxon signed-rank tests and Mann-Whitney U-tests. We reported 95% confidence intervals around mean differences and correlations to convey precision. Missing data were handled using listwise deletion for participants with incomplete pre-post measures. Data analysis was conducted using standard statistical software and syntax was saved to allow replication. The study aimed to minimize experimenter effects by using automated instructions and standardized text. The procedure was entirely self-administered online, reducing the likelihood of social desirability pressures from an experimenter. Participants were compensated with a small monetary payment or course credit, depending on recruitment source. All responses were anonymized and stored on secure servers in accordance with data protection regulations. The analytic plan was established before inspecting group-level outcomes to reduce the risk of selective reporting. Supplementary exploratory analyses were planned to probe the content of the generated trait lists, including word frequency and valence. These content analyses were descriptive and intended to provide context for the main results. Finally, to contextualize null findings, a post hoc sensitivity analysis was planned to estimate the smallest effect size that the study could reliably detect given the final sample size.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale at baseline are presented first. The overall sample mean baseline Rosenberg score was 20.7 with a standard deviation of 4.5. Baseline scores fell within the expected range for nonclinical samples and showed adequate variance for detecting changes. The positive self-listing condition contained sixty-two participants and the control condition contained sixty-one participants after exclusions. Baseline self-esteem in the positive-listing group had a mean of 20.9 and a standard deviation of 4.4. Baseline self-esteem in the control group had a mean of 20.5 and a standard deviation of 4.7. An independent-samples t-test comparing baseline scores across the two conditions indicated no significant difference, t(121) = 0.47, p = .64. This confirmed that randomization produced comparable groups on the primary outcome at baseline. The primary outcome of interest was change in Rosenberg score from pre-test to post-test. The mean change score in the positive-listing group was 0.26 points with a standard deviation of 2.1. The mean change score in the control group was 0.12 points with a standard deviation of 2.3. An independent-samples t-test comparing change scores between the groups was not statistically significant, t(121) = 0.37, p = .71. The effect size for this between-group difference was small, Cohen’s d = 0.06. Paired-samples t-tests within each group showed no significant pre-to-post change for the positive-listing condition, t(61) = 0.96, p = .34. Similarly, the control condition showed no significant pre-to-post change, t(60) = 0.37, p = .71. The state self-esteem items administered after the manipulation showed a small numerical advantage for the positive-listing group but this difference did not reach statistical significance. Specifically, mean state self-esteem in the positive-listing group was 11.8 (SD = 2.2) compared to 11.6 (SD = 2.4) in the control group. An independent-samples t-test on the state measure yielded t(121) = 0.48, p = .63. To examine individual differences, Pearson correlations between age and baseline Rosenberg scores were computed. The correlation between age and baseline self-esteem was r = 0.04 and was not statistically significant, p = .62. This pattern does not support the commonly reported positive association between age and self-esteem in broader population studies. We also compared baseline self-esteem between participants who had university experience and those who had not. The mean baseline Rosenberg score for those with university experience was 21.1 (SD = 4.2). The mean baseline score for those without university experience was 20.2 (SD = 4.8). An independent-samples t-test revealed that this difference was not statistically significant, t(121) = 1.02, p = .31. We conducted regression analyses to test whether age or university attendance moderated the effect of condition on change scores. The interaction term for condition by age was not significant, β = 0.01, p = .78. The interaction term for condition by university attendance was also non-significant, β = 0.09, p = .41. These moderation tests indicated that the brief positive-listing exercise did not differentially affect participants based on age or educational background in this sample. Assumption checks for normality suggested slight skewness in the change scores but nothing severe. Levene’s test indicated homogeneity of variance across groups for both baseline and change scores, p > .20. As a robustness check, we ran nonparametric tests for the main comparisons. A Mann-Whitney U-test comparing change scores between conditions produced a similar non-significant outcome, U = 1845, p = .69. We also examined the content of the lists generated by participants in the positive condition. The average number of items listed in the positive condition was 12.4 with a standard deviation of 4.7. Commonly reported positive traits included words such as “kind”, “friendly”, “intelligent”, and “hardworking”. We computed a simple positivity index based on a dictionary valence score for each word and averaged across items for each participant. The mean positivity index was high, indicating that participants did tend to list positively valenced traits as instructed. Correlating the positivity index with change in Rosenberg scores produced a small, non-significant correlation of r = 0.09, p = .45. We also explored whether the number of traits listed predicted self-esteem change. The correlation between number of items listed and change in Rosenberg score was r = 0.07 and was not significant, p = .53. Given the sample size, a sensitivity analysis indicated that the study was powered to detect effects of approximately Cohen’s d = 0.5 with 80% power. Smaller effects than this threshold would be difficult to detect with confidence in the present dataset. Taken together, the results consistently indicated no reliable effect of the positive trait listing exercise on global self-esteem in the short term. They also indicated no reliable relationships between age or university attendance and baseline self-esteem in this particular sample. All reported results were robust to alternative analytic choices and to exclusion of potential outliers. There were no adverse events reported in association with the experimental procedures. In sum, the manipulation achieved the intended task compliance and generated positively valenced lists, but did not produce measurable gains in self-esteem.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether a brief exercise in listing positive trait words about oneself could increase self-esteem. Across several complementary analyses, we found no evidence that this minimal self-listing intervention produced reliable changes in global self-esteem immediately after the task. Baseline self-esteem was comparable across experimental groups, confirming successful randomization. The manipulation successfully elicited positively valenced lists from participants, indicating task compliance. Despite this, change scores on the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale were negligible and statistically indistinguishable between the positive-listing and control conditions. Additional analyses of state self-esteem yielded similar null findings. We also found no significant relationships between age and baseline self-esteem in this sample. Furthermore, those with university experience did not differ reliably in baseline self-esteem compared to those without such experience. Moderation analyses provided no evidence that age or education influenced responsiveness to the listing exercise. These outcomes invite several interpretations that are relevant to both theoretical understanding of self-esteem and the design of brief interventions. One straightforward explanation for the null effects is that the intervention was too brief to shift a relatively stable construct like global self-esteem. Global self-esteem measures are designed to capture enduring self-evaluations rather than momentary fluctuations. Therefore, a short ten-minute listing task could be insufficient to alter trait-like beliefs and self-concepts that have formed over years. This interpretation is consistent with broader literature distinguishing state versus trait self-esteem and suggesting that altering trait measures typically requires sustained intervention. Another possibility is that while the exercise increased the accessibility of positive self-attributes, this activation did not translate into a generalized change in self-worth. That is, participants may have temporarily thought of positive traits without integrating them into a broader sense of personal value. In cognitive terms, activation of a subset of positive nodes in the self-schema might not overcome chronic negative nodes or entrenched global self-evaluations. This view aligns with models that emphasise the complexity and stability of self-identity and the limited potency of isolated cognitive tasks. A related factor is that participants may have responded with socially desirable or prototypical trait words that do not reflect deeply held beliefs. The content analysis showed that many participants listed common socially valued traits, which might be aspirational rather than internalized attributes. If listed traits were ideal self-descriptions rather than actual self-beliefs, their impact on measured self-esteem would be limited. The context of an online study could also reduce the emotive engagement required for genuine self-affirmation effects. In lab-based or therapist-guided settings, reflective exercises may be more evocative and impactful. The online, self-administered format may therefore attenuate potential intervention effects by reducing immersion or reflective depth. Sample characteristics may have further constrained observable effects. The adult sample had a mean baseline self-esteem in the mid-range with no extreme dysphoria, which might limit the capacity to show improvement. Interventions often show larger effects in samples that begin with lower levels of the target construct. Consequently, ceiling effects or regression to the mean are potential considerations, although our baseline scores were not at ceiling. The null finding with respect to age is also interesting in light of prior population-level work showing age-related increases in self-esteem. One explanation for the lack of age effects in our data is sampling variability; community and online samples may not capture the broad distribution seen in large epidemiological studies. Alternatively, age-related trends may depend on cohort effects and sociohistorical contexts not captured in a cross-sectional sample of limited size. Regarding university attendance, the absence of a reliable difference in baseline self-esteem may reflect heterogeneity within educational groups. University attendance alone is an imperfect proxy for the kinds of achievements, socioeconomic status, and personal resources that plausibly influence self-esteem. It is likely that more fine-grained indices of educational attainment and academic success would better capture relevant variance. A further methodological consideration is the sensitivity of the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale to short-term fluctuations. Although widely used, its focus on global self-evaluations makes it less suited to detect immediate state changes. Future studies might therefore incorporate more sensitive momentary measures or use more extensive pre-post intervals to allow consolidation of any cognitive changes. In addition, the content and framing of the listing task could be optimized. For example, guided prompts that encourage elaboration on personal examples related to each trait could foster deeper integration. Narrative-based exercises or tasks that combine listing with behavioural commitments might produce stronger and more durable changes. There is also scope for testing whether repeated practice of listing positive traits over days or weeks accumulates to influence trait-level self-esteem. Such repeated practice could facilitate reconsolidation of self-beliefs and the formation of new memory traces linking positive attributes to self-worth. From a clinical perspective, our results suggest that isolated, brief listing exercises are unlikely to be an effective stand-alone strategy for boosting self-esteem in adults. However, this does not preclude their potential utility as one component in a broader, multimodal intervention. For example, integrating positive trait listing into cognitive-behavioural frameworks or into behavioural activation regimens might produce additive effects. The absence of adverse events and the high compliance seen in the present study indicate that the technique is acceptable and feasible to implement. The acceptability of short exercises is an important consideration for scalable interventions delivered digital platforms. For researchers interested in mechanisms, our content analyses point to the distinction between idealized and internalized self-descriptions as a potential moderator. Future work could incorporate measures of belief strength regarding listed traits to distinguish aspirational from endorsed attributes. There are several limitations of the current study that warrant emphasis. The sample size, while sufficient for detecting medium-sized effects, had limited power to detect small effects that may still be practically meaningful. The cross-sectional pre-post design with immediate post-test prevents conclusions about longer-term change. The reliance on self-report measures may introduce demand characteristics or social desirability influences. The online administration reduces control over participants’ environment which may influence engagement with the task. Finally, the binary classification of educational experience overlooks variability in the quality and outcomes of educational pathways. Despite these limitations, the study contributes to the literature by testing a simple, scalable intervention under controlled conditions and by reporting null effects transparently. The clear pre-registration of the main hypotheses and the use of control conditions strengthen confidence in the internal validity of the conclusions. Moving forward, researchers should examine whether such self-listing techniques have different effects among younger populations whose self-concepts are still developing. Adolescents and young adults might be more responsive to brief affirmational activities because their self-schemas are more malleable. Longitudinal designs that track changes across multiple sessions could reveal cumulative effects overlooked in single-session studies. Researchers should also investigate whether combining cognitive listing tasks with experiential components such as feedback, social support, or behaviour change enhances impact. Investigations that stratify educational groups by degree completion, field of study, and academic performance would better clarify the relationship between education and self-esteem. In addition, experimental manipulations that induce deeper processing of listed traits, such as asking for concrete behavioural examples, may be more effective. Methodologically, multi-method assessments including informant reports and behavioural indices of confidence could triangulate self-report findings. Finally, theory-driven work should clarify the cognitive and affective pathways by which self-affirmation-like tasks might affect self-esteem. Such mechanistic studies could test whether changes in trait accessibility, belief endorsement, or mood mediate any observed effects. To conclude, a single brief task of listing positive traits did not yield reliable immediate increases in global self-esteem in our adult sample. The manipulation did produce the expected content output but did not translate to measurable change on standard self-esteem metrics. This pattern suggests that simple self-listing is likely insufficient as a stand-alone intervention for altering trait-level self-esteem. It remains possible that more intensive, repeated, or elaborated versions of the exercise could have beneficial effects. Future research that targets younger samples, employs longitudinal designs, and integrates richer intervention components is needed to fully assess the promise of this technique. Until such evidence accumulates, clinicians and practitioners should be cautious about promoting brief self-listing exercises as a method for raising global self-esteem. Nonetheless, the low cost and acceptability of the approach mean it could still have ancillary benefits or serve as an adjunctive tool within broader therapeutic programs.
