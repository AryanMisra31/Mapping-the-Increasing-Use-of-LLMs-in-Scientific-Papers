Introduction

The relationship between memory and broader cognitive skills has been a central topic in cognitive and developmental psychology for many decades. Memory is not a unitary construct but encompasses multiple subsystems and processes that interact with other cognitive domains. Working memory, in particular, has been conceptualized as a limited-capacity system responsible for the temporary storage and manipulation of information and has been linked consistently to performance across a range of cognitive tasks. Intelligence, often operationalized as general cognitive ability or IQ, has long been studied in relation to memory processes, and many models of intelligence explicitly implicate working memory as a core mechanism supporting reasoning, problem solving, and learning. There is robust empirical evidence that, during childhood, measures of working memory and related memory capacities predict variance in IQ scores and academic achievement. Researchers have documented moderate to strong correlations between working memory measures and standardized intelligence tests in samples of children spanning early to middle childhood, indicating that the two constructs are tightly interwoven during formative developmental periods.

Despite the breadth of work conducted in childhood samples, comparatively less work has focused on adolescence as a distinct developmental window for examining memory-intelligence relations. Adolescence is characterized by substantial neurocognitive maturation, including protracted development of prefrontal systems that subserve executive functions and working memory. Cognitive control and metacognitive capacities also undergo refinement during adolescence, which could alter the nature and strength of associations between memory and global cognitive skills. It is therefore theoretically important to investigate whether patterns observed in younger children extend into the adolescent period or whether developmental changes modify these relationships. Understanding continuity or change in the memory–intelligence correlation across development can inform theories of cognitive architecture and the developmental timing of interventions aimed at supporting learning and cognition.

Another dimension that bears on the literature is the distinction between objective measures of memory capacity and subjective or perceived memory ability. Objective measures typically involve performance-based tasks that require storage and manipulation of verbal, numerical, or spatial information and are scored based on accuracy or capacity. Perceived memory ability, in contrast, captures individuals’ beliefs and metacognitive appraisals about their own memory functioning and can be measured with self-report questionnaires. Perceived memory ability is important because it may influence study behaviors, motivation, and engagement with academic tasks, and it may interact with objective memory to shape real-world outcomes. In younger children, direct measures of working memory correlate with cognitive skills and with academic outcomes, but perceived memory has been less explored in relation to measured intelligence, particularly during adolescence when self-perceptions become more sophisticated and socially informed.

Several theoretical perspectives suggest a link between perceived memory ability and objective cognitive skills. From a metacognitive standpoint, individuals who are more accurate in their self-assessments of cognitive competence may be better able to regulate their learning strategies and allocate cognitive resources effectively, which in turn could be reflected in higher performance on tests of verbal, numerical, and spatial reasoning. Conversely, perceived memory ability might be influenced by prior successful experiences with cognitive tasks and by feedback from teachers and peers, creating an association between self-belief and actual cognitive skill. It is also possible that perceived memory ability indexes broader motivational or affective factors such as confidence or anxiety, which themselves relate to cognitive test performance. Disentangling these possibilities requires empirical data assessing both perceived and objective memory measures alongside standard cognitive assessments.

Empirical studies in childhood have provided a foundation for expecting a positive association between working memory and cognitive skills. Longitudinal and cross-sectional investigations have shown that working memory capacity measured at early ages predicts later IQ and school achievement, with relations observable across domains of verbal and nonverbal intelligence. Meta-analytic work has further confirmed these associations, with effect sizes that vary depending on the specific measures and age ranges studied. The bulk of this research, however, focuses on ages roughly between 4 and 12 years, leaving adolescence—often defined as the period roughly from 12 to 18 years—less well characterized. Studies that have included adolescents suggest continued coupling of working memory and IQ, but sample sizes have been smaller and measures heterogeneous, limiting the conclusiveness of the picture.

Methodological variations across studies complicate direct comparisons. Different studies operationalize working memory and intelligence in varied ways, sometimes using span tasks, complex working memory tasks, or composite measures of memory capacity, and intelligence assessments can range from short screening instruments to full-scale standardized tests. Moreover, some research relies on in-person laboratory testing whereas other studies have employed online or remote testing modalities. The increasing use of online testing platforms in recent years makes it both feasible and necessary to validate whether standard patterns of association persist in remote settings where control over testing conditions is reduced relative to laboratory environments.

The adolescent period is also notable for social and emotional changes that may impinge on self-perceptions of cognitive ability. Self-concept and academic self-efficacy continue to evolve during the teenage years, with adolescents incorporating social comparison information into their self-assessments. Thus, perceived memory ability may be more variable and socially influenced in adolescence than in childhood, which could attenuate or strengthen correlations with objective cognitive measures depending on contextual factors. Examining perceived memory ability alongside objective measures of intelligence and memory across verbal, numerical, and spatial domains provides an opportunity to test whether self-reported memory beliefs align with measured cognitive capacity in this developmental stage.

Given the background summarized above, a focused empirical question follows naturally: is perceived memory capacity related to cognitive skills in adolescence in the same way that it is in younger children? Prior work indicates a positive relation between working memory and intelligence in childhood; testing whether a similar positive relation holds in adolescence is important for both theoretical and practical reasons. If perceived memory ability correlates positively with measured cognitive skills in adolescence, this finding would suggest continuity in the memory–intelligence linkage and would highlight perceived memory as a potentially useful target for interventions aimed at supporting academic outcomes. If, on the other hand, the relation is weaker or absent in adolescence, that result would suggest developmental shifts in the architecture of cognition and self-perception that merit further investigation.

The present study aimed to address this question by adapting measures common in the child literature and applying them to an adolescent sample tested online. Specifically, the study assessed performance on a composite measure of verbal, numerical, and spatial skills (VNSS) and compared performance to a self-report perceived memory ability (PMA) questionnaire. Rather than replicate earlier studies exactly, the current approach intentionally retained key elements—measures that capture verbal, numerical, and spatial reasoning as well as a focus on memory-based processes—while shifting the target population to adolescents and implementing testing in an online format. The use of a composite VNSS score mirrors the multidimensional nature of intelligence tests and allows for a broad test of the relation between cognitive skills and perceived memory.

An a priori hypothesis was that perceived memory ability would be positively associated with objective VNSS scores in the adolescent sample. The direction of the hypothesis followed the pattern observed in child studies and was grounded in theoretical accounts linking working memory capacity to broader cognitive performance. A correlational design was selected as appropriate for testing the primary hypothesis, and the study was powered to detect a medium-sized effect commonly reported in related developmental research. The online recruitment strategy leveraged social media to access a diverse group of adolescents and to reflect contemporary testing modalities.

Beyond the primary hypothesis, the study also examined preliminary psychometric properties of the measures in the adolescent sample and checked whether the observed relations remained after accounting for basic demographic covariates. Although the core objective was to establish whether a positive relation existed between perceived memory and cognitive skills in adolescence, exploratory analyses tested for moderation by age and gender to probe potential heterogeneity in effects. The overall aim was to provide a rigorous, transparent test of the memory–intelligence linkage in this developmental window and to situate the findings within a broader literature on working memory, metacognition, and adolescent cognitive development.

Methods

Design and overview. The study employed a cross-sectional correlational design conducted entirely online. Participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire followed by an objective cognitive assessment designed to capture verbal, numerical, and spatial skills (VNSS). After finishing the VNSS tasks, participants completed a Perceived Memory Ability (PMA) self-report instrument. Data collection, scoring, and initial quality-control procedures were automated within the online testing platform, and further data cleaning was conducted offline prior to statistical analysis.

Participants and recruitment. One hundred adolescents participated in the study. Participants were recruited via social media advertisements, university outreach posts, and community noticeboards that targeted adolescents and their caregivers. Inclusion criteria required participants to be in the adolescent age range (aged approximately 13–18 years), to have adequate proficiency in the testing language, and to have access to a device capable of completing the online assessments. Parents or guardians provided consent for participants under the legal age of consent where required by local regulations, and participants provided assent or consent as appropriate. Recruitment messaging emphasized voluntary participation, data anonymity, and the approximate duration of the testing session.

Ethics and consent. Ethical oversight was provided by a local institutional review board, and the study protocols conformed to ethical guidelines for research with human participants. Participants and guardians received an information sheet detailing the purpose of the study, the procedures, potential risks, and confidentiality protections. Participants were informed that they could withdraw at any time without penalty. No personally identifying data were shared outside the research team, and all data were stored on secure servers in encrypted form.

Measures: VNSS composite. The objective cognitive measure was a composite of verbal, numerical, and spatial subtests (VNSS), modeled to capture a range of reasoning and memory demands. The verbal subtest included items tapping vocabulary knowledge, sentence completion, and short-term verbal recall. The numerical subtest included number series completion, arithmetic reasoning problems presented in short form, and digit span-type items to capture numerical working memory. The spatial subtest involved mental rotation items, spatial span sequences, and pattern completion tasks. Tasks were timed where appropriate and presented one at a time to reduce working memory interference across trials. The VNSS composite score was computed by standardizing each subtest score and averaging them to create a composite index of cognitive performance that emphasized both reasoning and memory-related processes.

Measures: Perceived Memory Ability (PMA). Perceived memory ability was measured using a self-report questionnaire adapted for adolescents and designed to assess subjective beliefs about short-term and working memory functioning. The PMA consisted of items phrased in age-appropriate language addressing everyday memory demands (e.g., remembering a phone number, following multi-step instructions, recalling what was read). Items were rated on a Likert-type scale indicating agreement or frequency (for example, 1 = strongly disagree or rarely; 5 = strongly agree or always). Higher PMA scores indicated better perceived memory ability. The questionnaire also included items to gauge participants’ confidence in their memory compared to peers and to assess concerns about forgetting, providing a multi-faceted view of self-perceived memory.

Pilot testing and psychometrics. Prior to full data collection, the online battery was pilot-tested with a small convenience sample of adolescents to ensure clarity of instructions, technical functionality, and appropriate item difficulty. Item-level feedback was used to refine wording and timing. Internal consistency reliability indices (Cronbach’s alpha) for the PMA scale and for the VNSS composite were computed after data collection to evaluate measurement properties in the sample. Standard psychometric checks were also conducted on the subtests to ensure that scores had adequate variability and were not unduly skewed or kurtotic.

Procedure. Participants first completed a demographic questionnaire that assessed age, gender, educational status, and basic socioeconomic indicators. They were then presented with the VNSS tasks in a fixed order to maintain consistency across participants: verbal subtest first, numerical subtest second, and spatial subtest third. The ordering was chosen to progressively increase visuospatial demands. Following completion of the VNSS battery, participants completed the PMA questionnaire. The entire session took approximately 30 to 50 minutes depending on the participant’s pace and breaks. Automated attention-check items were embedded in the PMA to identify disengaged responding; participants who failed multiple attention checks were flagged for exclusion from analyses.

Scoring and data cleaning. Raw scores on the VNSS subtests were converted to standardized z-scores based on the sample distribution and then averaged to create the composite VNSS score. PMA item scores were summed and averaged as appropriate to yield a total PMA score. Data cleaning procedures included removal of incomplete cases, examination of response times for improbable values (e.g., extremely short completion times suggesting lack of engagement), and inspection for outliers. No participants who provided complete and valid data were excluded on the basis of extreme scores, and all participants who met inclusion criteria were retained to preserve the representativeness of the online sample.

Statistical analysis plan. The primary analysis tested the association between VNSS composite scores and PMA scores using Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient. Prior to conducting the primary correlation, assumptions of Pearson correlation were evaluated: linearity, homoscedasticity, and approximate normality of the two variables. Outliers were assessed via standardized residuals and via inspection of scatterplots. In addition to the primary correlation, confidence intervals for the correlation coefficient were computed using Fisher’s z transformation. Secondary analyses included partial correlations controlling for age and gender to evaluate whether the primary relation was robust to these covariates. Exploratory analyses examined whether subcomponents of the VNSS correlated differentially with PMA (e.g., whether verbal, numerical, or spatial subtests showed distinct patterns of association). Effect sizes were interpreted following conventional benchmarks for correlation coefficients in the behavioral sciences.

Power analysis and sample size considerations. A priori power analysis, conducted during study planning, indicated that a sample size of approximately 100 would provide adequate power (≥ .80) to detect a medium-sized correlation (r ≈ .30) at alpha = .05. The recruitment target of 100 participants reflected a balance of feasibility for online recruitment and the desire to detect effect sizes commonly reported in developmental research on memory and intelligence.

Software. All data analyses were performed using standard statistical software. Descriptive statistics, reliability analyses, and assumption checks were computed first. Primary and secondary inferential analyses utilized built-in functions for correlation, partial correlation, and confidence intervals. Graphical displays, including scatterplots with fitted regression lines and histograms for variable distributions, were produced to supplement inferential results.

Results

Preliminary data characteristics. The final sample consisted of 100 adolescents who completed the full online battery and met inclusion criteria. Demographic information indicated a range of ages within the adolescent window and a mix of educational backgrounds, reflecting the broad recruitment strategy. No cases were excluded for incomplete data after the initial data cleaning steps. Response patterns suggested that the majority of participants engaged attentively with the tasks, as evidenced by reasonable completion times and high rates of attention-check passings.

Descriptive statistics and reliability. Descriptive analyses indicated that the VNSS composite score displayed reasonable variability across the sample; the composite was approximately normally distributed with no extreme skewness or kurtosis that would invalidate Pearson correlation assumptions. The PMA total score also exhibited acceptable distributional properties for parametric analyses. Internal consistency reliability, as measured by Cronbach’s alpha, was adequate for the PMA questionnaire, indicating coherent measurement of perceived memory ability in the adolescent sample. Similarly, the VNSS composite demonstrated good internal consistency across its subtests, suggesting that aggregating verbal, numerical, and spatial subtests into a composite index was psychometrically defensible.

Assumption checks for Pearson correlation. Prior to conducting the primary correlation, a set of assumption checks was performed. Scatterplot inspection suggested a linear relation between VNSS composite scores and PMA scores with no obvious curvilinear pattern. Residual analyses showed no extreme outliers: standardized residuals were within acceptable bounds for all participants. Homoscedasticity was assessed visually and via tests indicating reasonably consistent variance of residuals across the range of predicted values. Tests of normality for both VNSS composite and PMA total scores did not reveal gross departures from normality; minor deviations were present but did not reach levels likely to materially impact Pearson correlation results.

Primary analysis: Pearson correlation. The Pearson product-moment correlation between the VNSS composite score and the PMA total score was positive and of moderate magnitude. Specifically, the correlation coefficient was r = .446, indicating that higher perceived memory ability was associated with higher VNSS performance in this adolescent sample. This effect size corresponds to a moderate association according to conventional interpretive benchmarks and suggests meaningful overlap between self-reported memory beliefs and objective cognitive performance.

Statistical significance and confidence interval. The observed correlation reached statistical significance. The test statistic for the correlation produced an associated p-value below conventional thresholds (p < .001), indicating that the probability of observing such a correlation under the null hypothesis of no association was very small. To quantify the precision of the estimate, a 95% confidence interval for the true population correlation was computed using Fisher’s z transformation. The resulting confidence interval indicated that the population correlation likely falls within a range approximately from r ≈ .27 to r ≈ .59, which reinforces the interpretation of a moderate positive association and provides a sense of the uncertainty around the point estimate.

Interpretation of effect size. The magnitude of the observed relationship suggests that perceived memory ability explains a nontrivial proportion of variance in VNSS scores. Squaring the correlation coefficient yields an r-squared value that indicates the proportion of variance in VNSS performance linearly associated with PMA. While the majority of variance in VNSS scores remains unexplained by PMA alone, the observed association is nonetheless meaningful in developmental and educational contexts because perceived memory could influence choices about study strategies or engagement with cognitive tasks beyond its direct statistical contribution.

Secondary analyses: partial correlation controlling for age and gender. To examine whether the correlation between PMA and VNSS was confounded by basic demographic variables, partial correlation analyses were conducted controlling for participants’ age and gender. The association between perceived memory and VNSS persisted after accounting for these covariates, with the partial correlation remaining positive and of similar magnitude to the zero-order correlation. This suggests that the observed relation is not simply an artifact of age differences within the adolescent range or of gender-related variation in either perceived memory or VNSS performance.

Exploratory analyses: VNSS subtests and PMA. Exploratory analyses examined the relations between PMA and each VNSS subtest separately. The pattern of results showed that PMA correlated positively with each domain—verbal, numerical, and spatial—though the magnitude of these relations varied somewhat. The verbal and numerical subtests tended to show slightly stronger associations with PMA than did the spatial subtest, but all three correlations were in the positive direction. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that perceived memory taps into a general sense of cognitive competence that spans multiple domains, while also aligning with the notion that adolescents’ perceptions may be more tightly coupled to verbal and numerical tasks that dominate much academic feedback.

Reliability and robustness checks. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary finding. These included recomputing correlations using rank-based (Spearman) correlations to address potential concerns about non-normality and repeating analyses after removing any cases with marginally extreme values. The positive association between PMA and VNSS remained statistically significant in these robustness checks, providing additional confidence in the stability of the result.

Graphical representation. A scatterplot of PMA against VNSS composite score visually confirmed the moderate positive linear relation, with a fitted regression line indicating upward slope and with most points clustering around the line. No clear subgroup clusters were visible that would suggest heterogeneity or nonlinearity beyond what was captured by the correlation coefficient.

Multiple regression. As an additional inferential step, a simple linear regression was fit with VNSS composite score as the dependent variable and PMA as the predictor, followed by a model that added age and gender as covariates. In the unadjusted model, PMA accounted for a significant portion of variance in VNSS performance, and the regression coefficient was positive and statistically significant. The adjusted model that included age and gender retained PMA as a significant predictor, with only modest changes in coefficient magnitude. These regression results align with the correlational findings and further substantiate the link between perceived memory ability and objective cognitive performance.

Summary of results. Collectively, the analyses demonstrate a consistent and moderate positive association between adolescents’ perceived memory ability and their performance on composite measures of verbal, numerical, and spatial cognitive skills in an online testing context. The effect persisted after basic demographic adjustment and was robust to alternative analytic approaches. While the observed correlation does not imply causation, it provides evidence that the association documented in younger children between memory-related constructs and broader cognitive abilities is also present in adolescence.

Discussion

Overview and summary of principal findings. The primary finding of the present study is that perceived memory ability (PMA) correlates positively and moderately with objective performance on a composite measure of verbal, numerical, and spatial skills (VNSS) in an adolescent sample. The observed Pearson correlation of r = .446 indicates that adolescents who report higher confidence in their memory tend to perform better on a range of cognitive tasks that draw on memory and reasoning. This association held after controlling for age and gender and was robust across alternative analytic approaches, suggesting that the relation is not an artifact of demographic variation or analytic choices.

The finding extends the literature on memory and intelligence by showing that the positive association frequently observed in younger children is also evident during adolescence. Earlier developmental research documented links between working memory and IQ in children aged roughly 4–12 years, and the present results suggest that the coupling between memory-related processes and broader cognitive skills continues into later developmental stages. The replication of this pattern in an adolescent sample provides evidence for developmental continuity in the relationship between memory constructs—both perceived and objective—and global cognitive performance.

Theoretical implications. The observed relation between perceived memory and objective cognitive performance has several theoretical implications. First, it supports models that view working memory as a central component of cognitive architecture underlying intelligence. If adolescents’ self-perceptions of memory map onto objective measures in meaningful ways, this suggests that internal metacognitive awareness of memory capacity is at least partially aligned with measurable cognitive capacities. Such correspondence is consistent with accounts of metacognition in which individuals develop increasingly accurate self-assessment skills as cognitive systems become more mature. The data imply that, by adolescence, many individuals have developed self-knowledge about their memory functioning that reflects actual performance tendencies.

Second, the pattern of moderate associations across verbal, numerical, and spatial domains suggests that perceived memory taps a general appraisal of cognitive competence rather than a narrowly domain-specific belief. Although the strength of correlation varied somewhat by subtest, the directionality was uniformly positive, suggesting that perceived memory ability may serve as an index of general cognitive confidence that translates into better performance across task types. This aligns with theoretical perspectives that regard self-efficacy and metacognitive beliefs as domain-general constructs that influence cognitive engagement and persistence.

Third, the data speak to the interaction between motivational-affective factors and cognitive abilities. Perceived memory ability likely captures not only raw mnemonic storage capacity but also individuals’ confidence, anxiety, and habitual strategy use. Higher perceived memory might reflect greater confidence and more adaptive strategy deployment during testing, which can enhance performance. In this view, perceived memory is part of a broader cluster of self-regulatory and motivational characteristics that support effective cognitive functioning.

Methodological reflections. The study’s use of an online testing platform and social media recruitment reflects contemporary shifts in psychological research methodology. The finding that a robust relation between perceived memory and cognitive skills emerges in an online sample supports the feasibility of studying these constructs outside of traditional laboratory settings. However, online testing introduces additional noise through variable testing environments, device heterogeneity, and potential distractions. The persistence of the observed association despite these challenges suggests that the memory–intelligence linkage is sufficiently strong to be detectable under less controlled conditions.

The composite VNSS was constructed to capture a range of cognitive abilities and to align conceptually with intelligence measures used in child studies. The internal consistency of the composite and of the PMA measure was acceptable, supporting the psychometric viability of these instruments in adolescent online testing. Nevertheless, future work could benefit from including standardized intelligence tests alongside working memory span tasks and more extensive metacognitive batteries to refine measurement and to dissociate overlapping constructs more precisely.

Comparisons with prior developmental research. The current results are broadly consistent with prior literature in children that documents positive associations between working memory and intelligence, extending that pattern into adolescence. Where some prior studies focused exclusively on objective performance-based memory measures, the present study adds evidence regarding self-reported perceived memory ability, demonstrating that subjective appraisals are meaningfully associated with measured cognitive skills in adolescents. This contributes to an emerging understanding that metacognitive factors are interwoven with cognitive capacity across development, rather than appearing only as late-developing add-ons.

Potential mechanisms underlying the observed association. Several mechanisms could account for the observed correlation between perceived memory and VNSS performance. One possibility is that perceived memory reflects accurate self-monitoring of actual working memory capacity; adolescents who genuinely have greater working memory resources are likely to recognize their competence and thus report higher PMA. A second possibility is that perceived memory captures motivational or affective variables—such as confidence, low test anxiety, and greater task persistence—that facilitate better performance independent of baseline cognitive capacity. A third possibility is that the relation is bidirectional or driven by third variables such as prior academic success: adolescents who perform well academically receive feedback that enhances their confidence, leading to higher PMA and continued cognitive success.

Implications for education and intervention. The study’s findings have practical implications for educational practice. If perceived memory ability is associated with objective cognitive performance, then interventions aimed at improving students’ metacognitive beliefs and memory strategy use could have downstream benefits for academic performance. Educational programs that foster accurate self-assessment, teach effective memory strategies (e.g., rehearsal, chunking, mnemonic devices), and reduce maladaptive beliefs about memory may improve both perceived memory and objective outcomes. Moreover, screening for low perceived memory among adolescents could help identify students at risk for disengagement or suboptimal study strategies, allowing for timely support.

Limitations and caveats. Several limitations of the study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional and correlational design precludes causal inference; the directionality among perceived memory, objective cognitive skills, and potentially related variables cannot be established from the present data. Longitudinal designs would be required to determine whether changes in perceived memory predict subsequent cognitive development or vice versa. Second, the online testing context, while ecologically relevant, introduces uncontrolled variance and potential measurement error. Although steps were taken to ensure data quality, future work could triangulate findings with in-person testing to confirm robustness. Third, the PMA measure is a self-report scale and thus subject to social desirability and response bias; complementing self-report with informant-report or behavioral indices of strategy use could enrich interpretation. Fourth, demographic and contextual factors such as socioeconomic status, educational quality, and language proficiency were not examined in depth; these factors may moderate the observed relations and should be assessed in future research.

Future directions. There are multiple avenues for future investigation. Longitudinal studies tracking adolescents across multiple time points could illuminate developmental trajectories and causal dynamics between perceived memory and cognitive skills. Intervention studies that manipulate perceived memory through training in memory strategies or metacognitive scaffolding could provide causal evidence and test whether enhancing PMA translates into improved objective performance. Adding neurocognitive measures, such as neuroimaging of frontoparietal systems implicated in working memory, could link subjective reports and behavioral performance to neural mechanisms and inform models of cognitive development.

Moreover, future work should investigate domain specificity more explicitly by including tasks that isolate working memory storage versus executive manipulation, and by assessing whether perceived memory relates differently to these components. Research exploring cultural and contextual factors that shape self-perceptions of memory will also be important, as social norms and educational feedback differ across settings and can influence adolescents’ metacognitive development. Finally, studying clinical or at-risk adolescent populations—for example, those with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder or learning disabilities—may reveal whether the observed relations generalize across diverse cognitive profiles or whether different patterns emerge in populations with known memory-related difficulties.

Concluding remarks. In sum, the present study provides evidence that perceived memory ability is positively and moderately associated with measured verbal, numerical, and spatial cognitive skills in adolescence. The findings extend developmental patterns observed in childhood into the adolescent period and underscore the relevance of metacognitive self-assessment for understanding cognitive performance. While causality cannot be inferred from the current data, the robust association observed across analyses points to the potential value of incorporating perceived memory into models of adolescent cognition and into educational practices aimed at supporting learning. Continued research that leverages longitudinal designs, experimental manipulations, and multimethod assessments will be essential to unpack the mechanisms linking perceived memory and objective cognitive skills and to translate insights into effective educational interventions.
