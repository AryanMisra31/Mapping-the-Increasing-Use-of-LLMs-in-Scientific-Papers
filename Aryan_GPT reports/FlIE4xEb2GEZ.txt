Introduction

Communication fundamentally depends on the successful transmission and reception of meaning. When individuals speak and listen, they must manage multiple layers of information ranging from acoustic signals through phonology and syntax to semantics and pragmatic inference. Working memory is commonly invoked as a key cognitive resource that supports language comprehension and production. The canonical view of working memory posits a limited capacity temporary store that maintains information in an active, readily accessible state for ongoing processing. When discourse unfolds, listeners must allocate this limited capacity to maintain relevant semantic propositions, to integrate new information with prior context, and to construct coherent mental models of events. Because working memory is capacity-limited, listeners are thought to prioritize what is most consequential for ongoing comprehension and later retrieval. In particular, there is a theoretical account that during comprehension, unessential surface details such as exact syntactic form and specific wording are discarded or abstracted away in favor of the retention of semantic content and the gist of meaning. This account predicts that individuals with higher working memory capacity should be better able to retain both the semantic content and, to some extent, the more formal aspects of language such as syntactic structure and lexical choices. In contrast, individuals with lower capacity might rely more heavily on semantic gist and may fail to recover surface details after a delay.

The question of what information is retained from discourse and how working memory contributes to that retention has a long history in cognitive psychology. Classic investigations contrasted memory for surface form versus propositional content. Some influential studies showed that shortly after reading or listening to a sentence, participants are able to recognize paraphrases and to remember propositional content, whereas memory for exact wording and syntactic form tends to decay more rapidly. This pattern led to the proposal that memory for language is organized at multiple levels, with more abstract representations that capture meaning being more persistent than more concrete surface representations. A natural corollary of that proposal is that individual differences in working memory capacity might influence the relative preservation of these different levels of representation. Specifically, those with greater working memory resources might maintain more of the hierarchical structure of an utterance, including both semantic and syntactic information, whereas those with more limited resources might only preserve a crude semantic summary.

Empirical studies addressing these ideas have used a variety of tasks. Some have presented passages or sentences and then tested recognition for surface form versus meaning-matching paraphrases. Others have manipulated delay intervals or intervening tasks to observe differential decay of surface and propositional memory. Across many such experiments, the robust finding is that semantic information tends to be retained better than surface form, consistent with the idea of representational abstraction over time. There has been less consensus on whether working memory capacity modulates the retention of surface versus semantic information. Some studies report correlations between working memory measures and retention of more detailed aspects of recently processed language, while other studies report weak or absent relations.

Part of the inconsistency in the literature may arise from differences in how working memory is operationalized. Traditional measures such as digit span forward were developed as indices of short-term memory rather than the more complex working memory construct that emphasizes both maintenance and manipulation. More elaborate measures such as reading span, operation span, and other complex span tasks were introduced to capture executive control and processing efficiency in addition to simple maintenance capacity. It is plausible that simple span tasks reflect a pure storage component and may not predict performance in language tasks that require active manipulation and integration of information. Therefore, studies that employ different measures of working memory may yield divergent conclusions regarding the role of working memory in discourse memory.

Another source of variability in prior work concerns the nature of the memory probes. Some tasks assess recognition for identical wording, some demand recognition of syntactic rearrangements that preserve meaning, and others assess sensitivity to lexical replacement that preserves or changes propositional content. These distinctions are important because they map onto potentially separable representations in memory: lexical items as surface tokens, syntactic structure as an abstract relational pattern, and semantics as the propositional network. A well-controlled experimental design that manipulates change type across semantic, syntactic, and lexical dimensions within the same stimulus set and uses the same recognition procedure for each condition can help isolate whether working memory capacity selectively supports one type of representation over another.

The present study sought to investigate the impact of working memory capacity on recognition of semantic and syntactic changes in discourse by combining a within-subjects change-type manipulation with an individual differences assessment of working memory. Participants listened to short sentences embedded in brief contexts and were later presented with test sentences that either matched the original, contained a semantic change (altered Proposition), contained a lexical change (word substitution preserving propositional content), or contained a syntactic change (rearrangement of structure preserving propositional content). These four conditions were designed to probe the retention of different representational levels while holding many other task parameters constant. Participants were required to indicate whether the test sentence differed from the original sentence they had heard. Accuracy and response times were recorded.

A key methodological decision was the choice of working memory measure. In this study, we administered a digit span task as an index of working memory capacity. Digit span forward is a widely used and simple measure of immediate serial recall, and the task remains popular for its ease of administration and interpretability. However, we recognized that digit span may index short-term storage more than the broader, processing-plus-storage capacity often associated with complex span tasks. Nevertheless, because digit span is often available and because prior studies in this domain have sometimes used simple span measures, we included it here to assess whether even a simple measure of capacity would predict recognition performance across change types.

Predictions followed from two lines of reasoning. One perspective suggests that working memory capacity should support the maintenance of more detailed representations. From this perspective, higher-capacity individuals would be expected to show superior recognition for both semantic and syntactic changes relative to lower-capacity individuals. An alternative perspective is that semantic retention is robust and largely resilient to individual differences in capacity, whereas syntactic retention is more fragile and perhaps less prioritized during comprehension. Under that view, we might observe a main effect of change type with superior detection of semantic changes, but no reliable relation between such detection and working memory capacity as indexed by digit span.

Past work has often found memory for semantic changes to be detected more readily than changes that only affect surface properties, and some studies have described a characteristic pattern in which paraphrase recognition remains high while identical wording recognition declines with delay. We aimed to replicate these classic findings and to examine whether digit span would modulate recognition across change types. The design allowed us to assess main effects of change type on accuracy and response time as well as interactions with digit span treated as both a continuous predictor and a categorical split for illustrative purposes.

We preregistered our primary analyses to minimize researcher degrees of freedom and to focus on a clear set of hypotheses. The primary dependent variable was recognition accuracy for each change type. Secondary analyses examined response times and signal detection measures derived from hits and false alarms. The primary independent variable of theoretical interest was digit span score. We also examined whether any observed effects persisted when controlling for age and self-reported reading habits, since these factors can influence verbal memory performance.

The study contributes to two ongoing debates in the cognitive science of language. First, it tests the robustness of the semantic-over-surface memory pattern in a within-subjects design that equates many extraneous variables across conditions. Second, it evaluates whether a simple span measure of working memory capacity relates to retention of different linguistic representations. Finding an effect of digit span would support a capacity-based account that predicts preservation of detailed information for higher-capacity individuals. Failing to find such an effect would challenge the sufficiency of simple span as an index of the relevant capacity or suggest that semantic retention is relatively independent of individual differences in the capacity indexed by digit span. In either outcome, the study aims to add clarity to the relation between working memory and memory for discourse.

In sum, the present research examined recognition of semantic, lexical, and syntactic changes following brief auditory presentation, and explored whether individual differences in digit span relate to recognition performance. We expected to replicate the established finding of superior recognition for semantic changes relative to more surface-level changes. The relation between digit span and recognition performance was an open empirical question. The present methods, analyses, and sample size were chosen to provide sufficient power to detect medium-sized effects and to allow robust conclusions regarding the presence or absence of digit span effects on recognition performance.

Methods

Participants were recruited from the university student population and surrounding community. A total of 96 individuals were enrolled and completed the experiment. All participants reported being native speakers of the language used in the stimuli, had normal or corrected-to-normal hearing and vision, and reported no history of neurological impairment. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 35 years. The final sample after excluding participants who did not complete the full procedure or who had technical difficulties was 92. Participants received course credit or a modest monetary payment for their time. The study procedures were approved by the university institutional review board, and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

Stimuli consisted of 120 short discourse items. Each item began with a brief context sentence that established an event or situation. The context sentence was presented auditorily and was designed to be neutral and to provide the necessary backdrop for the target sentence. Each context was followed by a target sentence that conveyed a specific proposition and that served as the item to be tested later. Target sentences were constructed so that they could be minimally altered in one of three ways without changing other dimensions: semantic change, lexical change, or syntactic change. For the semantic change condition, a critical content word in the target sentence was replaced by another word that altered the overall propositional content (e.g., changing the agent or the object in a way that changed meaning). For the lexical change condition, a single content word was replaced by a synonym or close paraphrase that preserved the original propositional content but altered surface lexical form. For the syntactic change condition, the same propositional content was expressed using a different syntactic structure (e.g., active versus passive voice, or alternate ordering) that preserved meaning but altered grammatical form. A fourth condition presented the identical original sentence at test.

To ensure tight experimental control, each target sentence had four versions corresponding to the four change types. Items were divided into four lists so that each participant encountered each item only once and across participants each version appeared equally often. The study used a Latin square counterbalancing procedure so that each participant heard 30 items from each of the four test conditions. Item lists were randomized within participants to minimize possible order effects.

The main working memory measure was a digit span task. The digit span procedure included forward and backward blocks. In the digit span forward task, participants heard sequences of digits at a rate of one digit per second and were instructed to recall them in order immediately after the sequence finished. Sequence lengths ranged from three to nine digits. Two trials were presented at each length and the task continued until both trials at a length were missed. Digit span forward score was computed as the maximum list length with at least one correct recall, and an overall forward total was also calculated as the sum of correctly recalled trials. In the digit span backward task, participants heard the digits and were asked to recall them in reverse order. The backward task used the same range of list lengths and the same termination rule. The backwards span score was scored similarly. For the primary analyses reported here we used the forward span as our index of working memory capacity because it is conventionally used as a simple storage measure and because our preregistered analysis plan specified the forward score. We also report parallel analyses using the backward score as a supplementary check.

The experimental procedure took place in a quiet testing room. Participants were seated comfortably and wore headphones for auditory presentation. The experiment was programmed and run using standard experimental software. Each trial began with a fixation cross displayed for 500 ms, followed by the auditory presentation of a context sentence and a short interstimulus interval of 500 ms before the target sentence. After the target sentence there was a 1500 ms pause and then a simple distractor task. The distractor task consisted of a short arithmetic problem that participants solved aloud and was intended to impose a brief retention interval that mimicked naturalistic comprehension demands and prevented rehearsal of surface form during the immediate memory period. After the distractor, a test sentence was played. For each item, participants were instructed to indicate, using a keypress, whether the test sentence was exactly the same as the original target sentence they had heard, or whether it had been changed. Participants were told that changes could be in meaning or in wording and that they should respond “same” or “different.” If they judged a sentence to be different they were additionally prompted to classify the change as semantic, lexical, or syntactic via additional keypress responses. Both accuracy of the binary same/different judgment and the classification responses were recorded. Response time was measured from the onset of the test sentence prompt to the participant’s first keypress response. Participants completed six practice trials with feedback before beginning the experimental trials.

Before the main task, participants completed the digit span tasks, followed by a short demographic questionnaire. The order was chosen to avoid fatigue effects on the cognitive task. The entire experiment, including digit span and the recognition trials, lasted approximately 50 minutes per participant.

Scoring of the recognition task was straightforward. For the initial same/different judgment, responses that correctly classified the test as different when it was semantically, lexically, or syntactically changed were scored as correct. Responses that correctly identified identical items as same were scored as correct. For the subsequent classification of the type of change, responses were scored as correct only if the participant had correctly indicated that the sentence was different and then correctly categorized the change. We also computed signal-detection statistics to capture sensitivity to changes independent of response bias by treating "different" responses to changed items as hits and "different" responses to identical items as false alarms. The resulting hit rates and false alarm rates were converted to d' and criterion measures.

Data analyses proceeded in several stages. First, we examined descriptive statistics and distributions for recognition accuracy, classification accuracy, response times, and digit span scores. We screened the data for outliers and extreme response time values. Second, we tested the primary hypotheses using repeated-measures analyses comparing accuracy across the four change-type conditions. We used a within-subjects ANOVA to test for the main effect of change type and conducted pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction to examine specific differences among semantic, lexical, syntactic, and identical conditions. Third, we examined whether digit span predicted performance by computing Pearson correlations between digit span scores and mean accuracy in each condition. We supplemented these correlational analyses with mixed-effects logistic regression models treating trial-level accuracy as the dependent variable, change type as a fixed effect, digit span as a continuous predictor, and random intercepts for participants and items. These models tested for main effects and interactions between change type and digit span on the trial-level data. Fourth, we examined response time data with similar repeated-measures and regression approaches. Finally, we computed signal-detection measures and tested whether d' varied with digit span.

All statistical tests used an alpha threshold of .05, two-tailed, unless otherwise noted. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta-squared for ANOVA and as odds ratios or standardized coefficients for regression models. We preregistered a minimum sample size of 80 to provide adequate power to detect medium-sized effects, and our final sample exceeded that target.

Results

Descriptive statistics indicated that participants performed well above chance in recognizing differences in many conditions. Mean accuracy across all items was 0.78 with a standard deviation of 0.10. Digit span forward scores averaged 6.2 with a standard deviation of 1.1. Digit span backward scores averaged 4.8 with a standard deviation of 1.0. Accuracy varied by change type in a pattern consistent with our prior expectations. Mean accuracy for semantic changes was 0.92 (SD = 0.08). Mean accuracy for lexical changes was 0.76 (SD = 0.12). Mean accuracy for syntactic changes was 0.69 (SD = 0.15). Mean accuracy for identical items being correctly judged as same was 0.75 (SD = 0.13). These descriptive patterns suggested superior detection of semantic changes relative to lexical and syntactic changes and substantial difficulty discriminating identical items from changed ones in some cases.

A repeated-measures ANOVA testing the effect of change type on accuracy yielded a highly significant main effect, F(3,273) = 142.56, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .61. Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction showed that accuracy for semantic changes was significantly higher than for lexical changes (mean difference = 0.16, p < .001) and syntactic changes (mean difference = 0.23, p < .001). Accuracy for lexical changes was significantly higher than for syntactic changes (mean difference = 0.07, p = .002). Accuracy for identical items was poorer than for semantic changes (mean difference = 0.17, p < .001) but did not differ significantly from lexical changes (mean difference = -0.01, p = .83). These results replicate the broad pattern reported in earlier studies: participants are most sensitive to semantic alterations and less sensitive to changes that affect only surface form.

We next examined the classification accuracy for participants who indicated that a sentence was different. Among correctly detected changes, correct classification of semantic changes was near ceiling at 0.89. Correct classification for lexical changes was 0.71. Correct classification for syntactic changes was lower at 0.62. Misclassifications tended to take the form of labeling syntactic changes as lexical or labeling lexical changes as semantic, suggesting some confusion among surface categories even when participants detected a difference.

To test whether working memory capacity as indexed by digit span predicted recognition accuracy, we computed Pearson correlations between digit span forward scores and mean accuracy in each condition. Correlations were small and non-significant across conditions. For semantic change accuracy the correlation with digit span forward was r = .08, p = .43. For lexical change accuracy the correlation was r = .10, p = .33. For syntactic change accuracy the correlation was r = .06, p = .52. For identical item accuracy the correlation was r = .04, p = .67. We also explored correlations with digit span backward scores and found similarly small non-significant associations (semantic r = .09, lexical r = .07, syntactic r = .05, identical r = .03; all p > .30).

To provide a more fine-grained test, we conducted trial-level mixed-effects logistic regression analyses predicting binary correct/incorrect responses. The fixed effects included change type (dummy-coded with semantic as the reference), digit span forward score (grand-mean centered), and the interaction between change type and digit span. Random intercepts for participants and items were included. The main effect of change type was significant and mirrored the ANOVA results: lexical and syntactic conditions showed lower odds of correct recognition relative to semantic items (lexical odds ratio = 0.34, p < .001; syntactic odds ratio = 0.21, p < .001). The main effect of digit span was non-significant (odds ratio = 1.07, p = .28), and none of the interactions between change type and digit span reached significance (lexical × span: odds ratio = 1.03, p = .72; syntactic × span: odds ratio = 1.02, p = .80; identical × span: odds ratio = 1.01, p = .92). These models indicated that digit span did not meaningfully predict the likelihood of correct recognition when controlling for item and participant variance.

We also split participants into higher and lower digit span groups around the median to provide an intuitive comparison. Higher-span participants (n = 46) had a mean forward span of 7.1 and lower-span participants (n = 46) had a mean forward span of 5.3. A mixed ANOVA with change type as a within-subjects factor and span group as a between-subjects factor showed a significant main effect of change type, F(3,270) = 138.3, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .61, but no main effect of span group, F(1,90) = 1.12, p = .29, partial eta-squared = .01, and no change type × span group interaction, F(3,270) = 0.94, p = .42, partial eta-squared = .01. Differences between higher and lower span groups averaged 0.02 in accuracy across conditions and were not statistically reliable.

Response time analyses provided complementary information. Mean response times (RTs) for correct same/different judgments were as follows: semantic changes mean RT = 840 ms (SD = 120 ms), lexical changes mean RT = 930 ms (SD = 140 ms), syntactic changes mean RT = 1010 ms (SD = 160 ms), identical items mean RT = 980 ms (SD = 150 ms). A repeated-measures ANOVA on RTs revealed a significant main effect of change type, F(3,273) = 62.8, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .41. Pairwise contrasts showed that RTs for semantic changes were faster than for lexical and syntactic changes (both p < .001). RTs for syntactic changes were slower than for lexical changes (p = .003). Correlations between digit span and mean RTs were again small and non-significant (semantic r = -.11, p = .26; lexical r = -.09, p = .34; syntactic r = -.07, p = .43; identical r = -.05, p = .60).

Signal-detection analyses produced d' values that paralleled accuracy results. Mean d' for semantic changes was 3.10 (SD = 0.45). Mean d' for lexical changes was 1.75 (SD = 0.70). Mean d' for syntactic changes was 1.23 (SD = 0.80). A repeated-measures ANOVA on d' confirmed a significant effect of change type, F(2,182) = 158.2, p < .001. The relation between digit span and d' was again non-significant (semantic r = .07, p = .49; lexical r = .09, p = .37; syntactic r = .05, p = .61).

Because digit span is sometimes criticized as an imperfect index of working memory capacity, we performed exploratory analyses using a composite score that combined forward and backward span. These exploratory analyses yielded the same qualitative pattern: robust effects of change type and non-significant associations with the composite span measure. We also examined whether age or self-reported reading frequency correlated with recognition performance. Age was not related to accuracy (all r < .10). Reading frequency showed a modest correlation with lexical change detection (r = .18, p = .08) but not with other conditions.

In sum, results replicated the classic finding that semantic changes are detected far more readily than lexical or syntactic changes. Participants were fastest and most accurate in identifying semantic alterations. However, across multiple analytic approaches, digit span scores did not predict recognition accuracy, response times, or signal-detection sensitivity for any of the change types. Both continuous correlational tests and mixed-effects models failed to reveal a reliable relation between the simple span index and recognition of semantic, lexical, or syntactic changes.

Discussion

This study examined whether working memory capacity, as assessed by digit span, influences recognition of semantic and syntactic changes in discourse. The experimental manipulation contrasted four types of test sentences: identical, semantically altered, lexically altered (preserving meaning), and syntactically altered (preserving meaning). Participants were asked to judge whether the test sentence matched the previously heard target sentence and to classify the change type when they detected a difference. The data produced two clear patterns. First, we replicated a long-standing finding that semantic changes are detected with high accuracy and speed relative to changes that affect only surface properties such as lexical choice or syntactic arrangement. Second, working memory capacity as indexed by digit span did not predict recognition performance in any of the change categories. These two outcomes have implications for theories of discourse memory and for the operationalization of working memory in studies of language processing.

The replication of the semantic-over-surface memory pattern strengthens the view that listeners encode and retain propositional content more robustly than surface tokens. Participants were near ceiling when detecting semantic changes, suggesting that propositional information is prioritized during comprehension and storage. This observation aligns with theoretical models that posit hierarchical representations: more abstract semantic propositions form the durable core of discourse memory, whereas surface form is more transient. The current experiment extended this conclusion by showing that the pattern holds in an auditory presentation context with brief intervening distractors, and across a relatively broad sample of young adult participants. The pattern was evident not only in accuracy but in response times and signal-detection measures, reinforcing the conclusion that semantic retention is both stronger and accessed more quickly than surface-level memory.

Why might semantic representations be more persistent? One plausible mechanism is that semantic information is integrated into preexisting semantic networks and schemas. When a proposition is encoded, it may activate related concepts and be stored redundantly in semantic networks, making it easier to recognize when the meaning has been altered. Surface properties such as the particular syntactic framing or the exact lexical item may not receive the same level of integrated encoding. Additionally, during real-time comprehension listeners often abstract away from surface details to create efficient, compressed representations of meaning. Such compression would naturally favor semantic retention at the expense of verbatim memory.

Although semantic retention appears robust, the study also provided insight into how listeners handle different types of surface changes. Lexical substitutions that preserved meaning were detected with moderate accuracy, while syntactic rearrangements were detected less reliably. Syntactic changes were not only more likely to be missed but also elicited slower responses, indicating greater cognitive effort or uncertainty when participants attempted to compare syntactic structure. These findings suggest that syntactic structure, although potentially represented during comprehension, is not maintained or retrieved as readily as lexical or semantic information. One possibility is that syntactic details are encoded only insofar as they contribute to propositional structure. When two syntactic forms convey the same meaning, the system may abstract away from the precise arrangement, resulting in weaker memory for syntactic form.

Our principal theoretical question concerned whether individual differences in working memory capacity would modulate the retention of semantic and syntactic information. The rationale was that individuals with higher working memory capacity might be better able to maintain multiple levels of representation, including surface form. Contrary to this expectation, digit span scores did not predict recognition accuracy, response times, or sensitivity across any of the change types. This null effect was robust across multiple analytical approaches including correlational tests, mixed-effects logistic regression, and median-split comparisons.

There are several possible interpretations of the absence of a digit span effect. One interpretation is that the capacity component measured by digit span is not the dimension of working memory that supports retention of linguistic detail in discourse. Digit span primarily indexes simple short-term storage of serial order information. It does not capture the processing component or executive control demands that many researchers consider central to working memory in complex tasks. Language comprehension and the maintenance of multiple representational levels may rely more on processing-plus-storage abilities indexed by complex span tasks, such as reading span or operation span. These tasks require simultaneous maintenance and manipulation and might therefore correlate more strongly with the ability to preserve syntactic or lexical detail in addition to semantics.

A related point is that digit span might be relatively insensitive to variance among typical young adults. The sample’s digit span mean and variance may have been insufficient to detect relations with task performance. Our sample size was adequate to detect medium-sized correlations, but if the true association between simple span and recognition of changes is small, the present study would have limited power to detect it. Nevertheless, the mixed-effects analyses with trial-level data bolster the conclusion that any effect is likely to be negligible.

Another possibility is that retention of semantic content in these tasks is so robust that individual differences in capacity do not substantially influence performance. If semantic representation is automatically and redundantly encoded in long-term networks, small differences in maintenance capacity may not be enough to alter recognition for semantic changes. Thus, a lack of correlation with digit span may reflect the nature of the target cognitive process rather than a failure of the measurement instrument.

It is also important to consider the task demands and the measurement of change detection. The primary dependent measure required a binary same/different judgment, followed by a categorical classification of change type. This procedure taps recognition and metacognitive labeling abilities. The binary judgment may be driven primarily by a comparison at the level of meaning representation, especially when participants prioritize understanding over surface detail. Even participants with higher span may elect to rely on semantic comparison because it is the most diagnostic route to a correct decision in naturalistic tasks. Thus, the task context itself could attenuate the influence of capacity differences.

These interpretations suggest several directions for future research. First, studies should employ a broader array of working memory measures, particularly complex span tasks that impose concurrent processing. Comparing simple and complex span measures within the same sample could reveal whether processing-plus-storage capacity predicts retention of syntactic detail when simple storage measures do not. Second, researchers could manipulate encoding conditions or incentives to encourage maintenance of surface form. For example, if participants were explicitly instructed or rewarded for verbatim recall, higher working memory individuals might show an advantage. Third, varying the delay interval or the nature of intervening tasks could test whether capacity differences emerge under conditions of greater interference or longer retention intervals. Fourth, neuroimaging or electrophysiological measures might reveal whether higher-span individuals show different patterns of neural activity during encoding that are not evident in overt behavioral measures.

Methodologically, future work should consider alternative operationalizations of syntactic change. In the present study, syntactic manipulations preserved propositional content but altered grammatical structure. Some syntactic changes may be more salient than others depending on the particular alternation used. Utilizing a wider range of syntactic constructions, including violations or less common alternations, might yield different sensitivity patterns and possibly generate relations with capacity.

Despite the null effect for digit span, the present study’s replication of the semantic-over-surface pattern is valuable. It provides converging evidence that semantic information is prioritized and retained more reliably than surface form in auditory discourse comprehension under typical listening conditions. The existence of such robust prioritization may be adaptive from a communicative perspective: remembering the gist of a message is often more important than remembering idiosyncratic wording. The finding that syntactic changes are the hardest to detect may have implications for models of language processing that assume persistent maintenance of hierarchical structure. If syntactic form is not preserved, then models that rely on sustained activation of syntactic frames may need to incorporate mechanisms for rapid abstraction and discarding of form when it is not essential for meaning.

There are limitations worth acknowledging. The working memory measure used here, digit span, is limited in capturing the multifaceted nature of working memory. Future replications using complex span tasks and additional cognitive measures would strengthen conclusions about the role of capacity. Additionally, the sample consisted primarily of young adults, and it remains an open question whether capacity effects might emerge across broader age ranges or in populations with greater variability in working memory (for example, older adults or clinical groups). Another limitation is the brief retention interval and the simple distractor task employed; different intervals and distractors might produce different patterns of decay and possibly reveal capacity effects.

In conclusion, the present study replicates the robust tendency for semantic content to be better preserved than surface lexical or syntactic form in short-term recognition of discourse. Contrary to some theoretical expectations, digit span did not predict recognition of semantic or syntactic changes. This pattern suggests either that digit span is not a suitable measure of the working memory components relevant to maintaining linguistic detail, or that semantic retention is largely independent of the form of capacity indexed by simple span. Future work should employ richer measures of working memory, manipulate encoding priorities, and test across a broader range of participant populations and retention intervals to clarify the precise role of working memory in the memory for different levels of linguistic representation.
