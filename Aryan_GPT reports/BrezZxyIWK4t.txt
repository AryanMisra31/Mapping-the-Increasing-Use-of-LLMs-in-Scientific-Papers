Introduction

Human memory is a central topic of cognitive psychology and neuroscience, and understanding the factors that influence encoding, consolidation, and retrieval has been a longstanding goal in both basic and applied research. Memory is not a unitary construct, and different types of memory such as declarative and procedural memory show distinct patterns of sensitivity to biological and environmental influences. One of the most robust biological factors implicated in memory consolidation is sleep, and a large literature has documented that sleep following learning enhances later recall across a variety of tasks. Research examining the sleep-memory relationship has typically focused on the role of different sleep stages, the timing of sleep relative to learning, and the total amount or quality of sleep that a person receives. The interest in sleep and memory arises from both theoretical models and practical concerns, given the prevalence of sleep loss in modern societies and the importance of robust memory for education, work, and everyday functioning. Theoretical accounts such as the active systems consolidation hypothesis propose that sleep is a time during which newly encoded memory traces are reactivated and gradually redistributed from transient to more stable stores. This reactivation is hypothesized to depend on interactions between hippocampal and neocortical networks, and it appears particularly pronounced during slow-wave sleep, which has been linked to improvements in declarative memory. Empirically, studies have shown that participants who sleep after learning often perform better on delayed memory tests than participants who remain awake for comparable delays, and this benefit has been observed across different ages and experimental paradigms. For example, work that directly contrasts recall after a night of sleep with recall after an equivalent period of wakefulness frequently finds a sizable advantage for the sleep condition. Further refinement of the literature has examined early versus late nocturnal sleep, with some studies suggesting that particular sleep stages dominate in different parts of the night and that these stages may differentially support procedural versus declarative memory consolidation.

In parallel with the sleep literature, motivational factors have also been shown to influence memory. Motivation can be conceptualized in many ways, including intrinsic motivation, which emanates from the learner's internal interests, and extrinsic motivation, which is driven by external incentives such as monetary rewards. A body of work in cognitive psychology and behavioral economics has demonstrated that extrinsic rewards can increase attentional allocation, effortful processing, and strategic use of mnemonic techniques, thereby leading to enhanced encoding and later recall under some circumstances. Neurobiological studies have linked reward processing to dopaminergic signaling in the mesolimbic pathway, and dopamine has been implicated in synaptic plasticity and long-term potentiation, mechanisms that are foundational to long-term memory. Importantly, some research suggests that rewards presented at encoding can tag certain memory traces for preferential consolidation, potentially interacting with offline processes such as sleep-based reactivation. Thus, the relationship between reward and memory is potentially complex; reward could enhance immediate encoding, facilitate subsequent rehearsal, and interact with biological consolidation processes in ways that amplify memory outcomes.

Given the separate literatures on sleep and reward, a natural question is whether these factors operate independently or interactively in shaping long-term memory. Some recent theoretical accounts have proposed that reward-related neuromodulatory signals might bias the selection of memory traces to be consolidated during sleep, effectively prioritizing rewarded items for replay and strengthening. Empirical tests of such interactions have produced mixed results, with some studies reporting that rewards enhance sleep-dependent consolidation and others finding little or no interactive benefit. Methodological differences across studies such as the nature of the reward, the timing of reward delivery, the memory materials used, and the operationalization of sleep (e.g., total sleep time versus specific sleep stage measures) may account for some of the discrepancies. Moreover, many studies that examine interactions between sleep and reward use overnight manipulations where sleep or wake is experimentally controlled, whereas other studies rely on individual differences in habitual sleep quantity or quality measured with questionnaires. These methodological choices have implications for the generalizability of findings and for the degree to which one can infer causal mechanisms.

Beyond the main effects and interactions between sleep and reward, it is also important to consider boundary conditions that might influence whether an effect is observed. For instance, the salience of the reward, the value of the incentive, and the immediacy of reward delivery can all moderate whether reward affects encoding. Similarly, the amount of sleep needed to observe benefits may depend on the type of material learned and the prior knowledge of participants. Age is another moderator because sleep architecture changes across the lifespan, and the sleep-memory relationship can vary as a function of developmental stage. Experimental context, such as laboratory versus online testing, and individual differences like chronotype, stress level, and baseline cognitive ability, can further influence outcomes. Thus, when designing studies to examine sleep and reward, researchers must carefully consider these potential moderators and control for or measure them where possible.

The present experiment was designed to examine the independent and joint effects of habitual sleep and extrinsic reward on memory recall in a controlled laboratory-style task. The study used a within-subjects manipulation of extrinsic reward such that each participant completed two memory recall tests, one in which an extrinsic reward was offered contingent on performance and one in which no such reward was offered. In addition, participants completed a standardized sleep questionnaire that indexed habitual sleep over the prior week. Based on their scores, participants were categorized into high and low sleep groups to allow a between-subjects test of habitual sleep effects. This mixed design allowed us to test three primary questions: whether extrinsic reward improves recall relative to no reward, whether habitual sleep predicts differential recall, and whether there is an interaction in which reward effects are contingent on the amount of habitual sleep.

Several predictions were generated based on the existing literature. First, based on the large body of evidence supporting a beneficial role of sleep for declarative memory, we predicted that participants in the high-sleep group would demonstrate better overall recall than participants in the low-sleep group. Second, given studies reporting that extrinsic rewards can enhance encoding and strategic effort, we predicted that the rewarded list would be recalled more accurately than the non-rewarded list. Third, motivated by theories suggesting that reward biases consolidation during sleep, we hypothesized an interaction in which the beneficial effect of reward would be amplified among individuals who reported higher sleep, possibly because salient information tagged by reward signals is preferentially consolidated during sleep-driven replay. These predictions were tested using a mixed ANOVA and complementary analyses.

This experiment also served to address gaps in the literature where within-subject manipulations of reward have seldom been combined with measures of habitual sleep in healthy adult samples. By using a within-subject approach for reward, we minimized between-subject variability related to motivation or general mnemonic ability. By employing a questionnaire-based measure of sleep, we made use of a practical and widely available method for assessing habitual sleep that could be feasibly implemented in non-laboratory settings. We acknowledge that objective sleep measures such as actigraphy or polysomnography provide more precise information about sleep stages and continuity, but questionnaires nevertheless capture meaningful individual differences that correlate with objective measures and with cognitive outcomes in many studies.

The design of the present study also considered potential confounding factors. To guard against order effects, the assignment of reward to list 1 versus list 2 was counterbalanced across participants. Word lists were matched for length and average lexical properties to avoid systematic differences in task difficulty across conditions. The reward amount was chosen to be meaningful within the context of the experiment while remaining ethically appropriate and modest to avoid undue pressure. Participants were given standardized instructions and practice trials to reduce variability arising from misunderstanding task demands. Finally, demographic and sleep-related covariates such as age, habitual caffeine intake, and recent sleep disruptions were recorded to permit exploratory analyses and sensitivity checks.

Despite these careful design choices, we anticipated potential limitations that warranted caution. The use of a self-report sleep measure limits inferences about specific sleep stages that might mediate consolidation effects. The magnitude of the extrinsic reward and the laboratory context might influence motivational engagement differently than real-world incentives. Moreover, the sample size, while adequate for detecting medium-sized effects, might be insufficient for detecting small interaction effects; thus, non-significant interactions would need to be interpreted conservatively. Nevertheless, the mixed design and careful control of procedural factors make this study a useful contribution to the literature on sleep, reward, and memory.

Taken together, the present study sought to clarify whether extrinsic reward and habitual sleep exert independent or interacting influences on short-term recall of verbal lists. We tested a set of pre-registered hypotheses using a 2 (reward vs no reward; within-subject) by 2 (high vs low habitual sleep; between-subject) design. The study aims to contribute to both theoretical debates regarding the mechanisms by which sleep and reward influence memory and to applied considerations about optimizing learning through motivational and sleep-related interventions. By documenting the patterns of main effects and interactions, and by providing effect size estimates and exploratory correlations, the study provides evidence that can guide future research toward more mechanistic investigations and more precise operationalizations of sleep and reward.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and local community advertisements. A total of 60 participants completed the study and were included in the analyses. All participants were adults between the ages of 18 and 35 years. Participants reported being free from neurological and psychiatric diagnoses and were not taking medications known to affect cognition or sleep. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board, and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Participants received a base payment for participation and could earn additional money contingent on performance in the reward condition as described below.

A power analysis was conducted prior to data collection assuming a medium effect size for the main effects (f = 0.25) and aiming for 80% power at an alpha of .05 for the mixed ANOVA design. The power analysis indicated that a sample size of approximately 54 to 64 participants would be sufficient. Sixty participants were recruited to provide a small buffer for possible attrition or data exclusion. Data from all participants who completed the protocol were retained, and no outliers were excluded based on pre-registered criteria.

Materials included two matched lists of common English nouns. Each list consisted of 20 words chosen to be of moderate frequency and concreteness, and lists were matched on mean word length and subjective familiarity. The lists were piloted in a separate sample to ensure comparable baseline recall rates. Words were presented in a randomized order for each participant within each list. The reward manipulation was implemented at the list level such that one list was associated with monetary incentives for correct recall and the other list was not. The assignment of reward to list 1 or list 2 was counterbalanced across participants to control for order effects.

Habitual sleep was measured with a validated sleep questionnaire that assesses typical sleep duration and quality over the previous week. The questionnaire included items about average nightly sleep duration, sleep onset latency, number of awakenings, and subjective sleep quality. A composite sleep score was computed by summing standardized responses on these items such that higher values indicated better habitual sleep. Participants were then split into high and low sleep groups using a median split on the composite score. The median split produced approximately equal groups and allowed us to examine between-subject differences in habitual sleep as a categorical factor. In addition to the categorical grouping, continuous sleep measures were retained for correlational analyses.

The procedure began with participants completing the consent form and demographic questionnaire. Participants then completed the sleep questionnaire and other baseline measures, including items on recent caffeine intake and typical sleep schedules. After these baseline measures, participants received instructions for the memory task. Each participant completed two study-test cycles, one for each word list. The order of lists and the reward assignment were counterbalanced between participants. In the rewarded condition, participants were informed that they would receive a small monetary bonus for each correctly recalled word up to a specified maximum amount. The exact reward amount was modest and clearly communicated. In the no-reward condition, participants were told that their performance would not affect additional payment beyond the base compensation.

During each study phase, words were presented sequentially on a computer screen at a rate of 3 seconds per item with a 500 ms inter-stimulus interval. Participants were instructed to try to remember the words for a later memory test. Immediately following the study phase, participants completed a brief distractor task of simple arithmetic problems for two minutes to minimize reliance on short-term memory alone. After the distractor, participants completed a free recall test during which they typed any words they could remember from the studied list. No feedback about accuracy was provided during the test phase. The free recall time was fixed at five minutes for each list. The experimenter recorded the number of correctly recalled words for each list for subsequent scoring.

To check for possible order effects and fatigue, participants also completed a short subjective effort measure after each recall test. This measure asked participants to rate how much effort they had exerted during the preceding study phase on a Likert scale. These effort ratings were used as potential covariates in exploratory analyses. After completing both study-test cycles, participants completed a brief debriefing questionnaire that assessed their awareness of the reward manipulation, perceived difficulty of lists, and strategies they used. The debriefing questionnaire helped ensure that participants experienced both conditions as intended.

The primary dependent variable was the number of correctly recalled words for each list. Correct recall was scored strictly, requiring accurate reproduction of the target word; spelling errors that did not change the meaning were accepted. Intrusions and repetitions were recorded but were not counted as correct responses. Data were entered into a statistical software package for analysis. The main inferential test was a 2 (Reward: rewarded vs non-rewarded; within-subjects) by 2 (Sleep group: high vs low; between-subjects) mixed ANOVA on the number of correctly recalled words. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared for ANOVA tests and Cohen's d for t-tests.

Prior to conducting the ANOVA, assumptions were checked. Normality of residuals was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk tests and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. Homogeneity of variances across the sleep groups was assessed with Levene's test. Sphericity was not a concern for the two-level within-subject factor. When assumption violations were detected, robust or non-parametric alternatives were considered and reported. In addition to the primary ANOVA, paired-samples t-tests were used to examine within-subject differences between rewarded and non-rewarded lists separately for each sleep group. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare high and low sleep groups on overall recall and on each list separately. Pearson correlations were computed between continuous sleep measures and recall scores to assess dose-response relationships.

Exploratory analyses included testing whether effort ratings differed across reward and sleep conditions and whether they mediated any observed effects. Order effects were tested by including list order as a between-subjects factor in supplementary analyses. The potential influence of demographic covariates such as age and caffeine intake was also examined by including these variables in ANCOVAs when appropriate. In addition, Bayes factors were computed for key comparisons to quantify evidence for the absence of an effect when p-values were non-significant. All statistical tests used an alpha level of .05 for conventional significance, and where multiple comparisons were performed, p-values were interpreted with appropriate caution.

Results

Descriptive statistics for recall performance are presented first to provide an overview of the data. Across all participants, mean recall for the rewarded list was 10.8 items out of 20 with a standard deviation of 3.7. For the non-rewarded list, the mean recall was 11.1 items with a standard deviation of 3.5. The overall mean across both lists was 10.95 items with a standard deviation of 3.56. For the high-sleep group, mean recall on the rewarded list was 12.2 items with a standard deviation of 3.1. For the high-sleep group in the non-rewarded condition, mean recall was 12.4 items with a standard deviation of 3.0. For the low-sleep group, mean recall for the rewarded list was 9.4 items with a standard deviation of 3.6. The low-sleep group mean recall for the non-rewarded list was 9.8 items with a standard deviation of 3.3. These descriptive statistics suggest that the high-sleep group tended to recall more words overall than the low-sleep group, and that mean performance was slightly higher in the non-rewarded condition across the full sample.

The distributional properties of the primary dependent variables were examined prior to hypothesis testing. Visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots indicated that the residuals for recall scores approximated normality. Shapiro-Wilk tests conducted separately for reward and non-reward conditions did not indicate strong deviations from normality (all p-values > .05). Levene's test for equality of variances across the high and low sleep groups was non-significant for both lists, indicating homogeneity of variances (all p-values > .10). Given that assumptions appeared tenable, the planned mixed ANOVA was conducted.

A 2 (Reward: rewarded vs non-rewarded; within-subjects) by 2 (Sleep group: high vs low; between-subjects) mixed ANOVA was performed on the number of correctly recalled words. The main effect of Reward was not significant, F(1,58) = 0.95, p = .333, partial eta-squared = .016. This result indicates that, across the whole sample, the extrinsic reward manipulation did not produce a reliable difference in recall performance. The main effect of Sleep group was significant, F(1,58) = 9.12, p = .0035, partial eta-squared = .136. This effect indicates that individuals in the high-sleep group recalled more words overall than individuals in the low-sleep group. The Reward by Sleep group interaction was not significant, F(1,58) = 1.88, p = .176, partial eta-squared = .031. Thus, the effect of sleep did not significantly vary as a function of reward condition in the omnibus test.

Given the significant main effect of sleep and the non-significant interaction, follow-up comparisons were conducted to characterize the nature of the sleep effect. An independent-samples t-test comparing overall recall averaged across lists between the high-sleep and low-sleep groups revealed a significant difference, t(58) = 3.02, p = .004, Cohen's d = 0.78. The high-sleep group recalled significantly more words on average than the low-sleep group. When comparing groups separately for each list, the high-sleep group outperformed the low-sleep group on the rewarded list, t(58) = 2.86, p = .006, d = 0.74, and on the non-rewarded list, t(58) = 3.09, p = .003, d = 0.80. These follow-up tests show that the main effect of sleep is robust across both lists rather than being confined to one list.

Paired-samples t-tests were used to examine within-subject differences between rewarded and non-rewarded lists separately within each sleep group. For the high-sleep group, the difference in recall between the rewarded list (M = 12.2, SD = 3.1) and the non-rewarded list (M = 12.4, SD = 3.0) was not significant, t(29) = -0.65, p = .519, Cohen's d = 0.12. For the low-sleep group, the difference between the rewarded list (M = 9.4, SD = 3.6) and the non-rewarded list (M = 9.8, SD = 3.3) was also not significant, t(29) = -1.05, p = .302, d = 0.19. These within-group comparisons corroborate the omnibus ANOVA result that reward did not produce significant within-subject changes in recall.

Because the Reward main effect was null, Bayes factor analyses were conducted to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis relative to the alternative. A Bayesian paired-samples t-test comparing rewarded and non-rewarded lists across participants yielded a Bayes factor BF10 = 0.24, which indicates substantial evidence in favor of the null hypothesis that there is no difference between the reward and no-reward conditions. The Bayesian evidence supports the conclusion that the extrinsic reward manipulation, as implemented, did not meaningfully alter recall.

Correlation analyses were performed to examine the relationship between the continuous sleep composite score and recall performance. Across the full sample, the composite sleep score correlated positively with overall recall averaged across lists, r = .34, p = .009. The correlation between sleep score and recall was similar when examined separately for each list, with r = .31 for the rewarded list (p = .017) and r = .36 for the non-rewarded list (p = .005). These continuous associations reinforce the between-group comparisons and suggest a dose-response relationship wherein better habitual sleep is associated with better memory performance.

Potential confounds such as effort ratings and order effects were evaluated. Effort ratings were slightly higher in the reward condition compared with the no-reward condition across participants, but this difference did not reach significance, t(59) = 1.40, p = .166. Effort ratings did not differ significantly between the high and low sleep groups, t(58) = 0.92, p = .362. Including effort ratings as a covariate in an ANCOVA did not appreciably change the pattern of results; the main effect of sleep remained significant, F(1,57) = 8.74, p = .004, and the reward main effect remained non-significant, F(1,57) = 0.82, p = .370. Thus, subjective effort does not appear to explain the sleep group differences.

Order effects were tested by including the counterbalanced order of lists as a between-subjects factor. There was no significant main effect of order, F(1,56) = 0.58, p = .449, and there were no significant interactions involving order, all p-values > .20. This pattern indicates that the order in which lists were presented did not substantially influence the primary outcomes. Potential demographic covariates including age and self-reported caffeine intake were also examined. Age was not significantly correlated with recall in this sample (all p-values > .10), and controlling for caffeine intake did not alter the significance of the sleep main effect.

An examination of individual differences showed that the range of the sleep composite score was sufficient to detect associations with recall. The sleep composite showed acceptable internal consistency in this sample (Cronbach's alpha = .78), indicating that the questionnaire captured a coherent construct of habitual sleep. Inspection of scatterplots suggested a roughly linear relationship between sleep and recall with no obvious outliers driving the effect. Sensitivity analyses that removed participants with extreme sleep scores did not change the pattern of results.

Overall, the results show a clear benefit of habitual sleep on memory recall in this sample, with high-sleep participants recalling more words than low-sleep participants. In contrast, the extrinsic reward manipulation did not yield improved recall and there was no significant interaction between reward and sleep group. Bayesian analyses provided support for the null effect of reward. Correlational analyses using continuous sleep measures reinforced the sleep-recall association. Exploratory analyses indicated that effort, order, and demographic covariates did not meaningfully alter these conclusions.

Discussion

The present study tested the independent and joint effects of habitual sleep and extrinsic reward on short-term recall of verbal lists in a mixed within- and between-subjects design. The primary findings were that habitual sleep was associated with better recall performance, while the extrinsic reward manipulation did not significantly affect recall. The mixed ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of sleep group such that participants with higher habitual sleep recalled more words overall than participants with lower habitual sleep. In contrast, there was no significant main effect of reward, and Bayesian analyses supported the null hypothesis that the reward manipulation, as implemented, did not influence recall. The interaction between reward and sleep group was not significant, and follow-up tests indicated that the sleep advantage was present in both rewarded and non-rewarded conditions. These results align with some portions of the literature while diverging from other findings that have reported reward-related enhancements of sleep-dependent consolidation.

The robust association between habitual sleep and memory observed in this study contributes to a growing body of evidence that sleep supports declarative memory performance. The present findings extend prior laboratory studies which often manipulate sleep experimentally by comparing sleep and wake intervals, by showing that individual differences in habitual sleep, assessed by questionnaire, are meaningfully related to memory outcomes in a constrained memory task. The effect size observed for the sleep group comparison was moderate to large, suggesting that habitual sleep differences among young adults can translate into noticeable differences in cognitive performance. This result is consistent with theoretical accounts that emphasize the role of sleep in systems consolidation and synaptic homeostasis, both of which would be expected to enhance stabilization and accessibility of recently learned declarative material.

Several mechanisms could plausibly underlie the observed sleep-recall relationship. First, adequate habitual sleep may increase the likelihood that participants experienced sufficient amounts of slow-wave sleep and REM sleep across nights, both of which have been implicated in different aspects of memory consolidation. Slow-wave sleep in particular has been associated with hippocampus-dependent consolidation processes that benefit declarative memory. Second, habitual sleep quality may influence attentional and encoding processes at the time of learning, with better-rested individuals being more alert and efficient in encoding new information. Third, habitual sleep may protect against cumulative fatigue and elevated stress, both of which can impair memory. The correlational results are consistent with a composite influence wherein both encoding and consolidation processes are improved among individuals who habitually sleep more or better.

The absence of a reward effect in this study is notable in light of literature showing that extrinsic incentives can sometimes enhance memory. There are several potential explanations for the null result. One possibility is that the magnitude or the nature of the reward used here was insufficient to meaningfully alter motivational states or encoding strategies. The monetary bonus was intentionally modest for ethical and budgetary reasons, and prior research suggests that reward magnitude can modulate behavioral and neural responses. A second possibility is that the within-subjects manipulation of reward in a context where both rewarded and non-rewarded lists were studied within the same session may have led to spillover of strategic encoding processes into the no-reward condition. Participants knowing that one list would be rewarded might have adopted encoding strategies that they also applied to the non-rewarded list, thereby reducing differential effects. Third, it is possible that the memory materials—moderately difficult word lists—were not sufficiently sensitive to reward-driven enhancements in encoding. In tasks where reward has a clearer effect, such as associative learning or tasks requiring active strategy selection, reward may interact more strongly with memory processes.

Another explanation relates to the timing of motivational enhancement and consolidation processes. Some studies that have demonstrated reward-related biases in consolidation have used designs where reward signals are paired with specific items and where sleep is monitored overnight to capture reactivation processes. When reward is thought to tag specific traces for prioritization, the effect may be most apparent in experimental paradigms that permit objective measures of sleep physiology or where delayed tests occur after a night of sleep. In the present study, habitual sleep was measured but specific sleep stages were not recorded, so any facilitation of reward-tagged replay during, for example, slow-wave sleep, would be invisible to our measures. Therefore, the null reward result does not preclude the possibility that reward influences sleep-dependent consolidation at a physiological level that requires different methods to detect.

The lack of a significant interaction between sleep and reward suggests that in this sample and with this implementation, habitual sleep did not modulate the impact of extrinsic reward on recall. Follow-up comparisons did show that the high-sleep group outperformed the low-sleep group in both reward and no-reward conditions, indicating that the beneficial influence of sleep was general rather than reward-specific. This pattern implies that habitual sleep supports memory in a broad manner rather than selectively amplifying the consolidation of externally incentivized items. From a practical perspective, this suggests that promoting adequate sleep could be a robust strategy for improving memory across contexts, whereas relying on modest extrinsic rewards alone may be less effective.

The present findings both align with and differ from previous research, and examining these consistencies and discrepancies can help clarify boundary conditions. The beneficial role of sleep on memory conforms with numerous experimental sleep studies that have demonstrated improvements following sleep relative to wake. However, the absence of a reward effect contrasts with some reports that reward enhances memory and that reward-related signals interact with sleep-based consolidation. Differences in study design may account for these divergences. For instance, the within-subject reward manipulation used here is methodologically strong for controlling individual differences but may reduce the contrast between rewarded and non-rewarded conditions. Studies reporting reward benefits often employ between-subjects designs or item-specific reward cues paired with overnight polysomnographic monitoring. Furthermore, the intensity and salience of rewards vary widely across studies, and this heterogeneity likely contributes to mixed findings.

Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. First, the use of a median split to categorize participants into high and low sleep groups simplifies what is in reality a continuous variable and can reduce statistical power and precision. Although complementary correlational analyses using continuous sleep scores were performed and supported the main findings, future work could benefit from larger samples that permit more nuanced modeling of sleep as a continuous moderator. Second, sleep was measured via self-report questionnaire rather than objective measures such as actigraphy or polysomnography. Objective measures would enable assessment of specific sleep stages and continuity metrics that are theorized to be most relevant for memory consolidation. Third, the reward manipulation was modest and delivered within a single session; different reward magnitudes or delayed reinforcement schedules could produce different outcomes. Fourth, the memory task involved immediate study and short-delay recall and did not include a long-delay test following a night of sleep for all participants, which would be a stricter test of sleep-dependent consolidation.

Future research can build on these findings in several ways. One promising approach is to combine item-level reward manipulations with overnight polysomnographic recording to directly test whether rewarded items are preferentially replayed during specific sleep stages. Such designs would clarify whether reward tagging operates via sleep-dependent reactivation and would identify the sleep microarchitecture associated with prioritized consolidation. Another productive direction is to manipulate reward magnitude and delivery timing to determine the conditions under which reward influences encoding, rehearsal, or offline consolidation. Additionally, longitudinal or training studies could examine whether habitual sleep improvement interventions lead to sustained gains in memory and whether these gains interact with motivational incentives over extended learning periods. Including ecological learning tasks that more closely approximate real-world educational contexts would also enhance the practical relevance of findings.

The present results also have implications for applied settings. Educational strategies that rely on extrinsic rewards to motivate learning may need to be supplemented with interventions that support healthy sleep habits to maximize retention. Employers and students might benefit more from policies and schedules that promote sufficient sleep than from small monetary incentives aimed at improving short-term performance. At the same time, the mixed literature on reward suggests that motivational techniques should not be categorically dismissed but rather calibrated to the task demands, individual differences, and learning schedules involved.

In conclusion, this study found that habitual sleep is a reliable predictor of short-term verbal recall performance, whereas a modest extrinsic monetary reward did not influence recall under the conditions tested. The sleep benefit was present across rewarded and non-rewarded conditions, suggesting a general facilitative role for sleep rather than a selective enhancement of externally incentivized information. These findings reinforce the importance of habitual sleep for cognitive function and suggest that modest external incentives, at least in within-session paradigms like the one employed here, may have limited utility for enhancing memory. Future studies employing objective sleep measures, varied reward structures, and delayed testing following overnight sleep will be valuable in further elucidating how sleep and reward jointly shape memory consolidation and retrieval.
