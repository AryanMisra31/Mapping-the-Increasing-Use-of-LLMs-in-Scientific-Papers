Introduction

Mental representation and mental imagery have long been central constructs in cognitive psychology and cognitive neuroscience, and they play a prominent role in theories of language comprehension and perception. Researchers have argued that comprehension of linguistic material frequently engages sensory and perceptual systems, such that readers or listeners form quasi-perceptual representations of described scenes, objects, and actions. The idea that language processing recruits imagery-like processes has been supported by a variety of behavioral, neurophysiological, and neuroimaging studies showing modality-specific activation during the comprehension of sensory language. Within that broader literature, visual imagery has been characterized as a multidimensional construct: it varies in vividness, controllability, and fidelity to perception, and these qualities can differ markedly between individuals. Consequently, individual differences in imagery ability have been proposed as an important moderator of the degree to which language comprehension engages perceptual systems. If some participants naturally form vivid mental images while processing language, they may show stronger coupling between linguistic input and visual-perceptual processes than participants with less vivid imagery. Conversely, participants with weak imagery may rely on more abstract or propositional representations, leading to different patterns of perceptual activation and behavioral responses.

Despite the theoretical importance of imagery in language processing, relatively few studies have examined how between-subject variation in visual imagery relates to performance on tasks that index perceptual involvement during language comprehension. The dearth of work on this question is surprising given the widespread use of self-report inventories such as the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ) and the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory in cognitive research, and given the growing recognition of the influence of individual differences on cognitive processes more generally. A clear empirical gap remains: we do not yet have a systematic understanding of whether and how subjective vividness of imagery moderates behavioral signatures of perceptual stimulation during language tasks.

Another individual difference factor that has been linked to perceptual and attentional asymmetries is handedness. Numerous studies have documented lateralized patterns of attention allocation and perceptual bias that co-vary with handedness, consistent with hemispheric specialization. In the visual domain, handedness has been associated with biases in spatial attention and line bisection, with some evidence pointing to differential orienting tendencies for right- versus left-handed individuals. Because lateralized attention systems can influence the efficiency and dynamics of perceptual processing, handedness has been considered as a potential covariate or moderator in experimental paradigms that assess perceptual engagement.

A prior study by Estes and colleagues (hereafter referred to as the original study) sought to explore attention orientation in relation to handedness and used a task intended to index perceptual stimulation and attentional bias. The original study reported an effect of handedness on attention orientation measures and suggested a relationship between handedness and certain attentional tendencies. The design, measures, and statistical approach of that work influenced subsequent attempts to replicate or extend those findings in different domains, including language-related contexts.

Motivated by the theoretical linkage between imagery, perception, and language comprehension, the present study attempted to detect a link between visual vividness and language perception by adapting and replicating the attention-orientation paradigm used in the original work while introducing a language-based perceptual stimulation task. In particular, we assessed subjective visual imagery vividness using a widely used questionnaire and tested whether vividness scores predicted behavioral indices of perceptual stimulation when participants processed language stimuli that were likely to elicit visual imagery. We also included handedness as a covariate in our analyses, mirroring the original study’s focus and enabling a test of whether handedness effects would replicate and whether handedness would interact with imagery vividness.

There are several plausible mechanisms by which visual vividness might influence language-related perceptual measures. First, participants with more vivid imagery may spontaneously generate richer, more spatially detailed visual representations while comprehending sentences, and those representations might facilitate or bias attention to locations or features in a concurrent visual discrimination or detection task. Second, vivid imagers may be more prone to attend to the visual aspects of language, which could amplify any perceptual priming or interference effects produced by language. Third, if imagery recruits the same neural populations that are engaged in early perceptual processing, then imagery vividness could modulate perceptual sensitivity in a measurable way.

Alternatively, several reasons exist for expecting null or weak relations between subjective vividness and behavioral markers of perceptual stimulation. The self-report measures of vividness may capture introspective beliefs or trait-like dispositions that are only weakly tied to real-time perceptual processing. Vividness ratings might reflect the conscious experience of imagery without necessarily indexing the automaticity, timing, or neural engagement of visual systems during language processing. Moreover, common language comprehension tasks may be insensitive to individual differences in imagery because they rely on general cognitive or semantic processes that mask subtle perceptual contributions.

Given these theoretical considerations, the present study pursued three primary objectives. The first objective was to replicate the handedness-related attention orientation finding reported in the original study using a task closely modeled on that paradigm. The second objective was to evaluate whether individual differences in visual imagery vividness, as assessed by a standard questionnaire, predict the extent to which language stimuli produce measurable perceptual stimulation or attentional biases. The third objective was to test whether handedness and imagery vividness interact in shaping language-related perceptual measures, which could indicate that lateralized attentional tendencies modulate the influence of imagery on perception.

To address these objectives, we designed a language-perception experiment combining sentence stimuli that promote visual imagery with a concurrent perceptual discrimination task that captures attention orientation and perceptual sensitivity. Participants completed the VVIQ to provide an index of subjective imagery vividness and a handedness inventory to quantify laterality. The primary dependent variables included response time and accuracy on the perceptual task, and derived measures of attentional bias following language cues. We planned to use mixed-effects models and regression analyses to assess the main effects and interactions of vividness and handedness, while controlling for demographic covariates.

We anticipated three possible patterns of outcomes. One possible pattern was a strong link between VVIQ scores and perceptual task performance such that participants with higher vividness would show faster or more accurate performance on detection tasks following image-eliciting sentences, and potentially larger attentional biases. A second possible pattern was replication of the original handedness effect without any relation to vividness, suggesting that handedness-related asymmetries in attention operate independently of subjective imagery. A third possibility was that neither handedness nor imagery would predict performance, which would raise questions about the sensitivity of the measures and the robustness of the original findings.

The rationale for this investigation rests on bridging two literatures that have remained largely parallel: research on imagery and individual differences on the one hand, and research on lateralized attention and the behavioral consequences of handedness on the other hand. Integrating these approaches in a language processing context permits a more nuanced understanding of how trait-like characteristics and lateralized systems jointly influence perceptual involvement in comprehension. Furthermore, by attempting a conceptual replication of an important handedness-attention finding, we contribute to the cumulative assessment of the reliability of lateralization effects across tasks and samples.

It is also important to underscore methodological concerns that motivated specific design choices. Self-report measures of imagery are convenient and widely used, but they can be susceptible to demand characteristics, response biases, and limited construct coverage. To address some of these concerns, we supplemented the VVIQ with a task-based manipulation that included objective response metrics and included pre-registered analytical strategies to reduce researcher degrees of freedom. Similarly, handedness was assessed with a multi-item inventory rather than a single-item self-label, which increases measurement precision and allows for continuous modeling of laterality.

The present study has potential implications beyond the immediate empirical questions. If vividness reliably predicts perceptual involvement in language processing, then models of embodiment and grounded cognition will need to incorporate individual difference parameters to account for heterogeneity in empirical findings. Likewise, if handedness reliably shapes attentional orientation in language contexts, then researchers should routinely consider laterality as a covariate in perceptual and attentional studies. Conversely, null findings would prompt a re-evaluation of common measurement practices and suggest that more fine-grained or objective measures are necessary to capture the interplay between imagery, attention, and language.

Finally, we note that replication studies and investigations of individual differences are crucial for building robust and generalizable models of cognitive phenomena. The current work was motivated by the need to understand boundary conditions for the influence of imagery on perception and to test the generality of handedness effects across paradigms. Because such studies often confront challenges related to sample composition, measurement fidelity, and statistical power, we took a conservative approach in our design and analysis to maximize interpretability and to provide transparent accounts of limitations. In the sections that follow, we detail the methods, report the results, and discuss the implications of the findings for theory and future research.

Methods

Participants

A total of 168 participants were recruited from a university subject pool and from the surrounding community to participate in an experiment on language and perception for monetary compensation or course credit. Of these participants, 12 were excluded prior to analysis because they did not complete the full session or because their handedness inventory scores were missing, leaving a final analyzed sample of 156 participants. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 49 years (M = 21.6, SD = 4.3). The sample included 112 females and 44 males; gender was recorded but not used as a primary factor in the main analyses. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological disorders. The study was approved by the institutional review board and participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

Materials

Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ). Participants completed a widely used vividness questionnaire that asks them to imagine specific scenes and rate the vividness of their mental images on a multi-point scale. The VVIQ yields a total vividness score such that higher scores indicate more vivid visual imagery. The questionnaire was administered online prior to the experimental tasks to prevent potential carryover effects.

Handedness inventory. Handedness was assessed using a multi-item inventory asking participants to indicate their preferred hand for a variety of common activities (e.g., writing, throwing, use of scissors). Responses were combined into a laterality quotient ranging from -100 (strongly left-handed) to +100 (strongly right-handed). This continuous laterality score was used as a covariate in regression models and also dichotomized at conventional thresholds for some analyses to permit comparisons with the prior study’s categorical analyses.

Language stimuli. The language materials comprised 120 short sentences designed to elicit visual imagery. Half of the sentences described concrete, imageable scenes (image-eliciting sentences), and half described abstract or low-imageability content (non-image-eliciting sentences). Examples of image-eliciting sentences included “The bright red kite drifted across the clear blue sky,” whereas examples of non-image sentences included “The debate reached a critical turning point after the meeting.” Sentences were matched for length and frequency where possible.

Perceptual discrimination task. Following a brief delay after sentence presentation, participants completed a visual discrimination task that involved detecting a small target dot presented either in the left or right visual hemifield. The task was designed so that attention orientation induced by the language stimulus could plausibly bias detection performance. Targets were presented at a fixed eccentricity and for a brief duration calibrated in pilot testing to produce an overall accuracy level of approximately 80% under neutral conditions. Participants indicated target presence and location with speeded button presses.

Apparatus and presentation. Experimental stimuli were presented on a calibrated computer monitor at a viewing distance of approximately 60 cm in a quiet testing room. Responses were collected via a standard keyboard. Sentence stimuli were displayed centrally in black font on a gray background, and the target dot appeared against a uniform gray field.

Procedure

Participants first completed the VVIQ and the handedness inventory on the computer. They then received task instructions and completed 12 practice trials that included both image-eliciting and non-image-eliciting sentences followed by perceptual discrimination trials. The main experiment consisted of four blocks of 30 trials each, yielding 120 experimental trials. In each trial, a sentence was presented centrally for a fixed reading time calculated from average reading rates; participants were instructed to read for comprehension and to form any images that occurred naturally. After a 500 ms blank interval, the visual discrimination display appeared for 150 ms, followed by a response window. Inter-trial intervals were jittered between 800 and 1200 ms.

Trial order was randomized within blocks. Image-eliciting and non-image-eliciting sentences were intermixed, and the side of the target dot was counterbalanced across trials. To probe attentional bias, a subset of trials included neutral filler sentences that contained no spatial language, and these were used as a baseline for computing bias indices.

Data preprocessing

Response times (RTs) were recorded from target onset to the participant’s button press. Trials with RTs less than 150 ms or greater than 2000 ms were flagged as outliers and removed from RT analyses. Accuracy was scored as correct detection and correct location identification. Participants with overall accuracy below 60% were excluded; no participant met this exclusion criterion in the final sample. VVIQ total scores were inspected for range and distribution; scores were centered and standardized for regression analyses. Laterality quotient scores were also centered and used in continuous and categorical analyses.

Planned analyses

We planned to assess three primary relationships: (1) replication of the original handedness effect on attention orientation using a measure of lateralized bias derived from RT differences between left and right target trials, (2) the association between VVIQ vividness scores and perceptual task performance (RT and accuracy) following image-eliciting sentences, and (3) interaction effects between handedness and VVIQ in predicting the perceptual measures. Mixed-effects linear regression models were specified for RTs, with fixed effects for sentence type (image vs. non-image), target side (left vs. right), VVIQ score, laterality quotient, and relevant two- and three-way interactions. Subject-level random intercepts and random slopes for sentence type were included where possible. Accuracy analyses used generalized linear mixed models with a binomial link. In addition to frequentist analyses, we planned to compute Bayes factors for key null effects to quantify evidence in favor of the null where appropriate.

Exploratory analyses included separate analyses by handedness category (left-handed vs. right-handed based on laterality cutoffs), median splits on VVIQ for descriptive comparisons, and correlational analyses between vividness and bias indices derived from neutral filler trials. All analyses were conducted in a statistical computing environment with code archived for reproducibility.

Results

Descriptive statistics and preliminary checks. The final sample (N = 156) had VVIQ scores that ranged from low to high vividness, with mean VVIQ = 62.4, SD = 11.8 on the scale used (higher = more vivid). The distribution of VVIQ scores showed no extreme skewness (skew = -0.12), and there were no floor or ceiling effects. Laterality quotients ranged from -90 to +100, with a mean of 42.7 and a standard deviation of 47.1, indicating more right-handed than left-handed participants in the sample. Using a conventional cutoff of +40 for right-handed and -40 for left-handed participants resulted in 98 right-handed, 34 left-handed, and 24 ambidextrous participants; results reported below were consistent whether ambidextrous participants were excluded or categorized as a separate group.

Accuracy on the perceptual discrimination task averaged 81.3% (SD = 7.6%), consistent with pilot calibration. Mean RT across all correct trials was 468 ms (SD = 83 ms) following target onset after preprocessing and exclusion of outliers as detailed in the Methods. There was a modest practice effect across the four blocks (mean RT decreased by approximately 12 ms from block 1 to block 4), but block number was not a primary variable of interest and was included as a covariate where indicated.

Handedness replication check. To test for replication of the original handedness-related attention orientation effect, we computed a lateralized bias index for each participant defined as mean RT for right-side targets minus mean RT for left-side targets (positive values indicate faster responses to left-side targets). A one-sample t-test examining whether the bias index differed from zero in the full sample revealed a small but reliable leftward bias, t(155) = 2.09, p = .038, Cohen’s d = 0.17, 95% CI for mean = [0.9 ms, 15.2 ms], indicating slightly faster responses to left targets overall. More critically, laterality regression analyses showed that laterality quotient predicted the bias index: a linear regression with bias index as the dependent variable and laterality as predictor yielded β = -0.11 (SE = 0.04), t = -2.75, p = .007, indicating that greater right-handedness was associated with a reduced leftward bias (or a relative rightward bias). When participants were grouped categorically, left-handed participants exhibited a mean bias of +10.8 ms (favoring left targets) whereas right-handed participants exhibited a mean bias of -2.4 ms. An independent-samples t-test comparing left- and right-handed groups showed a significant difference, t(130) = 2.34, p = .021, supporting the presence of a handedness-related asymmetry in attentional orientation in our sample.

These results partially replicated the original study’s handedness-attention orientation finding. The effect size in our data was small-to-moderate and consistent in direction with the prior report. Random-effects mixed models predicting RT with target side, laterality, and their interaction further confirmed a significant laterality × target side interaction, χ²(1) = 6.04, p = .014, indicating that the effect of target side on RT was modulated by handedness.

Vividness and perceptual performance. We next examined whether individual differences in VVIQ vividness scores predicted perceptual task performance following image-eliciting sentences. In the primary mixed-effects linear regression model predicting RTs, fixed effects included sentence type (image vs. non-image), target side, VVIQ (standardized), laterality (standardized), and all two- and three-way interactions. The main effect of sentence type showed that RTs following image-eliciting sentences were slightly slower than following non-image sentences (mean difference = 6.7 ms), but this difference did not reach conventional significance in the RT model, β = 6.7, SE = 4.2, t = 1.60, p = .11. VVIQ did not predict RTs in the model: the main effect of VVIQ was non-significant, β = -1.9, SE = 2.6, t = -0.73, p = .47. Likewise, the critical VVIQ × sentence type interaction was non-significant, β = -2.0, SE = 3.4, t = -0.58, p = .56, indicating that vividness did not differentially affect RTs for image-eliciting versus non-image sentences.

Accuracy models yielded parallel outcomes. A generalized linear mixed model predicting accuracy with the same fixed effects revealed no significant main effect of VVIQ, z = 0.84, p = .40, and no VVIQ × sentence type interaction, z = -0.61, p = .54. The proportion of variance in accuracy explained by VVIQ was negligible, and the confidence intervals around the estimated effects included zero.

To quantify evidence for the absence of an effect of VVIQ, we computed Bayes factors for the VVIQ × sentence type interaction in the RT model using a conservative Cauchy prior. The resulting Bayes factor BF01 was approximately 4.2, indicating moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis relative to the alternative that VVIQ moderates the sentence type effect on RTs.

Interaction of handedness and vividness. Given the replicated handedness effect, we tested whether there was an interaction between laterality and VVIQ in predicting attentional bias and perceptual performance. In a regression predicting the lateralized bias index, the interaction term laterality × VVIQ was non-significant, β = 0.03 (SE = 0.03), t = 0.98, p = .33, suggesting no evidence that vivid imagery modulated the handedness-related bias. In the mixed-effects RT model, the three-way interaction among sentence type, target side, and VVIQ was likewise non-significant, χ²(1) = 0.76, p = .38. These findings indicate that although handedness influenced lateralized attention measures, vividness did not interact with handedness to alter the pattern of perceptual responses.

Exploratory analyses. We conducted several exploratory analyses to probe potential boundary conditions. First, we examined whether extreme vividness scores (top and bottom deciles) showed associations with perceptual measures. Comparison of the top 10% (n = 16) and bottom 10% (n = 16) of VVIQ scorers revealed no reliable differences in RTs following image sentences, t(30) = 0.47, p = .64, or in lateralized bias, t(30) = 0.81, p = .42. Second, restricting analyses to the subset of participants with strong laterality (|laterality| > 60) did not alter the null relation between VVIQ and perceptual performance. Third, correlational analyses between VVIQ and subjective ratings of imagery collected occasionally during the task (on a subset of trials where participants were prompted to report whether they had experienced a visual image) yielded a modest positive correlation, r = .28, p < .001, indicating that VVIQ did relate to self-reported imagery occurrence during the task but did not translate into perceptual performance differences.

Power analysis. Post hoc sensitivity analyses indicated that with our sample size (N = 156), we had 80% power to detect small-to-medium standardized effects of approximately d = 0.35 for group comparisons and approximately β = 0.22 for continuous predictors in regression models at α = .05. Given that observed VVIQ effects were near zero, the analyses suggest that any true effect of vividness on the perceptual measures is likely to be small, if present.

Summary of findings. In brief, we partially replicated the original study’s handedness-attention orientation finding, observing a small but reliable association between laterality and lateralized bias in the perceptual task. However, we did not find evidence that subjective vividness of visual imagery, as measured by the VVIQ, predicted perceptual sensitivity, response speed, or interacted with handedness in our language-perception paradigm. Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence in favor of the null for the key VVIQ interaction. Exploratory checks using extreme groups and sub-samples did not reveal hidden effects.

Discussion

This study set out to examine whether individual differences in subjective visual imagery vividness are associated with perceptual engagement during language processing and to replicate a previously reported handedness-related attention orientation effect in a language-perception paradigm. The data yielded a mixed picture. On one hand, we succeeded in partially replicating the handedness effect reported in the original work: laterality predicted lateralized bias in the visual discrimination task, and categorical comparisons showed that left- and right-handed participants differed in their relative speed to detect targets in different hemifields. These findings reinforce the idea that handedness and associated hemispheric specializations contribute to spatial attention biases that can be detected in behavioral tasks, including those embedded within language processing contexts. On the other hand, our primary hypothesis—that self-reported visual vividness would predict stronger perceptual stimulation following image-eliciting sentences—was not supported. VVIQ scores did not predict response times or accuracy on the perceptual task, and they did not interact with handedness to influence lateralized attention or perceptual performance. Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence in favor of the null regarding the hypothesized vividness effects.

Interpreting these results requires balancing several considerations. First, the presence of a handedness effect suggests that our paradigm had sufficient sensitivity to detect at least some individual difference effects related to attentional orientation and lateralization. The laterality effect, albeit small in magnitude, was robust across multiple analytic approaches and was evident in both continuous and categorical representations of handedness. The replication of this effect provides some assurance that the experimental setup—timing, visual target parameters, and the embedding of targets after sentence presentation—was capable of capturing attentional asymmetries.

Second, the absence of VVIQ effects suggests that subjective vividness, as operationalized in the VVIQ, may not translate directly into measurable enhancements of perceptual sensitivity or speed in the context of our language-perception task. There are multiple plausible reasons for this dissociation. One possibility is that the VVIQ primarily taps an introspective dimension of imagery that reflects conscious phenomenology rather than the automatic, rapid recruitment of visual-perceptual mechanisms during online language comprehension. Participants may report experiencing vivid imagery when reflecting on imagery tasks in the abstract, but those self-reports may not predict the degree to which sensory cortices are engaged at the rapid timescales relevant for the perceptual discrimination trials used here.

Another possibility is that while vivid imagers may indeed form richer internal visual representations, such representations could be formed after initial lexical-semantic processing and may not temporally overlap with the brief perceptual probe used in our task. If image formation tends to be slower or is triggered only under certain task demands (e.g., explicit imagery instructions), the brief delay and rapid visual probe in the current paradigm might have been insufficient to capture imagery-driven effects. Supporting this idea, our exploratory finding that VVIQ correlated modestly with trial-level self-reports of imagery occurrence suggests that the questionnaire relates to subjective experience but that the timing and task demands are crucial determinants of whether such experience translates into measurable perceptual facilitation.

A third consideration is measurement fidelity. Self-report questionnaires like the VVIQ are susceptible to individual differences in response style, introspective ability, and interpretation of scale items. These sources of measurement error can attenuate the observed relations between vividness scores and behavioral outcomes. Moreover, range restriction or sample composition could have reduced variability in vividness in our sample relative to the general population, thereby limiting the capacity to detect effects. Although our VVIQ distribution did not show extreme restriction, the majority of participants were university students, a demographic that may be more homogeneous on some cognitive traits.

It is also possible that the influence of imagery on perceptual measures is more domain-specific or context-dependent than assumed. For example, imagery that is object-based or color-focused might affect visual discrimination tasks that probe those exact features, whereas our target detection task was spatial and non-specific in feature content. If imagery aids perception more substantially when the probe shares representational content with the imagined scene (e.g., a color patch following an image sentence about a brightly colored object), then a general target detection probe might fail to capture that congruency-based facilitation. Future work could test more targeted congruency manipulations to assess feature-specific imagery effects.

The lack of an interaction between handedness and vividness further sharpens the interpretation. If handedness-associated attentional biases were to modulate the influence of imagery on perception, we might expect that vivid visualizers who are left-handed would differ from vivid visualizers who are right-handed in how imagery biases attention toward one hemifield or the other. The absence of such an interaction suggests that the attentional asymmetries indexed by handedness operate independently of the self-reported vividness trait measured here. This result does not rule out more subtle interactions that might be revealed with larger samples or with objective measures of imagery strength, but it does indicate that the simple combination of VVIQ and handedness is unlikely to account for major individual differences in perceptual sensitivity during language processing.

Methodological limitations in the present study warrant careful consideration and temper the conclusions that can be drawn. First, the sample, although larger than many prior studies and sufficient to detect small-to-moderate effects, was not pre-selected for extremes of imagery ability. Targeted recruitment of very high and very low imagers could provide greater power to detect vividness-related differences. Second, the VVIQ, while convenient, is a subjective instrument; combining self-report with objective imagery tasks (e.g., binocular rivalry priming, imagery-based detection thresholds, or neurophysiological markers) would provide a more comprehensive assessment of imagery ability. Third, the perceptual probe used here was spatial and relatively abstract; future experiments could employ feature-specific probes that more closely match the dominant attributes of the image-eliciting sentences (color, shape, motion) to maximize the likelihood of detecting imagery-driven facilitation.

Another limitation concerns the timing parameters. The interval between sentence offset and target onset was fixed at 500 ms, which assumes that imagery would be available by that time. However, individual differences in reading speed and imagery initiation could mean that some participants had not yet formed stable images at target onset. Incorporating on-line measures of image onset or varying the stimulus onset asynchrony systematically could help delineate the temporal dynamics of imagery’s influence on perception.

Sample composition issues are also salient given the gender imbalance and the predominance of right-handed participants in our sample. Although handedness effects were observed despite this imbalance, future research should aim for more balanced representation across handedness categories and across other demographic variables that might relate to imagery or attentional biases. Pre-screening for handedness and vividness during recruitment could facilitate stratified sampling and more powerful tests of interactions.

Beyond methodological caveats, these findings have theoretical implications. The dissociation between subjective imagery vividness and perceptual performance suggests that models positing a straightforward mapping between imagery strength and embodied perceptual engagement during language comprehension may be overly simplistic. Instead, imagery may function as one among several routes by which language can elicit sensory activations, and its behavioral consequences may depend on task demands, stimulus features, temporal parameters, and individual cognitive strategies. The observed independence of handedness effects points to a relatively stable influence of lateralized attentional systems on perceptual processing that does not simply hinge on subjective vividness.

The study also carries practical implications for researchers interested in individual differences and replication. Our partial replication of a handedness-related attention effect underscores the value of attempting to reproduce key findings with careful methodological alignment. At the same time, the null vividness results caution against uncritical reliance on single self-report measures as proxies for perceptual engagement. When investigating the role of imagery in cognitive tasks, researchers should complement questionnaires with behavioral or physiological indices and should consider pre-registering targeted hypotheses about timing, feature congruency, and sample composition.

Future research directions are numerous. One fruitful avenue involves integrating objective measures of imagery, such as perceptual sensitivity during imagery tasks or neuroimaging markers of visual cortex engagement, with language comprehension paradigms that manipulate imagery-eliciting content. Such multimodal approaches would help to link subjective vividness, neural activation, and behavioral indices. Another direction is to manipulate congruency between imagined content and perceptual probes to determine whether imagery effects are feature-specific. Additionally, longitudinal or training studies could assess whether imagery vividness is malleable with practice and whether such changes translate into altered perceptual processing during language comprehension. Finally, exploring developmental and clinical populations with atypical imagery or lateralization profiles could illuminate boundary conditions for the role of imagery in perception.

In conclusion, this replication and extension study yielded a nuanced pattern of results. Handedness-related lateralized attention effects were observed in a language-perception context, partially replicating prior work and supporting the role of laterality in shaping attention. However, subjective visual imagery vividness, as measured by a standard self-report questionnaire, did not predict perceptual stimulation or interact with handedness in our paradigm. These findings suggest that subjective vividness may not be a reliable predictor of rapid perceptual facilitation during language comprehension, at least under the task conditions tested. Researchers should be cautious when using questionnaires alone to index imagery and should pay close attention to participant selection, measurement tools, and task design in studies probing the interface of imagery, attention, and language.
