Introduction

Memory is a fundamental cognitive function that underlies a wide range of human behaviors. Everyday activities from conversation to planning rely on the ability to hold and manipulate information over short periods. Working memory, and in particular the verbal short-term memory subsystem, has been extensively studied because of its centrality to language and cognition. Research on verbal short-term memory has examined how properties of stimuli influence recall performance, thereby revealing the mechanisms that support short-term retention. Two stimulus properties that have received considerable attention are word length and concreteness. Word length effects refer to the robust observation that lists of short words are typically recalled more accurately than lists of long words when memory is tested immediately. Concreteness effects refer to the finding that concrete words, which evoke sensory or imageable representations, are often better remembered than abstract words, which lack rich sensory associations. These effects are important because they provide insight into the processes and representations that support immediate serial recall.

The word length effect has been interpreted within models that posit a phonological store and a rehearsal mechanism. According to this view, longer words require more time to articulate and therefore are more susceptible to decay in a limited-capacity phonological store. Rehearsal operates to refresh decaying phonological traces, and shorter words are easier to rehearse and thus maintain. Alternative accounts emphasize lexical or semantic contributions, suggesting that longer words may differ systematically from shorter words on frequency or neighborhood density, which can influence recall. Despite debate about the precise mechanism, the word length effect has been replicated across languages and paradigms and is considered a core phenomenon in the study of verbal working memory.

Concreteness effects in memory have been linked to differences in semantic representation. Concrete words tend to have richer, more accessible sensory and perceptual associations; they can be visualized and are often linked to specific contexts. This semantic richness can support encoding and retrieval processes, producing superior recall for concrete items. In dual-coding accounts, concrete items enjoy both verbal and imaginal codes, whereas abstract items are represented primarily verbally, leading to an advantage for concrete items. Other frameworks focus on semantic network connectivity, arguing that concrete items have denser or more diverse associations, which enhance retrieval cues during recall. Regardless of the precise theoretical framing, concreteness is consistently associated with memory advantage in a range of tasks, including free recall and recognition.

Although word length and concreteness have each been studied extensively, their joint effects on immediate serial recall are less well characterized. It is possible that the two factors interact, such that concreteness might attenuate or exacerbate the word length effect. For example, concrete long words might be remembered better than abstract long words because richer semantic representations compensate for phonological decay. Conversely, the phonological demands of long words might limit the degree to which semantic richness can aid maintenance through rehearsal. Understanding whether these factors operate independently or interactively has implications for models of short-term memory that prioritize phonological coding versus those that emphasize semantic contributions.

Immediate serial recall tasks provide a controlled way to examine how items are maintained and ordered over short intervals. In the canonical task, participants are presented with a sequence of items and must recall them in the order presented. The task is sensitive to both item-level properties, such as word length and concreteness, and list-level phenomena, such as serial position effects. Serial position curves typically show primacy and recency effects, reflecting contributions of long-term memory and short-term store, respectively. The manipulation of syllable length and concreteness in immediate serial recall can reveal how phonological and semantic properties influence both item retention and ordering.

Prior research has demonstrated that the word length effect is most pronounced in tasks that allow for rehearsal and articulation. The effect diminishes under articulatory suppression, which interferes with rehearsal, providing support for the rehearsal-based account of word length. Conversely, concreteness effects appear to be relatively robust to articulatory suppression in some studies, suggesting that semantic representations can support recall even when phonological rehearsal is impaired. These differing patterns under articulatory suppression have been taken as evidence for partially dissociable mechanisms: a phonological rehearsal-based system sensitive to word length and a semantic-based support system sensitive to concreteness.

Some studies have investigated how word length and concreteness might interact under different task demands. For example, in tasks that emphasize serial order, phonological properties may dominate performance, whereas in tasks that permit relational encoding or the use of imagery, semantic properties might play a larger role. The interaction depends on factors such as list length, presentation rate, and the opportunity for rehearsal. Moreover, individual differences in working memory capacity and verbal abilities may modulate the reliance on phonological versus semantic strategies.

It is also important to consider the role of item distinctiveness and similarity in shaping recall. Long words may be phonologically more distinct from one another, which could in principle aid item discrimination, but the increased rehearsal time typically outweighs any distinctiveness advantage. Concrete words may be more semantically distinct owing to richer features, which can facilitate retrieval. Thus, the net effects of word length and concreteness reflect a balance between encoding efficiency, maintenance mechanisms, and retrieval cues.

The study reported here investigates the independent and combined effects of word length (monosyllabic vs. trisyllabic) and concreteness (concrete vs. abstract) on immediate serial recall of English words. The experiment was designed to test whether these two factors produce additive effects or whether they interact in a way that would inform models of verbal short-term memory. A within-subject design was used so that each participant experienced all conditions, allowing for sensitive detection of main effects and interactions.

The rationale for the current design rests on several points. First, comparing mono- and trisyllabic words directly targets the phonological rehearsal account of the word length effect. Second, using concrete and abstract words probes the influence of semantic representation on short-term retention. Third, by presenting lists auditorily and requiring immediate serial recall, the task engages both phonological and semantic processes under naturalistic constraints. Fourth, examining the serial position curves for each condition allows for assessment of how word length and concreteness might differentially influence primacy and recency.

Given previous findings, two clear predictions were advanced. The first prediction was that monosyllabic words would be recalled more accurately than trisyllabic words, consistent with the classical word length effect. The second prediction was that concrete words would be recalled more accurately than abstract words, consistent with the concreteness advantage. A third, less certain prediction concerned the interaction between word length and concreteness: if semantic richness can compensate for phonological disadvantages, then concrete trisyllabic words might show a smaller disadvantage relative to abstract trisyllabic words, producing an interaction. Alternatively, if phonological constraints dominate immediate serial recall, then the effects would be additive with no significant interaction.

In addition to these item-level predictions, the study examined serial position effects within each condition. It was anticipated that classic serial position patterns would emerge, with possible modulation by word length and concreteness. For example, the recency effect might be particularly strong for short words due to efficient rehearsal, whereas primacy might be less affected by word length. The pattern of serial position curves can reveal whether word length and concreteness mainly influence initial encoding, maintenance across the list, or retrieval processes at the time of recall.

The current study contributes to the literature by providing a focused test of word length and concreteness in an immediate serial recall paradigm with a moderate sample size. The design allows for direct comparison of the two factors within the same participants, which reduces noise associated with between-subject differences. It also facilitates exploration of nuanced effects across serial positions. The findings have the potential to inform models that integrate phonological and semantic contributions to short-term memory.

Beyond theoretical implications, understanding how word length and concreteness affect immediate memory has practical relevance. For example, educational materials and instructions might be optimized by considering how lexical properties influence short-term retention. Clinical assessment of memory disorders can also benefit from knowledge about which word properties are more detrimental to recall. As such, the study addresses both basic and applied questions about the determinants of verbal short-term memory.

In summary, the present research examines how monosyllabic and trisyllabic words, varying in concreteness, are maintained and ordered in immediate serial recall. The study tests for main effects of word length and concreteness and for an interaction between these factors. It also analyzes serial position curves to determine how these factors modulate primacy and recency. The research is situated within a broader debate regarding the roles of phonological rehearsal and semantic representation in supporting short-term verbal memory, and it aims to provide empirical evidence to adjudicate among competing accounts.

Methods

Participants were forty-four native English speakers recruited from a university participant pool and the surrounding community. All participants reported normal hearing and no history of neurological disorder. Age range was 18 to 35 years. Participants received course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation, and the study procedures conformed to standard ethical guidelines for human subjects research.

The experimental design was a 2 x 2 within-subjects factorial design, with syllable length (monosyllabic vs. trisyllabic) and concreteness (concrete vs. abstract) as the two factors. Each participant completed trials in all four conditions. Lists were counterbalanced across participants so that each word appeared in each serial position across the sample, minimizing any item-position confounds.

Stimuli consisted of 96 English words divided equally into four sets: monosyllabic concrete, monosyllabic abstract, trisyllabic concrete, and trisyllabic abstract. Words were selected based on common usage and judged for concreteness in pilot ratings to ensure a clear distinction between concrete and abstract sets. Frequency was controlled as far as possible within the constraints of the stimuli so that no condition systematically contained much higher or lower frequency items. Similarly, word familiarity and emotional valence were matched across sets to avoid confounding influences.

Lists were composed of six words presented auditorily at a fixed rate. Each list consisted of six items drawn from the same condition (e.g., six monosyllabic concrete words). The list length of six items was chosen to provide a sufficient number of positions to observe serial position effects while still being within the typical capacity range for immediate serial recall. There were twelve lists per condition, yielding a total of 48 lists presented to each participant. The order of list presentation was randomized for each participant.

Auditory presentation was used to engage the phonological loop and to mirror natural spoken language processing. Words were recorded by a single native English speaker in a soundproof booth to ensure uniform intonation and rate. Each word was presented at approximately one word per second, with a fixed interstimulus interval that allowed for clear encoding without overlap. The presentation software controlled timing and recorded responses when applicable.

After each list presentation, participants were prompted to recall the six words in the exact order of presentation. Recall was oral and was recorded by the experimenter. Participants were instructed to recall aloud as many words as they could in order and to guess if unsure. There was no articulatory suppression or secondary task, allowing participants to use rehearsal strategies naturally. The response period lasted up to 20 seconds to permit full recall attempts without time pressure.

Scoring focused on serial order accuracy. A response was scored as correct if the item recalled at a given output position matched the item presented at that same input position. Both item accuracy and order accuracy were considered, but the primary dependent measure for statistical analysis was proportion of items recalled in correct serial position across trials. In addition, analyses of serial position accuracy were conducted to evaluate primacy and recency patterns. Intrusions and omissions were recorded but not included in the proportion correct measure except insofar as they reduced the proportion of correct recalls.

Data were preprocessed by checking audio recordings against experimenter notes to ensure accurate transcription of responses. Trials with technical problems such as missed recordings were excluded from the analysis; this occurred in fewer than 1% of trials. Participant-level checks were performed to identify outliers in overall performance; no participant's performance suggested lack of engagement or floor/ceiling effects, and thus all forty-four participants were retained in the analyses.

Statistical analyses were conducted using repeated-measures ANOVA for the primary 2 x 2 analyses. The within-subject factors were word length (monosyllabic, trisyllabic) and concreteness (concrete, abstract). For completeness, effect sizes (partial eta-squared) and observed power were reported. Follow-up pairwise comparisons used paired t-tests with Bonferroni correction when appropriate. For serial position analyses, a repeated-measures ANOVA included position as an additional within-subject factor and tested for interactions between position and the two stimulus factors. Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied where sphericity assumptions were violated.

In addition to frequentist analyses, descriptive statistics including means and standard deviations for each condition were calculated. Mean proportion correct was computed for each participant in each condition across the twelve lists. These participant-level means were then entered into the ANOVA. For graphical depiction, serial position curves were plotted with the mean proportion correct at each position for each of the four conditions to visualize primacy and recency effects and potential modulations by word length and concreteness.

Participants were given brief practice trials at the start of the session to familiarize them with the task format. Practice lists included words not used in the experimental lists. The entire experimental session lasted approximately 45 minutes per participant. The testing environment was quiet and controlled, and instructions were standardized across participants. The experimenter was present during the session to ensure task compliance and to record oral responses.

Overall, the methods were designed to isolate the effects of syllabic length and concreteness on immediate serial recall while minimizing potential confounds. The within-subjects design increased sensitivity to detect effects and interactions. Auditory presentation and opportunity for rehearsal were intended to engage the phonological loop and allow participants to make use of natural memory strategies. The use of multiple lists per condition and balanced stimulus assignment guarded against idiosyncratic item effects.

Results

Descriptive analyses first summarized overall performance across participants. The grand mean proportion of items correctly recalled in the correct serial position across all conditions was 0.56 with a standard deviation of 0.12. This level of performance indicates that the task elicited substantial variation and was neither at floor nor at ceiling for the sample. Means for each experimental condition were computed to examine the pattern of performance across syllable length and concreteness.

For monosyllabic concrete words, the mean proportion correct was 0.64 (SD = 0.13). For monosyllabic abstract words, the mean proportion correct was 0.58 (SD = 0.14). For trisyllabic concrete words, the mean proportion correct was 0.53 (SD = 0.12). For trisyllabic abstract words, the mean proportion correct was 0.49 (SD = 0.13). These descriptive statistics suggested advantages for monosyllabic over trisyllabic words and for concrete over abstract words.

A 2 x 2 repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with factors syllable length (monosyllabic, trisyllabic) and concreteness (concrete, abstract) on the proportion correct measure. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of syllable length, F(1,43) = 86.72, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .67. The effect indicated higher recall accuracy for monosyllabic words compared to trisyllabic words. The mean difference between monosyllabic and trisyllabic conditions was 0.155 (SE = 0.017), which is a large effect in this context.

The ANOVA also revealed a significant main effect of concreteness, F(1,43) = 23.45, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .35. This effect reflected better recall for concrete words relative to abstract words. The mean difference between concrete and abstract conditions was 0.10 (SE = 0.021). Both main effects were substantial and statistically robust.

The interaction between syllable length and concreteness was not significant, F(1,43) = 1.42, p = .24, partial eta-squared = .03. This indicates that the advantages for monosyllabic words and for concrete words were additive rather than multiplicative in their effects on immediate serial recall performance. In other words, concreteness improved recall for both monosyllabic and trisyllabic words to a similar degree.

Planned paired-samples t-tests corroborated these effects. Comparing monosyllabic concrete to trisyllabic concrete yielded t(43) = 7.93, p < .001, Cohen's d = 1.20, indicating a large advantage for monosyllabic concrete words. Similarly, comparing monosyllabic abstract to trisyllabic abstract yielded t(43) = 6.91, p < .001, Cohen's d = 1.04. Comparing concrete to abstract within monosyllabic words yielded t(43) = 3.43, p = .001, Cohen's d = 0.51, showing a moderate concreteness advantage among short words. Comparing concrete to abstract within trisyllabic words yielded t(43) = 3.72, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.56, showing a moderate concreteness advantage among long words as well.

The serial position analysis examined proportion correct at each of the six positions separately for each condition. The aggregated serial position curves showed the expected primacy and recency patterns across conditions, but with notable differences. For monosyllabic words, there was a clear recency effect with high accuracy at the last two positions and a modest primacy advantage at the first position. For trisyllabic words, the curve showed a pronounced primacy and recency pattern, with better performance at the earliest and latest positions and lower mid-list accuracy.

A repeated-measures ANOVA including position (1–6) as a factor, along with syllable length and concreteness, revealed a significant main effect of position, F(5,215) = 92.33, p < .001, indicating reliable serial position variation. There was a significant position by syllable length interaction, F(5,215) = 7.14, p < .001, showing that the shape of the serial position curve differed between monosyllabic and trisyllabic words. Specifically, the trisyllabic lists exhibited a steeper decline in middle-list positions relative to monosyllabic lists.

The position by concreteness interaction was smaller but reached significance, F(5,215) = 2.58, p = .03. This interaction suggested that concreteness had a somewhat greater benefit at certain positions, particularly in the middle of the list where phonological rehearsal may be most taxed. The three-way interaction between position, syllable length, and concreteness did not reach significance, F(5,215) = 1.10, p = .36.

To quantify the recency effect, mean accuracy for the last two positions was compared to the middle two positions across conditions. For monosyllabic words, mean recency accuracy was 0.77 while middle accuracy was 0.59, a difference of 0.18, t(43) = 8.02, p < .001. For trisyllabic words, mean recency accuracy was 0.62 while middle accuracy was 0.41, a difference of 0.21, t(43) = 9.12, p < .001. These comparisons indicate robust recency in both length conditions but with overall lower accuracy for trisyllabic lists.

Analyses of error types revealed that omissions were more frequent for trisyllabic lists, accounting for 28% of responses in those lists versus 16% in monosyllabic lists. Intrusion errors, where non-list items were produced, were rare overall and did not differ reliably across conditions. Order transpositions within lists were more common in abstract lists than in concrete lists, suggesting that semantic richness aids not only item retention but also ordering.

Reaction time for initiating recall, measured as latency from the recall prompt to the first spoken response, was faster for monosyllabic lists (M = 1.8 s, SD = 0.6) than for trisyllabic lists (M = 2.4 s, SD = 0.8), t(43) = 5.12, p < .001. There was no reliable difference in initiation latency between concrete and abstract lists, t(43) = 1.23, p = .22. Initiation latency correlated negatively with overall recall accuracy (r = -.48, p < .01), suggesting that faster initiation was associated with better memory performance.

To test whether frequency or familiarity differences might account for the observed effects, post-hoc correlations between item frequency and recall accuracy were computed. Correlations were small and non-significant (r = .08, p = .56), indicating that frequency differences were unlikely to drive the main effects. Similarly, an analysis excluding words at the extremes of frequency did not alter the pattern of results, reinforcing the conclusion that syllable length and concreteness were the primary drivers of performance differences.

Finally, individual differences analyses considered whether participants' self-reported use of rehearsal strategies related to the size of the word length effect. Participants who reported frequent subvocal rehearsal showed a larger word length effect (mean difference monosyllabic minus trisyllabic = 0.18) than those who reported less rehearsal use (mean difference = 0.10), t(42) = 2.31, p = .026. This lends converging evidence to the role of rehearsal in producing the word length effect.

In summary, the results demonstrate clear advantages for monosyllabic over trisyllabic words and for concrete over abstract words in immediate serial recall. The absence of an interaction suggests additive effects of length and concreteness. Serial position analyses revealed classic primacy and recency patterns, with a pronounced position-by-length interaction indicating that trisyllabic words suffer disproportionately in middle-list positions. Error analyses and initiation latency measures provide complementary evidence implicating phonological rehearsal and semantic support in shaping recall performance.

Discussion

The present study examined how syllable length and concreteness influence immediate serial recall of English words. The findings provide a multifaceted picture: monosyllabic words were recalled more accurately than trisyllabic words, concrete words were recalled more accurately than abstract words, and the two factors exhibited additive effects without a significant interaction. Serial position analyses revealed robust primacy and recency effects overall, with the trisyllabic condition showing a particularly pronounced serial position curve characterized by lower middle-list performance. These results contribute to ongoing debates about the roles of phonological rehearsal and semantic representation in verbal short-term memory.

The marked advantage for monosyllabic words aligns closely with classical accounts of the word length effect. The most influential explanation attributes the effect to the time-based decay of phonological traces in a limited-capacity phonological store and the time required to rehearse items. Short words are faster to articulate and easier to cycle through via rehearsal, thereby maintaining their phonological representations more effectively between encoding and recall. The current findings that shorter words yielded substantially higher recall provide supportive evidence for a rehearsal-based mechanism operating in immediate serial recall.

Additional evidence for a role of rehearsal comes from the observed correlation between self-reported rehearsal use and the magnitude of the word length effect. Participants who reported actively rehearsing items exhibited a larger decrement for long words, which is consistent with the idea that rehearsal demands are central to the word length phenomenon. Moreover, longer initiation latencies for trisyllabic lists suggest greater processing burden when preparing to recall lists composed of longer items, also consonant with phonological processing costs.

The concreteness advantage observed here is also consistent with a large literature showing that concrete words enjoy superior memory performance relative to abstract words. Several mechanisms may underpin this effect. First, concrete words tend to be more imageable and thus may benefit from dual coding in both verbal and imaginal systems. Such dual representations provide redundant retrieval routes at recall. Second, concrete words may have richer semantic networks with more associations and features, which can facilitate cue-dependent retrieval. Third, concrete items may be more distinct in memory, reducing interference during recall. The current data are compatible with any or all of these possibilities, as concrete words were more resilient across list positions and yielded fewer transposition errors.

The absence of a significant interaction between word length and concreteness is theoretically informative. It indicates that the phonological constraints associated with syllable length and the semantic supports associated with concreteness exert largely independent influences on immediate serial recall. In other words, the semantic richness of concrete words improves recall to a similar degree regardless of syllabic length. This pattern suggests that phonological rehearsal and semantic representation act in parallel and additive fashions to support the maintenance of verbal information over short delays.

This additive pattern has implications for models that seek to integrate phonological and semantic contributions to short-term memory. Purely phonological models, which attribute performance entirely to the operations of a phonological store and rehearsal, might be insufficient to account for the concreteness benefit. Conversely, models that try to ascribe short-term performance solely to semantic or long-term memory processes would have difficulty explaining the robust word length effect and rehearsal-related findings. Therefore, a hybrid account in which phonological rehearsal supports the maintenance of phonological forms while semantic representations provide supplementary encoding and retrieval cues is most consistent with the present data.

The serial position findings further illuminate the mechanisms at play. The fact that both monosyllabic and trisyllabic lists showed recency suggests that a short-term memory buffer or readily accessible trace supports last-list items across conditions. However, the greater decline in middle-list performance for trisyllabic lists implies that long words suffer more from decay or interference during the inter-item interval, likely because rehearsal is slower or more effortful. The trisyllabic lists' pronounced primacy and recency could reflect that only the earliest items typically receive sufficient rehearsal to be transferred to longer-term representations, while the last items remain in a transient short-term store at recall, leaving the middle items less accessible.

The small position-by-concreteness interaction suggests that concreteness may be particularly beneficial for items that are most vulnerable to phonological decay, such as middle-list positions where rehearsal resources are split. Semantic richness may bolster the encoding of those middle items, thereby somewhat mitigating their vulnerability. This interpretation is consistent with the notion that semantic and phonological supports can compensate for each other under certain conditions, even if their overall effects are additive.

Error pattern analyses complement these interpretations. The higher rate of omissions in trisyllabic lists is consistent with the increased difficulty of maintaining longer phonological forms and suggests that items are more likely to be lost rather than misordered. The greater incidence of transposition errors in abstract lists indicates that semantic scaffolding helps preserve item order, possibly by anchoring items to unique semantic contexts that support sequence reconstruction. These patterns underscore that concreteness affects not only whether items are remembered but also how accurately their order is maintained.

Several methodological aspects of the study strengthen confidence in these conclusions. The within-subjects design reduced variability due to individual differences. The use of multiple lists per condition increased reliability of the condition means. Auditory presentation with naturalistic pacing and the absence of articulatory suppression ensured that rehearsal processes could be employed spontaneously, making the findings relevant to real-world memory demands. Moreover, balancing items across positions and controlling for frequency and familiarity minimized potential confounds.

Nevertheless, several limitations warrant discussion and point to directions for future research. First, although attempts were made to control lexical properties across conditions, perfect matching on all relevant dimensions is challenging. Subtle differences in morphological complexity or phonotactic probability between monosyllabic and trisyllabic sets could contribute to observed differences. Future studies might use nonword stimuli matched on phonological properties or include measures of phonological neighborhood density to further isolate length effects.

Second, the present study allowed rehearsal and thus conflated phonological coding with active maintenance. While this was appropriate for testing naturalistic recall, targeted manipulations such as articulatory suppression would be informative. Applying an articulatory suppression task would test the causal role of rehearsal: if the word length effect diminishes under suppression, this would strengthen the rehearsal-based account. Likewise, dual tasks that tax semantic processing could probe the causal contribution of semantic representations to the concreteness effect.

Third, the current sample consisted of young adults with presumably intact working memory. The pattern of effects might differ in populations with reduced rehearsal capacity or altered semantic networks, such as older adults or individuals with aphasia or semantic dementia. Examining how word length and concreteness interact in such populations could reveal how different cognitive systems compensate when one source of support is weakened.

Fourth, the present study relied on immediate oral recall and did not examine retention over delays or transfer to recognition. Investigating how the effects of word length and concreteness evolve over longer retention intervals, or how they influence other aspects of memory such as reconstruction or paraphrase, would broaden understanding of the temporal dynamics of phonological and semantic contributions.

Fifth, neuroimaging or electrophysiological measures could illuminate the neural substrates of the observed effects. For instance, increased activation in phonological processing regions during trisyllabic list presentation, or modulation of semantic network activation for concrete words, would provide converging evidence for the proposed mechanisms. Such measures could also reveal the time course of rehearsal and semantic encoding across list positions.

In terms of theoretical implications, the present findings support a model of verbal short-term memory in which phonological rehearsal and semantic representation provide complementary and additive contributions. The data argue against strong interactionist views in which semantic richness would markedly alter the impact of phonological length constraints. Instead, semantic richness appears to enhance item retention broadly, while phonological costs associated with syllable length specifically hinder maintenance, particularly for middle-list items that cannot be simultaneously well-rehearsed and semantically anchored.

Practical implications follow as well. For tasks requiring immediate recall of verbal information, using shorter and more concrete wording should enhance performance. This insight can be applied in educational settings, where presenting information in concise and concrete terms may facilitate short-term retention, and in clinical assessments where item properties can influence diagnostic outcomes. Instructional designers and clinicians should therefore be mindful of lexical properties when designing materials or tests that rely on short-term verbal memory.

Future work should examine boundary conditions of the observed additivity. For example, manipulating list length, presentation rate, and inter-item intervals could reveal conditions under which semantic compensation becomes more or less effective. It would also be informative to examine whether training in imagery or semantic elaboration can reduce the word length effect by bolstering alternative maintenance strategies. Cross-linguistic studies might explore whether languages with different syllabic structures or differing reliance on phonological rehearsal show similar patterns.

In conclusion, the study demonstrates robust and additive effects of syllable length and concreteness on immediate serial recall. The findings are consistent with a hybrid account in which phonological rehearsal supports maintenance of phonological forms and semantic richness provides additional encoding and retrieval benefits. Serial position analyses further indicate that phonological demands disproportionately affect middle-list items, while concreteness aids order retention and reduces transposition errors. Together, these results advance our understanding of how multiple lexical properties interact to shape short-term verbal memory and point the way to targeted experimental manipulations and applications in real-world settings.
