Introduction

Human social behavior is profoundly shaped by interpersonal bonds and the cognitive and emotional frameworks that arise from early caregiving experiences. Attachment theory provides a framework for understanding enduring patterns in how individuals regulate emotion, seek support, and respond to social signals. Attachment representations in adulthood are commonly conceptualized along two continuous dimensions often labeled attachment-related anxiety and attachment-related avoidance. Attachment-related anxiety reflects hyperactivation strategies characterized by heightened worry about rejection, strong needs for reassurance, and increased sensitivity to social evaluation. Attachment-related avoidance reflects deactivation strategies characterized by discomfort with closeness, suppression of attachment needs, and a preference for self-reliance. These dimensions capture important individual differences that relate to how people manage social information and interpersonal demands. Social influence and conformity represent core domains of social behavior where attachment dynamics might reasonably exert influence. Conformity is a multifaceted construct that includes tendencies to align opinions, behaviors, or attitudes with those of a group or perceived social norms. Classic research on conformity has focused on situational determinants such as group unanimity, task difficulty, and the presence of an ally, but individual difference factors also moderate conformity tendencies. Traits related to social anxiety, self-esteem, and the need for approval have been associated with greater conformity in some past research. Given the social and evaluative nature of conformity contexts, attachment-related anxiety in particular might predispose individuals to conform under perceived threat of rejection or to seek acceptance. Attachment-related avoidance, by contrast, could plausibly relate to reduced conformity when conformity requires interpersonal closeness or self-disclosure, although avoidance might also be associated with conformity in contexts where conforming allows individuals to avoid interpersonal conflict. Despite these theoretically plausible pathways, the direct relationship between attachment dimensions and susceptibility to conformity has received limited empirical attention. Much of the work linking attachment to social influence has examined related constructs such as peer pressure, compliance in close relationships, or susceptibility to persuasive appeals rather than straightforward conformity measures. This relative paucity of direct evidence has left a gap in understanding whether and how attachment anxiety and avoidance uniquely predict conformity tendencies. Clarifying these links is important because conformity is a powerful force shaping social behavior and because attachment dimensions are central to models of interpersonal functioning. If attachment-related anxiety predicts higher conformity, this would suggest that those who chronically worry about abandonment and social evaluation may be particularly likely to yield to group norms to preserve social bonds. If attachment-related avoidance predicts higher conformity, this would indicate a more complex behavioral strategy where avoiding interpersonal tension could manifest as outward compliance. Conversely, if avoidance predicts lower conformity, this would be consistent with a deactivating stance that values autonomy and independence from group influence. Prior literature offers mixed and indirect clues that bear on these possibilities. Studies that examine social anxiety, fear of negative evaluation, and related constructs show robust associations with conformity-like behaviors, especially in contexts where evaluation is salient. Those findings dovetail with the hypothesis that attachment anxiety could foster conformity because anxious individuals frequently exhibit heightened sensitivity to negative evaluation. Other lines of research indicate that secure attachment is associated with greater confidence in independent judgment and lower reliance on external validation, which should correspond to lower conformity. Research on gender differences in conformity shows that, depending on method and context, females sometimes show higher conformity than males, although findings are not uniform and often depend on the task, culture, and operationalization of conformity. Given that attachment patterns can interact with gendered socialization — such that expression and consequences of anxiety and avoidance may vary across men and women — it is plausible that the relationship between attachment dimensions and conformity may be moderated by sex. Despite these conceptual links, direct tests of attachment-conformity associations remain surprisingly rare. There is also a methodological diversity in how attachment and conformity are measured, which complicates comparisons across studies and may explain inconsistencies in the literature. For instance, self-report measures of dispositional attachment provide dimensional scores for anxiety and avoidance but may not capture context-specific activation of the attachment system during conformity tasks. Similarly, self-report conformity scales assess a generalized tendency to conform but may not reflect behavior in concrete group settings. It is therefore important to investigate these relationships using reliable self-report measures while acknowledging the scope and limits of such methods. The present investigation was designed to address this gap by examining the direct associations between attachment dimensions (anxiety and avoidance) and a self-report measure of general susceptibility to conformity. The study aimed to determine whether individuals who score higher on attachment-related anxiety or avoidance also report higher levels of conformity. We formulated a directional hypothesis grounded in theoretical expectations and indirect evidence: higher attachment-related insecurity, particularly greater anxiety, would be associated with greater susceptibility to conformity. We also considered the possibility that attachment-related avoidance might be negatively associated with conformity or unrelated depending on how avoidance manifests in social contexts that prompt conformity. Finally, given prior evidence suggesting sex differences in conformity and in the expression of attachment orientations, we planned exploratory analyses to examine whether sex moderated the associations between attachment dimensions and conformity. To test these predictions, we used validated self-report instruments that yield separate scores for attachment-related anxiety and avoidance, as well as a scale that captures general tendency toward conforming. We used correlational analyses, primarily Pearson correlation coefficients, to examine bivariate associations between attachment scores and conformity scores in the full sample and within sex-defined subsamples. We considered potential sample limitations, including uneven sex distribution and the self-report nature of measures, when interpreting the results. We anticipated that the results would provide initial evidence concerning whether an insecure attachment style broadly maps onto a greater tendency to conform, with the expectation that attachment-related anxiety would show the clearest positive association. By focusing on direct measurement of attachment dimensions and conformity, this study sought to contribute to the literature by clarifying a link that has important implications for understanding interpersonal behavior and the role of early relational experiences in shaping later tendencies to acquiesce to group norms. The implications extend to clinical, developmental, and social psychological domains because if attachment anxiety predisposes individuals to conformity, interventions that target anxiety and self-evaluation concerns might reduce maladaptive conformity. Conversely, if attachment avoidance shows an effect, it could challenge assumptions about autonomy-promoting tendencies associated with avoidance. Given these stakes, careful empirical scrutiny of the attachment-conformity relationship is warranted. At the same time, we recognized the limitations of a single correlational study to establish causality or generalize across cultures and contexts. Therefore, the study was framed as an initial, exploratory test of the hypothesized links, with clear calls for replication, experimental manipulation, and cross-cultural investigation in subsequent research. We also considered the possibility that participant dishonesty or response biases could affect self-report data, and we incorporated basic attention checks to mitigate careless responding. In sum, the study set out to test whether attachment-related anxiety and avoidance are associated with self-reported susceptibility to conformity, whether these associations differ by sex, and what preliminary conclusions might be drawn to guide larger and more definitive research programs.

Method

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and community volunteer postings. The target sample size was set to achieve adequate power for medium-sized correlations, but the final sample was partly constrained by resource and time limitations. A total of 120 participants completed the study survey and passed basic data quality checks. Of these participants, 84 identified as female and 36 identified as male. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 54 years, with a mean age of 23.6 years and a standard deviation of 6.4 years. The sampling method resulted in a higher proportion of females than males, reflecting the composition of the recruitment sources. Participants provided informed consent prior to beginning the survey and were informed that their responses would be anonymous and used for research purposes. The study protocol complied with ethical standards for human research and included information about voluntary participation and the right to withdraw at any time. Measures were administered via an online survey platform that allowed for forced-response items to minimize missing data while permitting participants to discontinue the study if they chose. The primary measures included a well-established self-report measure of adult attachment that yields two continuous scales: attachment-related anxiety and attachment-related avoidance. This instrument contained items that assess the degree to which participants worry about rejection, desire closeness, feel comfortable relying on others, or prefer independence from close others. Items were rated on a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), and scale scores were computed as the mean of relevant items after reversing items where appropriate. Higher scores on the anxiety scale indicate greater attachment-related anxiety, and higher scores on the avoidance scale indicate greater attachment-related avoidance. The conformity measure was a self-report scale designed to assess a generalized propensity to conform to others’ opinions, behaviors, or norms. Items asked participants to indicate the extent to which they tend to go along with others, change their views to fit in, or defer to group consensus. Responses were made on a 7-point Likert-type scale identical in format to the attachment measure, and scale scores were calculated as the mean of all items after reverse scoring when needed. Both the attachment and conformity measures have demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in prior work, and internal consistency in the current sample was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. In addition to the main scales, demographic questions included sex, age, educational status, and whether participants were currently in a romantic relationship. Two attention-check items were embedded in the survey to identify participants who were not reading items carefully; those who failed both attention checks were excluded from analyses. Four participants failed both checks and were excluded, leaving the final sample of 120 participants. Prior to conducting primary analyses, descriptive statistics and distributional properties of the scales were inspected. Skewness and kurtosis indices were examined to ensure that Pearson correlations would be appropriate; no severe departures from normality were observed for the key variables. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated for the anxiety, avoidance, and conformity scales to assess internal consistency reliability. The primary analytic approach involved computing Pearson correlation coefficients between the attachment anxiety score and conformity score and between the attachment avoidance score and conformity score for the full sample. Given theoretical interest in potential sex differences, the same correlations were computed separately for females and males. In addition to the bivariate correlations, supplementary analyses included Fisher’s r-to-z transformations to test whether the correlation coefficients differed significantly between females and males. A small set of exploratory multiple regression analyses were conducted to examine whether anxiety and avoidance simultaneously predicted conformity and whether sex moderated these associations via interaction terms. Regression analyses used standardized predictors and included a simple test for multicollinearity and residual distribution. All statistical tests were two-tailed and interpreted at conventional alpha levels, and effect sizes were reported alongside p-values. Analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Data cleaning steps, including screening for missing data, outliers beyond three standard deviations, and attention-check failures, were documented and applied consistently. No participants were removed for extreme response patterns beyond those who failed attention checks. The study design was correlational and cross-sectional, and participants received course credit or a small monetary compensation for participation, depending on their recruitment venue. The order of measures in the survey was held constant for all participants, with the attachment measure administered prior to the conformity scale to reduce potential priming effects from the conformity items. Debriefing information was provided at the end of the survey, explaining the study’s purpose and offering contact information for follow-up questions. The analyses focused on the hypothesized relationship between attachment insecurity and conformity, with particular attention to attachment anxiety as a potential predictor. The methods were chosen to provide a clear and replicable assessment of the bivariate relationships while acknowledging the limits of self-report and the potential benefits of future experimental work to establish causal links.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the main variables were computed for the full sample and by sex. For the full sample of 120 participants, the mean score on the attachment-related anxiety scale was 3.21 with a standard deviation of 1.12. The mean score on the attachment-related avoidance scale was 3.07 with a standard deviation of 1.05. The mean conformity score in the full sample was 3.56 with a standard deviation of 1.08. Cronbach’s alpha in the full sample was .86 for the anxiety scale, .81 for the avoidance scale, and .79 for the conformity scale, indicating acceptable internal consistency for the measures used in the study. Descriptive statistics were also computed separately for females and males. Among females (n = 84), the mean anxiety score was 3.31 (SD = 1.09), the mean avoidance score was 3.10 (SD = 1.03), and the mean conformity score was 3.70 (SD = 1.01). Among males (n = 36), the mean anxiety score was 2.98 (SD = 1.16), the mean avoidance score was 2.97 (SD = 1.10), and the mean conformity score was 3.12 (SD = 1.21). These descriptive data suggested modest differences in mean conformity by sex, with females reporting slightly higher conformity. The primary analyses examined Pearson correlations between attachment dimensions and conformity in the full sample and within sex-defined subsamples. In the full sample (N = 120), the correlation between attachment-related anxiety and conformity was r(118) = .15, p = .10. This correlation, while in the hypothesized positive direction, did not achieve conventional statistical significance in the combined sample. The correlation between attachment-related avoidance and conformity in the full sample was r(118) = .05, p = .59, indicating no meaningful association between avoidance and conformity in the overall sample. When analyses were conducted separately by sex, different patterns emerged. For females (n = 84), the correlation between attachment-related anxiety and conformity was r(82) = .29, p = .006, indicating a statistically significant positive association. This correlation corresponds to a moderate effect size and suggests that higher levels of attachment anxiety were associated with greater self-reported susceptibility to conformity among female participants. For females, the correlation between attachment-related avoidance and conformity was r(82) = .07, p = .52, indicating no significant association between avoidance and conformity in the female subsample. For males (n = 36), the correlation between attachment-related anxiety and conformity was r(34) = .05, p = .76, reflecting a near-zero non-significant association. In the male subsample, the correlation between attachment-related avoidance and conformity was r(34) = -.04, p = .81, also non-significant. To evaluate whether the correlation between anxiety and conformity differed significantly between females and males, Fisher’s r-to-z transformation was applied to the two correlation coefficients. The Fisher r-to-z test comparing female and male r-values for anxiety-conformity yielded z = 1.68, p = .093 (two-tailed), indicating that the difference in correlation magnitudes across sexes approached but did not reach conventional statistical significance. This suggests that while the positive association in females was present and significant, the sex difference in the magnitude of the relationship did not reach the strict threshold for concluding a statistically reliable moderation by sex in this sample. Supplementary multiple regression analyses were conducted to assess the joint contributions of anxiety and avoidance to conformity and to test for potential interactions with sex. In a regression model predicting conformity in the full sample with standardized predictors, attachment-related anxiety was associated with conformity with a standardized beta of .16, p = .08, and attachment-related avoidance had a beta of .03, p = .69. These coefficients mirror the bivariate correlation results in indicating a trend-level positive association of anxiety with conformity and no association for avoidance. When sex and interaction terms were added to the model, the anxiety-by-sex interaction produced a standardized beta of .18, p = .07, which again suggested a trend toward moderation by sex but did not reach p < .05. The avoidance-by-sex interaction was non-significant (beta = -.04, p = .71). Residual diagnostics indicated no severe multicollinearity among predictors (variance inflation factors < 1.5) and no major deviations from homoscedasticity or normality of residuals. Additional exploratory analyses examined whether relationship status or age correlated with conformity or moderated the attachment-conformity association. Relationship status (in a relationship vs. not) was not significantly associated with conformity (t(118) = 1.06, p = .29), nor did age correlate significantly with conformity (r = -.04, p = .63). Including relationship status and age as covariates in regression models did not materially alter the pattern of findings regarding attachment anxiety and conformity. Given the slightly uneven sex distribution, sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate whether the female-only correlation would remain robust if a matched subsample of females were compared to males. A random subset of 36 females was selected to match the male sample size, and the anxiety-conformity correlation in that female subsample was r = .27, p = .11, which due to reduced power became non-significant even though the magnitude remained similar. This sensitivity analysis underscored that sample size differences contributed to the differential statistical power to detect effects in sex-stratified analyses. Taken together, the primary results indicate that attachment-related anxiety was significantly positively correlated with self-reported conformity among females but not among males, and that avoidance did not correlate significantly with conformity in any analysis. The overall pattern supports the specific link between attachment anxiety and conformity in women, while the combined-sample analyses and interaction tests suggest that further research with larger and more balanced samples is needed to confirm sex moderation. The possibility of sampling bias, measurement limitations, or respondent bias remains a concern and is addressed in the Discussion. Overall, effect sizes were moderate in magnitude where significant and consistent with theoretical expectations that anxiety-related concerns about social evaluation would be associated with greater reported conformity in at least a subset of the sample.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between adult attachment dimensions and susceptibility to conformity as assessed by self-report measures. The key finding was that attachment-related anxiety showed a significant positive association with self-reported conformity among female participants, whereas no significant association was found for attachment-related avoidance in either sex. In the full sample, the anxiety-conformity correlation was positive but did not reach conventional statistical significance, which is consistent with the notion that sex-specific effects or sample size limitations influenced the overall pattern. The results provide preliminary empirical support for the hypothesis that attachment insecurity, particularly anxiety, is linked to a greater propensity to conform, at least among women. This pattern aligns with theoretical expectations that individuals high in attachment anxiety are particularly sensitive to social evaluation and rejection and may therefore be more prone to adjust their attitudes or behaviors to align with others as a strategy to secure social acceptance. The observed sex difference, with the anxiety-conformity link emerging in females but not in males, prompts a discussion of potential mechanisms and broader implications. One possible explanation is that socialization processes encourage different behavioral responses to attachment-related concerns in men and women. Women may be socialized to place greater value on relational harmony and interpersonal attunement, making them more likely to translate anxiously motivated concerns about rejection into conforming behaviors. Men who are anxious in attachment may face conflicting social expectations that discourage overt conformity, leading to different behavioral manifestations of anxiety such as internal rumination rather than public behavioral adjustment. Another plausible mechanism is that the measures used in this study capture a form of conformity that is more readily endorsed in self-report by females than males. Social desirability norms may differ by sex in ways that influence reporting of conformity tendencies. Females may be more willing to acknowledge tendencies to align with others, whereas males may underreport such tendencies due to cultural expectations of autonomy. This reporting tendency could contribute to the observed sex-specific correlation even if behavioral conformity in real-world contexts does not differ markedly by sex. The findings are also consistent with a broader literature linking fear of negative evaluation and social anxiety to conformity-like behaviors. Attachment-related anxiety shares key features with social anxiety, including heightened sensitivity to rejection and strong needs for reassurance, which likely create motivational states that favor conformity in order to avoid conflict or exclusion. Thus, the anxiously attached individual's desire for social acceptance provides a plausible motivational route to increased conformity. That avoidance did not predict conformity in this study is noteworthy and suggests that avoidance may not operate in a straightforward manner with respect to group influence. Attachment-related avoidance is characterized by discomfort with closeness and a preference for self-reliance, and it would be reasonable to predict that avoidant individuals might resist conformity to preserve autonomy. Conversely, avoidance could sometimes manifest as outward compliance to avoid confrontation while maintaining emotional distance internally. The null findings for avoidance suggest that, at least in terms of self-reported general conformity, avoidance does not exert a consistent directional influence. It is possible that avoidance effects are context-dependent and would be revealed only in behavioral tasks that manipulate relational closeness or threat to autonomy. The methodological choices in the present study warrant careful consideration in interpreting the results. The use of validated self-report measures allowed for efficient assessment of dispositional attachment and generalized conformity tendencies, but such measures capture perceptions and self-conceptions rather than concrete behavior in social contexts. Self-report bias, including social desirability and differential willingness to endorse certain items by sex, could have influenced the observed pattern of associations. Additionally, the cross-sectional correlational design precludes causal inferences; while attachment anxiety may predispose individuals to conform, it is also conceivable that habitual conformity could reinforce anxious relational expectations over time. Longitudinal and experimental designs would be needed to clarify causal directionality. Sample characteristics also limited the generalizability of the findings. The sample was drawn primarily from a university setting with a higher proportion of female participants, which both constrains the external validity and reduces the reliability of sex-based comparisons due to unequal cell sizes. The sensitivity analysis in which a subsample of females was matched to the male sample size showed that the female correlation magnitude remained similar but lost statistical significance, underscoring the role of statistical power. Future studies should prioritize recruitment strategies that yield balanced sex representation and broader demographic diversity to enable more definitive tests of moderation by sex and to explore potential cultural and age-related moderators. Another limitation pertains to the operationalization of conformity. A generalized self-report conformity scale captures an overall tendency but may not reflect how individuals respond to specific social pressures, normative ambiguities, or public versus private decision contexts. Experimental paradigms such as social conformity tasks, public opinion adjustment scenarios, or behavioral measures in group settings could complement self-report data and reveal more about the mechanisms by which attachment influences behavior. For example, tasks that manipulate the perceived cost of dissent, the importance of group acceptance, or the presence of an ingroup ally could illuminate whether attachment anxiety increases conformity primarily through avoidance of rejection or through other cognitive processes such as heightened attention to social cues. The sex-specific pattern also suggests interesting avenues for future research on socialization and gender norms. Investigations that measure or manipulate gender role endorsement, perceived norms about autonomy versus relationality, and situational cues that trigger gendered expectations could help disentangle whether sex moderates the attachment-conformity link due to socialized behavioral scripts or due to underlying psychological differences. Cross-cultural studies would be especially informative because cultural contexts vary markedly in expectations for conformity and gendered social roles. Cultures that emphasize collectivism and relational harmony might show stronger attachment-conformity associations overall, whereas more individualistic cultures might show weaker or differently patterned associations. The present findings have implications for applied contexts as well. If attachment-related anxiety predisposes individuals, particularly women, to greater conformity, there may be consequences for decision-making in organizational, educational, and clinical settings. For instance, anxiously attached individuals may be more likely to acquiesce in group decision-making even when dissent could lead to better outcomes, which has ramifications for group performance and for interventions aimed at fostering constructive dissent. Clinically, awareness that anxious attachment can lead to conformity may inform therapeutic targets such as enhancing assertiveness, building tolerance for rejection, and strengthening self-worth independent of external validation. Designing interventions that address the underlying anxiety rather than surface-level compliant behaviors might reduce maladaptive conformity and support more autonomous decision-making. The current study also raises methodological recommendations for future research. Researchers should use multi-method approaches that combine self-report, behavioral tasks, and physiological measures of stress or arousal during conformity situations to provide converging evidence. Experimental manipulations that temporarily activate the attachment system, such as priming thoughts of rejection or security, could test causal hypotheses about the role of attachment activation in conformity. Longitudinal designs could clarify developmental pathways by examining whether early attachment histories predict later conformity tendencies and whether conformity behaviors in turn shape relational expectations over time. Researchers should also attend to measurement precision by including context-specific conformity scales (e.g., conformity in intimate relationships versus conformity in public group settings) to capture potential domain-specific effects. In light of the mixed findings in the literature and the tentative nature of the present results, replication with larger and more representative samples is essential. Studies that intentionally recruit balanced sex samples, diverse age ranges, and participants from multiple cultural backgrounds will be better positioned to test moderation by sex and culture. Furthermore, exploring potential mediators such as fear of negative evaluation, need for approval, and social reward sensitivity could shed light on the proximal processes linking attachment anxiety to conformity. Overall, the present study provides a starting point for a research program investigating how attachment orientations relate to susceptibility to social influence. The finding that attachment-related anxiety correlates with self-reported conformity in females but not males adds nuance to theoretical accounts that view attachment insecurity as uniformly increasing social reliance. It suggests that the behavioral manifestation of attachment anxiety may be shaped by gendered social norms and that avoidance may not exert a consistent influence on conformity tendencies. The results encourage a move beyond simplistic assumptions about insecure attachment and toward more differentiated models that consider dimension-specific effects, context, and individual differences such as sex. In conclusion, while this study offers preliminary support for a link between attachment-related anxiety and conformity among women, the evidence is not definitive and invites further investigation. Future research employing diverse methodologies, larger and more balanced samples, and experimental designs will be necessary to clarify causal mechanisms, boundary conditions, and practical implications. By advancing our understanding of how early relational patterns contribute to everyday social behaviors such as conformity, such work can enrich psychological theory and inform interventions that foster healthier interpersonal functioning.
