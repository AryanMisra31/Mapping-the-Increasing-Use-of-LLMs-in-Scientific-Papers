Introduction

The capacity to exert self-control is a central topic in psychological science because it relates to numerous domains of human functioning, including academic achievement, health behaviors, interpersonal regulation, and workplace performance. Early theoretical frameworks posited that self-control relies on a limited resource that can be consumed through effortful regulation, such that exerting self-control on one task reduces the capacity available for subsequent tasks. This conceptualization, often referred to as the strength model of self-control or ego depletion, has generated a substantial body of research that sought to test whether prior acts of regulation impair later performance on effortful tasks. A wide variety of manipulations have been used to test this idea, ranging from suppressing emotions to overriding habitual responses, from resisting tempting food to performing sustained attention tasks. The reported outcomes across this literature have been mixed, with some studies documenting clear decrements in subsequent performance and others failing to find reliable effects. This pattern of findings has led to both theoretical refinement and empirical re-examination of the conditions under which prior self-control affects future behavior.

One common manipulation employed in studies of depletion is emotional suppression, where participants are instructed to inhibit facial expressions, gestures, or verbalizations in response to emotionally evocative stimuli. Emotional suppression requires active monitoring of ongoing affective responses and the inhibition of natural expressive behavior, and thus it places demands on regulatory processes. Because the suppression of emotion is effortful and often experienced as aversive, it has long been viewed as a plausible means of testing whether prior regulation consumes a limited regulatory capacity. Several studies have reported that participants who suppress emotional expression subsequently perform worse on cognitive tasks requiring attention or problem solving compared with participants who do not suppress. However, other work has failed to replicate such effects, or has found that suppression affects subjective mood without measurable costs on cognitive tasks.

Beyond the question of whether prior self-control impairs subsequent performance, the temporal dynamics of any such depletion effect are theoretically important. If self-control relies on a consumable resource, one might expect a recovery process following exertion, whereby the depleted resource is gradually replenished over time. Researchers have tested recovery by introducing delays between the exertion of self-control and the measurement of subsequent performance, with varied intervals ranging from a few minutes to an hour. Some studies have found that performance deficits dissipate after a short delay, whereas others have found persistent effects. The timing and shape of recovery, if it exists, bears directly on the nature of the underlying mechanism: a slow biochemical replenishment process would suggest one theoretical account, whereas a rapid return of performance might be better explained by shifting motivation or attention rather than resource depletion.

The literature has also been complicated by broader methodological and conceptual critiques. Some commentators have argued that the original strength model relies on a metaphor that is difficult to operationalize and may obscure alternative explanations such as shifts in motivation, changes in attention, or altered perceptions of task value. Meta-analytic summaries of ego-depletion studies have revealed potential publication bias and heterogeneity in effects, leading to calls for more rigorous and transparent replications. In particular, the replicability of depletion effects using emotional suppression has been questioned, with some preregistered studies failing to observe significant carryover effects on subsequent cognitive tasks. This pattern raises the possibility that the effects of suppression on later tasks may be small, context-dependent, or moderated by participant characteristics and task parameters.

Given these mixed findings, it is important to test whether emotional suppression produces measurable declines in performance on a prototypical cognitive task and whether any such decline shows recovery over a short delay. Anagrams are a frequently used measure in self-control and cognitive research because they require focused attention, working memory, and problem-solving strategies. Performance on anagram tasks is sensitive to fatigue and attentional constraints, and thus they provide a reasonable assay for potential depletion effects. If emotional suppression consumes regulatory capacity in a way that is relevant for subsequent cognitive functioning, then participants who suppress emotion should, in principle, solve fewer anagrams correctly within a time limit than participants who do not suppress.

To date, however, the evidence linking emotional suppression to impaired anagram solving is equivocal. Some experiments have reported modest impairments following suppression, whereas others have observed no reliable differences. Part of this inconsistency may stem from variations in the suppression manipulation, variability in the difficulty and length of the anagram task, or differences in the timing between suppression and the cognitive assessment. In addition, individual differences in trait emotion regulation, baseline motivation, or beliefs about willpower may modulate whether suppression affects downstream performance. Unpacking these factors requires studies that manipulate suppression and recovery interval in a controlled, randomized design while also assessing manipulation fidelity and potential moderators.

The present study was designed to address two questions that follow directly from the gaps in the literature. First, does engaging in emotional suppression reduce subsequent performance on an anagram task relative to a control condition in which participants view the same stimuli without suppressing expression? Second, if suppression does impair performance, does a short delay of ten minutes allow for recovery of task performance relative to immediate testing? The two questions were operationalized in a 2 (suppression vs. no suppression) by 2 (immediate testing vs. 10-minute delay) factorial design, with percentage correct on a timed set of anagrams as the dependent variable. The design permits tests of the main effect of suppression, the main effect of delay, and the suppression-by-delay interaction that would indicate temporal recovery.

An important methodological concern in this literature is ensuring that the suppression manipulation actually prompts participants to exert regulatory effort. Therefore, the current study included manipulation checks assessing participants’ self-reported effort to inhibit expression and their perceived success at doing so. Demonstrating that participants in the suppression condition report greater regulatory effort than controls supports the interpretation that any observed performance differences are attributable to the intended manipulation rather than to failure to engage with instructions.

We also sought to address analytic issues that have been highlighted in recent debates about ego depletion. Specifically, in addition to standard null hypothesis significance tests, we planned to compute effect size estimates and complementary analyses that assess the strength of evidence for the null hypothesis. Equivalence testing and Bayesian analyses can be informative when observed effects are small or non-significant, because they help to determine whether a non-significant result is consistent with a meaningful null effect or simply a lack of power. Accordingly, the analysis plan included both frequentist ANOVA and Bayesian model comparison to evaluate the degree of support for the absence of effects.

A priori power considerations indicated that a sample large enough to detect small-to-medium effects would be desirable, given the modest effect sizes reported in prior meta-analyses and the heterogeneity across studies. Consequently, the study recruited a moderately sized convenience sample, randomly assigned to four experimental conditions in roughly equal numbers. The anagram task was standardized across conditions and administered under time pressure to increase sensitivity to potential decrements in performance.

We also considered several alternative outcomes and their implications. If suppression impaired anagram performance immediately but not after a delay, this would support a transient depletion account in which regulatory capacity can recover over short intervals. If suppression impaired performance regardless of delay, this would support a more persistent depletion model. If no effect of suppression was observed, even with an effective manipulation, this would suggest that emotional suppression may not tap a resource relevant to anagram solving, or that the resource model itself requires revision. Each of these outcomes carries distinct theoretical implications for our understanding of self-control and its temporal dynamics.

Finally, given the ongoing debates about replication and publication bias, the present study sought to contribute transparent evidence to the literature by clearly specifying design, materials, and analysis approach. We anticipated that the clarity of manipulation checks and inclusion of multiple analytic perspectives would render the study informative whether or not a depletion effect was observed. The following sections describe the methods, report the results, and discuss the implications for the broader theoretical discourse on self-control.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and the surrounding community. The initial sample comprised 128 adults who expressed interest and attended scheduled testing sessions. After applying predefined exclusion criteria for noncompliance with task instructions and incomplete data, eight participants were excluded, leaving a final sample of 120 participants. The final sample included individuals across a range of ages typical of university convenience samples. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions created by crossing the suppression manipulation (suppress vs. no suppress) with the delay condition (immediate vs. 10-minute delay), resulting in roughly 30 participants per cell. Random assignment was administered by a computer-based randomization routine that balanced assignments across session times.

Participants provided informed consent and were told they would complete several tasks and some questionnaires. They were informed that the study involved viewing short video clips and completing word puzzles, but they were not told the specific hypotheses regarding suppression and task performance. This cover limited demand characteristics while still allowing participants to follow suppression instructions in a straightforward manner.

The emotional stimulus used as the depletion manipulation was a standardized film clip known to elicit emotional expression. We selected a four-minute segment containing emotionally evocative content that reliably produces facial and vocal expressions in naive viewers. Participants in the suppression condition were given instructions to watch the clip attentively but to inhibit any facial expressions, gestures, or verbal reactions during viewing; they were instructed to keep their face neutral and avoid moving or smiling. Participants in the control condition were instructed to watch the clip naturally and to allow their reactions to occur spontaneously. Instructions were delivered in written form on a computer screen and read aloud by experimenters to enhance comprehension; experimenters ensured understanding by asking participants to summarize what they were to do before beginning.

To assess manipulation fidelity, we administered two brief self-report items immediately after the film clip. Participants rated, on a 7-point scale, how much effort they had exerted to inhibit their emotional expressions and how successful they felt they had been in doing so. These items were intended to index subjective regulatory effort and perceived success. Higher scores on the effort item were expected in the suppression condition relative to control, and the success item was used to ensure that suppression attempts were at least partly effective as experienced by participants.

Following the film clip and self-report manipulation checks, participants in the immediate conditions began the anagram task right away. Participants in the delay conditions completed a ten-minute filler period before receiving the anagram task. The filler period consisted of a neutral, low-demand activity in which participants sat quietly and completed unrelated demographic questions and neutral questionnaires about daily routines; the filler was designed to minimize additional regulation demands and to avoid introducing other cognitive fatigue.

The anagram task comprised 20 anagrams of moderate difficulty, presented one at a time on a computer screen. Each anagram could be solved by rearranging letters to form a common English word. Participants had a total of six minutes to work on the set, with no individual time limit per item; they could move forward and backward, and they were instructed to submit their best answer or leave an item blank if they could not solve it. The dependent variable was the percentage of correctly solved anagrams out of those attempted, calculated by dividing the number of correct solutions by the total number of items and multiplying by one hundred. This measure was chosen to reflect both accuracy and the capacity to produce correct solutions under time pressure.

Experimenters recorded the number of correctly solved items, number of attempted items, and time per item. Responses were scored by experimenters blind to participant condition to prevent bias in coding. Scoring used a predetermined answer key, and ambiguous answers were resolved by consulting a second scorer. Interrater agreement for a subset of responses exceeded 98% before final coding decisions, indicating reliable scoring.

Participants also completed a brief mood measure after the anagram task to detect any lingering affective differences between conditions. The mood measure consisted of several items assessing positive and negative affect on simple Likert scales, and these data were used as exploratory checks to ensure that mood alone did not account for any observed performance differences.

Data cleaning procedures entailed screening for outliers and noncompliance. Participants who reported failing to follow suppression instructions or who left more than half of the anagrams blank were excluded per pre-registered criteria. These exclusions accounted for the eight participants removed prior to analysis. The final dataset contained complete anagram performance, manipulation check scores, and mood ratings for all included participants.

The primary analysis plan entailed a two-way between-subjects ANOVA with suppression condition and delay condition as fixed factors, predicting percentage correct on the anagram task. Planned contrasts included testing the main effect of suppression, the main effect of delay, and the interaction effect. Effect sizes (partial eta-squared) were reported for ANOVA effects, and 95% confidence intervals around cell means were computed. Additionally, Bayesian analyses were conducted to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis relative to the alternative. Bayes factors were computed using default priors appropriate for ANOVA models, and interpretation followed conventional benchmarks where values greater than three indicate moderate evidence for one model over another.

Given ongoing concerns about statistical power in ego-depletion research, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to estimate the minimum detectable effect size with the final sample. With approximately 30 participants per cell and alpha set at .05, the design had 80% power to detect an effect size around f = .25 for the main effects, corresponding to a small-to-medium effect. This power estimation informed the interpretation of null findings, which could be reasonably taken to indicate the absence of effects of that magnitude or larger.

Exploratory analyses included correlations between self-reported suppression effort and anagram performance across the full sample to assess whether greater subjective regulatory effort was associated with lower performance. We also explored whether mood ratings after the anagram task were correlated with performance. Finally, we examined whether participant demographic variables such as age and gender related to performance, and whether including these variables as covariates changed the main results.

All analyses were performed with standard statistical software. Data inspection confirmed normality of the primary dependent variable to a degree appropriate for parametric testing; no transformations were necessary. Homogeneity of variance assumptions were checked with Levene’s test and found to be acceptable for the main effects. The results section reports the outcomes of these analyses in detail.

Results

The final sample consisted of 120 participants divided across four experimental conditions with approximately 30 participants per cell. Demographic characteristics were comparable across conditions, and no significant differences were observed in age or gender distribution between groups. The average percentage of correctly solved anagrams across the entire sample was 48.6% with a standard deviation of 15.1. Inspection of the distribution of the dependent variable revealed no extreme skewness or kurtosis that would necessitate data transformation.

Manipulation checks indicated that the suppression instructions produced the intended subjective effect. Participants in the suppression condition reported substantially greater effort to inhibit emotional expression (M = 5.9 on a 7-point scale, SD = 0.8) than did participants in the no-suppression control condition (M = 2.4, SD = 1.1). An independent-samples t-test confirmed that this difference was highly significant, t(118) = 19.1, p < .001, indicating that participants followed the suppression instructions and experienced them as effortful. On the perceived success item, suppression participants reported moderate success (M = 4.8, SD = 1.0) whereas control participants reported slightly lower success at inhibition because they had not attempted to inhibit (M = 3.1, SD = 1.2), and the difference was significant, t(118) = 10.4, p < .001. These manipulation-check results support the validity of the suppression manipulation.

We first report the results of the planned two-way ANOVA predicting percentage correct on the anagram task. The main effect of suppression (suppress vs. no suppress) was not significant, F(1,116) = 0.42, p = .52, partial eta-squared = .004. The main effect of delay (immediate vs. 10-minute delay) was also not significant, F(1,116) = 0.28, p = .60, partial eta-squared = .002. Importantly, the interaction between suppression and delay was not significant, F(1,116) = 0.05, p = .82, partial eta-squared < .001. Thus, neither suppression nor a ten-minute delay influenced performance in a statistically detectable way according to these analyses.

Descriptive statistics by cell showed closely similar means. Participants in the suppress-immediate condition had a mean percentage correct of 46.2% (SD = 15.3). Those in the suppress-delay condition had a mean of 47.5% (SD = 14.8). Participants in the no-suppress-immediate condition had a mean of 48.1% (SD = 14.9). Participants in the no-suppress-delay condition had a mean of 49.0% (SD = 15.1). Pairwise comparisons between these cells did not yield any significant differences after correction for multiple tests. The small raw differences in means translate to negligible standardized effects, and the partial eta-squared values from the ANOVA corroborate the minimal magnitude of effects.

Because non-significant p-values can arise from genuine null effects or from insufficient data to detect small effects, we complemented the ANOVA with Bayesian model comparison. The Bayesian analysis compared the full model including both main effects and the interaction against a null model that included only an intercept. The resulting Bayes factor (BF01) favored the null over the full model by approximately 4.2 to 1, indicating moderate evidence for the null hypothesis relative to the presence of suppression and delay effects. A comparison of a model including only the suppression main effect to the null model produced a Bayes factor similarly favoring the null by about 3.8 to 1. These Bayesian outcomes strengthen the interpretation that there is meaningful evidence against large depletion effects in the present study.

We also conducted equivalence testing to determine whether the observed effects could be considered statistically equivalent to zero within a prespecified effect size bound. Using a two one-sided tests (TOST) procedure with an equivalence bound corresponding to a small effect (Cohen’s d = ±0.35), results suggested that the effect of suppression on anagram performance was statistically equivalent to zero within these bounds, t1(119) = 2.47, p = .008 and t2(119) = -2.03, p = .023 for the two one-sided tests. The equivalence tests imply that any true effect of suppression, if present, is unlikely to exceed a small magnitude in either direction given the current sample size and design.

Exploratory correlational analyses assessed the relationship between self-reported suppression effort and anagram performance across the full sample. The correlation between reported effort and percentage correct was small and non-significant, r = -0.07, p = .45, indicating that greater subjective effort to inhibit expression did not predict reliably lower anagram performance. Similarly, perceived success at suppression was uncorrelated with performance, r = 0.03, p = .74. These correlations were consistent across suppression and control participants when examined separately, and no pattern emerged to suggest that individual differences in suppression engagement drove task performance.

Mood ratings collected after the anagram task showed no consistent differences across conditions that could account for performance. Post-task negative affect averaged 2.1 on a 7-point scale (SD = 1.0), and positive affect averaged 4.2 (SD = 1.0), with no significant differences by condition. Correlations between mood scores and anagram performance were negligible (|r| < .10), suggesting that transient mood differences did not confound the primary outcomes.

Additional exploratory analyses tested whether participant demographics were related to performance. Age bore a small negative correlation with percentage correct, r = -0.16, p = .09, but this relationship did not reach conventional significance. Gender differences were absent; males and females performed similarly on the anagram task (M_males = 49.1%, M_females = 48.2%, t(118) = 0.32, p = .75). Including age and gender as covariates in an ANCOVA did not alter the non-significant effects of suppression and delay on performance.

Time-on-task metrics during the anagram task showed similar patterns across conditions. The average time spent per item was approximately 18 seconds, with no significant differences by condition. The number of attempted items did not differ reliably across groups, indicating that motivation to attempt solutions was comparable regardless of prior suppression or delay.

Given the non-significant main effects and interaction, we investigated whether any subset of participants exhibited patterns consistent with depletion. We split the sample by median self-reported suppression effort and compared anagram performance among those reporting high effort versus low effort, irrespective of condition. This analysis showed no meaningful differences, with the high-effort subgroup solving 47.9% of anagrams correctly and the low-effort subgroup solving 49.2% correctly (t(118) = 0.44, p = .66). Thus, even among those who reported exerting more regulatory effort, performance did not significantly decline.

Finally, we considered the possibility that the anagram task may have been either too easy or too difficult to detect depletion effects. The average accuracy around 48% suggests task difficulty was moderate, and the variance in performance indicated no floor or ceiling effects. This observation reduces the likelihood that insensitivity of the dependent measure accounted for the null findings.

Overall, the results do not support the hypothesis that emotional suppression reduces performance on a subsequent anagram task, nor do they indicate that a ten-minute delay facilitates recovery from any suppression-related impairment. Both frequentist and Bayesian analyses, along with equivalence testing, converge on the conclusion that suppression and a short delay had negligible effects on anagram performance in the present sample.

Discussion

The present study examined whether engaging in emotional suppression would impair subsequent performance on an anagram task and whether a ten-minute delay would allow for recovery of any impairment. Across a randomized 2 by 2 design, neither suppression nor delay produced statistically significant effects on percentage correct anagram solving. The suppression manipulation produced the expected subjective experience of regulatory effort, as indicated by manipulation checks, but this effort did not translate into measurable decrements in subsequent cognitive performance. Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence in favor of the null model, and equivalence testing suggested that any true effects were unlikely to exceed small magnitudes. These outcomes have several implications for theories of self-control, research methodology, and future investigations.

First, the failure to observe a depletion effect in this experiment contributes to the growing body of literature that questions the generality of a simple resource-depletion interpretation for self-control. The strength model predicts that exerting self-control in one domain should reduce capacity for subsequent self-control-demanding tasks. Emotional suppression is a naturalistic and widely used manipulation because it involves active inhibition of expressive behavior; thus, the absence of an effect here is notable. The manipulation checks confirmed that participants in the suppression condition experienced effort and perceived themselves as attempting to inhibit expression, reducing the likelihood that null findings reflect participants ignoring the instructions. Given this, the results suggest that either emotional suppression does not consume the form of capacity that anagram solving requires, or that any consumed capacity recovers rapidly and was replenished by the time the anagram task was administered, even in the immediate condition.

Second, the temporal dynamics of any depletion effect warrant careful consideration. We included both an immediate test and a test after a ten-minute delay to detect potential recovery dynamics. If suppression produced a short-lived depletion effect, we might have expected performance deficits in the immediate condition that dissipated after ten minutes. However, no interaction between suppression and delay emerged. This pattern suggests that either suppression had no appreciable initial cost on anagram performance, or that the recovery process, if operative, operates on a timescale shorter than the immediate measurement window or longer than ten minutes. For instance, if replenishment occurs within seconds due to rapid affective or motivational adjustments, the immediate test may not capture the transient dip. Alternatively, if recovery requires longer than ten minutes or depends on restorative activities such as rest or glucose intake, our delay may have been insufficient. Future research should investigate a broader range of delays, including immediate second-by-second assessments and longer intervals extending to an hour or more, to map potential recovery functions.

Third, alternative theoretical explanations merit discussion. Motivational and attentional accounts propose that declines in self-control tasks reflect shifts in priorities, willingness to exert effort, or attentional disengagement rather than depletion of a resource. Under such models, people may reduce effort on subsequent tasks because of lowered motivation or altered valuation of the task relative to immediate alternatives. In the present study, motivation to solve anagrams appeared comparable across conditions, as indicated by similar numbers of attempted items and similar time-on-task metrics. This observation weakens a motivation-based explanation for null findings in the sense that lack of motivation would predict fewer attempts or faster disengagement, which we did not observe. However, it remains possible that subtle motivational factors or beliefs about willpower influenced individual responses in ways not captured by our measures.

Another possibility is that suppression primarily affects affective or interpersonal functioning rather than cognitive problem solving. Emotional suppression may impose costs on social connection, emotional memory, or physiological indices, but these effects may not generalize to tasks like anagram solving that rely heavily on verbal and search strategies. If suppression taxes a regulatory domain distinct from the cognitive processes required for anagram performance, then the absence of cross-domain effects could be expected. Differentiating domain-specific versus domain-general influences of regulatory acts should be a priority for future theoretical work and empirical tests.

Methodological nuances also warrant attention. The present study used a moderate-length anagram task with a six-minute time limit and 20 items, which produced average accuracy around 48%, suggesting appropriate difficulty. The manipulation check confirmed subjective effort, and scoring procedures were robust, with high interrater agreement. Despite these strengths, certain limitations could account for null effects. For example, our suppression manipulation used a single film clip of fixed length. Different emotional stimuli varying in intensity or valence might elicit differing levels of expressive activation and thus different magnitudes of suppression effort. It is possible that a more intense stimulus or longer suppression period would produce a stronger depletion effect. Likewise, different types of suppressed responses (e.g., suppressing emotion for longer durations or in social interaction contexts) might be more depleting.

Participant characteristics may have moderated effects. Individual differences in habitual emotion regulation strategies, belief in willpower, or baseline regulatory capacity could influence susceptibility to depletion. Although we explored correlations between self-reported effort and performance and found no reliable relationships, more nuanced measures of trait emotion regulation or capacity might reveal moderators. Future studies could assess and statistically model interactions with trait-level variables to examine heterogeneity in responses.

The null results also speak to broader debates about replicability and effect size in ego-depletion research. Meta-analyses and large-scale replication attempts have painted a complex picture of the robustness of depletion effects, with evidence of small average effects and substantial heterogeneity. Our findings align with this pattern by demonstrating no meaningful effect in a study adequately powered to detect small-to-medium effects. The Bayesian evidence favoring the null further underscores that the absence of a detectable effect is not merely due to low statistical power in this instance. This outcome reinforces the need for large, well-powered, and methodologically rigorous studies that preregistered hypotheses and analytic plans in order to obtain robust estimates of effect sizes.

Practically, the results suggest that common everyday acts of emotion suppression—at least when brief and in an experimental laboratory context—may not impair certain types of subsequent cognitive performance. If generalizable, this finding could alleviate concerns that short bouts of emotional self-regulation necessarily compromise problem solving in domains requiring verbal search and anagram-like processes. However, caution is warranted in generalizing to settings involving prolonged suppression, emotionally intense contexts, or tasks tapping different cognitive domains such as sustained attention or inhibitory control.

Future research directions are numerous. Studies should manipulate suppression intensity and duration systematically to test for dose-response relationships. Including physiological indices such as heart rate variability, electromyography of facial muscles, or cortisol measures would provide objective evidence of regulatory exertion and potential biological costs. Researchers should also vary the nature of the secondary task to determine domain specificity of any suppression effects, using tasks that more directly tap inhibitory control, working memory updating, or sustained attention. Additionally, testing a broader array of delay intervals will help clarify recovery dynamics and their potential moderators.

It would also be valuable to explore interactions between suppression and motivational incentives. For example, offering rewards for anagram performance might unmask latent depletion effects by motivating participants to overcome any transient costs, or conversely, it might reduce performance differences by elevating baseline motivation. Manipulating beliefs about willpower—by informing participants that self-control is either a limited resource or readily replenishable—could further shed light on the role of lay theories in mediating observed effects.

Another avenue is employing within-subject designs where participants experience both suppression and no-suppression conditions in counterbalanced order, thus increasing sensitivity to individual-level changes. Longitudinal designs could assess cumulative effects of repeated suppression across days or weeks, which may be more ecologically valid for understanding the impacts of habitual regulation in everyday life.

From a theoretical perspective, models of self-control must accommodate the heterogeneity of empirical findings, distinguishing between circumstances where prior regulation impairs subsequent tasks and those where it does not. Hybrid models that integrate resource-like constraints with motivational and attentional processes may be particularly fruitful. Such models could posit that resource depletion is one pathway among several, with its manifestation contingent on task demands, individual differences, and contextual incentives.

In summary, the current experiment found that instructing participants to suppress emotional expression did not reduce subsequent anagram performance, and that a ten-minute delay did not moderate this null effect. Manipulation checks confirmed that suppression was experienced as effortful, and both Bayesian and equivalence analyses supported the interpretation that any true effects are likely to be small. These results contribute to an increasingly nuanced understanding of self-control dynamics and underscore the importance of continued empirical scrutiny using diverse methodologies and analytic perspectives. Continued research that systematically varies suppression parameters, task domains, recovery intervals, and individual-difference moderators will be necessary to further delineate when and why acts of self-regulation influence subsequent functioning.
