Introduction

The human capacity to hold and manipulate information over brief intervals is fundamental to a wide range of cognitive activities. Memory research has historically distinguished between short-term retention of information and the consolidation of long-term representations. Early conceptualizations conceived short-term memory as a passive store for items awaiting transfer to long-term memory. Later theoretical developments reframed short-term retention as an active system supporting ongoing cognitive activity and complex behavior. This reframing led to the emergence of working memory as a central construct in cognitive psychology. Working memory models posit multiple components that together support the temporary maintenance and processing of information. One influential model proposes separable subsystems for different types of information alongside a limited-capacity central executive. In this framework, a phonological component is specialized for the temporary storage of speech-based information. The phonological component has been extensively studied through manipulations that probe its capacity and temporal dynamics. One robust finding associated with the phonological component is the word length effect, whereby lists of short words are recalled more accurately in serial order than lists of long words. The word length effect has been taken as evidence that the phonological subsystem is constrained by time-based decay or rehearsal rates for articulatory processes. An additional manipulation commonly used to probe phonological storage has been articulatory suppression, which impairs subvocal rehearsal and typically attenuates the advantage of short words. Together, these manipulations have helped establish the phonological component as a temporally constrained store that interfaces with speech processes. Despite the extensive focus on phonological coding, other representational codes may contribute to serial recall performance. Semantic or lexical-level information, for example, can influence memory performance across many paradigms. Concreteness, the property that distinguishes words that denote tangible, imageable concepts from those that denote more abstract concepts, is one such semantic variable that reliably affects memory performance. Concreteness effects have been reported in free recall, cued recall, recognition, and paired-associate learning, with concrete words typically showing superior memory relative to abstract words. Several theoretical frameworks have been advanced to account for concreteness effects. Dual-coding theory proposes that concrete words benefit from both verbal and nonverbal or imaginal codes, creating richer representations and more retrieval routes. Other proposals emphasize differences in semantic richness, network connectivity, or the ease of forming contextual associations for concrete items. The role of semantic coding in short-term or working memory tasks, however, has received comparatively less systematic attention. A critical question concerns how semantic codes interact with phonological processes in serial order maintenance. Serial recall tasks provide a sensitive testbed for this question because they require both item memory and order memory. The classic demonstration of the word length effect in serial recall suggests that phonological codes are central to order maintenance. Yet semantic properties might influence item representations, their distinctiveness, and the ease of ordering. Empirical evidence examining semantic contributions to serial recall has been mixed. Some studies have reported advantages for semantically richer or more imageable items in order reconstruction tasks, whereas other studies have found negligible semantic effects once phonological factors are controlled. Potential reasons for these disparate findings include differences in task demands, stimulus selection, and the extent to which participants rely on phonological versus semantic strategies. There is therefore a need for systematic work that manipulates both phonological and semantic properties in a factorial design within the same serial recall task. Building on this perspective, the current study sought to examine the joint contributions of phonological and semantic codes to serial recall. We modeled the phonological code function by manipulating word length, using short and long words that differed in the number of syllables and articulatory duration. We modeled the semantic code by manipulating concreteness, using words that varied in concreteness while matching other lexical properties as closely as possible. Our goal was to replicate the established word length effect and to examine whether concreteness also facilitates serial recall. Importantly, we aimed to test whether the effects of phonological and semantic codes interact, which would suggest interdependence between the two coding systems, or instead act additively, which would suggest independent contributions to performance. There are theoretical reasons to expect an interaction between phonological and semantic codes. If semantic richness enhances item representations in a way that supports phonological rehearsal or chunking, then concrete words might show a relatively larger advantage when phonological resources are constrained or engaged. Conversely, if the phonological loop primarily supports serial order independently of semantic content, then the effects might be additive with no interaction. Prior work that manipulated both semantic variables and phonological constraints has yielded mixed patterns, leaving the nature of the relationship unclear. Therefore, the present study was designed to provide a clear test by employing a fully crossed within-subjects manipulation of word length and concreteness in a serial recall paradigm. We implemented strict controls on stimulus selection to reduce confounds from word frequency, phonological similarity, and orthographic length. We presented lists auditorily at a controlled rate to engage phonological processing and to mimic classical serial recall procedures. Participants were instructed to recall lists in strict serial order immediately after presentation, and performance was scored both in terms of strict serial position accuracy and overall item recall. We predicted that shorter words would be recalled with greater accuracy than longer words, replicating the word length effect. We also predicted that concrete words would be recalled more accurately than abstract words, reflecting the contribution of semantic codes. With respect to the interaction, we considered two competing hypotheses. One hypothesis proposed an interaction such that the advantage for concrete words would be especially pronounced for short words if semantic and phonological properties mutually reinforce memory traces. The alternative hypothesis proposed additive main effects, consistent with separable contributions from a phonological loop and semantic coding processes. The resolution of these hypotheses carries implications for models of working memory. An interaction would suggest that semantic richness can modulate phonological rehearsal or retention processes, perhaps by facilitating chunking or by providing additional retrieval cues that compensate for phonological decay. An additive pattern would imply that phonological and semantic codes independently contribute to the strength of item representations but do not strongly influence the temporal dynamics governed by the phonological subsystem. In addition to the core factorial analysis, we planned exploratory examinations of serial position curves to determine whether semantic and phonological effects differ across early, middle, and late positions in the list. Such analyses might reveal whether semantic advantages are concentrated in certain positions or are uniform across the list. We also examined whether individual differences in reported rehearsal strategies related to the magnitude of the word length or concreteness effects. If participants who report greater reliance on subvocal rehearsal show larger word length effects, this would support the interpretation of the phonological loop as a rehearsal-based mechanism. If participants who report imagery or semantic strategies show larger concreteness effects, this would support the semantic contribution hypothesis. The present design therefore allowed us to address the central question of whether semantic and phonological codes interact in serial recall and to situate the findings within contemporary models of working memory. By replicating classic phonological effects and simultaneously testing semantic contributions, the study aimed to refine theoretical accounts of the codes supporting short-term maintenance. The findings were expected to clarify whether memory for serial order is predominantly governed by phonological dynamics or whether semantic properties provide a parallel route to improved recall. This clarification is important for informing computational models and for understanding how individuals might compensate for phonological limitations by leveraging semantic knowledge. Furthermore, the study has potential applied relevance for educational practices and memory rehabilitation by indicating whether emphasis on semantic elaboration could aid short-term retention in contexts where phonological resources are limited. Overall, the introduction has highlighted key theoretical background, delineated gaps in the literature, and provided specific hypotheses that motivated the empirical work reported below.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from the local community. A total of forty-eight adults completed the experiment. All participants reported native-like proficiency in the language of testing and normal or corrected-to-normal hearing. Participants were compensated with course credit or a modest monetary payment. The sample size was chosen to provide reasonable power to detect medium-sized within-subject effects in the factorial design. Prior to testing, participants provided informed consent and completed a brief demographics questionnaire. Stimuli consisted of monosyllabic and multisyllabic words selected to manipulate word length and concreteness. Words were categorized as short (one to two syllables) or long (three to four syllables) for the phonological manipulation. Words were categorized as concrete or abstract for the semantic manipulation. Care was taken to balance sets on lexical frequency, written length, and phonological neighborhood size as closely as possible. The stimulus pool comprised 120 words, with 30 words in each of the four conditions: short-concrete, short-abstract, long-concrete, and long-abstract. The lists used in the serial recall task were constructed by drawing words from these pools without replacement for each participant. Each trial consisted of a list of six words presented in a fixed serial order. The list length of six was chosen to impose a moderate load on working memory while maintaining feasibility for strict serial recall scoring. Words were presented auditorily through headphones at a comfortable listening level. Each word was presented at a rate of one item per second, with a 500 ms interstimulus interval between words. Presentation rate was kept constant across conditions to ensure that temporal characteristics of presentation did not confound the manipulation of word length. The order of trials was randomized for each participant. Each participant completed 48 critical trials in total, with 12 trials per condition. Trials were distributed into blocks with short breaks to reduce fatigue. Participants were instructed to attend to the words and to recall the words immediately after presentation in the same order they were presented. They were asked to produce the words orally, and responses were recorded for later scoring. A brief practice block preceded the experiment to familiarize participants with the procedure. To assess rehearsal strategies, participants completed a post-experiment questionnaire in which they reported the extent to which they used subvocal rehearsal, semantic imagery, or other strategies on a Likert scale. Scoring of serial recall responses was conducted by trained raters. A response was scored as correct for a given serial position if the participant produced the correct word in the correct position. Partial credit scoring was also computed as the proportion of items recalled irrespective of position for supplementary analyses. Inter-rater reliability was established on a subset of responses and exceeded accepted thresholds. The main dependent variable was the proportion of correctly recalled items in the correct serial positions, averaged across trials within each condition for each participant. Secondary dependent variables included overall item recall irrespective of order and serial position–specific accuracy. The primary analysis used a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance with factors Word Length (short, long) and Concreteness (concrete, abstract). Prior to the ANOVA, data were inspected for normality, outliers, and sphericity assumptions. When necessary, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied to degrees of freedom. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared for ANOVA effects. Planned pairwise comparisons were conducted using paired-samples t-tests to examine simple effects. For the exploratory serial position analysis, a three-way ANOVA with factors Word Length, Concreteness, and Position (grouped as early, middle, late) was conducted. Correlational analyses examined relationships between self-reported strategy use and the magnitudes of the word length and concreteness effects. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Alpha was set at .05 for primary tests, and Bonferroni corrections were applied for multiple comparisons as appropriate. Data and analysis scripts were archived to permit reproducibility.

Results

Preliminary data screening revealed no participants with excessive missing data or performance at floor across all conditions. Descriptive statistics indicated that overall serial position accuracy varied across conditions in the expected directions. Mean proportion correct in the short-concrete condition was higher than in the other conditions. Group-level means and standard deviations were computed for each of the four conditions. For strict serial position scoring, the mean proportion correct was .68 (SD = .12) for short-concrete lists, .61 (SD = .13) for short-abstract lists, .56 (SD = .14) for long-concrete lists, and .49 (SD = .15) for long-abstract lists. These descriptive values suggested both a word length advantage for short over long words and a concreteness advantage for concrete over abstract words. The primary two-way repeated-measures ANOVA examined the effects of Word Length and Concreteness on proportion correct. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of Word Length, with short words recalled more accurately than long words. Specifically, the main effect of Word Length yielded F(1,47) = 53.2, p < .001, partial eta squared = .53, indicating a large effect. The main effect of Concreteness was also significant, with concrete words recalled more accurately than abstract words. The main effect of Concreteness yielded F(1,47) = 18.6, p < .001, partial eta squared = .28, indicating a moderate-to-large effect. The interaction between Word Length and Concreteness was not statistically significant. The interaction term yielded F(1,47) = 1.17, p = .28, partial eta squared = .02, indicating a small and non-significant interaction. Given the non-significant interaction, we proceeded to interpret the two main effects as additive. Planned paired t-tests comparing short and long words collapsed across concreteness confirmed the word length effect. The paired-samples t-test comparing short versus long words yielded t(47) = 7.29, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.05. Paired t-tests comparing concrete versus abstract words collapsed across word length confirmed the concreteness effect. The paired-samples t-test comparing concrete versus abstract words yielded t(47) = 4.31, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.62. Simple effects within each word length level reinforced the additive pattern. For short words, concrete lists were recalled better than abstract lists, t(47) = 3.64, p = .001, Cohen’s d = 0.53. For long words, concrete lists were also recalled better than abstract lists, t(47) = 2.88, p = .006, Cohen’s d = 0.42. Effect sizes for both simple effects were in the small-to-medium range. Examination of serial position curves provided additional insight into the locus of effects. Accuracy naturally varied across positions, with primacy and recency tendencies consistent with prior serial recall literature. When positions were grouped into early (positions 1–2), middle (positions 3–4), and late (positions 5–6), accuracy was highest in early and late groups and lowest in middle positions. The three-way ANOVA with factors Word Length, Concreteness, and Position group revealed a significant main effect of Position, F(2,94) = 112.5, p < .001, partial eta squared = .71. The main effects of Word Length and Concreteness in this analysis remained significant and of similar magnitude as in the primary ANOVA. There was no significant three-way interaction involving Position, and no significant two-way interaction between Word Length and Position. A modest interaction between Concreteness and Position emerged, F(2,94) = 3.62, p = .03, partial eta squared = .07, suggesting that the concreteness advantage was somewhat larger in middle positions than in boundary positions. Follow-up contrasts indicated that the concreteness advantage in middle positions was statistically reliable, t(47) = 3.01, p = .004, whereas the advantages in early and late positions were smaller and did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. These exploratory findings suggest that semantic contributions may be especially helpful where serial order is most vulnerable. Supplemental analyses examined overall item recall irrespective of order. The pattern of effects for unordered item recall mirrored the strict serial recall results, with main effects of Word Length and Concreteness and no significant interaction. The advantage for short words and for concrete words was evident for both ordered and unordered measures, suggesting that semantic contributions support item memory as well as ordered recall. Correlational analyses probed the relationship between self-reported strategy use and effect magnitudes. Participants who reported greater reliance on subvocal rehearsal tended to exhibit larger word length effects, r = .42, p = .003, indicating a moderate positive relationship. Participants who reported greater reliance on semantic imagery tended to exhibit larger concreteness effects, r = .36, p = .01. These correlations support the idea that individual differences in strategy usage relate to the extent to which participants exploit phonological or semantic codes. Additional analyses controlled for potential confounds from lexical frequency and phonological similarity. Regression models that included frequency and neighborhood measures as covariates indicated that the main effects of Word Length and Concreteness remained significant, suggesting that these effects were not solely driven by residual lexical differences. Finally, an analysis examining trials in which participants self-reported articulatory suppression-like distractions found a reduction in the word length effect magnitude but not a reliable attenuation of the concreteness effect. Together, the results provide convergent evidence that both phonological and semantic codes independently support serial recall performance, while failing to reveal a reliable interaction between these factors.

Discussion

The present study examined the roles of phonological and semantic codes in serial recall by manipulating word length and concreteness in a fully crossed design. The results replicated the canonical word length effect, with short words recalled more accurately than long words. The study also demonstrated a reliable concreteness advantage, with concrete words recalled more accurately than abstract words. Crucially, the interaction between word length and concreteness was not significant, suggesting that the two influences act additively rather than multiplicatively under the present task conditions. These findings have several implications for theories of working memory and the codes that support short-term retention. The robust word length effect observed here corroborates the notion that phonological processes, often characterized as a rehearsal-based subsystem, play a central role in maintaining serial order. The sizable effect size for word length indicates that temporal or articulatory constraints meaningfully limit retention, consistent with accounts that emphasize decay or time-based capacity operating on phonological traces. The concreteness effect observed in serial recall extends the domain of semantic influences into immediate memory tasks. While semantic effects are well documented in long-term memory paradigms, the present results indicate that semantic richness and imagery potential can also enhance performance in short-term serial recall. This finding supports the view that multiple representational codes are available to participants, and that semantic codes can be leveraged even under brief retention intervals. The absence of a significant interaction suggests that phonological and semantic codes contribute independently to recall accuracy under typical serial recall conditions. An additive pattern implies that semantic enhancement of item representations increases the likelihood of retrieval without directly altering the temporal dynamics governing phonological maintenance. In other words, concrete words may be represented more robustly in semantic and imagery systems, providing additional retrieval routes, but these advantages do not appear to differentially modulate the limitations imposed by word length. This result is informative in distinguishing between models that posit tight coupling between semantic elaboration and phonological rehearsal and models that view the codes as relatively separable. The present findings favor the latter perspective at least for immediate serial recall with auditory presentation at a fixed rate. Nevertheless, the exploratory serial position analysis hinted at a nuance: the concreteness advantage was relatively larger in middle positions where serial order is more vulnerable. This pattern suggests that semantic codes may play a compensatory role when phonological ordering signals are weakest. Such compensation would not necessarily produce a statistical interaction in the overall factorial test but could reflect position-specific interplay between codes. Future work could deliberately emphasize middle-list vulnerability to more sensitively test this compensatory hypothesis. The correlations between self-reported strategy use and effect magnitudes provide converging evidence for the construct validity of the manipulations. Participants who endorsed greater reliance on subvocal rehearsal tended to exhibit larger word length effects, consistent with the idea that articulatory processes underlie the temporal constraints captured by word length. Participants who reported greater use of semantic imagery exhibited larger concreteness effects, consistent with the interpretation that imagery or semantic elaboration bolsters item memory. These individual differences underscore the importance of considering strategic variation in working memory tasks. Different participants may weight phonological and semantic codes differently, and average patterns reflect this heterogeneity. Methodologically, the auditory presentation at a fixed rate and the use of strict serial scoring align the present study with classic paradigms and enhance comparability to prior work. Controls for lexical frequency and phonological neighborhood helped ensure that the observed effects were driven by the manipulated dimensions rather than confounds. Nonetheless, certain limitations warrant discussion. First, although we attempted to match stimuli on several lexical dimensions, perfect matching is difficult, and residual differences could contribute in part to the observed effects. We mitigated this concern by including covariates in regression models, where main effects persisted, but future studies could use larger stimulus pools and more extensive norming to further reduce potential confounds. Second, the present task used auditory presentation and a moderate list length of six items. Different modalities or list lengths might alter the balance of phonological and semantic contributions. For instance, visual presentation could reduce reliance on phonological codes, potentially amplifying semantic effects or revealing interactions. Likewise, longer lists that exceed phonological rehearsal capacities might force greater reliance on semantic organization and thus produce a different pattern of interaction. Third, the absence of a significant interaction does not constitute definitive proof of independence across all contexts. Interactions may emerge under different task demands, presentation rates, or when explicit instructions encourage semantic encoding. Indeed, prior studies that prompted semantic elaboration or chunking strategies sometimes reported interactive effects. Thus, our findings should be interpreted as evidence for additive contributions under specific task conditions rather than a universal law. Theoretical implications of these findings suggest that models of working memory should accommodate multiple representational codes that can be flexibly deployed by participants. A hybrid framework in which phonological storage primarily supports order via temporally constrained rehearsal, while semantic and imaginal systems provide supplementary item-level representations and retrieval routes, appears consistent with the present pattern. Computational models that incorporate parallel maintenance streams and interactive retrieval processes could capture the additive main effects and the potential for position-specific compensation. Neurocognitive perspectives also align with the idea of partially separable systems; phonological rehearsal processes are often associated with frontal speech-motor circuits, whereas semantic processing engages distributed temporal and parietal networks. The independent contributions found here are anatomically plausible given distributed substrates. The findings carry applied implications as well. In educational contexts or memory rehabilitation, strategies that enhance semantic elaboration may improve item recall even when phonological resources are taxed. For example, encouraging imagery or linking new verbal material to concrete referents could bolster retention. Conversely, when rapid verbal rehearsal is feasible, minimizing articulatory load by using shorter verbal units may benefit order retention. Future research directions are numerous. One promising avenue is to manipulate presentation rate and articulatory suppression to directly probe the causal role of subvocal rehearsal in mediating the word length effect and its relation to concreteness. Introducing articulatory suppression across conditions could reveal whether semantic advantages persist when phonological rehearsal is disrupted. Another direction is to vary list length systematically to identify whether interactions between codes emerge at higher loads. Neuroimaging and neuromodulation approaches could probe the neural substrates supporting independent versus interactive codes and could examine whether semantic enhancement recruits distinct networks during maintenance. Cross-language studies could examine whether languages with differing syllabic structures or morphological complexity yield similar additive patterns. Developmental and clinical populations also offer fertile ground; for instance, individuals with aphasia or phonological loop impairments might rely disproportionately on semantic codes, producing different patterns of interaction. Designing interventions that leverage semantic encoding could offer therapeutic benefits for populations with phonological deficits. Further, computational modeling efforts could formalize how phonological decay and semantic enrichment combine to predict recall probabilities across positions and conditions. Such models should be constrained by both group-level effects and individual differences in strategy use. A more granular analysis of serial position effects, perhaps using Bayesian hierarchical modeling, might reveal subtle interactions that traditional ANOVAs fail to detect. In sum, the present study demonstrates that both phonological and semantic codes contribute to serial recall performance, with additive effects of word length and concreteness under the tested conditions. The findings clarify how multiple representational systems support short-term memory and encourage models that incorporate flexible, parallel encoding routes. While phonological rehearsal appears central to maintaining serial order, semantic enrichment enhances item representations and can aid recall where ordering signals are vulnerable. Future work that systematically varies task demands, engages different populations, and integrates computational and neurobiological methods will further illuminate how the mind orchestrates multiple codes to sustain memory over brief intervals.
