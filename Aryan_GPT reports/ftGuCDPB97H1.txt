Introduction

Music is a pervasive element of human experience that accompanies many daily activities, including study, work, and routine tasks. Researchers and laypeople alike have long been interested in whether the presence of background music can influence cognitive processes and task performance. Among the cognitive domains potentially affected by music, memory has received substantial attention because of its centrality to learning and everyday functioning. Short-term memory in particular is a fundamental cognitive ability that supports the temporary storage and manipulation of information and underpins more complex cognitive tasks. The question of whether background music enhances, impairs, or has no effect on short-term memory is thus practically important and theoretically informative about the mechanisms by which auditory context interacts with cognitive processing. Empirical findings on the effect of background music on memory have been mixed, with some studies reporting facilitation, some reporting interference, and others reporting null effects. This heterogeneity suggests the influence of moderators that shape when music will affect memory performance and when it will not.

Several theoretical accounts have been proposed to explain how music might influence memory. One class of accounts emphasizes the role of arousal and mood modulation. According to these accounts, upbeat or positively valenced music can elevate arousal and mood, and moderate increases in arousal can enhance attentional focus and memory encoding. Conversely, when music produces excessive arousal or negative affect, performance may deteriorate. Another set of accounts frames the issue in terms of attentional resources and distraction. Music with fluctuating acoustic properties, salient melodic contours, or meaningful lyrics can capture attention away from the task, reducing the resources available for encoding and maintenance and thereby impairing memory. A related account, the interference-by-content hypothesis, emphasizes that music containing verbal material (lyrics) can create direct interference with verbal material held in short-term memory through competition for phonological processing resources. Additionally, auditory scene analysis perspectives suggest that the perceptual segmentation of concurrent streams determines whether background music is integrated or segregated from task-relevant material, with implications for cognitive load and memory.

Beyond these process-level theories, individual difference factors are likely to further moderate music effects on memory. Variability across listeners in musical training, personality traits such as introversion or extraversion, habitual study habits, and preferences for background music all shape how music is experienced and how attention is allocated in the presence of music. For instance, individuals with more musical training may process music more deeply and thus be more susceptible to distraction by complex musical structure. Conversely, people who habitually study with music may have developed strategies to attenuate its disruptive effects. In this context, gender has sometimes been considered as an individual difference variable of interest. Investigators have explored whether males and females differ in susceptibility to auditory distraction or in the cognitive effects of music more generally. Hypotheses about gender differences have been grounded in both social-cognitive perspectives about different patterns of engagement with music and in biological perspectives about neurocognitive processing differences. Empirical work on gender differences in the cognitive effects of music has produced inconsistent evidence, with some reports of differential effects and others indicating no reliable gender-based moderation.

The mixed pattern of results across studies raises methodological concerns that complicate interpretation. Many studies vary in the type of music used, including whether music has lyrics or is instrumental, in tempo and loudness, and in familiarity to participants. Task characteristics also vary, for example whether memory is assessed via free recall, serial recall, recognition, or working memory span tasks. The memory load imposed by the task—as determined by the number of items to be remembered and the retention interval—can influence susceptibility to interference; tasks that are very easy or extremely difficult may yield floor or ceiling effects that mask subtle influences of music. The design of studies varies as well, with some employing between-subjects manipulations and others using within-subjects or mixed designs; the latter allow for better control of individual differences but can introduce order effects. Finally, sample sizes and statistical power vary substantially across studies, which affects the likelihood of detecting true effects and the vulnerability to Type I errors.

Given these considerations, there is a need for studies that carefully manipulate music presence while controlling for potential confounds and that explicitly test for gender differences. Short-term verbal memory tasks that use neutral word lists and brief retention intervals are a straightforward way to probe basic encoding and maintenance processes. A within-subjects manipulation of background music presence allows each participant to serve as their own control, thereby reducing variance due to individual differences. Including gender as a between-subjects factor enables an explicit test of whether males and females respond differently to the presence of music under matched task conditions. Moreover, using instrumental music without lyrics reduces the likelihood of direct phonological interference with verbal stimuli, isolating attentional and arousal-based mechanisms.

The present study was designed to address these issues. The primary objective was to determine whether background music affects performance on a short-term verbal memory task and whether any such effect is moderated by participant gender. To accomplish this, we recruited a sample of adult participants and had them complete a 20-word memory task on two occasions: once while background instrumental music was played and once in silence. The order of the music and silence conditions was counterbalanced across participants to control for practice and order effects. The study used short encoding and recall intervals in order to focus on short-term memory processes specifically. Each encoding period lasted 30 seconds, during which participants studied a list of 20 common, neutral words presented visually. After a one-minute filled retention interval, participants were given 30 seconds to recall as many words as they could. The one-minute interval was selected to reduce reliance on rehearsal over longer delays while remaining within the short-term memory timescale. The mixed design allowed for examination of within-subject effects of music presence and between-subjects effects of gender, as well as an interaction between these factors.

Several competing hypotheses were considered. One hypothesis, grounded in the arousal/mood framework, predicted that moderate background music might improve encoding and thus increase the number of words recalled compared to silence. A competing hypothesis, based on attentional distraction and interference accounts, predicted that the presence of music would reduce recall because auditory background would compete for attentional resources needed for encoding and rehearsal. A third possibility was that there would be no reliable main effect of music at the group level, and that any influence would be evident only within particular subgroups or under particular task parameters. Regarding gender, one line of thinking suggested that females and males might differ in their susceptibility to auditory distraction, perhaps due to differing patterns of attention or differences in habituation to background music, leading to either enhancement or impairment in one gender relative to the other. An alternative possibility was that gender would not moderate the effect; this outcome would suggest that the basic cognitive processes engaged by the task are similarly affected by background music across genders.

The study also aimed to explore secondary questions. We examined whether the order of conditions (music first vs. silence first) influenced performance, because practice or fatigue could interact with music presence. We assessed whether there was a primacy or recency pattern across serial positions in recall and whether music presence altered any serial position effects. We also considered whether individual differences in self-reported habitual use of music during study and in subjective enjoyment of the music used in the experiment related to the size of any music effect. Although the sample size limited the power to test complex interactions involving these covariates, exploratory analyses could suggest directions for subsequent research.

In designing the study, several methodological controls were implemented. Instrumental music was selected to reduce direct phonological interference, and its volume was standardized to a moderate level across participants. Word lists were constructed to be neutral in valence and comparable in average familiarity, length, and imageability in order to avoid systematic list effects. Word lists were counterbalanced across conditions to preclude confounding effects of list difficulty with music presence. Participants were naive to the specific hypotheses to minimize demand characteristics. The within-subjects nature of the music manipulation controlled for many stable individual differences. Nevertheless, it remained possible that uncontrolled factors, such as prior exposure to the specific music or transient mood, could contribute variability to performance.

Given the mixed prior literature and the controls implemented in the present design, the study set out to provide a rigorous test of whether background instrumental music influences short-term verbal memory and whether gender moderates that effect. It was anticipated that the study might find either a facilitative effect, an inhibitory effect, or no effect, with the recognition that the chosen music type and task parameters might bias toward reduced interference because instrumental music without lyrics has been less likely to produce phonological competition. The central hypothesis tested was therefore whether the number of words recalled would differ systematically between music and silence conditions and whether there would be an interaction with gender. The present work also aimed to contribute to the literature by clarifying whether previously reported gender differences might be attributable to methodological factors rather than robust cognitive differences.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from the surrounding community through advertisement. A total of 60 participants completed the study. The sample comprised 30 males and 30 females, and gender was self-reported. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 35 years, with an average age of approximately 22 years. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no diagnosed hearing impairments. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were debriefed after completing the tasks. The study protocol was approved by an institutional review body and was conducted in accordance with standard ethical guidelines for human subjects research.

Participants were compensated with course credit or a small monetary payment. Exclusion criteria included a history of neurological disorders or current use of medications known to affect cognition. Individuals with extensive formal musical training (defined for the purposes of recruitment as more than ten years of formal lessons) were not excluded, but musical training was assessed and recorded as a potential covariate in exploratory analyses. Habitual study-with-music behavior was assessed via a brief questionnaire item asking participants to indicate how often they typically listened to music while studying, on a four-point scale from never to frequently. Participants also reported whether they were familiar with the music used in the study.

Materials included two lists of 20 common English words each. Words were selected to be monosyllabic or bisyllabic, neutral in emotional valence, and comparable in frequency and concreteness. Lists were matched for average word length and perceptual familiarity. One list was used in the music condition and the other in the silence condition, with assignment of list to condition counterbalanced across participants to control for potential list effects. Word lists were presented visually on a computer screen, with all 20 words visible during the 30-second study period to avoid differences in presentation rate.

Background music consisted of instrumental music without lyrics, chosen for its moderate tempo and lack of abrupt dynamic changes. The piece was selected because it was generally perceived as neutral in valence and non-intrusive in pilot testing. Music was presented via headphones at a standardized moderate volume level calibrated to approximately 60 decibels equivalent; headphones were used to ensure consistent auditory presentation and to minimize ambient noise. During the silent condition, participants wore the same headphones but no music was played to preserve consistent equipment and comfort conditions and to minimize any expectancy effects based on wearing headphones.

The experiment used a mixed design with a within-subjects factor of background condition (music vs. silence) and a between-subjects factor of gender (male vs. female). The order of the two background conditions was counterbalanced across participants such that half the participants received the music condition first and the other half received the silence condition first. Study sessions were conducted individually in a quiet testing room to reduce external distraction.

The procedure began with informed consent, demographic and questionnaire items about musical habits, and instructions. Participants were told they would study lists of words and later be asked to recall as many words as possible. They were not informed of the specific predictions regarding music. Each participant completed two study-test cycles, one in the music condition and one in the silence condition. In each cycle, the relevant 20-word list was displayed in large font on the computer screen for 30 seconds, during which participants were instructed to study the words for later recall. After the 30-second study period, there was a one-minute filled retention interval during which participants completed a simple distractor task involving identifying whether visually presented single-digit numbers were odd or even; the distractor task was used to prevent active rehearsal and to approximate typical short retention intervals used in short-term memory paradigms. After the one-minute interval, participants were given 30 seconds to type, in any order, as many words as they could recall from the studied list. Responses were typed into the computer to provide an accurate record of recall and response timing. The same general procedure was repeated for the second condition. At the end of the session participants completed a short questionnaire indicating how much they enjoyed the music used in the experiment on a 5-point scale and whether they were familiar with it prior to the study.

Data scoring involved counting the number of correct words recalled for each trial, with correct responses defined as exact matches to list words. Minor spelling errors that did not alter the identity of the word were accepted as correct. Intrusions (words not on the studied list) were recorded but did not contribute to the correct score. Serial position of recalled items was retained in the data to permit analysis of primacy and recency effects, and response latencies were available from the typed responses.

Statistical analyses were conducted using standard software. The primary dependent variable was the number of correctly recalled words out of 20 in each condition for each participant. The primary analysis was a mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) with within-subjects factor Background (music vs. silence) and between-subjects factor Gender (male vs. female). Prior to analysis, data were examined for normality, outliers, and homogeneity of variances; assumptions for ANOVA were evaluated and met to a reasonable degree, and Greenhouse-Geisser corrections would be applied if sphericity were violated in any multi-level within-subjects factors, although the primary within-subjects factor had only two levels. Complementary paired-samples t-tests compared mean recall in the music and silence conditions across all participants. Effect sizes for key contrasts were reported using Cohen’s d for t-tests and partial eta-squared for ANOVA effects. Exploratory analyses tested for order effects by including order as an additional between-subjects factor and by testing for correlations between the magnitude of the music effect and self-reported habitual study-with-music and music enjoyment. Serial position curves were graphed and analyzed to assess any interaction between background condition and serial position effects.

A post hoc sensitivity analysis was performed to estimate the minimum effect size detectable with the given sample size, alpha level, and power. Power considerations and limitations were included in interpretation. All tests used a two-tailed alpha level of .05 for significance. Data cleaning and analyses were documented and preserved to ensure reproducibility.

Results

Descriptive statistics for recall performance in the two background conditions are presented first. Across the full sample of 60 participants, the mean number of words correctly recalled in the silence condition was 10.62 with a standard deviation of 2.87. In the music condition, the mean number of words recalled was 10.18 with a standard deviation of 2.78. Thus, raw means suggested a slight numerical advantage for the silence condition, but the magnitude of this difference was small. When broken down by gender, male participants (n = 30) recalled on average 10.40 words in the silence condition (SD = 2.95) and 10.02 words in the music condition (SD = 2.86). Female participants (n = 30) recalled on average 10.84 words in the silence condition (SD = 2.81) and 10.34 words in the music condition (SD = 2.72). These descriptive patterns indicated slightly higher recall in silence for both genders, and females had slightly higher average recall than males in both conditions, but the differences were modest.

The primary mixed-design ANOVA tested for a main effect of Background (music vs. silence), a main effect of Gender (male vs. female), and the Background × Gender interaction. The ANOVA revealed no significant main effect of Background, F(1,58) = 1.53, p = .221, partial eta-squared = .026. This indicates that overall recall did not differ reliably between music and silence conditions in the sample. The main effect of Gender was also not significant, F(1,58) = 0.58, p = .449, partial eta-squared = .010, indicating no reliable difference in recall between males and females. The interaction between Background and Gender was not significant, F(1,58) = 0.20, p = .655, partial eta-squared = .003, showing that the small numerical pattern of slightly greater silence-related advantage for females was not statistically reliable.

Complementary paired-samples t-tests were conducted to compare recall between the two conditions across all participants. The paired t-test comparing the number of words recalled in silence versus music across the full sample was not significant, t(59) = 1.20, p = .234, Cohen’s d = 0.155 (95% CI for mean difference = -0.19 to 0.98). The mean difference (silence minus music) was 0.44 words, with a standard deviation of the difference scores of 2.92. Separate paired t-tests conducted within each gender group also did not yield significant effects. For males, the difference between silence and music recall was not significant, t(29) = 1.00, p = .326, Cohen’s d = 0.183. For females, the difference was not significant, t(29) = 0.98, p = .334, Cohen’s d = 0.179.

An analysis testing for an order effect examined whether performance differed depending on whether the music condition occurred first or second. A two-way ANOVA including Order (music-first vs. silence-first) and Background revealed no significant Order × Background interaction, F(1,58) = 0.12, p = .729, suggesting that counterbalancing effectively controlled for practice or fatigue effects. In addition, the main effect of Order was not significant, F(1,58) = 0.37, p = .545. These analyses indicate that the order in which participants experienced the music and silence conditions did not systematically alter the pattern of results.

Exploratory analyses examined whether habitual study-with-music frequency or subjective enjoyment of the experimental music were associated with the size of the music effect (defined as recall in music minus recall in silence). Pearson correlations showed no significant association between study-with-music frequency and the music effect, r = 0.08, p = .532. Similarly, enjoyment ratings of the experimental music were not significantly correlated with the music effect, r = 0.10, p = .438. Familiarity with the music was not common in the sample; only a small minority reported prior familiarity, and those participants did not differ significantly in the magnitude of the music effect.

Serial position analyses were performed to evaluate whether primacy and recency patterns were present and whether they were influenced by background condition. As is typical in short-term recall tasks, a primacy effect was evident, with items from early positions recalled at higher rates than middle items, and a modest recency effect was also observed. The shape of the serial position curves was similar across music and silent conditions, and a repeated-measures ANOVA with factors Position (grouped into early, middle, late) and Background yielded a significant main effect of Position, F(2,116) = 48.12, p < .001, indicating differential recall across serial positions, but no Position × Background interaction, F(2,116) = 0.92, p = .401. This suggests that the presence of background music did not differentially affect encoding or retrieval across serial positions.

Examination of intrusion errors showed few instances of non-list words being produced, with no reliable difference between music and silence conditions. The mean number of intrusion errors per participant was low in both conditions (less than one per trial on average), and a paired t-test comparing intrusion counts between conditions was not significant, t(59) = 0.45, p = .655.

A post hoc sensitivity analysis using the achieved sample size indicated that, with 60 participants and an alpha of .05, the study had 80% power to detect a within-subject standardized effect size (Cohen’s d) of approximately 0.36 for the music versus silence contrast. The observed paired-sample effect size of d = 0.155 for the overall comparison falls well below this threshold, suggesting that if a true effect exists, it is likely to be small in magnitude given the present task parameters.

In summary, the analyses did not support the hypotheses that background instrumental music would either enhance or impair short-term verbal recall at a group level, nor did they support the hypothesis that gender would moderate the influence of background music under the conditions tested. Descriptive statistics suggested numerically small advantages for silence, but these differences were not statistically significant. Order effects, enjoyment, familiarity, and habitual study-with-music were not significantly associated with the magnitude of any music-related change.

Discussion

The present study examined whether the presence of background instrumental music affected short-term verbal recall and whether any such effect differed between male and female participants. Using a controlled mixed design, sixty participants completed two rounds of a 20-word recall task, once with background instrumental music and once in silence. Our principal result was the absence of statistically significant effects: there was no reliable difference in the number of words recalled between the music and silence conditions, no reliable difference in recall between genders, and no reliable Background × Gender interaction. Secondary analyses indicated no order effects, no association between habitual study-with-music behavior or enjoyment and the music effect, and no modulation of serial position effects by background music. These findings contribute to the literature by providing a well-controlled test under specific task and stimulus parameters that failed to find evidence for substantive music-related modulation of short-term verbal recall.

Interpreting a null effect requires careful consideration of whether the absence of an effect reflects a true lack of influence under the tested conditions or whether methodological or statistical factors masked a genuine influence. One relevant consideration is the nature of the music used. Instrumental music without lyrics was chosen deliberately to minimize direct phonological interference with verbal material. Many studies that have reported deleterious effects of music on verbal memory have used music with lyrics or highly salient melodic content that can engage verbal processing systems. By selecting instrumental music, the study may have reduced one key pathway by which music can disrupt verbal encoding. Consequently, the absence of an effect in this study is consistent with the hypothesis that music interferes with short-term verbal recall primarily when it contains verbal content or is otherwise highly linguistically engaging.

Task characteristics also likely influence susceptibility to music-related effects. The present task required encoding of 20 words during a 30-second study period with a one-minute filled delay and a 30-second recall period. This design places substantial demands on short-term memory but also provides opportunities for rehearsal during the study period because all words were visible during encoding. Allowing participants to view all words concurrently, rather than presenting them sequentially, reduces reliance on temporal order encoding and may favor strategies that are less vulnerable to auditory distraction. In tasks where items are presented serially and require maintenance over brief delays without the ability to re-view items, auditory background might have a larger disruptive effect. Thus, the specific presentation format used here likely contributed to the small magnitude of the observed music effect.

An additional point concerns the sample and statistical power. While the sample size of 60 provided reasonable power to detect small-to-moderate effects, the observed effect size was very small (Cohen’s d = 0.155) and the post hoc sensitivity analysis indicated limited power to detect effects of this magnitude. It is possible that background music has a very small effect on short-term verbal recall under these conditions that would require a substantially larger sample to detect with confidence. However, the practical significance of such a small effect would be limited even if statistically significant in a larger study. The absence of effects for gender also suggests that, at least for the current task and music type, males and females are similarly affected by (or resilient to) background music in terms of short-term recall performance.

Another consideration is individual differences that might moderate the effect of background music. Although we measured self-reported habitual study-with-music and enjoyment of the experimental music, neither variable was significantly associated with the size of the music effect. However, these self-report measures were coarse and may not capture the nuanced ways in which individual differences like musical training, attentional styles, working memory capacity, personality (e.g., introversion-extraversion), and noise sensitivity interact with background music. Prior research suggests that some of these individual differences can influence whether music helps or hinders cognitive performance. In particular, individuals with high working memory capacity may be more resistant to auditory distraction. Future work that measures these constructs with validated instruments and includes sufficient sample sizes to probe interactions would be valuable.

The lack of modulation of serial position effects by background music is also informative. The persistence of typical primacy and recency patterns across music and silence conditions suggests that music did not differentially impair encoding of early items or retrieval of late items. This pattern is consistent with the idea that instrumental music at moderate volume did not produce pervasive attentional capture or interference with rehearsal strategies. It is also consistent with the possibility that participants adopted compensatory strategies in the presence of music that preserved their encoding processes, such as increasing visual-focused rehearsal or intentionally ignoring auditory input.

Comparing the present findings to the broader literature highlights the role of methodological heterogeneity in producing mixed results. Studies that report music-related impairments often use lyric-laden or highly arousing music, louder presentation levels, or complex tasks that require continuous phonological processing. Conversely, studies that report no effect or facilitation sometimes use instrumental music, low volume, or tasks that benefit from elevated arousal. The current findings align with a view that background music does not inevitably harm short-term verbal memory and that specific features of the music and task critically determine the outcome. Researchers and practitioners should therefore avoid definitive prescriptions that background music will always impair or enhance learning and memory; instead, the likely influence depends on context.

The practical implications of the current findings suggest that listening to moderate-volume instrumental background music may not substantially alter short-term recall of visually presented word lists for young adults under controlled conditions. This conclusion is relevant to students and others who use instrumental music while studying; it suggests that at least for tasks similar to those tested here, instrumental music is unlikely to cause large decrements in short-term verbal recall. Nevertheless, for tasks that involve listening to or producing verbal material, or when music contains lyrics, the risk of interference may be greater and caution is warranted.

The study has several limitations that point to directions for future research. First, the choice of a single piece of instrumental music and a single moderate volume level limits generalizability. Future studies should manipulate music characteristics systematically, including presence versus absence of lyrics, tempo, complexity, familiarity, valence, and volume. Experimental designs that cross these variables can delineate which musical features drive facilitation or interference. Second, the current design used visually presented word lists with concurrent visible access during the study phase; sequential presentation paradigms and auditory presentation of target items should be included in future work to test modality-specific interference effects. Third, the sample consisted mainly of young adults, and effects may differ in children, older adults, or in populations with attentional or memory impairments. Fourth, individual differences were measured only coarsely; future research should include robust measures of working memory capacity, attentional control, noise sensitivity, personality, and detailed musical background to assess interactions. Fifth, the present study measured free recall of words; other memory measures such as serial recall, recognition memory, and complex working memory tasks could reveal different patterns of sensitivity to background music.

Methodological refinements could also improve sensitivity to detect meaningful effects. Increasing sample sizes would permit detection of smaller effects and allow reliable testing of interaction terms. Using within-subject manipulations across more than two music conditions could enhance power and clarify dose-response relationships. Incorporating objective physiological measures such as heart rate variability, skin conductance, or EEG could help link subjective arousal or attentional engagement to performance outcomes and illuminate mechanistic pathways. Neuroimaging approaches could further elucidate whether background music alters neural indices of encoding or maintenance in working memory networks.

A fruitful avenue for future research is to examine how situational context interacts with music effects. For instance, studying in environments with varying levels of ambient noise or cognitive load may reveal circumstances under which background music is beneficial because it masks disruptive noise or elevates arousal to an optimal level. Investigating real-world learning tasks that involve meaningful material and longer-term retention intervals would provide ecologically valid insights. Additionally, longitudinal work could assess whether habitual study-with-music practices produce adaptations over time that alter susceptibility to distraction.

Theoretically, the current results contribute to a nuanced understanding of auditory influences on memory. They support the view that music is not a monolithic modification to cognitive context but rather that its effects are contingent upon the interaction of stimulus features, task demands, and listener characteristics. The absence of a gender moderation effect in this study weakens simple accounts that posit broad gender differences in susceptibility to auditory distraction for short-term verbal memory. It suggests instead that any reported gender differences in prior work may derive from specific experimental features or sample idiosyncrasies rather than robust, generalizable cognitive differences. However, definitive conclusions about gender will require further targeted research that isolates potential mediating variables.

In conclusion, the present mixed-design study found no significant effect of moderate-volume instrumental background music on short-term verbal recall and no reliable gender differences in this effect under the specific task parameters used. These findings suggest that instrumental music of the kind studied here does not strongly modulate short-term verbal memory in young adults. Future research should systematically vary musical features, task modalities, participant characteristics, and measurement approaches to map the boundary conditions under which music affects memory. Such work will clarify the mechanisms of auditory distraction and arousal modulation, inform recommendations for study practices, and refine theoretical models of how background auditory environments interact with core cognitive processes.
