Introduction

Research into the psychological correlates of religious involvement has a long and varied history, encompassing a broad range of outcomes from mental health to social behavior. Over decades, scholars have documented associations between various aspects of religiosity and psychological well-being, with many studies reporting links between religious engagement and reduced depressive symptoms, greater life satisfaction, and enhanced coping with stress. At the same time, the literature is heterogeneous, and relationships between religion and psychological constructs are often complex, moderated by cultural context, measurement approaches, and individual differences. One psychological construct that has received comparatively less focused attention in relation to religious practice is dispositional optimism, a trait-like tendency to expect positive outcomes in the future. Optimism, typically measured through instruments such as the Life Orientation Test - Revised (LOT-R), has been associated with numerous positive outcomes including better physical health, enhanced coping, and greater resilience. Given the documented benefits of both religion and optimism for well-being, it is plausible to hypothesize that these constructs might be positively related. However, the nature of the relation between religious practice and optimism remains understudied and theoretically ambiguous.

A number of theoretical pathways suggest why religious practice might be associated with higher levels of optimism. First, many religious traditions promote narratives of meaning, hope, and a benevolent order to the world, which could foster positive expectancies about the future. Religious doctrine and ritual often emphasize themes of redemption, providence, or enduring purpose, and such themes may be internalized by adherents in ways that enhance generalized optimistic attitudes. Second, religious practice commonly includes social components such as participation in congregations, community support, and shared activities that may provide emotional resources and social capital. Social support has been repeatedly linked to higher optimism; hence, the social aspects of religion may indirectly bolster optimistic outlooks. Third, religious practice may provide coping frameworks and cognitive reappraisals that orient individuals toward constructive expectations in the face of adversity, for example by framing suffering as temporary or purposeful, which could generalize into a more optimistic stance.

Despite these plausible mechanisms, empirical findings linking religiosity and optimism are mixed. Some studies have reported positive associations, particularly when religiosity is conceptualized in terms of intrinsic religious commitment or spiritual well-being. For instance, research examining intrinsic religiosity—where religion is internalized as a guiding life orientation—has found correlations with hope and positive future expectancies in certain samples. Conversely, studies that emphasize extrinsic religiosity, or religious participation driven by social factors or external rewards, have yielded weaker or null associations with dispositional optimism. These divergent findings suggest that the particular dimension of religiosity under study is critical: private devotional practices, ritual frequency, personal prayer, and doctrinal belief may relate differently to optimism than formal attendance or affiliation.

Another factor that complicates interpretation of past findings is measurement heterogeneity. Studies vary in whether they use single-item measures of religious attendance, composite indices of practice, or multidimensional scales that separate belief, practice, and experience. Similarly, optimism has been operationalized in different ways, with some work focusing on domain-specific expectancies (e.g., health optimism) and other studies on generalized dispositional optimism measured by validated instruments like the LOT-R. Measurement choices influence effect sizes and the likelihood of detecting associations. For instance, the LOT-R, while widely used, is a brief scale that captures a relatively stable trait and may be more resistant to situational influences than state measures of hope or momentary positive expectations. If religious practice primarily affects more transient aspects of hope rather than enduring optimism, studies using the LOT-R might yield weaker associations.

Demographic and cultural moderators are also relevant. The psychological meaning and social consequences of religious practice vary across cultural contexts. In societies where religion is central to social life, regular religious practice may be more strongly linked to social support and normative life frameworks, which could in turn increase optimism. In more secularized contexts, religious practice may be less normative and associated with niche identity, producing different psychological correlates. Age and life stage can further moderate relations: older adults who draw on religious practice might derive comfort and optimism from faith traditions in ways distinct from younger people for whom religious practice may be less central. Gender differences have also been reported in both religiosity and optimism literature, and these could introduce additional complexity if men and women experience the psychological consequences of religious practice differently.

Beyond moderator variables, the direction of causality between religiosity and optimism is uncertain. A positive cross-sectional correlation between practice and optimism could reflect religious practice fostering optimism, or optimistic individuals being more attracted to or sustained in religious practices, or both being influenced by unmeasured third variables such as social support or personality traits. Longitudinal or experimental approaches are needed to disentangle these possibilities, but the bulk of existing work in non-clinical populations remains correlational. Thus, cross-sectional investigations can still provide valuable descriptive information and help identify patterns that warrant deeper study.

The literature also draws distinctions between different kinds of religiosity—behavioral, cognitive, and affective. Behavioral measures include observable practices such as attendance at services, frequency of prayer, and participation in religious events. Cognitive components include doctrinal beliefs and theological commitments. Affective dimensions encompass religious experiences, feelings of sacredness, and spiritual emotions like awe or gratitude. Each of these components may relate differently to optimism. For example, behavioral participation that yields social support might be associated with positive expectancies through social reinforcement, whereas doctrinal belief emphasizing a benevolent deity may foster generalized optimism through cognitive schemas. Given this complexity, studies that use unidimensional measures of religiosity may obscure important patterns.

Another strand of research relevant to the present study concerns the distinction between optimism and related constructs such as hope, gratitude, and meaning in life. While optimism refers to generalized expectancies about future outcomes, hope often entails goal-directed agency and pathways thinking, gratitude involves appreciation for past benefits, and meaning in life concerns perceptions of coherence and purpose. Religious practice may more strongly influence hope or meaning rather than dispositional optimism per se. Indeed, certain interventions based on religious or spiritual practices aim to enhance coping and meaning rather than altering a dispositional trait. Consequently, null associations between religious practice and the LOT-R might be compatible with positive links between religion and other future-oriented constructs.

Methodological considerations also play a crucial role in interpreting heterogenous findings. Sample size and statistical power determine the ability to detect small effects often observed in social psychological research. Studies with small samples may yield unstable estimates, while larger studies have greater sensitivity to detect modest associations. In addition, range restriction in measures of religiosity—if most participants have similar low or high levels of practice—can attenuate correlations. Self-report measures raise the potential for social desirability bias, especially for socially valued practices like religious attendance, which can distort associations. The context of recruitment, such as university samples versus community samples, introduces additional limitations to generalizability.

The present investigation was motivated by these theoretical, empirical, and methodological considerations. The central question addressed is whether the extent of religious practice is associated with dispositional optimism in a non-clinical adult sample. Based on prior findings that link religiosity with positive psychological states and resources, and on theoretical accounts that posit religion as a source of hope and future-oriented meaning, it was hypothesized that greater religious practice would be positively associated with higher levels of optimism. To test this hypothesis, a cross-sectional correlational design was employed using validated self-report measures: a composite questionnaire assessing frequency of religious practice and the Life Orientation Test - Revised to measure dispositional optimism.

The study sought not only to test the bivariate association but also to evaluate the robustness of any observed relation by examining internal reliabilities of measures, conducting sensitivity analyses with non-parametric statistics, and exploring potential demographic covariates that might confound or moderate the association. Given prior literature suggesting that intrinsic versus extrinsic religious orientations may differentially relate to psychological outcomes, the religious practice measure was constructed to emphasize behavioral aspects while acknowledging its limitations in capturing intrinsic belief. The aim was to provide an empirically clear test of whether routine religious practices, as commonly operationalized in survey research, co-vary with generalized optimistic outlooks.

In setting out this investigation, several competing possibilities were recognized. One possibility was that a meaningful positive correlation would emerge, consistent with theoretical expectations about religion as a source of hope and adaptive coping. Another possibility was that no significant association would be found, which could indicate that religious practice does not strongly influence dispositional optimism or that the relation is contingent on unmeasured variables such as intrinsic belief, spirituality, or cultural context. A final possibility was that a negative association could appear in specific subgroups if, for example, greater religious practice were associated with a worldview emphasizing sin or suffering, which might undermine optimistic expectations. The present study was designed to detect at least moderate-sized associations and to assess the evidence for the hypothesized positive relation.

Overall, the study contributes to ongoing debates about the psychological correlates of religiosity by focusing specifically on dispositional optimism and by applying multiple analytic approaches to ascertain the presence or absence of a correlation. The results are intended to inform theoretical models of how religious practice relates to psychological resources, to highlight methodological issues pertinent to this line of research, and to suggest pathways for future investigations that can better delineate when, how, and for whom religion is associated with optimism.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a combination of university subject pools and community advertisements over a three-month period. In total, 128 individuals initiated the study, and after data screening 118 participants provided complete and valid responses that were included in the analyses. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years of age and to be fluent in English. Exclusion criteria included failure of attention checks embedded in the survey and incomplete responses on key measures. The final sample comprised 118 adults with a mean age of 29.4 years (SD = 10.7), ranging from 18 to 65 years. Gender distribution in the sample was 70 female, 46 male, and 2 identifying as non-binary or other. Participants reported a variety of religious affiliations: 48 identified as Christian, 12 as Muslim, 8 as Hindu, 10 as Buddhist, 30 as having no religious affiliation, and 10 as affiliating with other or multiple religious identities. Educational attainment varied, with most participants reporting at least some post-secondary education.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the relevant institutional review board prior to data collection. All participants provided informed consent electronically before beginning the survey. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary, that they could withdraw at any time without penalty, and that responses would be anonymized and used for research purposes only. Participants recruited via the university subject pool received course credit for participation, while community participants received a modest monetary compensation or a small voucher equivalent.

The study employed an online questionnaire delivered through a secure survey platform. The survey included demographic questions, the religious practice questionnaire developed for the study, and the Life Orientation Test - Revised (LOT-R). The order of the religious practice questionnaire and the LOT-R was randomized across participants to minimize potential order effects. The religious practice questionnaire was designed to capture behavioral elements of religiosity, focusing on frequency and recency of religious activities. It consisted of eight items assessing: frequency of attendance at religious services, frequency of private prayer or meditation, frequency of reading religious texts, frequency of participation in religious community events, frequency of religious volunteering or charity work, frequency of religious observance of holidays, frequency of involvement in religious study groups, and frequency of engagement in religious rituals at home. Response options for each item ranged on a 6-point Likert-type scale from 0 (never) to 5 (daily), and items were summed to create a total religious practice score with theoretically possible values from 0 to 40.

The Life Orientation Test - Revised (LOT-R) was used to assess dispositional optimism. The LOT-R comprises ten items, of which six are scored to create the composite optimism score (three positively worded items and three negatively worded items that are reverse scored), and four are filler items. Items are rated on a 5-point scale from 0 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), producing a composite optimism score range of 0 to 24. Higher scores reflect greater dispositional optimism. The LOT-R is widely used and provides a concise estimate of generalized optimistic expectations. In the present study, internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and reported below.

Prior to analysis, data were screened for outliers, missing values, and response patterns indicative of non-engagement. Ten participants who failed embedded attention checks or provided incomplete data on key measures were excluded, leaving the final analytic sample of 118. Descriptive statistics were computed for demographic variables and for the primary measures. The distribution of the religious practice score and the LOT-R score were examined for normality using visual inspection of histograms and quantile-quantile plots, as well as through Shapiro-Wilk tests. Where appropriate, transformations were considered, although the primary analyses were conducted on raw scores given approximate normality.

Reliability analyses were conducted for the religious practice questionnaire and for the LOT-R. Cronbach’s alpha was computed to assess internal consistency for the multi-item scales. Given the composite nature of the religious practice construct in this study, internal reliability provided an index of the coherence of the behavioral practice items. For the LOT-R, alpha provides an indication of the scale’s reliability in the present sample and whether it performed comparably to prior research.

The primary analytic approach was to examine the bivariate association between total religious practice score and LOT-R optimism score. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was selected as the primary statistic given the continuous nature of the variables and their approximately normal distributions. To complement the Pearson correlation and to check the robustness of findings to non-normality or outliers, Spearman’s rank-order correlation was also computed. Statistical significance was evaluated using a two-tailed criterion at the alpha = .05 level. Effect sizes were interpreted according to conventional guidelines where small effects approximate r = .10 to .20, medium around r = .30, and large above r = .50.

Additional analyses were performed to probe the robustness of any observed association. A partial correlation was computed controlling for age and gender to examine whether demographic variables confounded the bivariate relation. Hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted with optimism as the dependent variable and religious practice entered as the focal predictor. In the regression models, demographic covariates including age, gender, and self-reported religious affiliation (dummy coded) were entered in an initial step, followed by religious practice in a subsequent step to evaluate incremental variance explained. Effect sizes for regression coefficients were reported using standardized betas.

To further assess evidence for the null hypothesis in the case of non-significant results, a Bayesian correlation analysis was conducted to estimate the Bayes factor comparing the null model (no correlation) to the alternative model (non-zero correlation). Bayesian analyses can quantify the degree to which the data support the null hypothesis relative to the alternative, offering a complement to frequentist significance testing. In addition, median split analyses were used to create high and low religious practice groups for sample descriptive comparisons and independent samples t-tests were conducted to test for group differences in LOT-R scores. Cohen’s d was computed as an effect size for these group comparisons.

A post-hoc sensitivity analysis for statistical power was carried out. Given the final sample size of 118, the minimum effect size detectable with 80% power at alpha = .05 for a two-tailed Pearson correlation was estimated. This sensitivity analysis provided context for interpreting null findings in terms of the smallest effect sizes that the study could reasonably be expected to detect.

All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Descriptive statistics, reliability analyses, correlations, t-tests, and regressions were computed using conventional packages, and Bayesian analyses were performed using a Bayesian module that computes Bayes factors for correlation tests. Results are reported with appropriate test statistics, degrees of freedom where applicable, p-values, confidence intervals for effect sizes, and Bayesian factors to provide a comprehensive account of the evidence.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary measures indicated a range of responses on both the religious practice questionnaire and the LOT-R. The mean total religious practice score in the final sample of 118 participants was 11.7 (SD = 9.6), with observed values spanning the full possible range from 0 to 40, though the distribution was positively skewed reflecting a greater number of low-frequency practitioners. The median religious practice score was 9, indicating that half the sample reported engagement in religious practices at or below this level. For the LOT-R, the mean optimism score was 14.8 (SD = 4.2) with observed scores ranging from 3 to 23, reflecting variability in dispositional optimism across participants. Examination of histograms and quantile-quantile plots suggested approximate normality for the LOT-R distribution and moderate positive skew for the religious practice distribution.

Internal reliability analyses indicated acceptable consistency for both measures in this sample. Cronbach’s alpha for the religious practice questionnaire was .84, suggesting that the behavioral items formed a coherent composite index of practice frequency. For the LOT-R, Cronbach’s alpha was .76, consistent with typical values reported in the literature and indicating acceptable internal consistency for the measure of dispositional optimism in the present sample. These reliability estimates supported the use of the composite scores in subsequent correlational analyses.

Initial tests of normality using Shapiro-Wilk statistics confirmed that the LOT-R scores did not deviate significantly from normality (W = .98, p = .12), but the religious practice scores showed a significant departure from normality (W = .92, p < .01), consistent with the visual evidence of positive skew. Given this pattern, the main analyses employed Pearson’s correlation as the primary test because of the reasonable approximation to normality for the dependent variable and the continuous nature of both measures; however, Spearman’s rank-order correlations were also computed as robustness checks.

The Pearson correlation between total religious practice score and LOT-R optimism score was r = .07 (95% CI: -0.10 to 0.24), which was not statistically significant, t(116) = 0.77, p = .44. The effect size was small and close to zero, indicating that only a negligible proportion of variance in optimism was accounted for by reported frequency of religious practice. The Spearman rho corroborated this finding, rho = .06, p = .52, suggesting that the lack of association was not an artifact of the mild skew in religious practice scores.

To assess whether demographic variables could obscure a bivariate relationship, partial correlations controlling for age and gender were computed. The partial correlation between religious practice and optimism controlling for age and gender was r_partial = .06, p = .49, again non-significant and similar in magnitude to the zero-order correlation. Hierarchical regression analyses provided further evidence on the absence of a meaningful association: in a regression predicting LOT-R scores, age and gender entered in the first step accounted for a negligible amount of variance, R^2 = .02, F(2, 115) = 1.12, p = .33. Adding religious practice in the second step increased R^2 by only .004, Delta F(1, 114) = 0.56, p = .46, with a standardized beta for religious practice of .07 (p = .46). The lack of significant incremental variance explained by religious practice indicated that it did not meaningfully predict optimism over and above basic demographics.

A median split was conducted to explore whether categorical differences between high and low religious practice groups might reveal patterns not evident in a continuous analysis. Participants were divided into low practice (scores <= 9, n = 60) and high practice (scores > 9, n = 58) groups. Independent samples t-test comparing LOT-R means across these groups showed no significant difference: mean LOT-R for the low practice group was 14.6 (SD = 4.3) and for the high practice group was 15.0 (SD = 4.1), t(116) = 0.44, p = .66. The observed Cohen’s d was 0.09 (95% CI: -0.24 to 0.42), indicating a negligible effect size.

Given the theoretical possibility that different religious affiliations might display distinct patterns, subgroup analyses were performed to inspect correlations within major affiliation categories. For participants identifying as Christian (n = 48), the correlation between religious practice and LOT-R was r = .08, p = .59. For Muslim participants (n = 12), r = .12, p = .72; for Hindu participants (n = 8), r = -.18, p = .69; for Buddhist participants (n = 10), r = .01, p = .98. None of these subgroup correlations reached significance, and the small sample sizes for non-Christian groups limited statistical power and interpretability. Participants reporting no religious affiliation (n = 30) by definition had very low practice scores, and among this subgroup the correlation was not applicable in the same manner.

To quantify the evidence for the null hypothesis more directly, a Bayesian correlation test was conducted. The Bayes factor comparing the null model (no correlation) to an unconstrained alternative model yielded BF01 = 4.12, indicating that the data were approximately four times more likely under the null hypothesis than under the alternative. This represents moderate evidence in favor of the absence of a correlation between religious practice and dispositional optimism in the present sample.

Because the religious practice measure contained multiple distinct behavioral items, secondary exploratory analyses examined correlations between individual items and LOT-R scores. None of the eight items showed significant associations after correcting for multiple comparisons. For example, frequency of private prayer correlated with LOT-R at r = .09 (p = .33) and frequency of attendance at religious services correlated at r = .05 (p = .59). These item-level analyses suggested that no specific behavioral component of practice was strongly linked to dispositional optimism in this sample.

A post-hoc sensitivity analysis regarding statistical power indicated that with N = 118, the study had 80% power to detect a correlation of approximately r = .25 at alpha = .05, two-tailed. The observed correlation of r = .07 was therefore below the threshold for detection of small effects and would not be estimated reliably in smaller samples. This sensitivity analysis contextualized the null result: while the study was reasonably powered to detect medium-sized effects, it was underpowered to detect small associations that might nonetheless be theoretically meaningful.

Checks for potential response biases were conducted by examining relationships between social desirability tendencies and the primary measures in a subsample where a brief social desirability index was included (n = 72). Social desirability correlated modestly with religious practice (r = .16, p = .18) but not significantly with LOT-R (r = .05, p = .69), and controlling for social desirability did not materially alter the observed correlation between practice and optimism.

Finally, exploratory analyses examined whether age moderated the association between religious practice and optimism by testing an interaction term in regression models. The interaction between religious practice and age was not significant (beta = .03, p = .72), indicating no evidence that the relationship varied with age. Similarly, tests for gender moderation using interaction terms did not reveal significant gender differences in the association (religious practice x gender interaction beta = -.04, p = .64).

Across these multiple analytic approaches—bivariate correlations, non-parametric correlations, partial correlations controlling for demographics, regression models, subgroup analyses, and Bayesian testing—the pattern was consistent: there was no compelling evidence of a positive association between frequency of religious practice and dispositional optimism in this sample. The evidence, including the Bayes factor, suggested that if an association exists, it is likely small in magnitude and not detectable with the current design and sample.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether the frequency of religious practice is associated with dispositional optimism in a non-clinical adult sample. Contrary to the initial hypothesis, which was grounded in theoretical accounts of religion as a source of hope and positive expectancies, the data provided no evidence of a meaningful positive association between the two constructs. Across a range of analyses including Pearson and Spearman correlations, partial correlations controlling for demographic covariates, hierarchical regression, median-split group comparisons, and subgroup analyses by affiliation, religious practice was not significantly related to LOT-R scores. Moreover, Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence favoring the null hypothesis over the presence of a correlation. These findings have several implications for theory and future research on the psychological correlates of religiosity.

One important implication is that behavioral measures of religious practice may not map onto dispositional optimism as measured by the LOT-R. The religious practice instrument used in this study emphasized frequency of observable behaviors—attendance, prayer, reading texts, ritual observance—rather than capturing internalized belief systems, spiritual experiences, or the subjective meaning derived from religious engagement. If religion promotes optimism primarily through internalized doctrinal belief, existential meaning, or spiritual experiences rather than through frequency of behaviors per se, then a behavioral index could fail to detect an important association. This distinction aligns with past literature that differentiates intrinsic and extrinsic religiosity; intrinsic religiosity, representing internalized faith, has often been more predictive of well-being-related constructs than extrinsic behavioral indices.

Relatedly, optimism as operationalized by the LOT-R is conceptualized as a relatively stable dispositional trait. Traits are by definition enduring and may be less sensitive to the influence of current behavioral practices. It is plausible that religious practice is more closely linked with state-based constructs such as momentary hope, situational expectancies, or affective well-being that can fluctuate with context and engagement. Therefore, the absence of a relation with dispositional optimism does not preclude religion influencing other future-oriented constructs. Studies that have documented links between religiosity and hope or meaning in life may be capturing effects that are distinct from the trait-level optimism assessed here.

Cultural and sample-specific factors may also help explain the null result. The sample in the present study was drawn primarily from a university-adjacent population and local community, including both religiously affiliated and unaffiliated individuals. In contexts where religious practice is not normative or where religious engagement is heterogeneous in motivation and quality, behavioral frequency may not confer the social and cognitive benefits that promote optimism. In highly religious communities, frequent religious practice might be tightly integrated with social support and coherent worldviews that foster optimistic expectations, whereas in more secular contexts, the same behaviors may not produce equivalent psychological effects. Thus, cultural moderators deserve attention in future work.

The study’s findings also raise questions about potential mechanisms linking religion and optimism posited in prior theoretical accounts. While religion can provide narratives of meaning, social support, and coping frameworks that theoretically promote optimistic outlooks, the current evidence suggests that these mechanisms may not manifest as broad changes in dispositional optimism measurable by the LOT-R. It is possible that the strengthening of immediate coping resources or enhancement of perceived social support occurs without altering generalized future expectancies. Alternatively, mechanisms like meaning and social support might operate in specific life domains (e.g., health-related optimism, relationship optimism) rather than producing a diffuse uplift in generalized optimism.

The methodological rigor of the study strengthens confidence in the observed null effect, but also highlights important limitations that contextualize interpretation. The religious practice measure demonstrated good internal consistency, and the LOT-R functioned reliably in the sample. The sample size of 118 provided adequate power to detect medium-sized effects but limited sensitivity to detect small effects. The post-hoc sensitivity analysis indicated that effects smaller than approximately r = .25 would likely be undetectable with 80% power given the present sample size. Thus, the possibility remains that very small associations exist between religious practice and dispositional optimism; such associations may be theoretically meaningful in larger populations but were beyond the detection threshold of the current design.

Another limitation concerns the cross-sectional and correlational nature of the study. Causal inferences cannot be made from these data. If a longitudinal relation exists—such that sustained religious practice over time fosters incremental increases in optimism—the present cross-sectional snapshot would be less likely to detect such gradual changes. Experimental manipulations of religiously relevant practices (e.g., structured prayer, communal ritual participation) and longitudinal designs tracking changes in both practice and optimism over time would provide stronger causal evidence.

Measurement limitations deserve emphasis as well. The religious practice questionnaire, while internally consistent, captured frequency rather than quality or meaning of practice. Frequency alone is an imperfect index of engagement; two individuals who both report weekly attendance may differ dramatically in the extent to which that attendance is experienced as meaningful, transformative, or socially supportive. Incorporating measures of intrinsic versus extrinsic orientation, spiritual well-being, religious coping styles, and subjective religious experience would provide a more nuanced understanding of how different facets of religiosity relate to optimism. Additionally, social desirability and self-report biases might differentially influence reports of religious practice and optimism; although analyses in a subsample suggested only modest influence of social desirability, future studies could benefit from multi-method approaches including behavioral observation or informant reports.

The heterogeneity of religious traditions represented in the sample complicates interpretation. Different traditions and denominations emphasize distinct theological positions regarding hope, suffering, and expectations for the future. For instance, some traditions explicitly promote eschatological hope or a benevolent deity overseeing life trajectories, while others emphasize acceptance of suffering without promising future rewards. Aggregating across such diverse traditions may mask tradition-specific associations. Future research with larger samples that allow for adequately powered subgroup analyses of major faith traditions could illuminate whether particular doctrinal emphases correlate more strongly with optimism.

In terms of theoretical contribution, the present findings suggest a need to refine models linking religiosity and well-being to account for specificity in both predictors and outcomes. The common tendency to treat religiosity as a unitary predictor of multiple positive psychological outcomes may obscure which aspects of religious life relate to which psychological constructs. The distinction between behavioral practice, belief, experience, and the social embeddedness of religious life must be carefully articulated in theoretical models. Moreover, the array of outcome constructs—optimism, hope, life satisfaction, depression, meaning—should not be assumed interchangeable; each may have distinct antecedents and dynamics. The lack of association observed here supports a more granular approach in both measurement and theorizing.

The Bayesian evidence favoring the null model is an important finding that contributes to cumulative knowledge by quantifying support for the absence of a correlation in this sample. Null results are informative when they prompt reconsideration of theoretical assumptions and clarify the boundaries of effects observed in prior literature. In this case, the null result suggests that religious practice frequency alone is insufficient as a predictor of dispositional optimism in a mixed, largely secularized sample. Such a conclusion narrows the scope of theoretical claims and directs future research to investigate moderating variables and alternative mechanisms.

Several concrete directions for future research follow from the present work. Longitudinal studies that track individuals’ religious practices and optimism over extended periods would clarify temporal dynamics and potential causal pathways. Experimental studies that manipulate elements of religious practice (e.g., increasing participation in communal rituals, structured private spiritual exercises) and assess subsequent changes in optimism and related constructs could provide causal evidence. Mixed-methods research incorporating qualitative interviews would be valuable for elucidating the subjective meaning of religious practice and how it might relate to future-oriented expectations. Incorporating broader assessment batteries that measure intrinsic/extrinsic religiosity, spiritual experiences, social support, coping strategies, meaning in life, and domain-specific optimism would enable comprehensive tests of competing mechanisms.

Further, research in more religiously homogeneous or more highly religious cultural contexts is warranted to test whether the relation between practice and optimism is context dependent. Comparative research across cultures with varying degrees of religiosity and differing theological emphases could reveal systematic patterns that are not detectable in mixed or predominantly secular samples. Additionally, research that examines potential moderators such as socioeconomic status, life stressors, and mental health history could clarify for whom religious practice might be most consequential in shaping optimistic expectancies.

Clinical and applied research could explore whether religiously informed interventions that focus on meaning-making, community integration, or reappraisal strategies have effects on optimism and related outcomes. Such interventions might be particularly relevant for populations experiencing significant life stress or existential concerns. However, given the null findings regarding dispositional optimism, applications should be cautious and consider targeting more state-like constructs or domain-specific outcome measures where religious interventions may have stronger effects.

In conclusion, the present study found no evidence of a meaningful positive association between frequency of religious practice and dispositional optimism measured by the LOT-R in a diverse, non-clinical sample. The findings suggest that behavioral engagement in religious activities, as operationalized here, does not translate straightforwardly into higher generalized optimistic expectancies. This outcome challenges simple narratives that equate religious practice with increased optimism and underscores the need for more nuanced theoretical models and methodologically sophisticated research that disentangles the multifaceted nature of religiosity and its psychological consequences. Future work that includes longitudinal designs, richer measures of religiosity and spiritual experience, culturally diverse samples, and experimental manipulation will be essential to fully understand the complex relations among religion, optimism, and psychological well-being.
