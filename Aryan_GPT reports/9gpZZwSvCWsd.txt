Introduction

Human decision making in situations that involve potential gains and losses is critically influenced by a range of physiological and psychological states. Sleep is one of the most fundamental physiological states that modulates cognitive control, affect regulation, and decision processes. The amount and quality of sleep a person obtains has been linked to attention, working memory, and executive function, all of which in turn shape choices under uncertainty. Risk taking behavior (RTB) denotes a propensity to choose options that have uncertain outcomes and potentially high variance in reward and loss, and RTB has been studied across domains ranging from financial decision making to health behavior. Sleep deprivation and chronic inadequate sleep have each been implicated in a tendency toward more impulsive and sometimes more risk-prone choices in laboratory paradigms. Conversely, better rested individuals often demonstrate improved deliberative processing and reduced impulsivity on tasks that assess risk preferences. Despite the relatively consistent evidence connecting sleep to decision processes, the literature is heterogeneous and shows important boundary conditions. One such condition is the role of stress, which itself affects both sleep and risk taking. Stress can disrupt sleep continuity and reduce sleep duration, and exposure to stressors can acutely alter reward sensitivity and risk preference. Thus, there are at least three variables—sleep, stress, and RTB—that are intimately connected through multiple pathways. When interpreting associations between sleep and risk taking, it is therefore essential to consider the potential confounding influence of stress. Several prior studies have documented that experimentally-induced sleep loss increases physiological stress markers and changes subjective stress levels. These alterations in stress could be on the causal pathway from sleep to RTB. At the same time, chronic stress is associated with a host of behavioral adaptations, including heightened reward seeking and altered risk evaluation. Because stress may act as both a mediator and a confounder in the relationship between sleep and RTB, empirical work that aims to isolate the direct association between sleep and risk taking should explicitly account for stress. The methodological strategy of partial correlation allows for the statistical control of a third variable while estimating the linear association between two primary variables. Partial correlation is particularly well suited for observational designs where experimental manipulation of every relevant variable is not feasible. Using partial correlation, researchers can quantify the extent to which the association between two variables persists after removing shared variability due to a third variable. Prior sleep and decision-making studies have sometimes adjusted for potential confounders such as age, gender, or socioeconomic status, but studies that have explicitly controlled for stress when examining sleep and RTB are surprisingly sparse. The present study addresses this gap by using a partial correlation approach to estimate the association between self-reported sleep and risk taking behavior while statistically controlling for perceived stress. The primary research question is whether the bivariate association between sleep duration and risk taking remains when levels of perceived stress are accounted for. A secondary question is to characterize the relationships among the three variables in the sample, specifically by estimating the zero-order correlations between sleep and perceived stress and between perceived stress and risk taking. The choice dilemma questionnaire (CDQ) is a well-established self-report measure that captures tendencies to prefer risky options in deliberative choice contexts. The CDQ provides a continuous score such that higher values index greater proclivity toward risky choices. For perceived stress, a commonly used self-report scale gives a global score reflecting subjective stress appraisal over a recent timeframe. For sleep, a simple and ecologically valid measure is average nightly sleep duration reported for a recent typical week or month. Using these measures, the study operationalized sleep as average nightly sleep in hours, RTB as CDQ total score, and stress as perceived stress scale total score. Given the existing theoretical and empirical literature, several predictions were formulated. First, more sleep was expected to be associated with lower levels of perceived stress because better-rested individuals generally report reduced subjective stress. Second, higher perceived stress was expected to be associated with higher levels of RTB as increased stress can bias individuals toward immediate rewards and risk-seeking strategies. Third, a simple zero-order correlation between sleep and RTB was predicted to be present but relatively weak, as prior findings on sleep and risk taking show variable effect sizes. Finally, based on the idea that stress could account for some or all of the association between sleep and RTB, the partial correlation between sleep and RTB while controlling for perceived stress was expected to be smaller in magnitude and possibly nonsignificant relative to the zero-order correlation. This set of predictions acknowledges both the direct links between sleep and decision-making and the indirect pathway through stress. The current study therefore implemented a cross-sectional observational design in which participants completed validated self-report measures of sleep, perceived stress, and risk taking tendency. Partial correlation analysis was used as the primary analytic approach, supplemented by descriptive and diagnostic analyses to ensure the appropriateness of Pearson-based statistics. The study also examined the distributions of the measures, and assessed potential covariates such as age and gender to probe robustness of the main findings. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, causal claims cannot be made, but the statistical pattern of correlations can inform theoretical models of how sleep and stress jointly relate to risk taking behavior. By explicitly controlling for perceived stress, this work aims to clarify whether the commonly observed associations between sleep and RTB are robust to adjustment for stress or whether stress explains a substantive portion of that relationship. Such clarifications have implications for both theoretical models and practical interventions. If stress substantially attenuates the association between sleep and RTB, then interventions that directly target stress may reduce risk taking even when sleep patterns do not change. Alternatively, if the association between sleep and RTB survives control for stress, then sleep-focused interventions may be direct pathways to reducing risk-prone choice tendencies. The present study is therefore positioned to offer empirical leverage on these competing possibilities using a moderate-sized community sample and straightforward self-report instruments. The use of the CDQ situates the work within a well-established tradition of measuring domain-general risk preference, while the perceived stress measure captures subjective appraisal processes that are mechanistically plausible as confounders. Taken together, the study's rationale is grounded in converging literatures on sleep, stress, and decision making, and the analytic strategy is tailored to isolate the unique association of interest while acknowledging the limits of cross-sectional data.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university-adjacent community using a combination of online advertisements and flyers posted in public locations. A total of 200 participants completed the full battery of questionnaires. The sample included adults aged 18 to 65 years and represented a range of educational backgrounds. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and the study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the hosting institution. Inclusion criteria required that participants be fluent in English and not currently under treatment for a diagnosed sleep disorder. Individuals who reported using medications known to markedly affect sleep architecture or acute psychostimulants on the day of testing were asked to reschedule; however, no participants were excluded for medication use in the final dataset. The sample was 58% female and had a mean age of 32.4 years with a standard deviation of 10.6 years. Participants attended a single in-person session lasting approximately 30 minutes, during which they completed a set of self-report questionnaires administered using paper-and-pencil forms. The primary variables were assessed with the following instruments. Sleep was measured as average nightly sleep duration over the past month, obtained by asking a single item: "On average, how many hours of sleep do you get per night?" Participants responded using a decimal value to allow for half-hour reporting. The perceived stress construct was assessed using a validated global perceived stress scale which asks respondents to rate how often they have experienced stress-related thoughts and feelings over the past month on a five-point Likert scale for each item. Scores were summed to produce a continuous perceived stress total ranging from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress. Risk taking behavior was measured using the Choice Dilemma Questionnaire (CDQ), a standardized self-report instrument consisting of a series of hypothetical decision scenarios that require the respondent to choose between safer and riskier options. Each CDQ item yields a numeric index of risk preference and the items were summed to create a CDQ total score, with higher scores reflecting greater propensity for risk taking. The CDQ total in the present sample ranged from low to high values and was treated as a continuous variable. Demographic items included age, gender, and a brief question about regular shift work status, given that shift work can affect sleep patterns. Fewer than 10% of participants reported regular night shift work, and shift workers were retained in the main analyses. Data cleaning procedures are described below. Prior to analysis, all questionnaires were inspected for missing data and response validity. Instances of missing item-level data were rare and when they occurred fewer than 10% of the items on a given scale were missing, and in such cases mean imputation using the individual's available items on that scale was applied. No participants were removed for excessive missingness. The distributions of the main variables—sleep hours, perceived stress total, and CDQ total—were examined visually and using skewness and kurtosis statistics. Sleep hours approximated a normal distribution with slight skew toward shorter sleep, perceived stress scores showed moderate right skew, and CDQ scores showed mild right skew. Because Pearson correlation and partial correlation analyses assume reasonably symmetric distributions, perceived stress and CDQ scores were inspected for potential transformation needs. A small square-root transformation of perceived stress and CDQ scores was computed for sensitivity analyses, although the primary analyses used untransformed scores because diagnostic checks indicated that Pearson correlation estimates were robust given the sample size. Outliers were identified using z-scores greater than ±3.0 for any of the primary variables, and three participants met this criterion for one variable each; however, inspection revealed these values to be plausible and representative of true variability, and these participants were retained in the final analyses. All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Descriptive statistics including means, standard deviations, and ranges were computed for the primary variables. Zero-order Pearson correlations were computed for the pairs of variables: sleep and CDQ total, sleep and perceived stress, and perceived stress and CDQ total. The main inferential test for the study was the partial correlation between sleep and CDQ total while controlling for perceived stress. This partial correlation quantifies the linear association between the residualized components of sleep and CDQ after removing variance attributable to perceived stress. Two-sided hypothesis tests were used throughout, with alpha set at 0.05 for significance. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional guidelines where correlation coefficients around 0.10 are considered small, around 0.30 are considered moderate, and around 0.50 are considered large. To quantify uncertainty, 95% bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals for all correlation estimates were computed using 5,000 bootstrap samples. In addition to the primary partial correlation, supplemental analyses examined whether the partial correlation result was robust to adjustment for demographic covariates. Specifically, multiple partial correlations were computed controlling for perceived stress and each of age and gender separately, and then controlling for perceived stress, age, and gender together. An exploratory linear regression model was also fitted, with CDQ total as the dependent variable and sleep hours and perceived stress as simultaneous predictors, to evaluate whether perceived stress statistically mediated or superseded the contribution of sleep in a multivariable context. Regression diagnostics included inspection of residual plots and variance inflation factors to assess multicollinearity. All results reported below reflect the full sample of N = 200 unless otherwise noted.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The mean self-reported average nightly sleep was 6.8 hours with a standard deviation of 1.3 hours, and values ranged from 3.5 to 9.5 hours per night. The mean perceived stress total was 17.2 with a standard deviation of 6.8 and a range from 3 to 36. The CDQ total score had a mean of 24.5 with a standard deviation of 7.9 and a range from 8 to 46. Inspection of variable distributions indicated modest positive skew for perceived stress and CDQ total but acceptable distributional properties for correlational analyses in a sample of this size. Zero-order Pearson correlations were computed for the three variable pairs. Sleep and CDQ total had a Pearson correlation of r = -0.12. The 95% bootstrap confidence interval for this correlation ranged from -0.25 to 0.02. The p-value associated with the sleep-CDQ correlation was p = 0.09, indicating that this weak negative correlation did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance. The magnitude of this zero-order correlation corresponds to a small effect size by conventional benchmarks. Sleep and perceived stress were more strongly associated. The Pearson correlation between sleep hours and perceived stress total was r = -0.45. The 95% bootstrap confidence interval for the sleep-stress correlation was -0.56 to -0.33. This correlation was statistically significant with p < 0.001. The negative sign indicates that greater sleep duration was associated with lower perceived stress. The observed magnitude corresponds to a moderate effect size. Perceived stress and CDQ total were positively correlated, with r = 0.48. The 95% bootstrap confidence interval for this correlation was 0.36 to 0.58, and the association was statistically significant with p < 0.001. This result indicates that higher levels of perceived stress were associated with greater reported proclivity for risk taking on the CDQ. The magnitude of this stress-CDQ association also corresponds to a moderate effect size. Having established the zero-order associations, the primary analysis focused on the partial correlation between sleep and CDQ total while statistically controlling for perceived stress. The partial correlation coefficient was rp = -0.02. The 95% bootstrap confidence interval for the partial correlation was -0.15 to 0.11. The p-value for the partial correlation was p = 0.78, indicating no evidence of a linear association between sleep and CDQ total after removing variance associated with perceived stress. In other words, the weak negative zero-order correlation between sleep and risk taking was fully attenuated when controlling for perceived stress. To probe the robustness of this result, additional analyses included demographic covariates. When age was added as an additional control variable in the partial correlation, the partial correlation between sleep and CDQ (controlling for perceived stress and age) remained essentially null at rp = -0.03 with p = 0.73. When gender was added instead of age, the partial correlation was rp = -0.01 with p = 0.84. When both age and gender were included alongside perceived stress as control variables, the partial correlation between sleep and CDQ was rp = -0.03 with p = 0.69. These supplemental partial correlations had bootstrap confidence intervals that overlapped zero and were consistent with the primary partial correlation. An exploratory multiple linear regression analysis was conducted next. In this model, CDQ total was regressed on sleep hours and perceived stress simultaneously. The overall model accounted for 28% of the variance in CDQ scores (R^2 = 0.28). Perceived stress was a significant predictor in the model, with a standardized beta of β = 0.46 and p < 0.001, indicating that higher perceived stress predicted higher CDQ scores when adjusting for sleep. Sleep hours did not predict CDQ scores in the multivariable model; its standardized beta was β = -0.04 with p = 0.63. Variance inflation factors for the predictors were below 1.5, suggesting that multicollinearity was not problematic in the regression. Residual diagnostics indicated acceptable model fit and no substantial departure from homoscedasticity. Sensitivity analyses examined transformed variables to address potential skew. When perceived stress and CDQ scores were square-root transformed, the pattern of correlations remained consistent. The zero-order correlation between sleep and transformed CDQ was r = -0.11 (p = 0.11), the zero-order correlation between sleep and transformed perceived stress was r = -0.42 (p < 0.001), and the partial correlation between sleep and transformed CDQ controlling for transformed perceived stress was rp = -0.03 (p = 0.69). These transformed-variable analyses corroborated that the attenuation of the sleep-CDQ association upon controlling for perceived stress was not an artifact of skew. Exploratory subgroup analyses separated participants reporting regular shift work from those who did not, although the number of shift workers was small (n = 18). Among non-shift workers, zero-order correlations mirrored those in the full sample, and the partial correlation controlling for perceived stress remained essentially zero. Among shift workers, correlations were more variable due to small sample size and did not yield clear patterns; therefore, these subgroup results are considered descriptive only. Additional checks evaluated whether extreme sleep durations might drive results. Excluding participants with fewer than 4.5 hours of average sleep or more than 9.0 hours per night led to similar findings: the zero-order sleep-CDQ correlation was r = -0.10 (p = 0.15) and the partial correlation controlling for perceived stress was rp = -0.02 (p = 0.80). Finally, bootstrap-based mediation logic was considered in an ad hoc manner. Given the strong negative association between sleep and perceived stress and the strong positive association between perceived stress and CDQ, a plausible indirect pathway exists in which less sleep relates to greater perceived stress which in turn relates to greater risk taking. Formal mediation testing was not the pre-registered analytic plan for this correlational dataset, but the observed pattern of zero-order and partial correlations is consistent with stress accounting for much of the bivariate sleep-risk relation. Taken together, the results show that sleep and CDQ scores exhibit a weak and statistically nonsignificant negative zero-order association. Sleep is moderately negatively correlated with perceived stress, and perceived stress is moderately positively correlated with risk taking. Controlling for perceived stress removes the small zero-order association between sleep and risk taking, producing a near-zero partial correlation with wide uncertainty around zero.

Discussion

The present study investigated the relationship between sleep and risk taking behavior while explicitly accounting for perceived stress as a potential confounder. The primary finding was that although there was a small negative zero-order association between average nightly sleep and the Choice Dilemma Questionnaire (CDQ) score, this association was not statistically significant and was fully attenuated when controlling for perceived stress. By contrast, sleep duration showed a moderate negative association with perceived stress, and perceived stress showed a moderate positive association with CDQ scores. This pattern suggests that perceived stress may function as an important explanatory variable that captures much of the covariance linking sleep and risk-prone decision tendencies. The results support the idea that the often-discussed link between sleep and risk taking cannot be adequately interpreted without considering the role of stress. The study's findings align with theoretical perspectives that posit stress as a proximal influence on decision making, functioning either as a mediator or a confounding variable in relations between physiological states and choice behavior. The observed negative relation between sleep and perceived stress replicates prior findings that better-rested individuals report lower levels of subjective stress. From a mechanistic standpoint, there are plausible pathways by which sleep affects stress regulation. Adequate sleep supports prefrontal regulatory control over limbic and hypothalamic systems that generate stress responses, whereas insufficient sleep can weaken such regulation and heighten perceived stress. The moderate magnitude of the sleep-stress association observed in the present sample (r ≈ -0.45) indicates that sleep hours account for a meaningful portion of variability in perceived stress in a community sample. Similarly, the positive association between perceived stress and CDQ scores in this study is consistent with empirical and theoretical work indicating that stress promotes more risk-seeking or impulsive choice patterns. Under conditions of elevated stress, individuals may prioritize immediate rewards, discount future costs, and shift toward experiential or affect-driven decision strategies, all of which can manifest as higher CDQ scores. The moderate size of the stress-risk association (r ≈ 0.48) underscores stress as a robust correlate of risk preference in self-report measures. Critically, when perceived stress was statistically controlled, the relation between sleep and RTB essentially disappeared. The partial correlation between sleep and CDQ controlling for stress was near zero and non-significant. This attenuation suggests that much of the weak bivariate relation initially observed between sleep and risk taking may be explained by differences in perceived stress associated with sleep. Interpreting this result requires careful consideration of causal models. One plausible interpretation is that sleep exerts indirect effects on risk taking through its influence on stress: less sleep increases perceived stress, and greater perceived stress, in turn, increases risk taking. Under this mediation model, controlling for perceived stress would remove the indirect effect and yield a negligible direct association between sleep and RTB. Another plausible interpretation is that stress acts as a confounder because unmeasured factors (for example, life circumstances that simultaneously reduce sleep and increase stress) generate a spurious association between sleep and risk taking. The cross-sectional design of the current study cannot discriminate between these causal possibilities, and formal mediation testing in cross-sectional data would be inappropriate for establishing temporal ordering. Nonetheless, the pattern of associations observed here is consistent with models that place stress as a central node linking sleep and choice behavior. It is important to situate these findings in the larger empirical context. Prior studies manipulating sleep have documented effects on various forms of decision making, including increased risk taking following acute sleep deprivation in some laboratory tasks. However, the literature is mixed, with some studies failing to find consistent effects across decision paradigms and others showing task-dependent patterns. The present study contributes to this literature by highlighting that subjective stress is a potent correlate of both sleep and risk preference and that failure to account for stress may lead to overestimation of direct sleep effects on risk taking in observational research. The results also have practical implications. Interventions aimed at reducing risky choices might profitably target stress reduction as a proximal lever, and sleep interventions that reduce stress could produce downstream benefits on decision making. Conversely, sleep interventions alone may not uniformly reduce risk taking unless they meaningfully lower subjective stress. The study's exploratory regression analysis further substantiated these points, showing that perceived stress remained a significant predictor of CDQ scores when both sleep and stress were included as simultaneous predictors, whereas sleep did not account for additional unique variance in the outcome. This multivariable perspective reinforces the idea that stress is a dominant predictor of self-reported risk propensity in this sample. Several methodological considerations temper the conclusions that can be drawn. The study relied on self-reported average nightly sleep duration rather than objective sleep measures. While self-report is ecologically valid and practical for larger samples, it is subject to recall bias and may not capture sleep continuity or sleep quality. Objective measures such as actigraphy or polysomnography could provide complementary information about sleep architecture and fragmentation, which may differentially relate to stress and decision-making processes. Additionally, the use of the CDQ as a self-report measure of risk taking captures participants' reported preferences in hypothetical scenarios but does not necessarily translate directly to behavioral choices in real stakes contexts. Laboratory tasks with incentivized lotteries or real monetary outcomes can yield more direct tests of risk preference, though they too have limitations. The cross-sectional observational design is another important limitation because it precludes causal inference. Longitudinal or experimental designs that manipulate sleep or stress are needed to clarify temporal ordering and mediation. For instance, randomized sleep extension studies that measure changes in perceived stress and consequential changes in risky decision making would be directly informative. Further, stress itself is a multi-faceted construct, and the perceived stress scale used here captures subjective appraisal rather than physiological stress indicators. Including biomarkers such as salivary cortisol or heart rate variability in future studies would provide a richer picture of stress-related processes and their relations to sleep and RTB. The robustness checks and sensitivity analyses in the present work strengthen confidence in the main findings. Transformations to address skew did not materially alter the pattern of associations, and the partial correlation remained null across specifications that adjusted for demographic covariates. Excluding extreme sleep durations also did not change the results. However, some exploratory subgroup analyses were underpowered, notably those involving shift workers, and should be considered preliminary. The generalizability of the results is bounded by the sample composition, which, though heterogeneous in age and education, was recruited in a university-adjacent community and included relatively few older adults and few individuals with clinical sleep disorders. Future work should assess whether the same pattern of relationships holds in clinical populations such as individuals with insomnia, major stress disorders, or substance use disorders, where sleep and stress dysregulation may be more pronounced. The present findings also point to several promising directions for future research. First, experimental manipulations of sleep duration and quality with concurrent measurement of subjective and physiological stress could directly test mediation models. Second, multi-method assessment of RTB that combines self-report measures like the CDQ with incentivized behavioral tasks would allow researchers to probe whether the mediating role of stress is consistent across measurement modalities. Third, longitudinal studies could illuminate whether chronic sleep deprivation leads to persistent increases in perceived stress and whether such increases predict long-term changes in risk-prone behaviors outside the laboratory. Fourth, interventions that target either sleep or stress—or both—should be evaluated for their effects on decision making in ecologically valid outcomes, such as health behaviors and financial choices. Finally, computational modeling of decision processes could be used to parse whether stress primarily affects risk preferences through changes in reward sensitivity, loss aversion, or noise in the decision process; such mechanistic insights could guide targeted interventions. In sum, the study provides evidence that perceived stress plays a central role in the observed associations between sleep and self-reported risk taking. While sleep and RTB showed a weak bivariate relation, the inclusion of perceived stress in statistical models removed this association, and stress was robustly related to both sleep and risk taking. These findings underscore the importance of considering stress in research on sleep and decision making and suggest that interventions aimed at reducing risk-prone behavior may benefit from explicitly addressing stress-related mechanisms.
