Introduction

Human memory for language operates across multiple levels of representation, ranging from the surface forms of words and sentences to the deeper semantic and conceptual representations that underlie discourse. Early work in cognitive psychology established that these different levels do not all persist equally over time and that memory for meaning is often more robust than memory for exact wording. One influential body of work demonstrated that when participants read prose and are later asked whether a sentence they are shown appeared verbatim in the passage, they are more likely to correctly recognize sentences that maintain the original meaning than sentences that have been altered in wording. This pattern has been taken to indicate that memory for connected discourse tends to preserve the gist or propositional content while surface form details decay more rapidly. The finding has been interpreted as evidence for a hierarchical organization of memory representations in which semantic or conceptual traces are more durable and more salient during retrieval than verbatim traces. The theoretical implications of this observation are broad because language comprehension and memory are central to many aspects of cognition, and understanding which features of discourse are preserved can inform models of reading, comprehension, and communication.

The literature investigating surface form versus gist memory encompasses several frameworks that emphasize different mechanisms for the observed dissociation. One class of models treats memory for discourse as comprising multiple trace types, with separate but interacting representations for surface form and deeper propositional content. In this view, surface traces are encoded but are more susceptible to forgetting and interference, whereas propositional traces are more abstract and resilient. Other accounts emphasize constructive processes during comprehension whereby readers actively generate inferences and integrate information, with those constructive processes favoring retention of meaning because it is more relevant for ongoing interpretation and inference generation. A third perspective frames the dissociation in terms of attentional allocation and encoding processes, suggesting that readers typically devote more attention to understanding meaning than to rehearsing or encoding precise wording, especially during naturalistic reading where exact wording is rarely required for downstream tasks.

Empirically, studies that compare memory for changes in meaning versus changes in surface form generally find higher recognition and recall for meaning changes. This pattern is evident across paradigms that present passages and later probe recognition of test sentences that are identical to sentences from the passage, that are paraphrases preserving meaning with different wording, or that alter meaning. Participants show relatively high accuracy at detecting semantic changes because those changes correspond to alterations in the propositional structure of the discourse and thus conflict strongly with stored gist representations. In contrast, paraphrases or minor syntactic adjustments that preserve meaning tend to be less detectable because the stored memory emphasizes the propositional content rather than precise lexical or syntactic instantiations. These empirical regularities have been observed across variations in materials, retention intervals, and task demands, suggesting a robust tendency for gist to be favored in memory for connected discourse.

While the literature on gist versus surface memory is well developed, relatively less attention has been paid to how encoding manipulations that alter the manner of reading might influence the balance between these memory types. One such manipulation that has received growing interest is reading aloud versus reading silently. Reading aloud entails overt speech production, which introduces additional sensory and motoric traces — including auditory feedback and motor plans — that are not present during silent reading. The production effect refers to the phenomenon that items produced aloud during encoding are often better remembered than items read silently. The production effect has been documented in a range of memory tasks, including item recognition and free recall, and it has been proposed to arise from increased distinctiveness, richer multimodal encoding, and enhanced attention at encoding when production occurs. Distinctiveness mechanisms emphasize that producing an item aloud creates a unique encoding episode that can be discriminated at retrieval, while production may also engage phonological and articulatory processes that embed additional cues into memory representations.

Given these considerations, an intriguing question arises about whether producing text aloud during reading can alter the relative preservation of surface form versus gist of sentences in connected discourse. On the one hand, reading aloud might strengthen memory for surface features because the act of production gives rise to sensory-motor traces linked to the exact wording of sentences, potentially reducing the typical decay of verbatim form. If production adds distinctive surface-level cues, participants who read aloud might be more likely to detect syntactic or lexical changes that preserve meaning but alter form. On the other hand, production might not differentially affect surface versus propositional traces in the context of connected discourse because the cognitive system may remain focused on comprehension and meaning integration during reading, so that the dominant encoding remains semantic. Additionally, the additional cognitive load of coordinating production with comprehension in an extended passage might shift resources away from precise surface encoding toward maintaining comprehension, thus attenuating any advantage for surface memory.

Prior empirical work on the production effect has primarily focused on single-word or sentence-level items presented in isolation rather than on sentences embedded in longer passages of connected discourse. In the single-item literature, production benefits are frequently observed and sometimes are larger for tasks that emphasize item recognition relative to tasks that emphasize relational or associative memory. However, when items are embedded within narratives or longer passages, the dynamics of encoding are different because comprehension processes continually update representations and integrate information across sentences. In such circumstances, the contribution of production to surface trace maintenance is less well understood. Furthermore, previous studies have rarely examined recognition of sentence changes in a connected discourse context as the dependent variable when manipulating reading aloud versus silent reading.

Another important factor is task instructions and retrieval format. When participants are tested for verbatim recognition, the task explicitly orients them to surface details, which might encourage retrieval of whatever surface traces remain. Conversely, when the task is framed more generally, participants may rely more on gist-based judgments. Thus the interplay of encoding modality, task demands, and the nature of the test materials may jointly determine the observed pattern of results. Studying recognition for altered sentences in connected discourse with a manipulation of reading style therefore has the potential to illuminate both the scope of the production effect and the boundary conditions of gist-dominant memory.

The present study was designed to address this gap by examining whether reading aloud versus silent reading differentially affects memory for changes in sentences embedded within short passages. The experimental paradigm follows the spirit of earlier investigations that contrasted memory for semantic changes versus changes in the form of sentences, and it adds a manipulation of reading style to test whether production can modulate the preservation of surface traces. Participants read a set of short passages and were later presented with test sentences that either appeared identically in the passage, preserved the original meaning but altered form, or changed the meaning relative to the original sentence. Two dependent measures were collected at test: a binary decision about whether the test sentence had appeared verbatim in the passage, and a prompted identification of the nature of any change observed between the test sentence and the original passage sentence.

It was hypothesized that the classic pattern of higher recognition rates for semantic changes relative to form changes would be replicated in the current study. This prediction follows from the extensive evidence that propositional content is more robust in memory for connected discourse. A secondary hypothesis was that reading aloud might lead to enhanced memory for form changes relative to silent reading due to the production effect. Specifically, reading aloud could be expected to increase recognition rates for syntactic or lexical changes that leave meaning intact because the production-related sensory-motor cues might preserve surface representations that are diagnostic for detecting such changes. Alternatively, if production primarily augments item-level distinctiveness without improving the encoding of surface relations in discourse, no differential effect on the detection of sentence changes might be observed. Considering these possibilities, the present study tested both the main effect of change type and the interaction between change type and reading style.

To test these predictions, the study employed a between-subjects manipulation of reading style with participants randomly assigned to either read aloud or read silently. The within-subjects factor was change type, including identical (verbatim), form change (syntactic/lexical alteration preserving meaning), and semantic change (meaning-altering). The dependent variables were accuracy on the verbatim recognition judgment and accuracy on the change identification prompt. This design allowed a direct test of whether production at encoding selectively enhances memory for surface form traces that are relevant to detecting syntactic or lexical changes, while also allowing comparison with the well-documented memory advantage for semantic changes.

The present investigation contributes to theoretical debates about the nature of memory representations for discourse by testing the capacity of production to protect surface information in a realistic comprehension task. If production were found to increase detection of form changes, this would suggest that motor-sensory traces created during reading aloud can serve as durable cues for surface details even within ongoing discourse. Conversely, if no production-related benefit emerges, the results would suggest that gist-based encoding predominates in natural reading contexts and that the production effect may not extend to the preservation of surface traces within connected prose. The study therefore aims to clarify the boundary conditions for the production effect and to further elucidate the mechanisms by which gist and surface representations are formed and maintained during reading. In the sections that follow, the methods, results, and implications of the study are described in detail.

Methods

Participants were recruited from an undergraduate participant pool and from the surrounding community and received either course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. A total of 80 participants were included in the final sample after exclusions, with 40 participants assigned to the read-aloud condition and 40 participants assigned to the silent-reading condition. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 35 years, with a mean age of 21.8 years and a standard deviation of 3.4 years. The sample included both male and female participants and was composed primarily of native speakers of English. During recruitment, participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of language impairment, and all provided informed consent in accordance with standard ethical procedures approved by the relevant institutional review board.

Materials consisted of 36 short passages, each approximately three to six sentences long and designed to be comprehensible in a single reading. The passages were crafted so that each contained a target sentence that could later be probed at test. For each target sentence, three test sentence variants were created: a verbatim version that matched the original sentence exactly, a form-change version that preserved the propositional content but altered wording through synonym substitution or syntactic rephrasing, and a semantic-change version that altered the meaning of the sentence while retaining superficial similarity to the original. Care was taken to ensure that form changes involved changes that would be detectable only if surface form traces were preserved, such as changing passive to active voice, swapping synonyms with different lexical forms, or rearranging clauses without altering propositional relations. Semantic changes included alterations that would change the truth conditions or core propositional content, such as replacing a subject or predicate with one that introduced a different state of affairs.

The set of 36 target items was divided into three lists such that across participants each target appeared in each of the three test conditions an equal number of times, following a Latin square design. Each participant therefore received one version of each target at test, with 12 items sampled from each test condition. The order of presentation of passages at study and the order of test sentences were randomized for each participant to minimize order effects. Additionally, 12 filler passages and corresponding surrogate test sentences were included to obscure the specific focus on sentence changes and to provide variability in content, bringing the total number of study passages to 48 and the total number of test trials to 48.

Procedure began with instructions informing participants that they would read a series of short passages and that they should read each passage for comprehension because they would later be tested. Participants assigned to the read-aloud condition were instructed to read every passage aloud at a natural pace, speaking clearly and at a comfortable volume. Participants in the silent condition were instructed to read the passages silently at a natural pace. Both groups were told to avoid subvocalization beyond what felt natural; the instructions emphasized natural reading rather than attempts to memorize wording. Each passage was displayed on a computer screen one at a time for a self-paced duration, with participants pressing the space bar to advance to the next passage once they finished reading the current one. The self-paced nature allowed participants to control their reading time, and reading times for each passage were recorded for exploratory analyses.

Following the study phase, participants completed a five-minute filler task consisting of simple arithmetic problems to reduce reliance on short-term memory and to introduce a brief delay before the recognition test. The test phase then began, consisting of 48 test sentences presented one at a time on the computer screen in randomized order. For each test sentence, participants made two responses. First, they made a binary yes/no judgment to the question “Did this sentence appear exactly as shown in the passage?” This response measured recognition of verbatim occurrence. Second, immediately after the yes/no response, participants responded to the prompt “If not identical, what changed?” by selecting one of three options presented on the screen: “Meaning changed,” “Form or wording changed but meaning same,” or “I’m not sure.” These two responses together provided measures of verbatim recognition and change-type identification.

Responses and response times at test were recorded. Participants were encouraged to respond based on their memory of the passages and were told that guessing was acceptable if they were unsure. The experimenter monitored the read-aloud sessions to ensure that participants complied with instructions to read aloud but otherwise refrained from interfering. At the end of the session participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire and a debriefing that explained the purpose of the experiment.

Data preprocessing included exclusion of trials with missing responses and exclusion of participants with overall accuracy below 50 percent on the filler test items, which indicated noncompliance or inattentiveness. Four participants were excluded for failing attention checks or for extensive missing data, and their data were replaced to preserve the intended sample size of 80. For the main analyses, recognition accuracy was computed separately for each participant and each change condition as the proportion of correct verbatim judgments. For identical trials, correct recognition corresponded to answering “yes” to the verbatim question, while for non-identical trials correct recognition corresponded to answering “no.” Change-identification accuracy was computed as the proportion of non-identical trials on which participants correctly selected “Meaning changed” for semantic-change items and “Form or wording changed but meaning same” for form-change items.

Statistical analyses were conducted using standard parametric procedures. The primary analysis for verbatim recognition accuracy involved a mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) with change type (form change vs semantic change) as a within-subjects factor and reading style (read aloud vs silent) as a between-subjects factor. Identical-item recognition trials were analyzed separately to examine baseline detection of verbatim sentences and potential overall effects of reading style. For change-identification accuracy, a similar mixed-design ANOVA examined the interaction between change type and reading style. Where appropriate, post hoc t-tests were used to probe significant interactions, and effect sizes (Cohen’s d for t-tests and partial eta-squared for ANOVAs) were computed to quantify the magnitude of effects. Additionally, exploratory correlations examined whether individual differences in study-phase reading time were related to recognition performance, and whether these relations differed by reading style. All reported p-values are two-tailed, and an alpha level of .05 was used for statistical significance.

Results

Descriptive statistics for recognition and change-identification accuracy are presented first to provide a clear picture of performance across conditions. For verbatim recognition accuracy on identical trials, participants in the read-aloud condition had a mean accuracy of .86 (SD = .09), whereas participants in the silent-reading condition had a mean accuracy of .84 (SD = .10). For non-identical trials, mean accuracy on semantic-change items was .79 (SD = .12) across the full sample, whereas mean accuracy on form-change items was .61 (SD = .14) across the full sample. These descriptive means indicate that participants were generally able to detect semantic changes more accurately than form changes, consistent with the hypothesis that memory for meaning is more robust than memory for surface form.

A mixed-design ANOVA was conducted with change type (semantic vs form change) as a within-subjects factor and reading style (read aloud vs silent) as a between-subjects factor, focusing on the recognition accuracy for non-identical trials. The analysis revealed a highly significant main effect of change type, F(1,78) = 112.46, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .59, indicating that participants were substantially better at recognizing semantic changes than form changes. The main effect of reading style was not significant in this analysis, F(1,78) = 0.81, p = .37, partial eta-squared = .01, suggesting no overall advantage of reading aloud versus silent reading for recognition accuracy on non-identical trials. The interaction between change type and reading style was also non-significant, F(1,78) = 0.45, p = .51, partial eta-squared = .01, indicating that the advantage for semantic changes over form changes did not differ between the read-aloud and silent-reading groups.

Follow-up paired-samples t-tests confirmed that participants within each reading-style group were significantly more accurate at recognizing semantic changes than form changes. In the read-aloud group, the mean accuracy for semantic changes was .80 (SD = .11) compared with .62 (SD = .13) for form changes, t(39) = 8.67, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.37. In the silent-reading group, the mean accuracy for semantic changes was .78 (SD = .12) compared with .60 (SD = .15) for form changes, t(39) = 7.59, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.20. These large effect sizes confirm a strong within-subject effect of change type in both encoding conditions.

Analyses of identical trials, which indexed baseline verbatim detection, showed a small and non-significant effect of reading style. A between-groups t-test comparing accuracy on identical trials between the read-aloud and silent-reading groups yielded t(78) = 0.81, p = .42, Cohen’s d = 0.18. This result indicates that both groups detected verbatim sentences at similarly high rates. The distribution of false alarms (i.e., indicating “yes” for non-identical trials) was also examined. False alarm rates for semantic-change items were low, reflecting the high correct detection of meaning alterations, whereas false alarm rates for form-change items were higher, reflecting the difficulty of detecting changes that preserved meaning. Importantly, false alarm rates did not differ reliably between reading-style groups for either change type.

For the change-identification prompt, accuracy was consistent with the recognition findings. Participants correctly identified semantic changes as “meaning changed” on .76 (SD = .13) of semantic-change trials overall, whereas they correctly identified form changes as “form changed” on .58 (SD = .15) of form-change trials overall. A mixed-design ANOVA with identification accuracy as the dependent variable revealed a significant main effect of change type, F(1,78) = 84.32, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .52, and no significant main effect of reading style, F(1,78) = 0.64, p = .43, partial eta-squared = .01. The interaction between change type and reading style was again non-significant, F(1,78) = 0.12, p = .73, partial eta-squared < .01. These analyses indicate that participants were considerably better at correctly labeling meaning changes than form changes, and that this pattern did not differ as a function of reading aloud versus silent reading.

Given the null effects of reading style on both recognition and change-identification measures, Bayesian t-tests were conducted as an exploratory analysis to quantify evidence for the absence of a reading-style effect. For recognition accuracy on form-change items, the Bayesian t-test yielded a Bayes factor (BF01) of approximately 3.2 in favor of the null hypothesis, indicating moderate evidence that reading style did not affect recognition of form changes. For recognition accuracy on semantic-change items, the Bayes factor in favor of the null was approximately 4.1, indicating moderate evidence that reading style did not affect recognition of semantic changes. These Bayesian analyses bolster the interpretation that the data were more consistent with no effect of reading style than with effects of the magnitude that might be expected based on a strong production effect.

Exploratory analyses examined whether individual differences in study-phase reading time were associated with recognition performance. Across all participants, mean reading time per passage was longer in the read-aloud group (M = 22.4 s, SD = 5.6) than in the silent-reading group (M = 15.8 s, SD = 4.3), reflecting the additional time required to articulate text aloud. Correlations between mean reading time and recognition accuracy were computed separately by reading style. In the read-aloud group, reading time correlated weakly with recognition of form-change items, r = .18, p = .27, and weakly with recognition of semantic-change items, r = .15, p = .35. In the silent group, reading time correlated weakly with recognition of form-change items, r = .12, p = .45, and with semantic-change items, r = .09, p = .56. None of these correlations reached significance, and Fisher r-to-z comparisons indicated no reliable difference in the magnitude of correlations across reading-style groups.

Reaction time data from the recognition judgment were also examined. Mean response times for the verbatim yes/no decision did not differ significantly between reading-style groups for either change type. For semantic-change trials, mean response time was 987 ms (SD = 210 ms) in the read-aloud group and 972 ms (SD = 205 ms) in the silent group; for form-change trials mean response time was 1,092 ms (SD = 240 ms) in the read-aloud group and 1,108 ms (SD = 255 ms) in the silent group. Mixed ANOVAs on response times yielded no significant main effects or interactions involving reading style, suggesting that decision processes at test were similar across groups.

Finally, additional checks explored potential order effects or item effects. Mixed-effects models that included item as a random factor were fit as supplementary analyses and produced the same qualitative pattern of results: robust fixed effects of change type and non-significant fixed effects of reading style and their interaction. Inspection of individual items revealed variability in detectability across specific targets, which is expected given natural differences in how easily a change in meaning or form can be detected; however, this item variability did not interact with reading style. Taken together, the pattern of results robustly supports the conclusion that semantic changes are recognized more accurately than form changes and that reading aloud, under these experimental conditions, does not appreciably alter recognition for sentence changes embedded in connected discourse.

Discussion

The present study investigated how reading style — specifically reading aloud versus reading silently — influences memory for sentence changes embedded in connected discourse, with a particular focus on the classic distinction between memory for semantic changes and memory for changes in surface form. The results provide a clear replication of the long-standing observation that semantic changes are much more readily detected than form changes when participants are presented with test sentences that may or may not match sentences from a previously read passage. This replicates and extends prior findings by demonstrating the robustness of the semantic advantage in a task that probed both verbatim recognition and explicit identification of the type of change. Importantly, the study also tested whether the production effect would alter this pattern by strengthening surface-level traces through overt articulation during reading. The data indicated no reliable effect of reading aloud on either verbatim recognition or change-identification accuracy, and Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis. This pattern suggests that the production effect, at least as operationalized in this experiment, does not substantially modulate the balance between gist and surface memory for sentences in connected discourse.

The replication of superior detection for semantic changes supports the view that memory for discourse is organized around propositional or gist-level representations. When participants later encounter a sentence that alters the propositional content, such a change almost necessarily conflicts with the stored gist and thus is readily detected. In contrast, form changes that preserve meaning create fewer conflicts with the stored propositional representation and therefore are more likely to be missed. The current study employed both a verbatim recognition judgment and an explicit change-identification prompt, and both dependent measures converged on the same pattern, strengthening confidence in the generality of the semantic advantage. The robustness of this effect across both read-aloud and silent-reading participants underscores that the dominance of propositional encoding in discourse memory is not easily overturned by simple encoding manipulations.

The absence of a reading-style effect is theoretically informative about the conditions under which the production effect operates. Much of the production-effect literature documents enhanced memory for items produced aloud relative to items read silently, particularly for isolated words or short lists. One interpretation of the production effect is that it increases item distinctiveness through an additional auditory-verbal trace created at encoding. If that mechanism were sufficient to preserve verbatim traces in connected discourse, one would expect reading aloud to improve detection of form changes that rely on preserved surface information. However, the present results suggest that any production-related enhancement either does not translate into stronger surface representations for sentences embedded in passages, or that the advantage is too small to influence performance in a task dominated by propositional encoding and integration processes.

There are several possible reasons why reading aloud did not produce a measurable advantage in this context. First, the cognitive demands of comprehending and integrating information across a multi-sentence passage may orient attention predominantly toward meaning, thereby limiting the extent to which surface details are encoded, even when words are articulated aloud. Overt speech production imposes additional processing demands and may in some instances compete with comprehension resources, potentially offsetting any distinctiveness gained from production. Second, the naturalistic reading instructions emphasized comprehension rather than memorization of wording, which mirrors real-world reading goals and may thus reduce the likelihood that readers will attend to or rehearse surface details. Third, the production effect documented in isolated-item paradigms may rely on contrasts between produced and silently read items presented within the same encoding session; when production is applied to all items in an encoding set, as in a between-subjects design, the distinctiveness benefit may be attenuated. In the present study, reading style was manipulated between subjects, and thus the production-related distinctiveness of produced items was not contrasted with silently read items within the same participant. Fourth, the temporal dynamics of forgetting may differ for surface form and meaning in ways that interact with production; for example, production might momentarily strengthen a surface trace but not sufficiently to survive the retention interval and test procedure used here.

The finding that reading aloud produced longer reading times but did not translate into higher recognition performance is consistent with the notion that additional time spent encoding does not necessarily equate to improved memory for surface features in discourse. Participants who read aloud took more time per passage on average than silent readers, yet this additional encoding time did not confer a measurable recognition advantage. This raises questions about the nature of attention and encoding during reading aloud; the additional time may be primarily consumed by articulatory processes rather than by enhanced cognitive processing of lexical or syntactic form. Additionally, the articulatory aspect of production might create modality-specific traces (auditory, motor) that are not particularly informative for the cognitive operations required to detect form changes in sentences that are integrated into a larger context.

The Bayesian analyses that favored the null hypothesis for reading-style effects provide stronger inferential grounds than non-significant p-values alone. The moderate Bayes factors suggest that the data were more consistent with no meaningful effect of reading style than with a substantial production advantage. This outcome narrows the plausible scope of the production effect, implying that its influence may be context-dependent and potentially limited when items are embedded within larger cohesive texts and when the test requires discriminating subtle surface changes. Thus, the present findings contribute to delineating the boundary conditions of the production effect and highlight the importance of task and material characteristics.

Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. The between-subjects manipulation of reading style maximized experimental control and avoided potential carryover effects across conditions, but it may have reduced sensitivity to detect small production effects that could emerge in a within-subjects contrast. Future work might adopt a within-subjects design where participants read some passages aloud and others silently to maximize sensitivity to production-related differences, although such designs introduce concerns about participant strategy shifts and differential attention. Another limitation is that the present materials consisted of relatively short passages with single target sentences that were probed at test; different kinds of materials, such as longer narratives or texts with more salient stylistic variation, might yield different patterns. It is also possible that production effects could emerge at different retention intervals; the brief delay used here may not capture longer-term consolidation processes that might differentially affect surface and propositional traces.

Future research directions could address these limitations and extend the present findings in several ways. One avenue would be to manipulate the within-subject contrast of reading style, allowing for a direct item-level comparison of produced and silently read sentences within the same participant. Another approach is to vary the retention interval systematically to examine whether production-related enhancements emerge or decay over different time scales. It would also be informative to introduce tasks that explicitly encourage attention to surface form during encoding, such as instructing participants to focus on precise wording or to rehearse sentences for verbatim reproduction, to assess whether production can interact with strategic encoding to preserve surface details. Neuroimaging or electrophysiological methods could provide converging evidence about whether production creates distinct neural signatures that correspond to surface-level encoding even if those signatures do not translate into behavioral advantage in certain tasks.

Additional studies could explore whether partial production manipulations, such as whispering, subvocal articulation, or reading with enhanced prosody, differentially influence memory for surface versus meaning. Moreover, investigating populations with differences in language processing, such as second-language learners or older adults, could reveal whether production effects vary with linguistic proficiency or age-related changes in memory and attention. Finally, the current paradigm could be extended to other languages and orthographic systems to assess the generality of the findings across diverse reading contexts.

In conclusion, the present study replicates the robust finding that semantic changes in sentences are more easily detected than form changes when test sentences are probed after reading connected discourse. The study also tested a theoretically motivated prediction from production-effect research but found no evidence that reading aloud appreciably alters recognition of sentence changes in connected discourse. These results suggest that gist-based memory representations exert a dominant influence on recognition performance in naturalistic reading tasks and that overt production alone is insufficient to shift the balance in favor of surface trace preservation under the conditions tested. This conclusion refines our understanding of the boundary conditions for production-related memory benefits and highlights the resilience of propositional encoding in discourse comprehension.
