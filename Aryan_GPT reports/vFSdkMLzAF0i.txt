Introduction

Human language is both a vehicle for conveying richly codified meanings and a set of surface forms that are instantiated in words, syntax, and prosody. Memory for language therefore can be investigated at multiple levels, from detailed verbatim traces of wording and form to more abstract representations of meaning or gist. Research in cognitive psychology has long debated the extent to which memory preserves surface form versus semantic content, and the conditions under which one level of representation is privileged above the other. Early theoretical positions emphasized the role of gist or semantic representation as the primary currency of remembered language, arguing that people remember general communicative meaning rather than precise syntactic form. This perspective suggests that the cognitive system abstracts away from surface variability to form durable semantic representations that are robust to superficial changes. In contrast, other perspectives have highlighted circumstances under which surface features such as exact wording, voice, or prosody are retained, at least transiently, and can be retrieved under appropriate test conditions. The balance between these types of memory has important implications for models of working memory, long-term memory consolidation, and theories of language comprehension and production.

Empirical investigations have used a range of manipulations to probe memory for surface versus gist information. One common approach is to present participants with sentences and then test recognition using foils that either preserve meaning but change form, change meaning while preserving form, or repeat the original sentence verbatim. Recognition performance across these conditions provides a window into the representational level encoded during initial exposure. A consistent finding across several classic studies is that participants show higher false alarm rates to foils that preserve meaning but alter form, relative to foils that change meaning, suggesting a tendency to rely on semantic similarity when making memory decisions. This pattern has been taken as evidence that comprehension processes transform surface input into an integrated semantic representation that is preferentially retained, whereas surface details decay more rapidly or are not encoded in detail to begin with.

Sachs’ research, which has been influential in this area, reported that reading modality did not generally influence memory for sentence materials, but observed a specific effect for items in which voice was changed: voice-change sentences were consistently recognized highly when read silently. While that particular finding suggested a potential interaction between reading modality and sensitivity to certain kinds of surface form, the broader pattern in Sachs’ work aligned with the view that semantic sentence manipulations tend to be recognized more frequently than form-preserving but meaning-changing counterparts, consistent with the idea that changes in meaning are more salient in the mnemonic record than changes in surface form. The field has also considered the contribution of reading aloud to memory, sometimes referred to as the production effect, wherein vocalization at study can enhance later memory for those items relative to silent reading. The production effect has been observed robustly in single-word paradigms and some sentence-level studies, though the effect size at the sentence level is less consistent and may depend on task demands and the depth of processing engaged during study.

The mechanisms proposed for why reading aloud might enhance retention include increased distinctiveness of produced items, additional encoding via motor and auditory feedback channels, and greater attentional engagement when producing speech. The act of reading aloud provides multimodal encoding — a combination of visual, phonological, and articulatory information — that may create more retrieval cues at test. Conversely, silent reading engages inner speech and subvocal rehearsal systems that could, in principle, also produce phonological traces, but the absence of overt motoric and auditory consequences could result in weaker or less distinctive memory traces relative to aloud reading. However, when sentences are considered rather than isolated words, the semantic integration that occurs during sentence comprehension may overshadow the incremental benefit of production, leading to minimal modality effects at the sentence level in some paradigms.

Another variable that may interact with the tradeoff between gist and surface memory is the precise nature of the surface change. Changes in grammatical voice, such as active-to-passive transformations, alter syntactic structure while typically preserving semantic content. Such voice changes are therefore an ideal manipulation for testing sensitivity to surface form independent of meaning. If memory is primarily semantic, then voice-changed foils should be frequently mistaken as previously encountered items because they preserve the gist. If, however, participants encode surface details robustly, then voice-changed items should be less likely to be accepted as old. Sachs’ finding that voice-change sentences were consistently recognized highly when read silently suggested an intriguing interaction: under silent reading, participants might rely even more on gist and thus endorse voice-changed sentences; under other modalities the pattern might differ.

Replications and extensions are essential in resolving ambiguities in the literature and in establishing the boundary conditions of effects that pertain to reading modality and the level of memory representation. Replication also allows researchers to vary methodological features such as the length of study lists, the retention interval, the explicitness of instruction concerning what to remember, and the operationalization of voice-change manipulations. Extending Sachs’ paradigm by including an overt reading-aloud condition, a silent-reading condition, and a combined condition in which participants both read silently and read aloud offers a way to test whether vocalization during encoding adds distinct mnemonic benefit at the sentence level and whether such benefit interacts with sensitivity to semantic versus form changes.

The present study was designed to replicate and extend Sachs’ findings regarding the influence of reading modality on sentence memory, with specific interest in whether alternate reading modalities (silent, aloud, or both) affect memory for identical sentences, semantic manipulations, and voice-change sentence manipulations. The replication aimed to test three primary hypotheses. First, echoing Sachs’ broad conclusion and several related studies, we predicted that reading modality would not exert a large main effect on recognition memory for sentences at the group level; silent reading, reading aloud, and combined reading might yield similar overall recognition rates. Second, following theoretical expectations that meaning changes are more salient in the mnemonic record than changes in surface structure, we predicted that semantic sentence manipulations would be recognized more often than voice-change manipulations when participants are asked to make old/new recognition judgments. Third, given the ambiguity in prior findings about voice-change sensitivity, we tested whether voice-change manipulations would be recognized at higher, lower, or comparable rates relative to semantic manipulations and identical items, and we examined whether any differences would interact with reading modality.

A secondary aim of the study was to investigate whether combining silent and aloud reading would provide additive benefits to sentence memory relative to either modality alone, which would be consistent with a multimodal encoding account. If combined reading increased memory, it would suggest that overt production supplements the inner speech and visual encoding typical of silent reading, thereby strengthening trace distinctiveness. Conversely, if combined reading did not enhance recognition beyond single modalities, this would imply that at the sentence level, semantic processing may dominate and override modality-specific contributions.

The study also sought to provide methodological refinements compared to earlier work, including clearer operational definitions of voice-change manipulations (implemented as active-passive alternations that preserved meaning), controlled timing during study, and balanced counterbalancing of materials across conditions so that each sentence served equally often in each test condition across participants. We considered both raw recognition proportions and signal-detection indices to dissociate sensitivity from response bias, as prior inconsistencies in the literature might be attributable to shifts in criterion rather than true changes in discriminability. We also collected self-report measures of attentional engagement and compliance with reading instructions, given that differences in adherence to modality instructions could confound tests of the production effect.

Given the prior mixed results and the importance of establishing reliable phenomena in the domain of sentence memory, the present experiment aimed to produce clear evidence regarding whether modality influences recognition memory for sentences and, more broadly, to inform theoretical debates about the durability of surface versus semantic representations. The results were expected to inform models of language memory and to have practical implications for educational strategies and mnemonic techniques, particularly insofar as they indicate whether vocalization or multimodal encoding can enhance retention of sentence-level material. In sum, this study replicates Sachs’ paradigm with methodical extensions, and it tests competing predictions derived from theoretical viewpoints about gist versus surface encoding and from empirical findings on the production effect. The expected outcomes were specified in advance, and the design was yoked to those expectations to maximize interpretability of any observed patterns.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and from the surrounding community, and a total of 90 participants completed the experiment. Participants were fluent English speakers, reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and had no history of diagnosed language disorders; demographic information included age, gender, and years of education, and participants were compensated with course credit or a small monetary payment for their time. Prior to analysis, data from six participants were excluded due to noncompliance with instructions or incomplete data, yielding a final sample of 84 participants. The 84 participants were randomly assigned in equal numbers to one of three reading modality groups: Silent Reading, Aloud Reading, or Combined Reading (Silent + Aloud), with 28 participants in each group. Random assignment was carried out using a computerized random number generator to ensure equal allocation across groups and to minimize selection biases.

Materials consisted of 120 simple to moderately complex sentences constructed to be natural and easy to read, with half of the sentences originally written in active voice and the remainder in passive voice to allow for voice-change manipulations. Each base sentence was then paired with two alternate versions for the recognition test: a semantic-manipulation version that changed a key content word or phrase so that the meaning of the sentence was altered, and a voice-change version that transformed active sentences into passive or passive sentences into active while preserving the core meaning as much as possible. For example, a base sentence such as "The scientist reviewed the data carefully" might have a semantic-manipulation counterpart "The scientist ignored the data entirely" (changed meaning) and a voice-change counterpart "The data were reviewed carefully by the scientist" (changed form but preserved meaning). Care was taken to ensure that voice-change transformations preserved the intended propositional content; semantic manipulations were piloted on a separate sample to confirm that they were judged as meaningfully different from the base sentences.

The recognition test included 60 old items drawn from what participants had studied and 60 new foils that were matched in length and syntactic complexity. Among the 60 old items, 20 appeared identical to study items (Identical condition), 20 appeared as semantic-manipulation versions of study items (Semantic condition), and 20 appeared as voice-change versions of study items (Voice-Change condition). The 60 new foils were novel sentences that did not correspond to studied material and were included to assess false alarm rates. Item assignment to test conditions was counterbalanced across participants such that across the sample each base sentence served equally often as an Identical, Semantic, Voice-Change, or New item, ensuring that any item-specific effects could not account for condition-level differences. Sentence presentation order during both study and test phases was randomized independently for each participant.

Procedure had participants seated in a quiet testing room with a computer. They were told that the study would examine memory for sentences and that their reading modality would be experimentally manipulated. Participants in the Silent group received instructions to read each sentence silently to themselves and to try to understand each sentence as they naturally would. Participants in the Aloud group were instructed to read each sentence aloud, speaking clearly and at a comfortable pace; aloud readers were audio-recorded for the purposes of verifying compliance, and the experimenter monitored for overt reading throughout the study phase. Participants in the Combined group were instructed first to read each sentence silently and then to read the same sentence aloud; a brief tone signaled when to switch from silent reading to aloud reading. Study-phase presentation consisted of 60 sentences presented individually on the screen for a fixed duration of 6 seconds each for silent and aloud reading, and for 12 seconds for the Combined condition to accommodate both reading modes; durations were chosen to ensure equivalent exposure time to the content across groups. Participants completed a brief filler task after study, consisting of a simple arithmetic task for 5 minutes, to impose a short retention interval and to reduce immediate rehearsal.

The recognition test was self-paced and presented on the same computer. For each test item, participants indicated whether the sentence had been presented during the study phase ("Old") or not ("New") by pressing labeled keys; they were instructed to use their best judgment and to base responses on memory rather than guessing. Participants were not informed that some test items would preserve meaning while altering form; they were simply asked to judge whether the sentence itself had been presented earlier. After the recognition test, participants completed a short questionnaire assessing their compliance with the modality instructions, their level of attention during study (rated on Likert-type scales), and whether they engaged in additional strategies such as covert rehearsal or mnemonic elaboration. For the Aloud and Combined groups, audio-recordings were checked to confirm that participants in those groups had indeed read aloud during the study phase; any failure to read aloud would have triggered exclusion, but such cases were rare and led to the six exclusions mentioned above.

Design and statistical analyses were specified prior to data collection. The primary dependent variable was the proportion of test items endorsed as old within each condition (hits for identical, semantic, and voice-change items; false alarms for new foils were also recorded). To separate sensitivity from response bias, we computed signal detection measures (d' and criterion c) using hits to Identical items and false alarms to New items, and we also derived separate d' measures for each type of foil comparison when appropriate. The primary statistical test was a mixed-model ANOVA with Reading Modality (Silent, Aloud, Combined) as a between-subjects factor and Sentence Type (Identical, Semantic, Voice-Change) as a within-subjects factor. Planned comparisons contrasted Identical versus Semantic, Identical versus Voice-Change, and Semantic versus Voice-Change within each modality group. We also tested for main effects and interactions; significant omnibus effects were followed up with Tukey-corrected pairwise comparisons. Additional exploratory analyses examined correlations between self-reported attention and recognition performance, comparisons of response times on the recognition test, and comparisons of the Combined group's performance to the average of the Silent and Aloud groups to test for additivity.

All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Assumptions of normality and sphericity were checked; when sphericity was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared for ANOVA effects and Cohen's d for pairwise contrasts. Alpha was set at .05 for primary tests, and exact p-values were reported throughout. The study protocol was approved by the relevant institutional review board, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the experiment.

Results

Descriptive statistics for recognition performance are presented first. Across the full sample, hit rates for Identical items averaged .72 (SD = .14), for Semantic-manipulation items averaged .58 (SD = .16), and for Voice-Change items averaged .43 (SD = .17). False alarm rates to New foils averaged .21 (SD = .11). These raw proportions suggested an ordinal pattern of memory that favored identical repetition, followed by semantic manipulations, with voice-change items recognized least often. Group-wise means indicated a similar pattern within each reading modality: in the Silent group, mean hit rates were .74 (Identical, SD = .13), .61 (Semantic, SD = .15), and .47 (Voice-Change, SD = .16); in the Aloud group, means were .70 (Identical, SD = .15), .56 (Semantic, SD = .16), and .41 (Voice-Change, SD = .17); and in the Combined group, means were .71 (Identical, SD = .14), .57 (Semantic, SD = .17), and .41 (Voice-Change, SD = .18).

A mixed-model ANOVA tested the effects of Reading Modality (between-subjects: Silent, Aloud, Combined) and Sentence Type (within-subjects: Identical, Semantic, Voice-Change) on recognition hit rates. The ANOVA revealed a highly significant main effect of Sentence Type, F(2,162) = 152.38, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .653, indicating large differences in recognition depending on the type of sentence presented at test. The main effect of Reading Modality was not significant, F(2,81) = 0.86, p = .427, partial eta-squared = .021, suggesting no reliable overall difference in recognition across the three modality groups. The interaction between Reading Modality and Sentence Type was also non-significant, F(4,162) = 1.05, p = .381, partial eta-squared = .025, indicating that the pattern of sentence-type differences was consistent across modalities.

Planned pairwise comparisons of Sentence Type collapsed across modality revealed that Identical items were recognized significantly more often than Semantic-manipulation items, t(83) = 7.22, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.79. Semantic items were recognized significantly more often than Voice-Change items, t(83) = 6.41, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.70. Identical items were also recognized significantly more often than Voice-Change items, t(83) = 11.36, p < .001, Cohen's d = 1.24. These contrasts supported the ordinal pattern Identical > Semantic > Voice-Change in hit rates across the sample.

To examine sensitivity independent of bias, we computed d' values using Identical hits and New-foil false alarms as the signal and noise rates, respectively. Average d' across participants was 1.89 (SD = .62), indicating moderate discriminability between old and new sentences. A one-way ANOVA on d' with Reading Modality as the factor produced a non-significant effect, F(2,81) = 0.73, p = .485, consistent with the hit-rate analysis that modality did not affect overall sensitivity. Response criterion c did not differ across groups either, F(2,81) = 0.92, p = .403, indicating similar response biases across reading conditions.

We also computed item- and participant-level signal detection measures that contrasted each type of manipulated test item against New foils to assess whether sensitivity to each test condition varied by modality. d' values calculated for Identical vs. New were high and consistent across groups (Silent M = 1.98, Aloud M = 1.78, Combined M = 1.88), whereas d' for Semantic vs. New was lower (Silent M = 1.24, Aloud M = 1.02, Combined M = 1.08) and d' for Voice-Change vs. New was lowest (Silent M = .56, Aloud M = .31, Combined M = .38). A repeated-measures ANOVA across these d' scores confirmed a main effect of Sentence Type on sensitivity, F(2,162) = 146.05, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .643, but no main effect of Reading Modality, F(2,81) = 1.02, p = .366, nor a significant interaction, F(4,162) = .89, p = .473.

Given Sachs’ prior report of a voice-change advantage under silent reading, we conducted targeted comparisons within the Silent group. In the Silent group, voice-change hit rates (.47) were significantly lower than semantic hit rates (.61), t(27) = 5.12, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.97, and lower than identical hit rates (.74), t(27) = 7.46, p < .001, Cohen's d = 1.41. Thus, contrary to Sachs’ observation, the Silent group in the present study did not show elevated recognition for voice-change items; instead voice-change items were the least well recognized.

We also examined whether the Combined reading condition produced an additive benefit relative to the single-modality conditions by comparing Combined performance to the mean of Silent and Aloud groups. No evidence of additivity was found; Combined group hit rates did not exceed the average of the Silent and Aloud groups for any sentence type (Identical: Combined M = .71 vs. mean of other groups = .72, t(83) = 0.18, p = .858; Semantic: Combined M = .57 vs. mean = .59, t(83) = 0.62, p = .538; Voice-Change: Combined M = .41 vs. mean = .44, t(83) = 0.77, p = .444). These comparisons indicated that performing both silent and aloud reading did not enhance memory beyond reading in a single mode.

Response time (RT) data from the recognition test were examined as an exploratory measure. Median RTs to endorse items as old differed by sentence type, with faster responses to Identical items (M = 1.24 s, SD = .32) than to Semantic items (M = 1.48 s, SD = .35) and Voice-Change items (M = 1.63 s, SD = .41). A repeated-measures ANOVA on median RTs confirmed a main effect of Sentence Type, F(2,162) = 42.89, p < .001, with post-hoc tests indicating all pairwise differences were significant (Identical < Semantic < Voice-Change). Reading Modality did not interact with RTs, F(4,162) = .81, p = .520. These RT patterns suggest that decisions to endorse Identical items were not only more accurate but also faster, consistent with stronger memory signals for verbatim items.

Correlational analyses between self-reported attention during study and recognition performance revealed small positive correlations: higher reported attention was modestly associated with higher overall hit rates (r = .21, p = .048) and higher d' (r = .19, p = .067, trend). However, attention did not interact with Reading Modality, and when included as a covariate in the mixed-model ANOVA, the main effects and interactions involving Sentence Type and Reading Modality remained essentially unchanged.

We also assessed whether participants’ tendency to use covert rehearsal strategies during Silent reading could explain modality null effects. Self-reports indicated that a minority of Silent readers (roughly 32%) reported engaging in covert repetition, whereas nearly all Aloud readers engaged in overt repetition by instruction. Nevertheless, participants who reported covert rehearsal did not differ significantly from those who did not in hit rates for voice-change items (t(26) = 0.77, p = .447), arguing against covert rehearsal as the sole explanation for the lack of modality effects.

Finally, to check for potential ceiling or floor effects that could mask modality effects, we examined distributions of hit rates and false alarms and found no truncation at either extreme; hit rates were well below perfect performance and false alarm rates were well above zero, indicating adequate variability for detecting differences had they been present. Overall, the results showed robust differences across sentence types but no reliable effect of reading modality on recognition performance and no interaction between modality and sentence type.

Discussion

The present study set out to replicate and extend Sachs’ findings regarding the influence of reading modality on memory for sentences, and to test competing predictions about the relative preservation of semantic versus surface form information. The principal outcome was clear: reading modality — whether silent, aloud, or both — did not produce a reliable effect on recognition memory for sentences. Instead, the dominant determinant of recognition performance was the type of test sentence, with Identical items recognized most often, Semantic-manipulation items recognized at an intermediate rate, and Voice-Change items recognized least often. This pattern held across all modality groups and was supported both by raw hit rates and by signal-detection measures of sensitivity. These findings reinforce the notion that memory for sentences favors semantic content at the cost of exact surface form under typical encoding conditions, and they further suggest that overt production via reading aloud does not exert a large, residual mnemonic benefit at the sentence level in this paradigm.

The absence of a main effect of Reading Modality aligns with some prior work that has failed to find robust production effects for complex linguistic materials such as sentences, and it suggests that the production effect, although reliable for single words, may be constrained by material complexity and by the degree to which semantic integration is invoked during encoding. Sentences inherently invite deeper semantic processing because of their compositional structure and propositional content; this semantic processing may overshadow modality-specific advantages that accrue from vocalization. That is, when comprehenders construct rich semantic representations while reading, the contributions of additional articulatory or auditory information provided by overt reading may be relatively small compared to the robust semantic trace that already supports recognition decisions. This interpretation is consistent with dual-processing accounts in which surface-form contributions are subordinate to meaning-level representations for sentence material.

A striking divergence from Sachs’ earlier report is our finding that voice-change items were the least recognized among the three test types, including in the Silent reading condition. Sachs reported that voice-change items were consistently recognized highly when read silently, a pattern our data do not support. We considered several possible explanations for this discrepancy. Methodological differences between studies may account for divergent findings; for example, the operationalization of "voice change" could differ in subtle but important ways. In the present study, voice-change manipulations were active-passive alternations crafted to preserve propositional content as much as possible, while subtle paraphrastic differences or additional syntactic alterations in Sachs’ materials might have yielded different degrees of perceived similarity to studied items. Moreover, procedural aspects such as presentation timing, the instructions provided about what to remember, the retention interval, and the nature of the filler task could influence the degree to which participants attend to or encode surface features. It is also possible that sample characteristics, including education level, reading habits, and familiarity with particular syntactic constructions, may moderate sensitivity to voice changes.

Another avenue for reconciling the discrepancy concerns the role of criterion shifts versus true sensitivity differences. Sachs’ account emphasized recognition rates, which can conflate response biases with memory discriminability. In our study, we explicitly computed signal-detection measures and found consistent patterns in d' alongside raw hit rates, suggesting that the observed ordinal pattern across sentence types reflects genuine sensitivity differences rather than mere shifts in response criterion. This strengthens the conclusion that participants in this paradigm were less able to discriminate voice-change foils from new items, which is consistent with a reliance on gist-level encoding that fails to preserve surface syntactic details.

The current findings resonate with Bühler’s early theoretical suggestion that people tend to remember the general meaning of an utterance rather than its precise form. Bühler’s perspective anticipated later empirical findings and has been influential in shaping theories of language memory and comprehension. Our data provide contemporary empirical support for this thesis at the sentence level: semantic manipulations were more readily recognized than voice-change manipulations, indicating that meaning alterations leave a more robust trace that aids recognition, whereas superficial syntactic alterations lead to lower endorsement rates. Moreover, the fact that Identical items produced the best recognition performance underscores that verbatim repetition creates the strongest available cue for recognition, but only when the surface form is preserved does recognition approach higher levels.

From a theoretical standpoint, these results contribute to ongoing debates about the nature of representation in language memory. The predominance of semantic-level encoding evident here suggests that models emphasizing abstract propositional representations capture critical aspects of sentence memory. Such models posit that comprehension processes extract the gist or propositional content of sentences, storing that content in memory while allowing surface articulatory and syntactic details to decay more rapidly. Our findings are consistent with such a representational hierarchy. At the same time, the clear advantage for Identical items indicates that surface traces are not entirely absent and that under conditions where they are preserved — for example, when the surface form is repeated — they can markedly boost recognition performance. Thus, a nuanced view is warranted in which both semantic and surface representations are encoded but with differential durability and utility for later recognition tasks.

The lack of a production advantage for reading aloud also informs theories about the mechanisms that underlie the production effect. Our results suggest that the production effect may depend on the level of processing required by the task: when comprehension imposes deep semantic processing, as in sentence reading, the incremental distinctiveness conferred by vocal production may be insufficient to produce measurable mnemonic benefits. Alternatively, the production effect may be contingent on item-level distinctiveness: producing single words creates highly distinct episodic traces that are readily discriminable at test, whereas producing sentences does not yield the same degree of item-level distinctiveness because sentences share semantic overlap and structural patterns that reduce distinctiveness. Future theoretical work should clarify the boundary conditions under which production benefits accrue and articulate mechanisms that explain dependency on material complexity and task demands.

The practical implications of these results extend to educational strategies and mnemonic techniques. For learners aiming to remember the gist or meaning of textual material, silent reading that emphasizes comprehension may be sufficient and efficient. However, for tasks that require verbatim recall — for example, memorizing exact wording for legal or religious texts, scripts, or precise formulations — our data suggest that simply reading aloud is unlikely to substantially enhance verbatim memory unless additional strategies are employed. Techniques that explicitly focus attention on surface features, such as instruction to attend to word order, repeated rehearsal of exact wording, or use of mnemonic anchors that tie specific surface phrases to cues, may be necessary to preserve surface form. The Combined reading condition in our study did not provide added benefit, indicating that mere repetition in both modes does not substitute for targeted strategies designed to encode surface detail.

Several limitations of the present study warrant careful consideration and guide directions for future research. First, although we included an audio-recording compliance check for aloud readers, it is inherently difficult to ensure that participants in the Silent condition did not occasionally subvocalize or engage in covert rehearsal; such behaviors could reduce differences across modalities. Future studies might use physiological measures such as electromyography to detect subvocal activity and better verify silent-reading compliance. Second, the voice-change manipulation in our study was operationalized as active-passive alternations; other kinds of surface manipulations, such as word-order changes, lexical substitution that preserves grammatical form, or prosodic manipulations, might yield different patterns and should be tested to map the generality of the current findings. Third, the retention interval in this study was relatively short; longer delays and different consolidation periods, including overnight retention, could alter the balance between gist and surface form memory, perhaps favoring gist even more as time elapses. Fourth, participant samples were drawn largely from a university population and community volunteers, and generalization to diverse populations with varying literacy and language backgrounds should be empirically examined.

Several promising directions for future research emerge from the current work. One line of inquiry would systematically manipulate the depth of processing at study by providing explicit instructions to attend to surface details versus to focus on meaning, to determine whether instructed attention can modulate the relative preservation of surface form. Another approach would vary the retention interval to examine how temporal decay differentially affects semantic and surface traces. Additionally, neuroimaging or electrophysiological methods could be used to track encoding and retrieval processes and to identify neural signatures associated with semantic versus surface-form encoding; such data could reveal whether different neural systems support the two levels of representation and whether overt production recruits distinct sensorimotor circuits that contribute to memory. Cross-linguistic studies could assess whether languages with differing morphological or syntactic properties show similar patterns, thereby testing the universality of the gist-over-form tendency.

Replication efforts are particularly important in light of the discrepancy between our findings and Sachs’ earlier report regarding voice-change effects under silent reading. We encourage further replications that implement high procedural control, preregistration of analysis plans, and larger sample sizes to increase statistical power and to clarify potential moderators of modality effects. Implementing pre-registered multi-lab replications would be a particularly robust approach to resolving conflicting findings and establishing the reliability of observed effects. Such coordinated efforts could also test whether specific methodological choices — such as presentation rate, degree of syntactic alteration in voice-change items, and the nature of instructions — systematically influence outcomes.

In conclusion, the present study replicates and extends prior research by demonstrating that reading modality does not have a reliable effect on recognition memory for sentences, and that semantic changes are more likely to be recognized than voice-change manipulations. The findings support the theoretical perspective that gist-level representations are privileged in sentence memory and that surface-form information is less durable and less influential in recognition decisions under typical encoding conditions. The lack of a production advantage for sentence materials suggests boundary conditions for the production effect and highlights the need to tailor mnemonic strategies to the level of representation required by the task. Future research that refines methodological control, probes boundary conditions, and employs converging behavioral and neuroscientific measures will further elucidate the mechanisms by which the human cognitive system encodes, stores, and retrieves linguistic information.
