Introduction

Tattoos have become an increasingly visible form of body modification in many contemporary societies. What was once a marker of subcultural identity or deviance has gradually entered mainstream fashion and self-expression. Parallel to this cultural shift, psychological research has sought to understand who chooses to get tattoos and what personality characteristics are associated with tattooed individuals. Early research in this area tended to focus on negative stereotypes and traits, often framing tattooed individuals in terms of deviance, risk-taking, or nonconformity. Over time, this perspective broadened as the prevalence of tattoos increased and diversity among tattooed people grew. Recent work has therefore examined a wider array of personality characteristics, including both negative and positive dimensions. Among the personality traits that have been investigated in relation to tattoos, extraversion has been of particular interest. Extraversion is a fundamental dimension of personality, characterized by sociability, assertiveness, and a tendency to seek out stimulation and social engagement. Theoretically, extraversion might be linked to tattooing for several reasons. Extraverted individuals may be more likely to use visible body art as a form of social signaling. They may also be more comfortable with attention and social scrutiny, and more willing to take impulsive actions that have social consequences. Conversely, tattooing is not necessarily only the domain of extraverts. Introverts might also use tattoos for private meanings or as a controlled form of self-expression that does not require continual social interaction. Therefore, the relationship between tattooing and extraversion is not trivially obvious. Empirically, studies have reported mixed findings. Some investigations found higher levels of extraversion among individuals with tattoos compared to those without, suggesting that tattooing is associated with social boldness and a higher need for social engagement. Other studies, however, did not find such associations or found associations with other personality dimensions such as openness to experience or sensation seeking rather than with extraversion specifically. These mixed findings point to potential heterogeneity in both the behaviors under study and in the samples used across research. One methodological issue that may contribute to inconsistent results is the way researchers classify and compare groups. Many studies have contrasted those with any tattoo against those without any tattoo. Such binary classification may obscure meaningful variation among people who do not currently have tattoos but are considering them. Individuals who are contemplating tattooing may share some characteristics with those who already have tattoos, such as openness to new experiences or a greater tolerance for risk. At the same time, they may differ from both current tattooed individuals and those who have no interest in tattooing. If a substantial proportion of "non-tattooed" respondents are in fact considering tattoos, this could dilute group differences and lead to null findings. Despite this plausible mechanism, few studies have explicitly included a category for those considering tattoos. An explicit focus on the "considering" group could therefore help clarify the nature of personality differences related to tattooing. Another factor that complicates interpretation of prior findings is the way in which the number and visibility of tattoos are measured. Some researchers have used a dichotomous indicator of tattoo presence, while others have used counts of tattoos or coded for visibility and size. The number of tattoos might plausibly relate to personality in a linear fashion, with more tattoos associated with higher levels of traits such as extraversion or sensation seeking. Alternatively, there may be no monotonic relationship, and any association may be driven by presence versus absence rather than by number. Methodological variability in measurement likely contributes to disparate results across studies. A third consideration concerns sample composition and recruitment methods. Many investigations have relied on convenience samples, including university student populations or clinic samples, which may be atypical in terms of age, social background, or motivations for tattooing. The social meanings of tattoos also vary across age cohorts and cultural groups, and these contextual factors likely influence both the prevalence of tattooing and its associations with personality. Younger cohorts may view tattoos as less transgressive, and in such groups the link between extraversion and tattooing may be weaker or different than in older cohorts for whom tattoos remain a stronger marker of countercultural identity. In addition, gender differences may moderate associations between tattooing and personality. Men and women may differ in the prevalence of tattooing, the social reception of tattoos, and the motivations for getting tattooed. Such gender differences could influence observed relationships between tattoos and extraversion and may account for variability across samples if gender is not adequately controlled. Beyond these methodological factors, theoretical accounts for why extraversion might be linked to tattooing remain underspecified. One account emphasizes the signaling function of visible tattoos. From this perspective, extraverted individuals use tattoos to communicate aspects of identity to others and to facilitate social interactions, which is consistent with theories that link extraversion to social motivation and reward sensitivity. Another account highlights novelty seeking and sensation seeking, traits related to but distinct from extraversion. Individuals high in sensation seeking might be drawn to tattoos as a novel and intense experience. These motivations could intersect with extraversion in complex ways, such that an association between tattoos and extraversion might be secondary to more proximal factors like openness to experience or impulsivity. A further line of thought emphasizes identity consolidation and meaning-making. Tattoos can serve as symbols of personal history, values, or group membership. Individuals seeking identity expression may vary in extraversion, and tattoos may be chosen for reasons that are not inherently social. Thus, any simple association between tattoos and extraversion must be situated within a broader understanding of motivations for tattooing. Given the mixed evidence and the conceptual ambiguities, empirical work that addresses specific methodological issues is warranted. In particular, research that distinguishes among those with tattoos, those without tattoos, and those actively considering getting tattoos may help clarify whether extraversion differs across these meaningful groups. Such an approach allows for the possibility that those considering tattoos form an intermediate category rather than fitting neatly into either binary classification. If the considering group has extraversion scores similar to the tattooed group, this could suggest that personality differences predate tattoo acquisition. Conversely, if they resemble the non-tattooed group, personality differences might emerge only after tattoo acquisition or be related to other factors. The present study was designed to address these issues directly. It focused on associations between extraversion and tattooing while including a specific category for individuals who are considering tattoos but do not yet have them. The study also measured the number of tattoos to test whether quantity was associated with extraversion. The sample size was modest but sufficient for detecting medium-sized effects, and analyses included both correlational and group-comparison approaches. The central hypotheses were twofold. First, it was hypothesized that extraversion would not be reliably correlated with the number of tattoos. This hypothesis reflected the expectation that a simple count of tattoos might not capture the complex motivations and social signaling associated with tattooing. Second, it was hypothesized that extraversion scores would differ across the three groups, such that those with tattoos would show higher extraversion than those without tattoos, and those considering tattoos would show intermediate scores. The rationale for the second hypothesis drew on theories that link tattoos to social signaling and identity expression and on previous reports indicating higher levels of extraversion among tattooed individuals. By examining both the number of tattoos and the categorical status of tattooing intentions, the study aimed to clarify the nature of the relationship between extraversion and tattooing. The study further explored whether any observed group differences would hold after considering potential confounds such as age and gender. Although the study was not powered to test complex interactions, it included basic checks to ensure that any group differences were not trivially due to demographic imbalances. In sum, the study addressed a gap in the literature by explicitly including a group of participants considering tattoos and by analyzing both the number of tattoos and categorical tattooing status in relation to extraversion. This design permitted tests of two plausible pathways linking extraversion and tattooing: a linear relationship with the number of tattoos, and a categorical relationship tied to tattooing status. The findings were expected to shed light on why prior research has produced inconsistent results and to suggest possible directions for future investigation.

Methods

A total of 106 participants took part in the study. Participants were recruited through a combination of university participant pools, social media postings, and community outreach to local organizations. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. The recruitment materials stated that the study concerned personality and self-expression and that participation involved completing an online questionnaire. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old and to be fluent in English. No financial compensation was provided, and participants were informed that they could withdraw at any time prior to submission of the questionnaire. The study procedures were reviewed and approved by an institutional ethics committee prior to data collection. All participants provided informed consent by clicking an acknowledgment box at the start of the online survey. The questionnaire was administered via a secure online survey platform. The instrument comprised demographic questions, items assessing tattoo status and number, and a standardized self-report measure of extraversion. Demographic items included age, gender, and whether the participant was a student. Gender options included male, female, and other; a small number of participants selected other and were included in analyses without further disaggregation due to sample size limitations. Tattoo-related items first asked whether the participant currently had any tattoos. Response options were "Yes, I have one or more tattoos," "No, I do not have any tattoos," and "No, but I am considering getting a tattoo." This categorical item was used to create the primary grouping variable for analyses. Participants were then asked to indicate the exact number of tattoos they currently had, with a numeric response allowed. For participants who indicated that they did not have tattoos and were considering getting one, the number of tattoos was recorded as zero for correlation analyses that included the full sample. Participants who indicated that they had tattoos were also asked follow-up questions about tattoo visibility and approximate size, but these variables were treated as exploratory and are not central to the principal analyses reported here. Extraversion was measured with a 12-item self-report scale adapted from widely used personality inventories. Items assessed facets of extraversion such as sociability, assertiveness, preference for social activities, and energy levels. Participants rated each item on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Items were summed and divided by the number of items to produce an average extraversion score on the original five-point scale. Higher scores indicated higher levels of extraversion. The scale has been shown in prior research to have acceptable psychometric properties, and internal consistency in the present sample was examined. The online survey included attention-check items to reduce careless responding. Two such items asked participants to select a specific response option to demonstrate attentiveness. Participants who failed attention checks were excluded from analyses. In total, three responses were excluded prior to analysis for failure to complete the questionnaire or failure to pass attention checks, and the remaining 106 responses constituted the analytic sample. Data screening procedures were followed prior to statistical analysis. Continuous variables were assessed for missingness, skewness, and kurtosis. Little's test for missing data patterns was considered, and where small amounts of missing data occurred on individual extraversion items, mean imputation was used for that participant if fewer than 10% of items were missing. No participant had more than one or two missing items on the extraversion measure. The distribution of extraversion scores was examined visually with histograms and statistically with skewness and kurtosis statistics. The distribution approximated normality sufficiently to proceed with parametric analyses. The number of tattoos variable was positively skewed, as expected, because most participants had zero or only a few tattoos. Transformations were considered but ultimately analyses using the number-of-tattoos variable relied on Pearson correlation coefficients with robust standard errors and were supplemented by nonparametric correlation checks where relevant. Group sizes for the categorical tattoo status variable were determined by participants' responses. In the sample of 106 participants, 40 individuals reported having at least one tattoo, 45 reported having no tattoos and not considering getting one, and 21 reported that they did not have tattoos but were considering getting one. These group sizes provided reasonable power to detect medium-sized effects in group comparisons, though detection of small effects would be limited. The primary analyses consisted of two parts. First, Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to examine the relationship between extraversion scores and the number of tattoos. Correlations were computed both for the full sample (treating number of tattoos as zero for non-tattooed individuals) and for the subsample of participants who reported at least one tattoo. Second, an analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare extraversion scores across the three tattoo status groups: those with tattoos, those without tattoos, and those considering tattoos. Prior to conducting the ANOVA, assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated. Levene's test was used to assess equality of variances across groups. Where assumptions were met, a one-way ANOVA was executed. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using the least significant difference (LSD) method to probe pairwise differences among the three groups. The LSD procedure was chosen for its sensitivity in detecting pairwise differences given the modest number of groups, with the caveat that it does not correct as conservatively for multiple comparisons as some other methods. Because age and gender are potential confounds, exploratory analyses included analyses of covariance (ANCOVA) to determine whether any group differences remained after adjusting for these demographic variables. Gender was coded dichotomously for these exploratory models (male vs. female) due to small cell sizes in the "other" category. Additional exploratory analyses examined whether the effect of tattoo status on extraversion varied by gender, but the sample size did not afford robust tests of interaction effects. Internal consistency of the extraversion measure was evaluated with Cronbach's alpha. All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Alpha was set at .05 for all primary tests, and exact p-values are reported. Effect sizes are reported where appropriate, including eta-squared for ANOVA effects and Cohen's d for pairwise comparisons. Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations for extraversion scores within each tattoo status group, were computed to aid interpretation of group differences.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the sample and the main study variables are presented first. The sample consisted of 106 participants with a mean age of 24.7 years (SD = 6.1). The majority of participants identified as female (n = 60), with 46 identifying as male and 0 selecting other in the final analyzed sample after the exclusions were applied. Of the 106 participants, 40 reported having one or more tattoos, 45 reported having no tattoos and not considering one, and 21 reported that they did not have any tattoos but were considering getting one. The mean extraversion score for the full sample was 3.60 (SD = 0.67) on the five-point scale. Internal consistency for the 12-item extraversion scale in the present sample was good, with Cronbach's alpha = .84. This level of reliability supported use of the composite extraversion score in subsequent analyses. The distribution of extraversion scores approximated normality. Skewness for the extraversion distribution was -0.10 (SE = 0.24), and kurtosis was -0.56 (SE = 0.48), indicating no severe departure from normality. Visual inspection of histograms confirmed these findings. The number of tattoos variable was positively skewed, with a median of 0 and an interquartile range of 0 to 2. For descriptive purposes, among the 40 participants who reported at least one tattoo, the mean number of tattoos was 3.1 (SD = 2.6). Because most participants did not have tattoos, analyses that treated number of tattoos as a continuous predictor across the entire sample included many zeros and were supplemented by correlational analyses restricted to the tattooed subgroup. The first set of analyses examined correlations between the number of tattoos and extraversion. When considering the full sample and treating number of tattoos as zero for non-tattooed participants, the Pearson correlation between number of tattoos and extraversion was r = .12. This correlation was not statistically significant, p = .21. When the correlation was computed only among participants who reported at least one tattoo (n = 40), the association between the number of tattoos and extraversion was r = .09 and was also not statistically significant, p = .58. A nonparametric Spearman correlation for the full sample yielded rho = .11, p = .25, consistent with the Pearson result. Thus, across both approaches, there was no evidence of a meaningful linear relationship between the number of tattoos and extraversion. The second set of analyses compared extraversion scores across the three tattoo status groups. Group-specific descriptive statistics showed that participants with tattoos (n = 40) had a mean extraversion score of 3.85 (SD = 0.60). Participants without tattoos and not considering one (n = 45) had a mean extraversion score of 3.45 (SD = 0.70). Participants considering tattoos (n = 21) had a mean extraversion score of 3.62 (SD = 0.68). These means suggested that individuals with tattoos tended to have higher extraversion scores than those without tattoos, with the considering group falling intermediate between the other two groups. The assumption of homogeneity of variances across the three groups was evaluated with Levene's test. Levene's statistic was 1.15 with p = .32, indicating that the assumption of equal variances was reasonable. Given the normality and homogeneity checks, a one-way ANOVA was conducted to test for differences in extraversion across tattoo status groups. The ANOVA yielded a statistically significant effect of group on extraversion, F(2, 103) = 4.65, p = .012. The effect size for the group difference was modest, with eta-squared = .083, indicating that approximately 8.3% of the variance in extraversion scores was associated with tattoo status. To clarify the nature of the group differences, pairwise comparisons using the LSD method were performed. The comparison between those with tattoos and those without tattoos and not considering one revealed a mean difference of 0.40 in extraversion scores in favor of the tattooed group. This difference was statistically significant, t(103) = 2.69, p = .008. The corresponding Cohen's d for this contrast was 0.60, which is conventionally interpreted as a medium effect size. The comparison between those with tattoos and those considering tattoos showed a mean difference of 0.23 in extraversion scores, but this difference did not reach statistical significance, t(103) = 1.48, p = .14. The comparison between those considering tattoos and those without tattoos showed a mean difference of 0.17, which was also not statistically significant, t(103) = 1.16, p = .25. In summary, pairwise tests indicated a significant difference in extraversion between those with tattoos and those without tattoos, but the considering group did not differ significantly from either of the other two groups. Because age and gender could potentially confound the relationship between tattoo status and extraversion, exploratory analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of the group effect. Age was modestly correlated with extraversion in the present sample (r = -0.18, p = .07), suggesting a weak tendency for younger participants to be slightly more extraverted. Gender comparisons indicated that mean extraversion scores were slightly higher for females (M = 3.68, SD = 0.65) than for males (M = 3.49, SD = 0.69), but this difference was not statistically significant, t(104) = 1.48, p = .14. An ANCOVA including tattoo status as the independent variable and age and gender as covariates produced a significant main effect of tattoo status on extraversion, F(2, 100) = 4.02, p = .02, indicating that the group difference persisted after adjusting for these demographic variables. Post-hoc contrasts in the ANCOVA framework mirrored the ANOVA findings: the difference between tattooed and non-tattooed participants remained significant (p = .01), while differences involving the considering group remained non-significant. An exploratory examination of possible gender moderation of the tattoo status effect was conducted by computing descriptive means within gender-by-status cells. The cell sizes were small for some combinations, limiting interpretability. Visual inspection suggested that the pattern of higher extraversion among tattooed individuals held for both genders, but a formal interaction test in a two-way ANOVA did not yield a statistically significant interaction between gender and tattoo status, F(2, 100) = 0.58, p = .56. Given the small sample sizes within some cells, the power for detecting interactions was insufficient. Finally, robustness checks were performed using nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis tests to compare extraversion ranks across tattoo status groups. The Kruskal-Wallis test indicated a significant difference across groups, χ2(2) = 7.52, p = .023, consistent with the ANOVA results. Pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests showed a significant difference between tattooed and non-tattooed participants (p = .01) but non-significant differences involving the considering group. Overall, the pattern of results was consistent across parametric and nonparametric approaches: no reliable correlation between number of tattoos and extraversion, and a categorical difference between tattooed and non-tattooed individuals, with the considering group occupying an intermediate and statistically ambiguous position.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between tattoos and extraversion by employing both correlational and group-comparison approaches and by explicitly including a group of individuals who were considering getting a tattoo. The primary results were twofold. First, there was no significant correlation between the number of tattoos and extraversion, whether the analysis included the full sample with many zeros or was restricted to those with at least one tattoo. Second, there was a significant difference in mean extraversion scores across tattoo status groups. Specifically, individuals with tattoos had higher extraversion scores than those without tattoos, whereas those considering tattoos had intermediate scores that did not differ significantly from either of the other groups. These findings contribute to the literature by clarifying two points. The first point is that the quantity of tattoos alone does not appear to be linearly related to extraversion in this sample. The absence of a significant correlation between number of tattoos and extraversion suggests that simply counting tattoos does not capture the psychological processes that link tattooing and personality. This null result aligns with a view that the decision to get one or more tattoos may be driven by a variety of motives, not all of which are tied to social engagement or the behavioral tendencies encapsulated by extraversion. For instance, some individuals may acquire multiple tattoos for private or commemorative reasons, which might be unrelated to sociability. Conversely, a single highly visible tattoo might be used to signal identity in a social context, which could relate more strongly to extraversion despite being only a single tattoo. Therefore, the lack of a monotonic relationship between number and personality is theoretically plausible and suggests that future work should consider richer characterizations of tattoo characteristics, including visibility, meaning, and motivations, rather than relying solely on counts. The second point is that tattoo status as a categorical variable was associated with extraversion, with tattooed individuals scoring higher on average than non-tattooed individuals. This finding supports theories that link tattooing to social signaling and identity expression that are more readily embraced by extraverted individuals. Extraverts' greater comfort with attention and social interaction may make the visible display of body art more attractive or less aversive. On the other hand, this result does not imply that extraversion is the sole or dominant determinant of tattoo acquisition. The effect size was modest, and a substantial overlap in extraversion scores existed across groups, indicating that many tattooed individuals are not particularly high in extraversion and that many non-tattooed individuals report relatively high extraversion. Thus, while extraversion may be a contributing factor, it is only one piece of a multifaceted motivational picture. The intermediate position of the considering group yields a nuanced insight into why prior research has been inconsistent. If researchers have classified considering individuals variably — sometimes as non-tattooed, sometimes excluding them, or sometimes failing to assess intention altogether — then group comparisons may differ in substantive ways across studies. In the present sample, the considering group did not differ significantly from either the tattooed or the non-tattooed group. This ambiguity suggests that intention-to-tattoo is a heterogeneous state. Some individuals who are considering tattoos may already have personality profiles similar to those who eventually become tattooed, whereas others may be contemplating tattoos for situational reasons that do not reflect stable personality tendencies. Consequently, the inclusion or exclusion of the considering group could attenuate observed group differences in different studies, thereby contributing to mixed findings in the literature. The exploratory ANCOVA controlling for age and gender indicated that the group difference in extraversion remained after adjusting for these demographics. This result makes it less likely that the observed differences were merely an artifact of age or gender composition across groups. Nonetheless, the present study's design limits the extent to which causal claims can be made. The cross-sectional nature of the data precludes inferences about whether extraversion predisposes individuals to get tattoos, whether tattoo acquisition influences extraversion, or whether both are influenced by unmeasured third variables such as cultural context or peer group norms. Longitudinal research is needed to establish temporal precedence. The modest sample size and convenience sampling approach also limit the generalizability of the findings. The study primarily included young adults, many of whom were university-affiliated, and the social meaning of tattoos in this population may differ from that in older cohorts or in different cultural settings. For example, in cohorts where tattoos are highly normative, the association with extraversion might be weaker because tattoos are less distinctive as a form of social signaling. Future research should therefore include more diverse samples in terms of age, socioeconomic status, and cultural background to test the boundary conditions of the observed associations. Another limitation concerns the measurement of tattoos. Although the study asked participants to report the number of tattoos and to indicate whether they were considering getting a tattoo, it did not systematically assess the visibility, size, style, or personal meaning of the tattoos in sufficient detail to allow nuanced analyses. As noted earlier, these features may be more proximate predictors of associations with extraversion than sheer quantity. For instance, highly visible tattoos on the forearms or neck may be more strongly associated with extraversion than hidden tattoos on the torso. Similarly, tattoos acquired for social reasons, such as group affiliation, may link more strongly to extraversion than tattoos acquired for private memorial purposes. Therefore, subsequent studies should incorporate more detailed measures of tattoo characteristics and motivations. The study also relied on self-report measures for both tattoo status and personality. Self-report is standard in personality research, but it introduces the possibility of reporting biases. Individuals may present themselves in ways they perceive as socially desirable or may differ in the extent to which they interpret questionnaire items consistently. Triangulating self-report with informant reports or behavioral indicators of extraversion (e.g., observed social behavior) could strengthen conclusions. Despite these limitations, the findings have several implications for future research. First, researchers studying personality correlates of tattooing should assess tattoo intentions explicitly and consider classifying participants who are considering tattoos as a separate group. Doing so can reduce misclassification and allow for more nuanced hypotheses about the developmental pathways linking personality and tattooing. Second, future studies should move beyond simple counts and incorporate measures of tattoo visibility, placement, thematic content, and motivational context. These dimensions may help explain why some individuals with few tattoos are highly extraverted while others with many tattoos are not. Third, longitudinal designs that follow individuals from pre-intention through tattoo acquisition and beyond could illuminate whether personality traits predict tattoo uptake or whether the experience of being tattooed affects personality or social behavior over time. Fourth, qualitative work could complement quantitative analyses by exploring individuals' subjective motivations and social experiences related to tattoos, thereby clarifying how extraversion interacts with identity and social signaling processes in the lived experiences of tattooed individuals. Fifth, examining moderating variables such as cultural norms, occupational contexts, and peer group influences could reveal contingencies that shape the relationship between tattoos and personality. For example, workplaces with strict appearance policies may alter the relationship between extraversion and tattoo visibility choices. Theoretical implications of the present study include the idea that social signaling and identity expression are plausible pathways linking extraversion to tattooing but that these pathways are neither necessary nor sufficient explanations for tattoo acquisition. The modest effect size suggests that extraversion may predispose some people to use visible body art as a social tool, but other psychological and situational factors are clearly involved. Integrative models that consider multiple motives — social, aesthetic, commemorative, and impulsive — are likely to provide a more comprehensive account. Methodologically, the study underscores the importance of careful group demarcation and measurement choices in research on bodily practices. Small methodological variations, such as whether or not to ask about tattoo intentions, can meaningfully alter group compositions and therefore study conclusions. In practical terms, understanding personality correlates of tattooing can inform clinicians, employers, and policymakers about the diverse motivations underlying tattoo acquisition and the fallacy of treating tattooed individuals as a homogeneous group with uniformly deviant or risky traits. Such understanding promotes a more nuanced and less stigmatizing perspective on bodily modification practices. In conclusion, this study found that tattooed individuals scored higher on extraversion than non-tattooed individuals, while those considering tattoos exhibited intermediate and statistically unclear extraversion levels. The number of tattoos was not related to extraversion. These results suggest that personality differences between tattooed and non-tattooed people exist but are subtle and may be impacted by how researchers classify individuals who are considering tattoos. Future research should use longitudinal and multi-method approaches, broaden sample diversity, and measure tattoo features and motivations in more detail to further unpack the complex relationship between tattooing and personality.
