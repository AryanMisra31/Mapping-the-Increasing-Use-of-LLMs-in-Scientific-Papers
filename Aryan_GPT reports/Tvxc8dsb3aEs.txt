Introduction

Sociability is a central domain of human behavior that shapes how people form, sustain, and derive benefit from social relationships. People who score high on sociability tend to seek social contact, engage with others, and derive reward from interpersonal interactions. The development of sociability begins early in life and continues to be shaped by a complex interplay of familial, peer, and broader environmental influences. Among early environments, the family has long been considered a crucial context for social learning and practice. Sibling relationships in particular have been proposed as a natural laboratory for developing social skills because siblings provide frequent, prolonged, and emotionally salient opportunities for interaction. Through play, conflict resolution, cooperation, and the negotiation of shared resources, siblings offer repeated practice in communication, perspective taking, and emotion regulation. The sibling environment therefore has the potential to influence stable dispositions related to sociability that persist into adulthood. Gender differences in social behavior are also widely discussed in the literature on sociability. Differences between men and women in tendencies to seek social contact, disclose emotions, and engage in affiliative behaviors have been described in various populations. These gender-linked patterns are often attributed to both biological predispositions and sociocultural expectations that shape socialization practices from early childhood onward. For example, boys and girls may be encouraged to engage in different forms of play, to adopt differing emotional expressiveness, or to cultivate distinct interactional styles, all of which could plausibly influence adult sociability. The literature examining the combined influence of sibling experiences and gender on sociability yields a nuanced picture. Some theoretical accounts suggest that sibling experiences might interact with gender, such that the presence and number of siblings differentially shape sociability for males and females. For instance, girls with older siblings might gain social skills through role modeling, whereas boys might benefit from competitive play that fosters assertive social approaches. Conversely, other accounts emphasize the possibility that sibling number simply provides more opportunities for social interaction regardless of gender. Empirically, however, findings have been mixed. Several studies have reported modest associations between having siblings and higher measures of sociability or related constructs, but effect sizes tend to be small and are often attenuated when controlling for other family background variables. Other studies have reported little or no association between sibling number and adult sociability, suggesting that direct influences of siblings may be weaker than commonly presumed. Beyond sibling quantity, the quality of sibling interaction, birth order, age spacing, and gender composition of siblings appear relevant and may account for variability in observed associations. Temperament is another important domain linked to sociability, and temperament constructs are often used to operationalize stable individual differences that influence social approach and engagement. Measures of temperament typically include dimensions such as emotionality, activity, sociability, and impulsivity. The sociability dimension in temperament inventories captures tendencies toward social approach and interest in interacting with others. Emotionality, by contrast, reflects proneness to negative affect and emotional reactivity. The relationship between emotionality and sociability is theoretically complex because high emotional reactivity could either inhibit social approach due to anxiety and withdrawal or increase social behavior through seeking support and comfort. Thus, examining emotionality alongside sociability can illuminate whether affective tendencies modulate social approach tendencies. The present study was motivated by these intersecting literatures on family context, gender, and temperament. We sought to examine whether the number of siblings and gender predict self-reported sociability among a sample of university students. We also sought to evaluate whether emotionality, one temperament dimension, is associated with sociability in a theoretically meaningful subgroup. Specifically, we were interested in women who report having siblings because some prior theoretical perspectives suggest that social learning dynamics in sister-sibling contexts may be particularly influential for female social development. University students represent an important group to study because they are typically in a developmental period where social networks and interpersonal skill deployment are salient for academic and personal outcomes. However, university samples are also relatively homogeneous in educational attainment and often in age, which can reduce variability and may influence detectability of effects. In designing this study we considered alternate ways of operationalizing sibling experience. One straightforward operationalization is to treat sibling number as a dichotomy contrasting only children with those who have one or more siblings. This approach simplifies analysis and addresses a common practical question about whether being an only child is associated with social functioning differences. An alternative approach is to treat sibling number as a continuous count or as multiple categories, but these approaches require larger samples to detect graded effects. Given practical constraints and the sample size available, the dichotomous classification was selected as a parsimonious and interpretable strategy. We also considered how gender would be operationalized. For this study gender was characterized by self-reported male or female identification. Although contemporary thinking recognizes that gender exists on a spectrum and that nonbinary identities are important to include, the available sample in this study was predominantly composed of participants who identified as male or female, and statistical power to examine nonbinary groups would have been insufficient. The temperament measure selected for this study includes discrete subscales for sociability and emotionality among others. Using a validated multi-item scale allows for reliable assessment of these constructs in a concise format. By focusing on the sociability subscale as the primary dependent variable, we align measurement with the theoretical construct of interest. We hypothesized that women would report higher sociability compared with men, consistent with common findings of higher affiliative tendencies in women on average. We also hypothesized that participants who have siblings would report higher sociability than only children, reflecting the presumed social learning benefits of sibling interactions. Moreover, we predicted that gender and sibling number might interact, such that the effect of sibling status on sociability would be more pronounced for women than for men. Finally, we hypothesized that within women who have siblings, emotionality would be positively associated with sociability, reflecting the idea that emotionally reactive women may seek social contact and support from sibling relationships, which would be reflected in higher sociability scores. Testing these hypotheses provides a basis for evaluating the relative influence of familial structure and temperamental affectivity on self-reported sociability in a contemporary student population. We also aimed to assess the robustness of findings given the constraints of sample size and measurement. It is important to note that null findings are informative when interpreted in the context of statistical power, measurement limits, and sample characteristics. Therefore, the present study was designed not only to test directional hypotheses but also to provide descriptive information about effect sizes and confidence intervals that can inform future research planning. The broader theoretical significance of this work lies in clarifying whether relatively immutable aspects of early family composition, such as sibling number, or more stable individual dispositions, such as temperament, are predominant influences on adult sociability. If sibling number has a robust effect, then family planning and sibling experiences would warrant greater emphasis in models of social development. If gender differences are robust, then gendered socialization remains a central lens for understanding adult social behavior. Alternatively, if neither sibling number nor gender accounts for meaningful variance, then attention should shift to other determinants such as personality traits beyond basic temperament, educational experience, peer networks, and cultural norms. The current investigation therefore addresses a question with both theoretical and practical implications for developmental and personality psychology. By clarifying the pattern of associations among gender, sibling number, emotionality, and sociability, this study aims to refine our understanding of the antecedents of social approach behavior. The remainder of this article details the methods used to collect and analyze data, reports the results of the statistical tests, and discusses the implications and limitations of the findings for theory and future research.

Methods

Participants were one hundred university students recruited from introductory psychology courses and campus-wide announcements. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 30 years, with a mean age of 20.9 years and a standard deviation of 2.1 years. The sample included 54 participants who identified as female and 46 participants who identified as male. The majority of participants were undergraduate students enrolled in their first through fourth years of study. Participants reported their sibling status as either only child or having one or more siblings. In the full sample, 28 participants identified as only children and 72 participants reported having at least one sibling. The study excluded potential participants who reported a history of severe developmental disorder that would impede comprehension of the survey items. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and received course credit or a small monetary reimbursement for their time. Data collection took place using an online survey platform accessible via a secure link. Participants completed a demographics questionnaire followed by the temperament inventory. The temperament inventory used in this study is a brief, self-report measure that assesses four dimensions: emotionality, activity, sociability, and impulsivity. Each subscale is composed of multiple items that participants rate on a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The sociability subscale captures tendencies to seek out and enjoy social interaction, to be comfortable with others, and to prefer engaging in group activities. The emotionality subscale assesses tendencies toward emotional reactivity, nervousness, and susceptibility to negative feelings. The inventory was selected for its brevity and conceptual alignment with the constructs of interest. Items were presented in randomized order to reduce potential response sets linked to item sequencing. Scale scores were computed by averaging item responses within each subscale after reversing any negatively keyed items as specified by the inventory guidelines. Internal consistency reliability was examined using Cronbach’s alpha for the sociability and emotionality subscales. The sociability subscale demonstrated acceptable reliability in the present sample, and the emotionality subscale demonstrated similar acceptable reliability. Prior to hypothesis testing, descriptive statistics were computed for age, gender, sibling status, sociability scores, and emotionality scores. Data were screened for missing values and response patterns indicative of inattentive responding. Participants with substantial missing data or implausibly short completion times were excluded from analysis; however, no participants met exclusion criteria for poor data quality in this sample. Analytic procedures were planned to address three primary questions. First, whether gender and sibling status independently predict sociability scores. Second, whether there is an interaction between gender and sibling status in predicting sociability. Third, whether emotionality is associated with sociability within the subsample of women who report having siblings. To address the first two questions, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted with gender (male, female) and sibling status (only child, has siblings) as between-subjects factors and the sociability score as the dependent variable. Prior to running the ANOVA, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were evaluated visually using histograms and formally using Levene’s test. Given the sample sizes in the cells, the ANOVA procedure is robust to moderate deviations from normality. Effect sizes for ANOVA effects were reported using partial eta squared to provide an estimate of the proportion of variance associated with each factor. Confidence intervals for key means and effects were also calculated to aid interpretation. For the third question, Pearson correlational analyses were conducted between emotionality and sociability within the subgroup of women who reported having siblings. In addition, a simple linear regression predicting sociability from emotionality was computed in that subgroup to examine the magnitude and direction of the association while providing an estimate of explained variance. All tests used an alpha level of .05 for determining statistical significance. Given the modest sample size, attention was placed upon effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than reliance solely on p-values. A post hoc sensitivity assessment was performed to estimate the smallest effect size detectable with 80% power given the sample sizes in our two primary groups. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Results were tabulated and double-checked for accuracy. Throughout, results were interpreted in light of the sample characteristics and measurement properties. Ethical approval for the study procedures was obtained from the relevant institutional review board prior to data collection. Participants were debriefed at the conclusion of their participation and provided with contact information for psychological support services in the event that any survey items raised concerns. Data were stored on secure servers and all reporting was conducted at an aggregate level to protect participant confidentiality.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the full sample are presented first. The mean sociability score for the entire sample was 3.78 on the 1-to-5 scale, with a standard deviation of 0.66. The mean emotionality score for the full sample was 2.90, with a standard deviation of 0.71. For female participants, the mean sociability score was 3.82 with a standard deviation of 0.62. For male participants, the mean sociability score was 3.74 with a standard deviation of 0.71. Only children had a mean sociability score of 3.75 with a standard deviation of 0.65, while participants with siblings had a mean sociability score of 3.79 with a standard deviation of 0.66. The distribution of sociability scores was approximately normal across the full sample and within subgroups, as indicated by skewness and kurtosis values within acceptable ranges and by visual inspection of histograms. Levene’s test for equality of variances did not indicate significant heterogeneity of variances across gender or sibling status groups, with p-values above .10 in both cases. The two-way ANOVA testing main effects of gender and sibling status, and their interaction on sociability, was conducted next. The main effect of gender on sociability was small and not statistically significant, F(1,96) = 0.45, p = .50, partial eta squared = .005. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference between females and males included zero and was relatively narrow, indicating that large gender differences in sociability were not supported by the data. The main effect of sibling status (only child vs. has siblings) on sociability was also not significant, F(1,96) = 0.02, p = .88, partial eta squared < .001. The 95% confidence interval for the difference between only children and participants with siblings likewise included zero. The interaction of gender and sibling status was not significant, F(1,96) = 0.12, p = .73, partial eta squared = .001. Cell means and standard deviations are presented here for transparency: only-child females (n = 15) had a mean sociability of 3.80 (SD = 0.63); female participants with siblings (n = 39) had a mean sociability of 3.83 (SD = 0.62); only-child males (n = 13) had a mean sociability of 3.68 (SD = 0.67); and male participants with siblings (n = 33) had a mean sociability of 3.77 (SD = 0.74). Given the small cell sizes for some categories, follow-up pairwise comparisons were interpreted cautiously. None of the pairwise contrasts between gender-sibling status cell means approached statistical significance at the .05 level. Overall, the ANOVA results indicate that neither gender nor sibling number, nor their interaction, accounted for appreciable variance in sociability scores in this sample. Because one of the study’s theoretical interests was whether emotionality is associated with sociability among women who have siblings, correlational and regression analyses were conducted in that subgroup. The subgroup of women with siblings included 39 participants. In this subgroup, the Pearson correlation between emotionality and sociability was r = .12, p = .45. The magnitude of this correlation was small and statistically non-significant. A simple linear regression predicting sociability from emotionality in this subgroup yielded an unstandardized B coefficient of 0.08, a standardized beta of .12, and an R-squared of .01, with a p-value of .45 for the predictor. This indicates that emotionality explained approximately 1% of the variance in sociability among women with siblings in this sample, which is negligible in practical terms. To provide context, correlations between sociability and the other temperament subscales were computed across the full sample. Sociability correlated modestly with activity (r = .22, p = .03) and had a small negative correlation with impulsivity (r = -.15, p = .14), though only the correlation with activity reached conventional statistical significance in this sample. Emotionality and impulsivity were moderately positively correlated (r = .31, p = .002), consistent with temperament constructs tapping related affective and regulatory domains. Given the null findings for gender and sibling status, exploratory analyses were conducted to examine whether age or year in university were related to sociability. Neither age nor year in university showed significant associations with sociability scores (age r = .05, p = .62; year r = -.07, p = .48). These exploratory results suggest that the observed lack of differences in sociability was not simply a byproduct of age or academic seniority differences within the sample. A post hoc sensitivity analysis indicated that, with the available sample sizes and a conventional alpha of .05, the two-way ANOVA had adequate power (80%) to detect medium-sized effects corresponding roughly to partial eta squared values of approximately .06 or larger. The observed effect sizes for gender, sibling status, and their interaction were well below this threshold, which implies that the study was not well powered to detect very small effects but was adequately powered to detect medium or larger effects. Consequently, the present null effects are most consistent with the absence of medium or large effects of gender or sibling status on sociability in this sample. Confidence intervals for key parameters were reported to provide an estimate of precision. For example, the 95% confidence interval around the mean difference in sociability between females and males spanned from -0.28 to 0.44 on the sociability scale, reinforcing the conclusion that any true effect, if present, is likely small. Similarly, the confidence interval for the association between emotionality and sociability within women who have siblings covered both negative and positive values and encompassed zero, indicating uncertainty about the presence of a reliable association in either direction. Taken together, the primary analyses did not support hypotheses that gender and sibling number have meaningful main effects or interactive effects on self-reported sociability in this university student sample. Additionally, emotionality did not show a significant positive association with sociability among women with siblings. These results are summarized and interpreted further in the Discussion, which addresses potential explanations for the null findings, considers limitations, and outlines directions for future research.

Discussion

The present study tested hypotheses concerning the relations among gender, sibling number, and sociability among university students and further examined whether emotionality was associated with sociability among women who have siblings. The primary findings were null with respect to effects of gender and sibling status on sociability and a null association between emotionality and sociability within the specified female-sibling subgroup. These findings suggest that in this sample of young adults with comparable educational attainment, neither being male versus female nor being an only child versus having siblings predicted self-reported sociability to a meaningful extent. The lack of an interaction between gender and sibling status further indicates that the socializing role of siblings does not appear to differ systematically across gender categories in this sample. These results warrant careful consideration in light of theoretical expectations and the existing literature. Theoretical accounts that emphasize the role of early family composition in shaping social behavior posit that siblings provide repeated opportunities for social learning that could result in enduring differences in sociability. Yet, the null findings here suggest that simple counts of siblings may be an insufficient proxy for the social experiences that shape adult sociability. In other words, having siblings in itself may not be a strong determinant of adult sociability when measured at a coarse level such as only child status versus having any siblings. Instead, more nuanced aspects of sibling experience—such as the quality of sibling relationships, the frequency and types of interactions, birth order, gender composition of the sibling set, age spacing between siblings, and the presence of conflict or support—may be the critical factors that influence social developmental trajectories. These qualitative and structural features were not captured in the present study and thus represent likely sources of unobserved variance. The null gender effect also invites reflection. Gender differences in sociality are often reported in population-level studies and are frequently attributed to socialization practices and societal expectations. However, the magnitude and direction of gender differences can be context-dependent. In a university sample, especially among students who self-select into higher education, gendered patterns of sociability may be attenuated. Educational environments may promote convergence in social practices and expectations across genders, particularly in modern contexts where prosocial engagement and collaborative skills are widely emphasized for academic success. In addition, the measures used to operationalize sociability capture self-reported tendencies rather than observed social behavior, and gender differences in self-perception or reporting norms could obscure behavioral differences. Measurement considerations are therefore important for interpreting null results. The temperament inventory used in this study provides a concise assessment of sociability and emotionality, but no single self-report measure can capture all relevant facets of social approach behavior. The sociability subscale assesses general tendencies to seek social contact, but it may not be sensitive to situational variability or domain-specific forms of sociability such as affiliative behavior in romantic relationships, workplace networking, or group-based social leadership. Furthermore, self-reports are susceptible to social desirability and to differential insight into one’s own patterns of sociability. Behavioral measures, informant reports, and real-world social network metrics could provide complementary evidence that might reveal associations not evident in self-report data. The lack of association between emotionality and sociability among women with siblings deserves attention as well. Theoretically, emotionality could either facilitate seeking social contact (e.g., through help seeking and affiliation) or inhibit social approach (e.g., through social anxiety and withdrawal). The null association suggests that within this subgroup emotional reactivity does not translate uniformly into greater or lesser social approach as assessed by the sociability scale. This outcome underscores the need to consider moderating variables that may influence how emotionality manifests in social behavior. For example, emotion regulation strategies, attachment security, and social competence could moderate whether emotional reactivity leads to approach versus avoidance. Women with effective regulation skills may channel emotionality into affiliative behaviors, whereas those with less effective regulation may retreat socially. The present study did not assess such moderating constructs, which constrains interpretation. Several methodological factors may explain the null effects. First, sample characteristics: university students represent a relatively homogeneous sample in terms of age, socioeconomic aspiration, and educational attainment. Homogeneity reduces between-person variance in many social and psychological constructs and thereby diminishes the likelihood of detecting associations with predictor variables like sibling number. Second, statistical power: although the sample of 100 participants was adequate to detect medium-sized effects, it was underpowered to detect small effects. If the true effects of gender or sibling number on sociability are small, the present study would have limited ability to detect them. Third, the operationalization of sibling number as a dichotomy (only child vs. has siblings) may have masked graded effects that would be observable with a more fine-grained measure of sibling structure. Fourth, the cross-sectional and correlational design precludes causal inference. Even if an association had been observed, directionality and potential confounding variables would require careful assessment in longitudinal and experimental designs. Despite these limitations, the null findings are theoretically meaningful because they point to the possibility that other factors are more important determinants of adult sociability than gender or mere sibling presence. For instance, broad personality traits such as extraversion are strong predictors of sociability. Educational experiences and peer contexts shape social skill development substantially during adolescence and early adulthood. Cultural norms and socioeconomic conditions can differentially reward or discourage sociable behavior. Moreover, contemporary communication technologies and social media have altered the landscape of social interaction, perhaps reducing the relative influence of family composition on adult sociability. The findings therefore suggest that future research should expand the set of predictors considered when seeking to account for variability in sociability. Several concrete directions for future research emerge from this study. First, larger and more diverse samples should be used to increase generalizability and statistical power. Samples that vary broadly in age, cultural background, and educational attainment would allow tests of whether sibling influences on sociability vary across contexts. Second, more granular measurement of sibling experience is needed. Future studies should assess sibling quality, frequency of interaction across developmental stages, birth order, age spacing, and gender composition of siblings to identify which aspects of sibling systems, if any, are most consequential for social development. Third, longitudinal designs that follow children into adolescence and adulthood would clarify temporal relations and potentially causal pathways linking sibling experience to later sociability. Fourth, incorporation of behavioral measures and social network analyses would complement self-report measures and provide richer indicators of actual sociability in naturalistic settings. Fifth, examination of potential moderators and mediators such as attachment security, emotion regulation, parental socialization practices, and peer influences would refine theoretical models about how early experiences translate into adult social tendencies. Sixth, studying samples in different cultural contexts would illuminate whether cultural norms about sibling roles and gender influence the magnitude and direction of associations. The present findings also bear on applied considerations. If simple sibling counts are not strong predictors of adult sociability, interventions aimed at fostering social skills should focus more on skill-building, emotion regulation, and peer-based experiences rather than on family composition per se. Educational programs that promote cooperative learning, communication skills, and conflict resolution may be particularly effective in bolstering sociability regardless of family background. In addition, clinicians working with individuals who perceive themselves as socially isolated should consider assessing a broader array of personal and contextual factors rather than attributing social difficulties to being an only child or to one’s gender. It is also worth emphasizing that null results do not imply that siblings never matter; rather, they suggest that simple counts of siblings do not capture the complex ways in which family relationships contribute to social development. In sum, this study found no evidence that gender or sibling number, nor their interaction, predicted self-reported sociability in a sample of university students, and found no association between emotionality and sociability among women with siblings. These results indicate that other determinants, including broader personality traits, qualitative family experiences, educational and peer contexts, and cultural influences, are likely more powerful in shaping adult sociability. Future research that employs larger and more diverse samples, longitudinal designs, refined measurement of sibling processes, behavioral assessment of sociability, and testing of potential moderators and mediators will be important for building a more complete understanding of the antecedents of sociability.
