Introduction

Human memory is a constructive process that integrates perceptual input with prior knowledge, expectations, and post-event information. The malleability of memory has profound implications for everyday cognition and for applied domains such as the legal system where eyewitness testimony can determine outcomes. Early experimental work in cognitive psychology established that memories are not veridical recordings but are reconstructive, often subject to distortion through a variety of mechanisms. Among the most influential demonstrations of memory distortion is the work showing that wording of questions can shape subsequent recollections of an event. Loftus and Palmer’s seminal experiments showed that simple changes in verb choice within a question about an automobile collision influenced participants’ speed estimates and later recollections of broken glass. This finding drew attention to the "misinformation effect," wherein post-event information, even when relatively subtle or merely suggestive, can alter memory reports. The classic demonstration by Loftus and Palmer specifically used verbs that varied in implied severity, such as "contacted," "hit," "bumped," "collided," and "smashed," and reported systematic differences in estimated speed corresponding to verb intensity. The Loftus and Palmer paradigm has since become a staple in eyewitness memory research and an archetype of how suggestive questioning can bias memory reports.

Subsequent research has extended and refined our understanding of how leading questions influence memory. Experiments have examined the boundary conditions of verb-induced distortions, including the timing of questioning, the nature of the stimuli (video clips, staged events, slides), the delay between event and questioning, and individual differences among witnesses. The evidence collectively indicates that post-event suggestive information can influence both immediate judgement and later memory, sometimes producing enduring distortions. Some studies have reported that misinformation is more likely when original encoding is weak, when attention during the event is divided, or when source monitoring abilities are compromised. However, other work has shown that strong initial encoding or repeated exposure to the original event can resist the influence of leading information. These nuances highlight that the phenomenon is not monolithic and that moderator variables shape the magnitude and persistence of suggestive influences on memory.

Confidence is another crucial variable that has been tightly linked to memory performance and its legal relevance. The confidence people express about their memories is often taken by laypersons and some practitioners as a proxy for accuracy, and higher confidence is sometimes thought to indicate that a memory is more reliable. Empirical work has examined the confidence-accuracy relationship across recognition and identification tasks and has yielded a nuanced picture. On average, within-person correlations between confidence and accuracy can be positive, especially for high-quality retrieval conditions; however, confidence can also be inflated by post-event feedback, social reinforcement, or metacognitive biases. In the context of suggestive questioning, confidence might play a dual role: it could either moderate susceptibility to misinformation or be itself altered by post-event information. For instance, receiving confirming feedback after providing a response can bolster confidence and potentially consolidate an inaccurate recollection. Conversely, individuals with more robust baseline confidence or better metacognitive monitoring might be less swayed by leading questions. Thus, the interplay between confidence and suggestive influences constitutes an important avenue for understanding when and why memory distortions occur.

Experimental efforts to manipulate confidence have used various approaches, including providing false feedback about performance on ostensibly related tasks, employing outcome-based reinforcement, or eliciting self-evaluations that prime high or low confidence states. False feedback paradigms are notable because they can induce transient changes in self-perceived competence and confidence without altering actual memory content. For example, participants who are given positive feedback about their performance on an unrelated task subsequently report greater confidence in their judgments and may behave differently in subsequent cognitive tasks. These manipulations have been used to study metacognitive effects on decision making, susceptibility to persuasion, and memory conformity. In the context of eyewitness memory, however, the efficacy and consequences of such confidence inductions have been less consistently studied. It remains an open empirical question whether transiently altering an individual's confidence state via feedback will modulate the degree to which leading questions influence memory reports.

There are at least two theoretical reasons to expect that confidence manipulations might influence the effect of leading questions. One account, derived from source monitoring frameworks, suggests that individuals evaluate memory traces and infer their origins based in part on subjective qualities such as vividness and confidence. If a confidence induction increases the subjective strength of a memory trace or inflates the person’s general confidence in their own judgments, the person might be more likely to discount post-event suggestions and rely on their own recollection. Under this view, a high confidence induction could serve a protective function against suggestive misinformation. An alternative account, however, predicts the opposite: that increased confidence might produce overreliance on internal cues or a closure effect that makes individuals less receptive to corrective information and more willing to assimilate suggestive post-event cues when these appear to cohere with an inferred narrative. On this account, higher confidence could paradoxically increase the assimilation of misinformation if the suggestive content appears to confirm an internally constructed interpretation.

Empirical tests of these competing possibilities have produced mixed results. Some studies have found that higher confidence reduces susceptibility to misinformation, particularly when confidence reflects accurate metacognitive monitoring. Other studies have reported that induced confidence increases adherence to suggested information when the suggestions are plausible or when individuals seek cognitive closure. These discrepant findings underscore the need to examine the conditions under which confidence acts as a protective versus permissive factor with respect to suggestive influences.

The present study aimed to investigate whether experimentally induced confidence moderates the effect of leading questions on memory for a witnessed automobile accident. Specifically, the study adapted the original Loftus and Palmer methodology by presenting all participants with the same short video clip of a car collision and then asking a critical speed estimate question that contained either a high-severity verb ("smashed") or a low-severity verb ("contacted"). In addition to the standard leading question manipulation, participants were randomly assigned to receive false feedback intended to induce either a high or low confidence state prior to the memory question. The confidence induction was implemented using an ostensibly diagnostic cognitive task followed by scripted positive or negative feedback about performance. Self-reported confidence was collected both as a manipulation check and as a continuous covariate for secondary analyses.

Based on the theoretical accounts and the mixed empirical literature, two primary hypotheses were specified. First, consistent with Loftus and Palmer, it was predicted that participants exposed to the high-severity verb would estimate higher speeds than participants exposed to the low-severity verb. Second, it was predicted that induced confidence would moderate the verb effect, but the direction of moderation was left exploratory due to conflicting theoretical predictions. A protective hypothesis stated that high induced confidence would attenuate the verb effect relative to low induced confidence, whereas an assimilation hypothesis stated the opposite, that high confidence would amplify the verb effect.

A secondary set of exploratory analyses examined whether self-reported confidence measured after the manipulation correlated with the accuracy of memory reports and whether confidence mediated any observed relationship between verb condition and speed estimate. In addition, the study included routine checks for potential confounds such as age, gender, attention check performance, and the time spent viewing the video clip.

To ensure adequate power to detect moderate effects, a sample of 60 participants was recruited and randomly assigned to the four experimental conditions in a 2 (verb severity: smashed vs contacted) × 2 (induced confidence: high vs low) between-subjects design. The sample size was chosen as a pragmatic compromise given resource constraints and consistent with similar laboratory-based replications of the Loftus and Palmer paradigm that have successfully detected medium-sized effects with comparable sample sizes. The study was conducted online to increase accessibility and to reflect contemporary trends in behavioral research recruitment, while the procedures sought to retain the essential manipulations and measurement fidelity of the original experiment. All procedures were approved by an institutional review board, participants provided informed consent, and data quality measures including attention checks were implemented.

In summary, the current investigation attempted to elucidate how momentary changes in confidence might interact with leading question framing to shape eyewitness-like memory reports. The study built on a rich literature documenting the influence of post-event information on memory and aimed to clarify the role of metacognitive states in moderating suggestibility. The following sections describe the method, the results of the statistical analyses, and an interpretation of the findings in light of theoretical frameworks and prior empirical work.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures were designed to assemble an online sample reflective of typical undergraduate-age and community participants engaged in similar cognitive tasks. Sixty participants were included in the final analyses. Participants were recruited via an online research panel and were compensated with a modest monetary payment commensurate with the estimated time to complete the survey. Inclusion criteria required participants to be aged between 18 and 65, fluent in English, and to report normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Before data collection, the study received approval from the relevant institutional review board and was conducted in accordance with ethical standards for research with human participants. Informed consent was obtained electronically from each participant prior to beginning the experiment.

The experimental design was a 2 (verb severity: smashed vs contacted) × 2 (induced confidence: high vs low) between-subjects design, with participants randomly assigned to one of the four conditions. Randomization was performed by the online survey platform using a built-in random assignment function to ensure balanced allocation. Group sizes were approximately equal, with 15 participants per cell in the final sample. The study used a single video stimulus for all participants to ensure that any observed differences in speed estimates would be attributable to the experimental manipulations and not to differences in the stimulus.

Materials included a short video clip depicting a car accident adapted from publicly available stimuli commonly used in memory research. The clip lasted approximately 30 seconds and showed two vehicles approaching an intersection and colliding. The clip contained no ambient audio that would provide additional cues to speed and showed clear visual information about the collision to allow for plausible speed estimation. The critical question immediately following the clip asked participants to estimate the speed of the vehicles at the moment of impact. The wording of the critical question varied depending on the verb severity condition; participants were asked either "About how fast were the cars going when they smashed into each other?" (high-severity verb) or "About how fast were the cars going when they contacted each other?" (low-severity verb). Participants typed a numerical estimate in miles per hour into a response box.

To manipulate induced confidence, a bogus cognitive task was used that ostensibly assessed analytic reasoning. Participants completed a brief series of five practice arithmetic and pattern-matching items presented as a "calibration" task. After completing these items, participants were randomly assigned to receive scripted feedback indicating that they had performed either extremely well (high confidence induction) or poorly (low confidence induction) compared to others. The feedback was framed as normative performance information and used a percentile statement (e.g., "Your performance placed you in the 90th percentile compared to prior participants" for the high confidence condition or "Your performance placed you in the 10th percentile compared to prior participants" for the low confidence condition). The feedback was accompanied by a short message that highlighted strengths (high confidence) or weaknesses (low confidence) to reinforce the manipulation. The feedback procedure was identical in form across conditions except for the valence of the message.

As a manipulation check, participants completed a three-item self-report measure of current task confidence immediately after receiving the feedback and prior to watching the video. Items assessed perceived competence, certainty about upcoming responses, and general confidence on a seven-point Likert scale. The three items were averaged to create a composite confidence score with higher values indicating greater induced confidence. After the memory task, participants also reported their confidence in their speed estimate on a seven-point scale as an additional metric of subjective confidence related to the critical judgment.

Additional measures included attention checks embedded in the survey, self-reported engagement with the video (rated on a five-point scale), and demographic questions (age, gender, education). Time stamps were recorded to ensure participants were attending to the video for its entire duration. Participants who failed the attention checks, who reported not watching the video, or whose completion times suggested inattention were excluded from the final analyses. Four participants were excluded for failing attention checks, yielding the final sample of 60.

The procedure began with informed consent and demographic questions, followed by the bogus cognitive task used for the confidence induction. After the cognitive task and the delivery of feedback, participants completed the confidence manipulation check. Participants then viewed the video clip of the car accident embedded in the survey. The video was presented only once and participants were instructed not to rewind or pause. Immediately after viewing the video, participants received the leading question with either the high-severity or low-severity verb and provided a numerical estimate of speed. Participants then rated their confidence in that estimate and completed several filler questions before debriefing. The debriefing explained the purpose of the feedback manipulation and assured participants that the performance feedback had been experimentally programmed and did not reflect their actual abilities.

Statistical analyses were pre-registered before data inspection and included primary analyses using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with speed estimate as the dependent variable and verb severity and induced confidence as between-subjects factors. Planned contrasts compared the smashed and contacted conditions within each confidence level. Manipulation checks were evaluated using independent samples t-tests on the composite confidence score to confirm that the feedback altered self-reported confidence. Secondary analyses included Pearson correlations between self-reported confidence and speed estimates and exploratory regression analyses to test for potential covariate effects of age and engagement. Assumptions of ANOVA were evaluated through tests of normality (Shapiro-Wilk) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test), and influential outliers were assessed using standardized residuals and Cook’s distance.

Effect sizes were reported using partial eta squared for ANOVAs and Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons. Where appropriate, 95% confidence intervals for means and mean differences were provided. Given the possibility of null results and the interest in quantifying evidence in favor of the null hypothesis, Bayesian analyses were conducted as complements to frequentist tests. Bayesian ANOVAs and t-tests were performed using default priors to compute Bayes factors comparing the null model to models including main effects and interactions. All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and all code and anonymized data were archived in a secure repository in accordance with open science practices.

Results

Descriptive statistics for speed estimates by condition are presented first to provide an overview of the data prior to inferential testing. Across all participants, the mean speed estimate was 41.0 miles per hour with a standard deviation of 10.2. In the low induced confidence/contacted verb condition (n = 15), the mean speed estimate was 38.2 mph (SD = 9.1). In the low induced confidence/smashed verb condition (n = 15), the mean speed estimate was 42.3 mph (SD = 10.4). In the high induced confidence/contacted verb condition (n = 15), the mean speed estimate was 40.1 mph (SD = 10.0). In the high induced confidence/smashed verb condition (n = 15), the mean speed estimate was 41.5 mph (SD = 11.2). These cell means show a pattern in which the smashed verb is associated with numerically higher speed estimates than the contacted verb in both confidence conditions, but the differences are modest.

Before conducting the primary ANOVA, the manipulation check for the induced confidence manipulation was evaluated. The composite confidence score obtained after the false feedback but before viewing the video indicated that participants in the high induced confidence condition reported substantially greater confidence (M = 6.12, SD = 0.67) than participants in the low induced confidence condition (M = 3.08, SD = 0.89). An independent samples t-test confirmed that this difference was statistically significant, t(58) = 14.89, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 3.85. This result demonstrates that the feedback manipulation successfully altered participants’ transient self-reported confidence prior to the memory task.

Given successful manipulation of state confidence, the primary analysis examined the effects of verb severity and induced confidence on speed estimates using a two-way ANOVA. The ANOVA tested main effects for verb severity (smashed vs contacted) and induced confidence (high vs low), as well as their interaction. Assumption checks indicated that speed estimates were approximately normally distributed within each group based on Shapiro-Wilk tests that did not reach significance after adjusting for multiple comparisons (all p > .05) and that Levene’s test did not indicate heterogeneity of variances across the four groups, F(3,56) = 1.02, p = .39. No extreme outliers were identified based on standardized residuals greater than ±3 or Cook’s distance values exceeding conventional thresholds, so all 60 participants were retained in the ANOVA.

The main effect of verb severity was not statistically significant, F(1,56) = 1.90, p = .17, partial eta squared = .033. The mean speed estimate for the smashed verb condition (M = 41.9, SD = 10.7) was only numerically higher than the contacted verb condition (M = 39.3, SD = 9.5), and the 95% confidence interval for the mean difference included zero (mean difference = 2.6 mph, 95% CI [-1.2, 6.4]). The main effect of induced confidence was likewise not statistically significant, F(1,56) = 0.50, p = .48, partial eta squared = .009, with the high induced confidence group yielding a mean speed estimate of 40.8 mph (SD = 10.7) and the low induced confidence group yielding a mean of 40.5 mph (SD = 9.9). The induced confidence by verb severity interaction was also not significant, F(1,56) = 0.02, p = .89, partial eta squared < .001. The interaction effect size was extremely small and the 95% confidence interval for the interaction term included values close to zero, indicating no reliable evidence that induced confidence moderated the effect of verb severity on speed estimates.

Planned pairwise comparisons examined the simple effect of verb within each induced confidence condition. In the low induced confidence group, the smashed condition (M = 42.3, SD = 10.4) was not significantly different from the contacted condition (M = 38.2, SD = 9.1), t(28) = 1.16, p = .26, Cohen’s d = 0.43, 95% CI for the mean difference = [-2.1, 10.7]. In the high induced confidence group, the smashed condition (M = 41.5, SD = 11.2) was not significantly different from the contacted condition (M = 40.1, SD = 10.0), t(28) = 0.36, p = .72, Cohen’s d = 0.13, 95% CI for the mean difference = [-4.9, 7.3]. These pairwise comparisons confirm that the numerical differences observed were not statistically reliable within either confidence condition.

Given the null results in the classical ANOVA framework, Bayesian analyses were conducted to quantify evidence in favor of the null relative to models including the experimental factors. A Bayesian ANOVA comparing models with main effects and interaction indicated that the null model (no effects) was favored over the model including verb severity alone with a Bayes factor BF01 = 3.8, suggesting moderate evidence for the null hypothesis relative to the verb effect. The Bayesian comparison also favored the null over the model including the induced confidence main effect with BF01 = 6.1, indicating substantial evidence for the null for the confidence effect. For the interaction term, the Bayes factor BF01 = 8.2 provided substantial evidence against the presence of a confidence × verb interaction. These Bayesian results corroborate the frequentist findings and suggest that the data are more consistent with no effect of the manipulations on speed estimates than with the alternative hypotheses.

Additional exploratory analyses investigated the relationship between self-reported confidence in the speed estimate (collected after the memory response) and the numerical estimate. Across all participants, the Pearson correlation between post-response confidence and speed estimate was r = .05, p = .70, indicating no meaningful linear relationship. Correlations computed separately within each cell also failed to reveal reliable associations (all |r| < .15, all p > .40). A regression analysis that included age, gender, and engagement with the video as covariates found that none of these variables significantly predicted speed estimates, and inclusion of the covariates did not alter the non-significant main or interaction effects of the experimental manipulations.

To assess the possibility that the induced confidence manipulation might have affected the variance of speed estimates even if the mean differences were negligible, variance comparisons were performed. Levene’s test comparing the variance of speed estimates across the high and low induced confidence groups yielded a non-significant result, F(1,58) = 0.36, p = .55, indicating no significant change in variability attributable to the confidence induction. Similar variance comparisons between smashed and contacted verb groups were also non-significant.

Finally, participant debriefing responses were reviewed for any indication that participants suspected the feedback was false or that the tasks were related. A small proportion of participants (3 out of 60) mentioned in open-ended responses that the performance feedback seemed generic but none reported that they believed the video or the leading question manipulation was linked to the feedback. Excluding these three participants from the ANOVA did not meaningfully change the pattern of results, and the main and interaction effects remained non-significant. In sum, the results indicate that although the induced confidence manipulation successfully altered self-reported state confidence, it did not impact speed estimates nor did it moderate the modest numerical effect of leading verb severity on speed judgments.

Discussion

The present study examined whether experimentally induced confidence would moderate the influence of leading question wording on eyewitness-like speed estimates in a replication-style adaptation of Loftus and Palmer’s paradigm. The core finding was straightforward: the confidence induction successfully altered participants’ self-reported state confidence prior to witnessing the event, but this change in confidence did not translate into differential susceptibility to the leading question manipulation. Specifically, although the smashed verb condition produced numerically higher speed estimates than the contacted verb condition consistent with the direction of the classical Loftus and Palmer effect, these differences were small and not statistically significant. Moreover, induced confidence had neither a main effect on speed estimates nor an interactive effect with verb severity. Bayesian analyses supported the lack of an effect, providing moderate to substantial evidence in favor of the null hypothesis relative to alternative models that included verb severity or confidence effects. These results suggest that, under the specific conditions implemented in this study, transient changes in participants’ general confidence via false feedback do not substantially influence how post-event leading language shapes numerical speed estimates.

The successful manipulation of transient confidence prior to the memory task is an important methodological result. The feedback procedure produced a large and significant difference in pre-event self-reported confidence between the high and low induced confidence groups, demonstrating that the manipulation produced the intended subjective state change. This finding is consistent with a large body of work showing that false feedback can robustly shift perceived competence and confidence in the short term. The absence of downstream effects on memory reports therefore cannot be attributed to a failure to induce the targeted confidence state. Instead, the data suggest that the cognitive processes underlying the integration of leading linguistic cues into speed estimates may be relatively robust to short-lived changes in global confidence, at least as induced by the current feedback protocol.

One interpretation of the null interaction is that the mechanisms by which leading questions influence speed estimates are primarily perceptual or semantic and may operate independently of broad metacognitive states such as general confidence. The verb effect observed by Loftus and Palmer can be conceptualized as arising from interrogative suggestibility or from framed inference processes whereby an emotionally or semantically loaded verb activates certain speed schemas. If the integration of such semantic cues into a numeric estimate relies mainly on automatic or stimulus-driven processes, then transient shifts in general confidence might not alter the degree to which these semantic cues influence the final judgment. Under this account, confidence might be more influential when the post-event information involves explicit social feedback about the memory trace itself, rather than global feedback about unrelated task performance.

An alternative account is that the null result reflects a boundary condition related to the type of confidence induction used. The feedback task was framed as a general diagnostic of analytic ability and succeeded at shifting general confidence ratings. However, such generalized confidence may be insufficient to change domain-specific metacognitive processes that govern eyewitness judgments. Confidence is multifaceted and can differ across task domains; for example, confidence about one's reasoning abilities may not strongly transfer to confidence about visual episodic memories. Consequently, a manipulation that targets memory-specific confidence—such as confirming or disconfirming feedback about the eyewitness judgment itself—might yield different outcomes. Future studies could test whether feedback directly tied to memory performance, rather than generic cognitive ability, would more effectively moderate susceptibility to leading questions.

The online modality of the present study may also contribute to the small effect sizes observed for the verb manipulation. Loftus and Palmer’s original experiments were conducted in controlled laboratory settings, often with participants viewing the stimulus under consistent environmental conditions and with experimenters present. In contrast, the present study was conducted online where participants viewed the clip in uncontrolled environments with potential distractions and variable display conditions. Such variability can increase measurement noise and reduce the ability to detect subtle effects. Although attention checks and engagement ratings were used to screen for poor data quality, residual heterogeneity in viewing conditions may nonetheless attenuate effect sizes. This consideration suggests that further replication efforts might benefit from a laboratory-based approach or from online designs that incorporate stricter viewing environment controls.

Another plausible explanation for the lack of significant verb effects is the sample size and the subsequent statistical power. Although the sample of 60 participants provided reasonable power to detect moderate effects, it may have been insufficient to detect smaller, yet theoretically meaningful effects. The effect sizes observed in the current data were small, and detecting them with conventional power (e.g., 0.80) would require substantially larger samples. This observation underscores the importance of considering expected effect sizes and conducting power analyses informed by meta-analytic estimates when designing studies in this area. Future work that pools data across multiple sites or that uses larger online samples could provide greater sensitivity to detect subtle moderator effects.

Individual differences in memory and metacognition could also play a role in the observed null results. The confidence induction might have interacted with participants’ baseline metacognitive tendencies, such that only certain subgroups are affected in ways that alter susceptibility to leading questions. For instance, individuals with high trait suggestibility, low baseline memory accuracy, or impaired source monitoring might be particularly vulnerable to the combination of low induced confidence and leading language. The current study did not include detailed assessments of trait suggestibility or source monitoring ability, and exploratory analyses with available demographic covariates did not reveal clear moderators. Future investigations could incorporate measures of personality, cognitive ability, and trait metacognition to identify vulnerable subpopulations and to specify the boundary conditions for confidence effects on suggestibility.

From a theoretical perspective, the present findings contribute to the literature by clarifying that not all forms of induced confidence reconfigure susceptibility to misinformation in the same way. This nuance is significant for models that posit a straightforward protective role for confidence. The data suggest that while confidence may correlate with accuracy under certain conditions, experimentally raising or lowering general confidence does not guarantee corresponding changes in how people integrate post-event suggestions. The result highlights the need to distinguish between trait and state confidence, as well as between domain-general and domain-specific confidence, in theories about metacognitive moderation of memory distortion.

The findings also have practical implications for forensic contexts where feedback about memory accuracy is sometimes given, intentionally or inadvertently, by legal actors. The present study suggests that receiving positive feedback about unrelated cognitive performance does not necessarily inoculate witnesses against suggestive questioning. However, the study does not rule out the possibility that feedback directly related to the memory could have such effects; indeed, real-world reports indicate that confirmatory feedback about an eyewitness identification can inflate confidence and potentially consolidate inaccurate memories. Therefore, care should still be taken in legal settings to avoid feedback that could bias witnesses’ memory reports.

Several limitations of the present study merit recognition and point to directions for future research. First, as noted, the confidence induction targeted a general cognitive domain and not memory-specific evaluative processes; future work should compare memory-focused versus general confidence manipulations. Second, the stimulus used was relatively brief and the retention interval was minimal; longer delays between event and questioning might yield different patterns, perhaps magnifying suggestibility and making moderation by confidence more detectable. Third, the sample size, while adequate for detecting moderate effects, was limited for the detection of small interaction effects; subsequent studies should adopt larger samples or meta-analytic approaches to assess the robustness of potential moderators. Fourth, the online testing environment introduced uncontrolled variability; replication in controlled lab settings would help to delineate whether environmental noise contributed to null effects.

Future research could also explore alternative manipulations of confidence, such as social endorsement, group consensus feedback, or repeated questioning that produces escalating confidence through consolidation processes. Studies could examine whether confidence changes that are accompanied by changes in memory vividness or narrative coherence are more likely to moderate the impact of leading questions. Longitudinal designs could assess whether transient confidence manipulations have delayed or cumulative effects on memory reconstruction over time.

Another promising direction involves integrating neurocognitive measures to understand the mechanisms underpinning resistance or susceptibility to misinformation. For example, functional neuroimaging or psychophysiological measures could reveal whether confidence inductions alter neural markers of memory strength or source monitoring. Such multimethod approaches could elucidate whether confidence modulates encoding, retrieval, or post-retrieval monitoring processes that contribute to memory distortion.

In conclusion, the present study found that although experimental feedback effectively altered participants’ reported general confidence, this manipulation did not alter the extent to which leading verbs influenced speed estimates for a witnessed automobile collision. The overall pattern indicates that the classic verb effect was not reliably replicated under the specific online conditions and sample used here, and that induced general confidence did not moderate the effect. These outcomes suggest that the relationship between confidence and susceptibility to post-event suggestion is complex and may hinge on the specificity of the confidence manipulation, the nature of the post-event information, and contextual factors. Future work with larger samples, alternative confidence inductions, and laboratory controls will be important for clarifying when and how confidence interacts with suggestive questioning to shape memory reports.
