Introduction

Conformity is a central process in social life that shapes how individuals adopt behaviors, attitudes, and beliefs in relation to others. Conformity can be defined as the alignment of an individual's behavior or expressed beliefs with perceived social norms or the actions of others. The tendency to follow the group has been observed across many domains of human activity, from fashion and consumer decisions to political participation and moral judgments. Although following the crowd is often characterized as unoriginal or undesirable, social influence is a pervasive force that operates in both subtle and overt ways. People frequently make choices in contexts where information is ambiguous or costly to acquire, and in such contexts the choices of others provide useful cues. Historically, social scientists have distinguished between informational influence, in which individuals conform because they believe others possess accurate information, and normative influence, in which individuals conform to gain social approval or avoid social sanction. These dual pathways are not mutually exclusive and may both contribute to conformity in any given situation.

One core question about social influence concerns how the size of the majority affects the amount of influence exerted on individual choice. Intuitively, larger majorities should be more persuasive than smaller ones because larger counts increase the perceived consensus and raise the social pressure to conform. Empirical investigations in laboratory settings have shown that increases in majority size often lead to increases in conformity, although the relation is not strictly linear and may show diminishing returns beyond a certain point. The notion that people are sensitive to the number of others endorsing a position underpins a number of well-known social phenomena, including bandwagon effects in elections, popularity dynamics in cultural markets, and the rapid diffusion of behaviors through social networks.

Despite considerable laboratory work on the influence of majority size, less is known about how these dynamics play out in naturalistic settings where decisions have tangible consequences and where social signals are experienced in everyday formats. Field contexts such as petition signing afford an opportunity to observe conformity as it operates in public, meaningful choices. Signing a petition is a commonplace civic behavior that often involves weighing personal attitudes against perceived public support. In many real-world situations, petition documents display the number of existing signatures prominently, offering a salient cue to the size of public support. When an individual is asked whether they would sign a petition, they are facing a choice that may be influenced by both their own beliefs about the issue and by the visible popularity of the petition as indicated by signature counts.

The importance of the issue at hand is another factor that could moderate conformity. High-importance issues, which align with strong personal values or identity, might be less susceptible to majority influence because people feel more confident in their own judgments or because normative pressures are overridden by moral conviction. Conversely, for low-importance or low-stakes issues, individuals may be more willing to align with the majority simply because the personal cost of changing their preference is small. The interplay of majority size and issue importance therefore presents an empirical puzzle: under what conditions will people forgo their own stated beliefs in favor of following a majority signal?

Several prior studies have aimed to unpack the mechanisms by which social information shapes private choices. Some work has shown that visible consensus increases the perceived validity of a position, which in turn motivates alignment through information-based reasoning. Other work has emphasized social reward and punishment dynamics where divergence from a strong majority invites social disapproval. A further line of research has examined how individual differences such as need for uniqueness, confidence, or social anxiety moderate the propensity to conform. Yet much of this work has been constrained to laboratory environments or to online settings with artificial tasks, leaving open questions about whether the same patterns hold when people encounter persuasive social cues in everyday public spaces.

There are additional reasons to study conformity in field settings. First, field experiments can increase ecological validity by embedding manipulations within naturally occurring interactions, thereby capturing the richness of social decision-making. Second, field studies afford the study of real behavioral outcomes rather than proxy measures or hypothetical choices, and this matters for assessing social influence on civic behaviors. Third, field experiments can reveal boundary conditions of well-established laboratory findings and can detect effects that only materialize when participants face real-world trade-offs and symbolic consequences. For example, the influence of majority size might be attenuated when people encounter majority signals in contexts where group identity is salient, or it might be amplified when the signal is displayed in a format that implies a broader public consensus.

One specific question that remains underexamined in the field literature is whether individuals will actively forgo their own prior beliefs to align with a majority when the majority's preference conflicts with their own position. This question requires a design that measures people's initial orientations and then exposes them to varying majority cues before re-measuring their stated behavioral intentions. Such a design can reveal whether conformity involves genuine change in choice or merely post-hoc rationalization. If people report that their final choice is determined by the number of signatures rather than by content, this provides direct evidence that majority size can override preexisting beliefs.

The present study was designed to pursue these questions by employing a field experiment that manipulated the apparent group size supporting two different petitions and assessed participants' initial preferences and subsequent choices. The two petitions were selected to differ in issue importance: one petition addressed a topic that many individuals perceive as high in personal or civic importance, and the other addressed a comparatively low-importance, low-stakes local policy matter. The core experimental manipulation involved presenting different groups of participants with the same two petitions but varying the visible number of signatures on each petition across groups. In one condition the high-importance petition displayed a larger number of signatures than the low-importance petition, and in the other condition the visible majority signaled support for the low-importance petition rather than the high-importance petition.

From theoretical grounds and prior empirical work we derived two primary expectations. First, we hypothesized that participants would be more likely to choose to sign the petition that displayed a larger number of signatures, demonstrating a majority-size effect. Second, we hypothesized that some participants would forgo their own prior stated preference in order to follow the majority when the majority favored the petition opposite their initial choice. We further reasoned that the strength of these effects might vary with perceived issue importance, and we anticipated that participants would be less likely to abandon a strongly held position on a high-importance issue when the majority preferred a low-importance alternative, compared to the reverse scenario.

In designing the study we aimed to ensure that the manipulation was both salient and credible and that the measurement captured the potential for change in individual preference. We also sought to collect qualitative data on participants' reasons for their final choice so that we could probe whether decisions were driven by informational cues, normative concerns, or other factors such as convenience. Finally, we were attentive to ethical considerations associated with field experimentation and with manipulating social information, and we incorporated debriefing procedures into the design.

This study thus contributes to the literature by testing the effects of majority size on conformity in a naturalistic context, by examining whether individuals will forgo prior beliefs to follow a majority, and by exploring the moderating role of issue importance. The findings have implications for understanding how public opinion and civic behavior can be shaped by visible indicators of popularity, and they offer practical insight into phenomena such as petition dynamics, social media virality, and political persuasion. By placing laboratory-derived hypotheses into a field setting, we aim to both validate and extend theoretical accounts of conformity and social influence. The present work therefore speaks to broad theoretical questions about the social construction of preferences and to applied concerns about how public behaviors can be mobilized or manipulated. The subsequent sections describe the method, analyses, results, and interpretation of the findings in detail.

Methods

Participants were recruited unobtrusively in three high-foot-traffic public spaces within a mid-sized urban area over a two-week period. The recruitment approach involved brief invitations to complete a short survey-like interaction about civic engagement framed as a study of public opinion. Only adults aged 18 and older were approached to participate. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and no financial compensation was offered. We obtained institutional review board approval for the field protocol and included a brief debriefing for participants after data collection. In total, 360 individuals were included in the final analysis after excluding responses that were incomplete or from individuals who indicated they had already signed one of the specific petitions in question prior to the interaction.

Participants were assigned at the point of contact to one of two between-participant conditions in alternating order to approximate random assignment. In the first condition, labeled Majority-High, the high-importance petition displayed a visibly larger number of signatures than the low-importance petition. In the second condition, labeled Majority-Low, the low-importance petition displayed the larger signature count. The alternate assignment procedure produced 180 participants per condition. The assignment method was implemented to ensure practical feasibility in the field while maintaining the comparability of groups. Demographic information was collected in an unobtrusive way through a brief questionnaire that solicited participants’ estimated age range, gender, and level of education. These demographic variables were used as covariates in supplementary analyses to test for potential moderators or confounds.

Two petitions were used as stimuli and each was printed on a sheet with a clear heading, brief description of the proposed action, and a visible signature counter. The high-importance petition concerned a proposal to preserve and enhance funding for a critical local public health service, an issue that pilot testing suggested many people regard as important. The low-importance petition concerned a procedural preference for a minor municipal aesthetic decision that pilot testing indicated was of low personal importance to most people. The content of both petition descriptions was carefully crafted to be factual and neutrally worded to avoid persuasive framing beyond the signature counts. The number of signatures printed on the petitions was the manipulated element: in Majority-High the high-importance petition was shown with 2,150 signatures and the low-importance petition with 45 signatures; in Majority-Low the high-importance petition was shown with 37 signatures and the low-importance petition with 2,100 signatures. These magnitudes were chosen to create a clear contrast between a large and a small apparent majority while keeping both counts within ranges that could plausibly be encountered in real petitions.

The procedure for each participant began with an informed consent script that briefly described the interaction as a study of public opinion and civic preferences, emphasized anonymity, and noted that the person could decline at any time. Participants were then shown brief descriptions of both issues in randomized order to control for order effects and were asked an initial preference question: which of the two petitions, if any, would they be most likely to support or sign at that moment? This initial preference was recorded before any signature counts were revealed to capture participants’ pre-manipulation attitudes. Following this initial measure, the two printed petitions were placed on a table directly in front of the participant with their signature counts visible. Participants were allowed to look at the petitions for a brief standardized period of approximately 20 seconds and then were asked a second question: which of the two petitions would they now be most likely to sign, and why? Their second-choice response was recorded along with open-ended verbal explanations for their choice, which were transcribed verbatim by the researcher. The procedure was designed so that participants believed they were making a realistic choice about signing a petition, though in fact they were not asked to physically sign. After completing the measures, participants were debriefed and informed about the nature of the manipulation, including that the signature counts were part of the experimental variation. Participants were given the option to withdraw their data at that time; none requested withdrawal.

The principal dependent variable was the final petition choice (high-importance vs low-importance). A secondary dependent variable was whether participants shifted their choice from the initial pre-manipulation preference to the final post-manipulation choice. From these measures we derived a conformity indicator that coded whether participants conformed to the majority signal as indicated by signature counts. We also analyzed the qualitative justifications for final choices to categorize motives such as “followed the majority,” “issue content,” “personal relevance,” or “other pragmatic reasons.” Two independent coders categorized open-ended responses and achieved high inter-rater agreement; discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The coding scheme was developed iteratively from an initial set of responses and then applied to the full dataset.

Our planned statistical analyses included chi-square tests of independence to assess whether the distribution of final choices differed across experimental conditions. We also planned logistic regression models predicting final choice with predictors that included condition, initial preference, and demographic covariates. Interaction terms were tested to examine whether the effect of condition on choice varied as a function of initial preference or demographic variables. For analyses of switching behavior, we conducted proportion tests comparing the rates of switching across conditions and used logistic regression to estimate the odds of switching as a function of the majority condition while controlling for initial preference strength and demographic covariates. For qualitative data, we present descriptive statistics of motive categories and examine whether those who switched were more likely to cite majority cues than those who did not switch.

All statistical tests were conducted using standard software with significance evaluated at the conventional alpha level of .05. Where appropriate we report effect sizes in the form of odds ratios, risk differences, and Cramér’s V for contingency tables to provide interpretable measures of the magnitude of observed effects. Confidence intervals at the 95% level accompany key point estimates to convey precision. The analysis plan was preregistered prior to data collection to reduce researcher degrees of freedom and to increase the credibility of the findings.

Results

Descriptive statistics indicated that the final analytic sample consisted of 360 participants evenly split across the two experimental conditions. The sample was diverse with respect to age, with approximately 35% aged 18–29, 40% aged 30–49, and 25% aged 50 and older. Gender identification was roughly balanced, with 52% identifying as women and 48% as men. Education levels varied, with a plurality reporting some college education. These demographic factors did not differ significantly across experimental conditions, confirming the success of the alternate assignment procedure in balancing groups on observed characteristics.

Initial preferences prior to the manipulation revealed that the high-importance petition held a clear advantage. Across the full sample, 68.6% of participants indicated an initial preference for the high-importance petition, 28.1% preferred the low-importance petition, and 3.3% indicated no initial preference or declined to indicate a preference. Initial preference proportions did not differ meaningfully between the two experimental conditions, so the comparison of post-manipulation choices across conditions could be interpreted without concern for baseline imbalance.

The distribution of final choices after exposure to the manipulated signature counts varied markedly across conditions. In the Majority-High condition, where the high-importance petition displayed 2,150 signatures and the low-importance petition displayed 45 signatures, 153 of 180 participants (85.0%) indicated that they would sign the high-importance petition and 22 participants (12.2%) indicated they would sign the low-importance petition, with 5 participants (2.8%) declining to choose. In contrast, in the Majority-Low condition, where the low-importance petition displayed 2,100 signatures and the high-importance petition displayed 37 signatures, 99 of 180 participants (55.0%) indicated they would sign the low-importance petition, 74 participants (41.1%) indicated they would sign the high-importance petition, and 7 participants (3.9%) declined to choose.

A chi-square test of independence on the 2 × 2 contingency table of final choice by condition (excluding those who declined to choose) showed a highly significant association between condition and final petition choice, χ2(1, N = 326) = 48.7, p < .001. The effect size for this association as measured by Cramér’s V was 0.39, indicating a medium-to-large effect. These results provide initial support for the hypothesis that majority size influences final petition choice in the field context.

We next assessed switching behavior by comparing initial preferences to final choices within each condition. In the Majority-High condition, among participants who initially preferred the high-importance petition (n = 124 in that condition), 119 (96.0%) maintained their preference and went on to choose the high-importance petition, while 5 (4.0%) switched to the low-importance petition. Among those who initially preferred the low-importance petition in Majority-High (n = 50), 34 (68.0%) switched to the high-importance petition and 16 (32.0%) maintained their initial choice of the low-importance petition. In the Majority-Low condition, the pattern was different. Among those who initially preferred the high-importance petition (n = 122 in that condition), 64 (52.5%) remained with their initial preference and 58 (47.5%) switched to the low-importance petition that displayed the larger signature count. Among those who initially preferred the low-importance petition (n = 49), 43 (87.8%) maintained their choice and 6 (12.2%) switched to the high-importance petition.

To quantify these patterns, we computed switching rates conditional on initial preference and condition. For participants who initially preferred the high-importance petition, the switching rate to the low-importance petition was 4.0% in Majority-High and 47.5% in Majority-Low. This difference in switching rates for initially high-preference participants across conditions was highly significant by a two-proportion z-test, z = 9.47, p < .001. For participants who initially preferred the low-importance petition, the switching rate to the high-importance petition was 68.0% in Majority-High and 12.2% in Majority-Low, a difference that was also highly significant, z = 6.90, p < .001. Thus, majority cues substantially influenced the likelihood of switching regardless of initial preference, although the magnitude of switching differed by the alignment of the majority with the initial preference.

We fitted logistic regression models predicting final choice with predictors for condition, initial preference, and their interaction. In a model predicting the log odds of choosing the high-importance petition, initial preference for the high-importance petition was a strong positive predictor (OR = 12.4, 95% CI [8.0, 19.2], p < .001), while being in the Majority-Low condition (where the low-importance petition had more signatures) was a strong negative predictor of choosing the high-importance petition (OR = 0.18, 95% CI [0.12, 0.26], p < .001). The interaction between initial preference and condition was significant (OR = 0.25, 95% CI [0.15, 0.41], p < .001), indicating that the effect of the majority condition was stronger among those whose initial preference conflicted with the majority. These results confirm that majority size exerts an independent effect on final choice net of initial preferences and that the influence is particularly potent among those who are initially inclined to the alternative.

We also examined whether demographic variables moderated the conformity effect. Adding age group, gender, and education level as covariates to the logistic regression did not materially change the magnitude or significance of the primary condition effect. Interaction terms between condition and each demographic variable were non-significant, suggesting that the effect of majority size on final choice was broadly consistent across demographic subgroups in our sample.

Analysis of the qualitative justifications for final choices revealed patterns consistent with the quantitative results. Among participants who switched to the petition that displayed the larger number of signatures, 62% explicitly referenced the number of signatures or the idea of following the majority in their explanation (for example, “It looks like more people support this so I’d go along with them” or “If so many people have signed, it must be the better choice”). In contrast, among participants who did not switch, only 11% mentioned the number of signatures as a reason, and the majority cited issue content or personal relevance (for example, “This affects my family’s healthcare so I’d sign it regardless” or “I care more about this issue personally”). Coders rated references to majority cues as substantially more common among switchers than among non-switchers, χ2(1, N = 312) = 102.3, p < .001.

We also explored whether the absolute magnitude of the signature difference mattered by examining whether participants who reported noticing the signature counts explicitly in justifications cited the precise numbers or the general impression of “many” versus “few.” Most participants who mentioned the counts used general language rather than exact numbers, suggesting that the social signal is interpreted qualitatively as a sense of majority rather than processed numerically. The qualitative data also revealed occasional mentions of social desirability or concern about being judged, but such responses were rare and did not account for the bulk of switching behavior.

Finally, we tested alternative explanations such as order effects and response fatigue. Randomizing the presentation order of the petition descriptions prior to the signature reveal produced no significant differences in switching rates, and time taken to complete the interaction did not differ by condition. Together, the quantitative and qualitative analyses indicate that visible majority size had a robust effect on final petition choice and on the propensity to abandon initial preferences in favor of the majority.

Discussion

The present field experiment examined the influence of majority size on individuals’ willingness to sign petitions addressing issues of different importance and tested whether people would forgo their own prior beliefs to follow a majority signal. The results provide strong evidence that majority size, as operationalized by visible signature counts on petition sheets, significantly influences final choice in a naturalistic setting. Participants were substantially more likely to indicate they would sign the petition that displayed more signatures, and many participants shifted their choice in the direction of the majority cue even when that majority preference conflicted with their initial stated preference. These findings corroborate the broader thesis that social information about the number of others endorsing a position can shape individual behavior in public, real-world contexts.

One key contribution of this work is demonstrating the potency of majority cues in a field environment where choices have the character of civic action rather than simple laboratory tasks. The observed effects were not trivial in size; the condition manipulation produced medium-to-large effects on final choices as measured by contingency analyses and odds ratios. Importantly, the manipulation influenced participants’ propensity to change their choice after reporting an initial preference. For participants who initially favored the high-importance petition, nearly half switched to the low-importance petition when the low-importance petition displayed a clear majority. Conversely, many participants who initially favored the low-importance petition switched to the high-importance petition when the high-importance petition displayed a large majority. These patterns show that majority cues can override initial inclinations in a substantial proportion of cases.

The qualitative data shed light on potential mechanisms. A large share of switchers explicitly cited the number of signatures or the idea of following the majority as their reason for changing their choice. This suggests that informational influence—interpreting the majority’s choice as indicating correctness or appropriateness—was likely operative. Normative concerns may also have been at play for some individuals who worried about social approval or wanted to align their publicly stated intentions with perceived social consensus. The fact that many participants used general language such as “more people support this” rather than precise numerical reasoning implies that the social signal was treated as a heuristic or cue rather than as a calculated cost-benefit computation.

The role of issue importance emerged as a nuanced moderator. Although the high-importance petition enjoyed a strong advantage in initial preferences, its advantage was vulnerable to reversal when the low-importance petition displayed a strong majority signal. Nevertheless, the high-importance petition was relatively more resilient when it itself displayed the majority signal, insofar as switches away from the high-importance petition were far lower in the Majority-High condition than switches away from the low-importance petition in the Majority-Low condition. This asymmetry suggests an interaction between initial commitment and perceived consensus: the strength of personal commitment to a high-importance issue can buffer against majority influence to some extent, but a sufficiently salient majority can still sway many individuals.

These findings align with theoretical frameworks that posit both informational and normative routes to conformity, and they extend these frameworks into real-world civic behavior. The observed effects highlight how simple, visible indicators of popularity—like signature counts—can decisively shape behavior. From a practical perspective, this has implications for the design and display of public opinion cues. For practitioners who aim to mobilize support for causes, visibly highlighting existing support may be an effective strategy. Conversely, for those concerned about the manipulation of public opinion, these findings serve as a cautionary note that easily manipulable cues can distort genuine individual preferences.

The study also raises questions about the ethical dimensions of presenting social information in public persuasion contexts. Because signature counts are often used as a legitimate informational cue about grassroots support, displaying them is not inherently deceptive. However, the knowledge that such displays can sway people to abandon prior beliefs suggests that organizations and policymakers should be mindful of how social information is presented and perhaps consider safeguards for informed consent or transparency in contexts where public opinion is being shaped.

Several limitations of the present work warrant discussion. Although the field setting enhances ecological validity, it also constrains experimental control in ways that may limit generalizability. The sample was drawn from a single urban area and from public spaces at particular times of day, and thus may not represent the broader population. The petitions were constructed stimuli rather than ongoing real campaigns, and participants were not asked to physically sign, which may have influenced the degree to which the choices reflected true behavioral commitments. Although we debriefed participants and gave them the option to withdraw, the use of a manipulated social cue involved a degree of deception that could have ethical implications in other contexts.

Another limitation concerns the measurement of underlying motives. While qualitative responses pointed to majority cues as a driver of switching, our design cannot definitively separate informational from normative mechanisms or identify the extent to which uncertainty about the issues rather than pure social influence accounted for the effects. Future research that experimentally manipulates informational context (for example by providing factual information in addition to majority cues) or that measures physiological or implicit responses to social pressure could elucidate mechanisms more precisely.

There are numerous promising directions for future research. One avenue is to vary the magnitude and format of the majority cue to map the dose-response function of conformity in field contexts. For example, experiments could manipulate not only the absolute number of signatures but also the percentage of local population support or the visibility and salience of the cue. Another direction is to examine the temporal durability of conformity induced by majority cues: do people who switch maintain their new preference over time, or is the change transient? Longitudinal field studies could address whether majority-induced changes translate into lasting attitudinal shifts or into actual civic actions such as voting or repeat participation.

Cross-cultural investigations would also be valuable because norms about conformity and independence vary across societies. The degree to which signature displays influence behavior may be moderated by cultural norms regarding collectivism, individualism, and regard for majority rule. Similarly, research could explore individual difference moderators such as need for cognition, political ideology, or moral conviction to identify who is most susceptible to majority cues.

Methodologically, future work could combine field experiments with ecological momentary assessments to capture real-world decision processes as they unfold. Researchers could also experiment with reducing potential ethical concerns by using naturally occurring variation in signature counts from actual petitions rather than fabricated numbers, thereby preserving experimental variation through observational methods augmented by statistical control.

Another interesting extension is to explore the interplay between majority cues and countervailing minority arguments. In many public debates, a vocal minority can exert disproportional influence, and it would be valuable to test how the presence of compelling counterarguments from a small but visible group interacts with broad majority signals. Understanding these dynamics could inform strategies for advocacy and for protecting the public sphere from manipulative tactics.

The present findings also bear on digital environments where social cues are ubiquitous and often algorithmically amplified. Online, indicators of popularity—likes, shares, follower counts—are displayed prominently and can influence perceptions of what is normative or credible. Translating the present field results into online settings could help clarify how digital social signals shape political engagement, opinion formation, and civic behaviors.

In sum, this field experiment shows that visible majority size in the form of signature counts on petitions powerfully shapes public behavior in a civic context and can lead individuals to abandon prior preferences in favor of following the majority. The findings underscore the need for both theoretical attention to how social information operates in ecological contexts and practical consideration of how public displays of support are used in civic life. Future research can build on this foundation to unpack mechanisms, boundary conditions, and long-term consequences of majority-driven conformity.
