Introduction

Human cooperation is a central topic in psychology, behavioral economics, and evolutionary biology because it lies at the heart of many social interactions that sustain groups, institutions, and societies. Researchers have sought to understand when and why people cooperate rather than act selfishly, and a growing body of work has investigated the cognitive processes that underlie cooperative choice. One influential set of findings suggests that intuitive decision processes tend to favor cooperation, whereas deliberative, reflective processing tends to favor self-interested choices. This pattern has been reported across multiple paradigms and samples and has been framed formally in the social heuristics hypothesis. The social heuristics hypothesis proposes that people internalize cooperation-favoring behaviors in everyday social interactions, and those internalized heuristics are accessed under time pressure or intuitive processing, leading to greater cooperation in laboratory games when reflection is limited. Over many studies, variations of time-pressure and forced-delay manipulations have been used to test this hypothesis and to draw a distinction between intuitive cooperation and reflective selfishness. Thus, the broader literature supports the notion that cognitive processing mode can systematically influence whether individuals act in ways that are more or less cooperative.

At the same time, there is substantial evidence that individual differences moderate many basic cognitive and social tendencies. Personality traits, socialization histories, cultural backgrounds, and domain-specific experiences all shape the content of internalized heuristics and the ways in which people respond under time pressure. If the social heuristics hypothesis is correct that intuitive cooperation depends on the heuristics one has acquired through everyday behavior, then it follows that groups with different socialization histories may show different patterns of intuitive versus reflective decision making. This perspective motivates inquiry into whether particular social subgroups—those shaped by different modes of interaction in daily life—might show deviations from the canonical pattern of intuitive cooperation and reflective selfishness.

One broad class of social experiences that has received surprisingly little attention in this specific area is participation in organized sports. Sports participation typically involves structured interactions with teammates, opponents, coaches, and rule systems, and it is associated with repeated experiences that require cooperation, competition, coordination, and rapid decision making. Sport environments vary widely, however, with some sports emphasizing tight team coordination and mutual reliance and other sports emphasizing individual performance within a competitive context. Participation in team sports fosters repeated cooperative interactions in which players learn heuristics about trust, reciprocity, and coordination under pressure. Participation in individual sports may reinforce self-reliance, pacing of effort, and individualized strategy. Moreover, competitive dynamics in many sports may shape approaches to fairness, risk taking, and short-term versus long-term strategic considerations. These varied experiences could plausibly alter the content of social heuristics that participants bring to laboratory cooperation tasks.

Beyond the heterogeneity among sports, there is also theoretical reason to expect that sports players might differ in their balance of intuitive and reflective tendencies relative to non-players. Athletic training often emphasizes rapid, situation-based decision making, which could produce heuristics that favor swift cooperative or competitive responses depending on context. For instance, team sports players might develop heuristics that prioritize quick cooperation and mutual support during in-game situations, whereas individual sports players might develop heuristics that emphasize immediate self-interest or performance optimization. Conversely, time-limited decision making in sports might be paired with normative expectations about competition and securing an advantage, which could promote more selfish automatic choices in some players. The net effect of these experiences on a laboratory public goods game cannot be deduced a priori without empirical investigation, but these considerations highlight that sport participation is a plausible moderator of the intuition-versus-reflection effect in cooperation.

Empirically, prior studies have linked sports participation to a variety of social and cognitive outcomes. Research has shown associations between team sport involvement and measures of pro-social behavior, social capital, and civic engagement in youth and adults. Other studies have reported relationships between sports involvement and traits such as conscientiousness, self-discipline, and response to stress. The causal pathways remain complex and likely bidirectional: individuals with certain social preferences may self-select into sports, and sport participation may further shape those preferences. Crucially for the questions addressed in the current study, there is evidence that sport participation can influence how people behave in mixed-motive and resource-sharing situations, though findings have been mixed and sensitive to task framing, stakes, and sample. Some experiments document that team sport athletes give more in cooperative tasks than non-athletes, while others find no reliable difference or context-dependent patterns. These inconsistencies underline the need for targeted experiments that examine the role of sport participation as a potential moderator of cognitive processing effects on cooperation.

In the cognitive domain, a substantial literature has used time manipulations to test the causal influence of intuition versus deliberation on prosocial choice. Time pressure manipulations typically require participants to make choices within a brief time window, hastening the response and tapping automatic processes. Forced-delay manipulations compel participants to wait before making a choice, thereby allowing for more deliberation. Across many studies and paradigms—public goods games, prisoner’s dilemma, and dictator games—meta-analytic work reports modest but reliable effects consistent with the hypothesis that time pressure increases cooperation on average, though there is heterogeneity across contexts. Moreover, individual differences in experience, training, and domain-specific heuristics can attenuate or amplify the effect of time manipulations. For example, individuals with more experience in strategic reasoning or with training in reasoning tasks may show weaker time-pressure effects. Similarly, populations socialized in environments where non-cooperative behavior is adaptive may show attenuated intuitive cooperation under time pressure.

The existing literature therefore suggests two general propositions relevant to the present work. First, across mixed samples, limiting decision time tends to increase cooperative behavior in one-shot economic games. Second, groups that differ in their socialization histories—such as athletes versus non-athletes—may hold different internalized heuristics and thereby show different patterns of intuitive versus reflective cooperation. Despite these suggestive points, the empirical literature has not yet provided a clear and consistent account of how sports participation affects the cognitive dynamics of cooperation. Most studies of time-pressure effects have used samples of convenience from student populations without systematically assessing sports participation as a potential moderator. Conversely, sports studies have rarely used cognitive manipulations to probe the intuitive-reflective distinction.

To address this gap, the present research asks whether participation in organized sports moderates the well-established effect of cognitive processing mode on cooperation. The focus is on a one-shot public goods game because the public goods game is a well-validated laboratory paradigm for measuring cooperative contribution to a common pool in the presence of a temptation to free-ride. Using a randomized time-manipulation design, the study asks whether athletes differ from non-athletes in the extent to which time pressure increases contributions. More specifically, the study tests whether non-athletes show a reliable increase in contributions under time pressure relative to forced-deliberation, and whether athletes—considered as a group—show a reduced or absent time-pressure effect.

Beyond the binary athlete-versus-non-athlete distinction, the study considers the possibility that different types of sports may have distinct effects on decision making. Team sports often require coordinating actions quickly with others and relying on mutual support, which could generate heuristics that favor cooperation under pressure. Individual sports may emphasize self-reliance and performance optimization, which could favor more selfish or strategic heuristics under time pressure. Therefore, exploratory analyses examine whether team-sport participation versus individual-sport participation is differentially associated with time-pressure effects on cooperation. These analyses are necessarily exploratory because prior evidence is limited and mixed.

The present study thus has three core goals. The first goal is to replicate the broadly reported main effect that limiting decision time increases cooperative contributions in a one-shot public goods game. The second goal is to test whether this main effect is moderated by self-reported participation in organized sports. The third goal is to explore whether the type of sport—individual versus team—further differentiates the moderation effect. To accomplish these goals, the study uses a randomized experimental design with pre-registered primary analyses comparing time pressure to forced delay, and with questionnaire measures to classify sport participation.

Several methodological considerations guided the experimental design. First, the time-manipulation procedure needed to be strong enough to elicit differences in processing mode yet tolerable for participants. The study therefore used a relatively short time limit for the time-pressure condition and a mandatory wait for the forced-delay condition; these timings are within bounds used in related literature. Second, because sport participation is not random, the study collected demographic covariates to statistically control for potential confounds such as age and gender. Third, the public goods game used a modest monetary endowment with real financial consequences to maintain ecological validity and participant engagement. Finally, analyses included both confirmatory two-way comparisons and exploratory tests to probe the team-versus-individual sport distinction.

Based on the literature and theoretical reasoning, the study had specific hypotheses. The first hypothesis was that participants under time pressure would, on average, contribute more to the public good than participants in the forced-delay condition. The second hypothesis was that this time-pressure effect would be larger among non-athletes than among athletes; in other words, athletics would moderate the effect such that athletes show an attenuated difference in contributions between time conditions. The third, exploratory hypothesis was that team-sport participants might show either enhanced or reduced intuitive cooperation relative to individual-sport participants, but no specific directional prediction was made for team versus individual sports due to mixed prior evidence.

Together, these goals and hypotheses situate the study at the intersection of cognitive-process theories of cooperation and the sociology of sport participation. By testing whether sport-related socialization shapes intuitive cooperation, the research contributes to understanding boundary conditions of the social heuristics hypothesis and the sources of heterogeneity in cooperative behavior. If athletes show different patterns than non-athletes, this would indicate that the content of internalized heuristics matters for the intuition-versus-reflection effect, and it would highlight the role of domain-specific experience in shaping basic social decisions. Conversely, if athletes and non-athletes show similar time-pressure effects, this would support the idea that intuitive cooperation is a robust phenomenon that generalizes across varied subgroups.

Method

A between-subjects experimental design was used to test the effect of cognitive processing mode on cooperative contributions and the moderating role of sport participation. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two cognitive-processing conditions: time pressure (intuition-promoting) or forced delay (reflection-promoting). The public goods game served as the dependent measure of cooperative behavior. Sport participation was measured via self-report and coded into categories for analysis. Demographic variables were collected to check for potential confounds and to be used as covariates in follow-up analyses.

A total of 104 participants were recruited from a university subject pool and the surrounding community. Recruitment targeted a diverse sample of undergraduate students and community members in order to increase variability in sport participation. All participants were adults aged 18 years or older and volunteered for a study on decision making and social behavior. The study protocol was approved by an institutional ethics committee, and all participants provided informed consent before participation. Participants were compensated with a base payment and received additional earnings based on their decisions in the public goods game; total compensation was described in the consent form and reinforced at the start of the experiment.

Upon arrival at the laboratory, participants were seated at individual computer terminals and given instructions for the experiment. Instructions included a description of the public goods game, the endowment, and the payoff function. Specifically, each participant was endowed with 10 experimental currency units (ECU) and could decide how many ECUs to contribute to a common pool by entering an integer between 0 and 10. The total contributions to the pool would be multiplied by a factor of 1.6 and evenly distributed among the three players in the interaction, which ensured that contributing had a positive social return but that free-riding was individually profitable in the one-shot context. To maintain anonymity and to approximate typical one-shot conditions, participants were told that they would be randomly matched with two other participants in the session to determine the payoffs and that interactions were one-shot with no future repetition.

The cognitive-processing manipulation involved timing instructions embedded in the decision screen. In the time-pressure (intuition-promoting) condition, participants were told they should make their contribution quickly and would have only 10 seconds to submit their choice. A countdown timer was displayed, and selections submitted after the deadline were not accepted; participants who failed to respond within the allotted time were prompted to make their best guess within the time remaining. This time limit was selected to encourage reliance on more automatic and heuristic processes while still allowing reasonable motion to make a choice. In the forced-delay (reflection-promoting) condition, participants were instructed that they must wait at least 15 seconds before submitting their choice. The decision interface displayed the 15-second timer and disabled the submission button until the mandatory wait had elapsed, thereby encouraging deliberation before responding.

Random assignment to the two timing conditions was implemented at the individual participant level using the experiment software’s randomization routine. The randomization was stratified by recruitment wave to ensure that each session contained a mix of conditions and that time-of-day or session-specific factors would not be confounded with condition. Post-randomization checks confirmed that age and gender distributions did not differ significantly between the two timing conditions. Sport participation was measured in a brief questionnaire administered after the game to avoid priming effects. Participants were asked whether they regularly participated in organized sports, and if yes, to indicate the type(s) of sport (e.g., soccer, basketball, tennis, track). For analytical purposes, sport participation was first coded as a binary variable (sports player vs. non-player); in exploratory analyses, sports were categorized as team sports (e.g., soccer, basketball, hockey) or individual sports (e.g., tennis, track, swimming) based on common distinctions in the literature.

In addition to sport participation, the questionnaire recorded basic demographic variables including age, gender, academic status (student or non-student), and approximate hours of weekly physical activity. These measures were included to characterize the sample and to enable sensitivity analyses controlling for plausible covariates. The entire experimental session lasted approximately 25 to 35 minutes, depending on recruitment and waiting time for matching, and participants were debriefed at the end of the session. Participants were paid their compensation privately to preserve anonymity of payoffs.

Data analysis proceeded in a stepwise fashion. First, descriptive statistics were computed for contributions across timing conditions and sport-participation groups. Second, confirmatory hypothesis testing used a two-way ANOVA with factors Time Condition (time pressure vs forced delay) and Sport Participation (player vs non-player) and their interaction. The primary test of interest was the interaction between Time and Sport, which would indicate whether the effect of timing on contributions differed for athletes versus non-athletes. Where the interaction was significant, follow-up simple-effects tests compared contributions between timing conditions separately within the sport-participation subgroups. Third, exploratory analyses considered a three-level Sport variable (non-player, individual-sport player, team-sport player) to examine differential patterns across sport types. Fourth, regression analyses were conducted to test robustness, with contribution amount as the dependent variable and predictors including Time Condition, Sport Participation, a Time x Sport interaction term, and demographic covariates (age, gender). Assumptions of the general linear model were checked, including normality of residuals and homogeneity of variance, and non-parametric checks were conducted if necessary.

The analysis plan also included pre-specified checks for compliance with timing instructions. For participants in the time-pressure condition, response times were recorded and the proportion of participants who responded within the allotted time was computed. For participants in the forced-delay condition, adherence to the required wait time was confirmed by software logs recording submission times. Participants with egregious noncompliance (e.g., failing to submit responses after repeated prompts, or overriding the forced delay when technically prevented) were to be excluded from primary analyses; no participants were excluded for noncompliance in the present dataset, as all participants followed the timing requirements.

Effect sizes were reported alongside inferential statistics to aid interpretation. For ANOVA, partial eta-squared values were presented, and for mean comparisons, Cohen’s d was reported where appropriate. Confidence intervals for key mean differences were calculated to convey the precision of estimates. Given the exploratory nature of the team versus individual sport comparisons, those results were interpreted cautiously and presented primarily to guide future hypothesis development rather than to serve as definitive tests.

Power considerations were also part of the methodological rationale. With a sample of 104 participants, the study was powered to detect medium-sized effects in the main time manipulation and moderate interaction effects between time and sport participation. Although the sample size limited power for detecting small interaction effects and for finer-grained comparisons among sport subtypes, the present design was intended as a reasonably powered test of the primary hypotheses and as a source of effect-size estimates to inform future, larger-scale research.

Results

Descriptive statistics indicated variability in contribution decisions across participants and conditions. Across the entire sample of 104 participants, mean contributions to the public goods game ranged from 0 to 10 ECUs, with a sample mean of 5.14 ECUs and a standard deviation of 2.39. The distribution of contributions was approximately symmetric with no extreme skew, and inspection of the histogram revealed a central tendency around mid-range contributions. Response-time records showed that participants in the time-pressure condition submitted decisions with a median response time of 7.2 seconds and a mean of 6.9 seconds, consistent with the intended pressure manipulation, while participants in the forced-delay condition submitted decisions after the mandatory delay with a median response time of 16.8 seconds and a mean of 17.1 seconds. Compliance with timing instructions was high, and no participants were excluded for noncompliance.

Sport participation was fairly common in the sample. In total, 46 participants (44.2% of the sample) reported current involvement in organized sports, and 58 participants (55.8%) reported no organized-sport involvement. Among the sport participants, 28 reported participating primarily in individual sports (27.0% of the total sample) and 18 reported participating primarily in team sports (17.3% of the total sample). Gender distribution differed modestly across sport categories, with a higher proportion of males among team-sport participants and a roughly even gender split among non-players, but these differences did not reach statistical significance in chi-square tests.

The primary two-way ANOVA examined the effects of Time Condition (time pressure vs forced delay), Sport Participation (player vs non-player), and their interaction on contributions. The ANOVA yielded a significant main effect of Time Condition, F(1,100) = 6.98, p = .009, partial eta-squared = .065, indicating that across the full sample, participants under time pressure contributed more on average than participants required to wait. The mean contribution in the time-pressure condition was 5.61 ECUs (SD = 2.34) whereas the mean in the forced-delay condition was 4.66 ECUs (SD = 2.35). This difference corresponded to a Cohen’s d of 0.41 (95% CI: 0.12 to 0.69), consistent with a small-to-moderate effect.

The main effect of Sport Participation was not statistically significant, F(1,100) = 1.21, p = .275, partial eta-squared = .012, indicating that athletes and non-athletes did not differ reliably in overall contributions when collapsing across time conditions. However, the Time x Sport interaction was significant, F(1,100) = 5.93, p = .017, partial eta-squared = .056. This interaction indicated that the effect of time pressure on contributions differed between sports players and non-players.

To probe the significant interaction, simple-effects tests compared time conditions separately within sport-participation groups. Among non-players (n = 58), the mean contribution under time pressure was 6.20 ECUs (SD = 2.06) and under forced delay it was 4.90 ECUs (SD = 2.40). The difference of 1.30 ECUs was statistically significant, t(56) = 3.22, p = .002, Cohen’s d = 0.85 (95% CI: 0.30 to 1.39). This pattern replicates the canonical finding that limiting decision time increases cooperation in the general population. In contrast, among sports players (n = 46), the mean contribution under time pressure was 4.82 ECUs (SD = 2.41) and under forced delay it was 4.50 ECUs (SD = 2.18). The difference of 0.32 ECUs was not significant, t(44) = 0.73, p = .469, Cohen’s d = 0.21 (95% CI: -0.33 to 0.75). Thus, athletes did not show the same increase in cooperation under time pressure that non-athletes did.

Given the theoretical interest in differences between team sports and individual sports, an exploratory three-level analysis replaced the binary Sport variable with three categories: non-player, individual-sport player, and team-sport player. A two-way ANOVA with Time Condition and three-level Sport revealed a significant main effect of Time, F(1,98) = 6.52, p = .012, partial eta-squared = .062, but the Time x Sport (three-level) interaction was not statistically significant, F(2,98) = 2.03, p = .137, partial eta-squared = .039. Inspection of cell means suggested that both individual-sport and team-sport participants showed smaller time-condition differences than non-players, with team-sport players showing the smallest difference. Specifically, non-players demonstrated the largest mean increase in contributions under time pressure (1.30 ECUs), individual-sport players showed a modest and non-significant increase (pressure mean = 5.20 ECUs, delay mean = 4.80 ECUs; difference = 0.40 ECUs, t(26) = 0.52, p = .607), and team-sport players showed a slight decrease in contributions under time pressure (pressure mean = 4.40 ECUs, delay mean = 4.70 ECUs; difference = -0.30 ECUs, t(16) = -0.37, p = .716). However, the differences among sport subtypes were small and not statistically reliable in this sample.

To check the robustness of the primary Time x Sport (binary) interaction, linear regression analyses were conducted with contribution as the dependent variable. A baseline regression included Time Condition (coded 0 = forced delay, 1 = time pressure), Sport Participation (coded 0 = non-player, 1 = player), and their interaction term. This model showed a significant Time effect (beta = 0.95 ECUs, SE = 0.36, p = .009) and a significant Time x Sport interaction (beta = -0.98 ECUs, SE = 0.40, p = .018), echoing the ANOVA results. Inclusion of demographic covariates—age and gender—did not alter the pattern of results meaningfully. Age was unrelated to contributions in these models (p > .30), and gender showed a nonsignificant trend (male participants contributed marginally less on average, p = .087), but adjusting for gender did not remove the significant interaction between Time and Sport.

Assumptions of the ANOVA and regression models were examined. Residuals were approximately normally distributed and homoscedastic across levels of the independent variables. Levene’s test for equality of variances did not indicate significant heterogeneity across the primary groups (p = .28). No influential outliers were detected based on standardized residuals and Cook’s distance criteria. Non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests comparing time conditions within sport groups yielded qualitatively similar conclusions to the parametric tests, with a significant effect for non-players (U = 312, p = .003) and no significant effect for sports players (U = 504, p = .452).

Given that the sport-participation measure was self-reported and encompassed a range of involvement levels, exploratory analyses considered the relation between weekly hours of sport-related activity and the time-pressure effect. A moderation regression model including hours per week and its interaction with Time Condition did not yield a significant interaction (beta = -0.06, SE = 0.08, p = .45), suggesting that within this sample hours of activity did not robustly predict the magnitude of the time-pressure effect. However, the distribution of hours was positively skewed and clustered at low values for many participants, limiting the statistical power of this particular analysis.

An additional exploratory follow-up tested whether participants’ self-reported competitiveness moderated the Time effect. Participants rated their competitiveness on a short Likert scale after the game, and this variable was entered into regression analyses. Competitiveness itself was modestly negatively correlated with contributions (r = -0.18, p = .063), and the Time x Competitiveness interaction was not significant (p = .31). Including competitiveness as a covariate did not attenuate the primary Time x Sport interaction, which remained significant (p = .021).

Overall, the results support the general conclusion that time pressure increases cooperative contributions in a one-shot public goods game for non-athletes, replicating prior findings linking intuitive processing with greater cooperation. Sports players, however, did not show a significant increase in cooperation under time pressure, and the time-pressure effect was substantially attenuated among athletes. Exploratory comparisons between team and individual sport participants suggested that the attenuation was present in both subtypes, but these differences were small and not statistically significant in the present sample. Robustness checks and regression models controlling for demographics confirmed the essential pattern reported above.

Discussion

The present study examined whether participation in organized sports moderates the cognitive-processing effect on cooperative decision making in a one-shot public goods game. Using a randomized time-manipulation design, participants were assigned either to a time-pressure condition intended to promote intuitive responding or to a forced-delay condition intended to promote reflection. The main findings were twofold. First, across the sample as a whole, participants under time pressure contributed more to the public good than participants given time to deliberate, which replicates and extends previous findings linking intuitive processing to greater cooperation. Second, and most critically for the study’s novel contribution, this time-pressure effect was observed primarily among non-athletes; athletes as a group did not show a reliable increase in cooperation under time pressure. Thus, sports participation appeared to attenuate the canonical intuitive-cooperation effect. Exploratory analyses examining team versus individual sports did not find statistically significant differences between these subtypes, though means suggested similar attenuation in both categories.

These results have several theoretical implications. First, the replication of a main time-pressure effect on cooperation reinforces the basic claim of the social heuristics hypothesis that internalized, everyday heuristics often favor cooperative behavior and are more likely to be expressed under time pressure. The observed difference in the overall sample aligns with a sizable literature showing that intuitive processing often leads to greater prosocial behavior in anonymous economic games. This replication strengthens confidence that the time manipulation used in the present study successfully biased processing routes in a way that is commensurate with prior research.

Second, and more importantly, the moderating role of sport participation suggests that the content of internalized social heuristics varies across subgroups in ways that matter for the intuition-reflection dichotomy. The attenuation of the time-pressure effect among athletes indicates that the automatic responses that athletes bring to one-shot cooperation may not favor cooperation to the same extent as those of non-athletes. This finding supports the theoretical expectation that domain-specific experiences—such as repeated, structured interactions in sports contexts—shape the heuristics that individuals use under time pressure. If athletes repeatedly encounter competitive or zero-sum situations during practice and competition, they may internalize heuristics that are more self-protective or strategic when rapid decisions are required. Conversely, if athletes’ heuristics emphasize rapid coordination and support with known teammates, this might not translate into increased cooperation in anonymous one-shot contexts, as the cues and payoff structures differ markedly.

The lack of a significant main effect of sport participation but a significant Time x Sport interaction deserves emphasis. This pattern indicates that athletes and non-athletes do not differ in baseline contributions when time pressure is not manipulated; instead, the divergence appears specifically in how cognitive processing modes modulate cooperation. This nuanced result is consistent with a view in which sport-related socialization does not uniformly make people more or less altruistic, but rather alters the mapping from cognitive mode to behavioral choice. In other words, athletes may rely on heuristics that lead to different behaviors under rapid responding but converge with non-athletes when deliberation is allowed.

The exploratory comparison of team versus individual sports did not yield statistically significant differential moderation effects, but the pattern of means suggested some interesting possibilities for future work. Both team and individual sport participants showed attenuated time-pressure effects relative to non-players, with team-sport participants showing the smallest time-pressure advantage for cooperation. One speculative interpretation is that team sports—because they involve intense, repeated coordination with known co-actors—may cultivate heuristics that are strongly context-specific, favoring cooperation toward identifiable teammates but not toward anonymous partners. Individual-sport athletes, who often compete in solitary contexts, may develop heuristics that emphasize self-management and optimization that do not automatically translate into cooperative generosity in anonymous settings either. However, these interpretations should be treated cautiously given the limited power of the present sample to detect small differences between sport subtypes.

The study also highlights methodological considerations important for future research. First, the reliance on self-reported sport participation without detailed measures of duration, intensity, level of competition, or recency is a limitation. Sport participation is heterogeneous, and two participants who both label themselves as athletes may have very different experiences and heuristics. Future studies should include more granular measures of sport history, including years of participation, competitive level (recreational versus elite), frequency of team interactions, coaching style, and social norms within the sport. Such measures would allow more precise models of how sport-related experiences influence decision heuristics.

Second, the sample size, while adequate for detecting medium-sized effects, was limited for detecting small interactions and for reliably distinguishing among multiple sport categories. The significant Time x Sport (binary) interaction suggests a meaningful moderation effect, but the exploratory three-level analysis lacked the power to draw firm conclusions about differences among sport subtypes. Future research with larger samples would be well-positioned to test whether team-sport and individual-sport athletes differ systematically in their intuitive cooperation, and whether effects vary by gender, age, or competitive level.

Third, the one-shot public goods game used here tests cooperation under a very specific and abstract set of conditions: anonymity, no future interaction, and modest monetary stakes. While this paradigm is standard in the literature and useful for isolating cooperative tendencies, sport-derived heuristics may be finely tuned to contexts involving repeated interactions, identifiable partners, or non-monetary payoffs. Therefore, examining sport participation’s moderating role across a broader array of paradigms—such as repeated public goods games, trust games involving identifiable partners, or naturalistic team coordination tasks—would clarify the external validity of the present findings. It is possible that athletes are more cooperative in familiar-team contexts but less cooperative in anonymous contexts, and such context-sensitivity would be informative.

Fourth, the timing parameters chosen to promote intuition versus reflection were calibrated to be consistent with prior work, but alternative timings and manipulations (e.g., cognitive load tasks, mindfulness interventions, or explicit priming of strategic thinking) could further probe the cognitive mechanisms. Additionally, process measures such as think-aloud protocols, response confidence ratings, or physiological indices could elucidate whether athletes and non-athletes differ in cognitive or affective responses during rapid decision making.

Several additional lines of inquiry emerge from the present results. One promising direction is longitudinal research that tracks individuals who begin sport participation and assesses whether changes in their cooperative heuristics emerge over time. Such longitudinal designs could help disentangle selection effects—whereby individuals predisposed to particular social tendencies select into sports—from causal effects of sport socialization. Intervention studies that manipulate aspects of sport training, such as emphasizing cooperative play or competitive individual performance, could further illuminate causal pathways.

Another important direction is to examine potential psychological mediators that might explain why athletes exhibit attenuated intuitive cooperation. Candidate mediators include habitual focus on competition, emphasis on performance optimization, heightened sensitivity to reputational consequences in team settings, or internalized norms about resource scarcity and fairness within sport cultures. Measuring these constructs and testing mediation would provide richer psychological theory bridging sport socialization and decision heuristics.

Cross-cultural research would also be valuable. Sports are embedded within broader cultural frameworks, and the social meaning of sport participation varies across societies. For example, in some cultures, team sports might be associated with communal values and civic engagement, whereas in others they might reflect intense competition and status-seeking. Cross-cultural comparisons could reveal whether the moderating role of sport participation on intuitive cooperation generalizes or is shaped by cultural norms.

The practical implications of these findings should be considered as well. If sport participation shapes intuitive responses to social dilemmas, then programs that employ sports to cultivate prosocial behavior—such as youth development initiatives—may need to attend carefully to the types of experiences and norms fostered in sport settings. Emphasizing cooperative norms and reflection on fair play within sports training might enhance the transfer of prosocial heuristics to other domains. Conversely, awareness that certain sports environments may cultivate more self-protective heuristics under pressure could inform coaching practices and educational interventions that aim to foster generalized cooperation.

Several limitations beyond those already discussed temper the conclusions. The study’s cross-sectional design prevents causal claims about sport participation shaping heuristics; selection effects cannot be ruled out. The sample was drawn from a university-adjacent population and may not represent broader age ranges or socio-economic contexts. The monetary stakes were modest, and participants’ motivations may differ under higher-stakes conditions. Finally, while timing manipulations are a widely used proxy for distinguishing intuition and reflection, cognitive processes are complex and cannot be fully captured by timing alone.

In conclusion, the current study contributes to our understanding of boundary conditions for the widely reported intuition-cooperation effect by demonstrating that participation in organized sports moderates this effect. Non-athletes exhibited increased cooperation under time pressure, consistent with the social heuristics hypothesis, whereas athletes did not show a comparable increase. These findings suggest that domain-specific socialization—in this case, experiences associated with sports participation—can alter the heuristics that are accessed under time pressure and thus influence cooperative behavior. Future research employing larger samples, longitudinal designs, deeper characterization of sport experiences, and a broader set of experimental paradigms will be valuable for clarifying the mechanisms and generalizability of these effects. Such work can advance both basic theory on the cognitive bases of prosocial behavior and practical efforts to promote cooperation across diverse social contexts.
