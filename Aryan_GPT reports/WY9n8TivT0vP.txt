Introduction

Human beings develop intuitive expectations about the physical world from very early in life, and these expectations often take the form of stable, structured representations that guide perception and action. These intuitive physical beliefs have been described variously as naive physics, intuitive physics, or folk physics, and they play a central role in how people interpret everyday events involving objects, forces, and motion. A large body of research has documented systematic errors in naive physics, showing that laypeople often make predictable mistakes about the trajectories of objects, the behavior of forces, and the outcomes of collisions. One influential demonstration of such systematic error was reported by McCloskey, Caramazza, and Green, who presented participants with diagrams of balls traveling through curved tubes and probed judgments about the subsequent paths of the balls after they left the tubes. Their work showed that university students frequently exhibited non-Newtonian responses, in particular endorsing trajectories that followed the curvature of the tube rather than leaving tangentially at the exit point in accordance with Newtonian mechanics. The McCloskey et al. findings have been widely cited as evidence that even educated adults can retain robust misconceptions about basic physical phenomena, and that these misconceptions may persist despite formal instruction in physics. Subsequent research has further explored the conditions under which naive physical intuitions dominate judgment and the ways that domain knowledge can modulate perceptual and reasoning outcomes. One line of work has conceptualized the interplay between knowledge and perception in terms of top-down versus bottom-up processing. In that framing, bottom-up processing emphasizes the role of incoming sensory information and local perceptual cues, whereas top-down processing involves the influence of stored knowledge, expectations, and theories on interpretation of sensory input. From a top-down perspective, a person with well-articulated knowledge of Newtonian mechanics would be expected to map diagrams of motion onto theoretical expectations and thus predict tangential motion when a ball exits a curved pipe. From a bottom-up perspective, a person relying on local perceptual features or heuristics might be more likely to extrapolate along the curved path and thus predict follow-through motion that remains curved after exit. Several experimental paradigms have attempted to test predictions derived from the top-down versus bottom-up account by manipulating either the nature of the stimuli or the knowledge available to participants. Some researchers have found that explicit instruction or prompting to attend to physical principles can shift judgments in the direction of physics-based expectations, while other studies have reported limited or inconsistent effects of instruction, suggesting that the activation of top-down knowledge is neither automatic nor guaranteed. A key theoretical question, therefore, concerns the conditions required for stored physical knowledge to exert reliable top-down influences on motion judgments: does mere exposure to a brief summary of physical laws suffice, or is more active, deeply encoded knowledge necessary? The presence of individual differences in prior physics knowledge, such as course-taking history, has also been implicated in the variability of responses to naive physics tasks. In particular, participants with formal training in physics tend to show more accurate, Newtonian-consistent predictions on motion tasks relative to their peers without such training, and this training effect interacts with the complexity of the stimulus. Complexity here is often operationalized in terms of tube curvature or the number of inflection points in a constrained motion path; more complex curvatures tend to exacerbate errors for naive reasoners and sometimes even for those with partial knowledge. It remains an open empirical question whether brief contextual primes that present core physical principles can substitute for extended formal training and thereby produce an immediate top-down influence sufficient to correct naive responses on motion tasks. The current study was designed to address this question by providing some participants with a concise contextual presentation of Newton’s laws of motion immediately prior to administering a set of motion path problems adapted from McCloskey et al.'s classic paradigm. The rationale was straightforward: if the activation of abstract knowledge about forces and inertia can orient participants to the physically relevant properties of the task, then those participants should be better able to focus on the diagnostic features of the diagrams and apply theory-consistent reasoning when judging post-exit trajectories. Conversely, participants who receive no contextual information would be expected to rely primarily on bottom-up perceptual features and heuristics and therefore exhibit the classic non-Newtonian tendencies documented by McCloskey and colleagues. This hypothesis involves two linked expectations. The first is an overall advantage for the context group relative to the control group in terms of accuracy on the motion path judgments. The second is an influence of stimulus curvature: as curvature increases, the task becomes more challenging and the benefit of context (if present) might be amplified because theory-based reasoning would be particularly diagnostic in resolving ambiguous local cues. In addition to testing the effect of a contextual prime, the study also sought to extend the original stimulus set by including two additional tube geometries that amplified curvature or introduced compound turns to probe the robustness of curvature effects. These extensions were intended to produce a wider range of item difficulty so that potential interactions between item curvature and participant knowledge activation could be examined. A further consideration was individual differences in prior physics knowledge. Because participants in university samples vary in their exposure to formal mechanics instruction, differential baseline knowledge could moderate the effect of the contextual prime. In the present design we therefore collected self-reported physics course history as a covariate and also conducted exploratory analyses to examine whether prior physics training predicted performance and whether it interacted with the contextual manipulation. It was also possible that simply reading a concise description of Newton’s laws would be insufficient to produce robust top-down effects, in which case the null hypothesis would remain viable: contextual primes would not significantly alter accuracy relative to control, and curvature effects would persist independently of the prime. In framing these alternatives we drew on the broader cognitive literature on instruction and transfer, which suggests that brief declarative information often fails to produce immediate changes in problem solving unless the information is engaged in active, task-relevant processing. Taken together, these lines of reasoning generated a clear empirical test. If a brief contextual presentation of Newtonian mechanics is enough to activate top-down processing and correct naive physics errors, then participants who received that context should outperform the control participants on the adapted McCloskey problems, with the effect potentially growing as curvature increases. If not, then no reliable performance advantage should be observed and curvature effects should be similar across groups. The study reported here therefore implemented a between-subjects design in which one group received a short context passage on Newton’s laws and their application to object motion before solving a set of tube exit problems, whereas a control group received an unrelated passage and then completed the same items. The items included the original problems used by McCloskey et al. and two additional problems designed to extend the curvature range and probe compound turning conditions. A relatively large sample size was selected to provide adequate power to detect medium-sized effects and to support exploratory analyses examining the role of prior physics coursework. The primary outcome measure was item-level accuracy, scored dichotomously as Newtonian-consistent or not, and analyzed both at the aggregate level and with item-based analyses to inspect curvature effects. Secondary measures included response confidence and response times, which were collected to assess whether the contextual manipulation influenced subjective certainty or deliberation time, even if it did not produce outright changes in accuracy. By integrating these measures and analytic strategies, the study aimed to clarify whether activating physical theory via brief context can immediately influence perceptual judgments about motion trajectories, and whether curvature interacts with this activation to produce differential outcomes. This experimental approach therefore sought to contribute to ongoing debates about the accessibility of domain knowledge in perception and the conditions under which top-down influences emerge to shape intuitive physical reasoning.

Methods

Participants were one hundred undergraduate students recruited from a large metropolitan university and enrolled in introductory-level courses across multiple departments. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 31 years, with a mean age of 20.3 years. The sample included 58 female participants, 40 male participants, and 2 participants who endorsed another gender identity. Recruitment was conducted through a subject pool and via in-class announcements, and participants received course credit or a small monetary stipend for their time. All participants provided informed consent and study procedures were approved by the institution's human subjects review committee. Inclusion criteria required that participants be fluent in English and have normal or corrected-to-normal vision; participants with self-reported neurological conditions or medications that could affect cognitive performance were excluded. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two between-subjects conditions in equal numbers: a context condition (n = 50) and a control condition (n = 50). Random assignment was implemented using a pseudo-random sequence generated prior to data collection and administered by the experimenter. The experiment was administered in small groups in a quiet laboratory space, with participants seated at individual computer stations to prevent interaction and to standardize presentation. Stimuli consisted of six schematic diagrams depicting a ball traveling through a tube and exiting into an open region; four of these diagrams were adapted directly from the classical McCloskey et al. paradigm, and two additional diagrams were created for this study to increase curvature variability. All diagrams were black-and-white line drawings presented on a neutral gray background, and each diagram included a clearly marked exit point and two or three plausible post-exit trajectory alternatives. For each item, participants were instructed to select the option that best represented the path the ball would take once it left the tube. The answer alternatives were designed so that one choice reflected the Newtonian tangent trajectory, one reflected a continuation of the curvature (i.e., curved trajectory), and in some items an intermediate or oblique trajectory was offered to capture partial-curvature responses. Item order was randomized for each participant to control for order effects. Prior to the main set of items, participants in the context condition read a concise, two-page passage summarizing the relevant principles of Newtonian mechanics, with emphasis on inertia and the expected motion of objects when external constraints are removed. The passage described Newton’s first law (inertia), the idea that an object in motion tends to continue in a straight line in the absence of unbalanced forces, and practical examples illustrating tangential motion when a constraint disappears. The passage was written to be accessible to participants without advanced physics backgrounds and took approximately five minutes to read; comprehension was encouraged but no explicit comprehension test was administered. Participants in the control condition read a two-page passage of similar length and reading complexity on an unrelated topic (general study habits and time management), designed to equalize time on task and minimize demand characteristics. After reading their assigned passage, participants completed the six diagram items. For each item they indicated their selected trajectory option and then provided a confidence rating on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all confident) to 7 (completely confident). Response times from item onset to selection were recorded automatically by the experimental software as a supplementary measure. After completing the items, participants provided demographic information and self-reported their prior formal education in physics by indicating whether they had taken no physics courses, an introductory physics course, or advanced physics courses (e.g., physics for scientists, engineering physics). They also reported their highest level of mathematics coursework as a rough index of quantitative background. No deception was used, and participants were fully debriefed at the completion of the experiment. Scoring of the primary outcome employed a binary coding scheme: selections that corresponded to a Newtonian tangent trajectory were coded as correct (1), and other selections were coded as incorrect (0). Two independent coders verified item keying and coding procedures prior to analytic work, and any discrepancies in coding were reconciled through discussion. Interrater agreement on coding for a 10% random subset of items across participants exceeded 98%, indicating reliable scoring. Data preprocessing involved excluding trials with response times less than 300 ms or greater than 60 seconds to remove inattentive or extreme trials; this led to the exclusion of 12 trials across the dataset, representing fewer than 1% of trials. The analytic plan specified three main analyses. First, a between-subjects comparison of overall accuracy (percentage of items answered correctly) was conducted to test the primary hypothesis that context improves Newtonian-consistent responses relative to control. This comparison used both an independent-samples t-test on participant-level mean accuracy and a generalized linear mixed model for item-level binary accuracy with random intercepts for participants and items to account for repeated measures. Second, curvature effects were assessed by treating items as varying in curvature level (low, medium, high) and conducting a mixed ANOVA with curvature as a within-subjects factor and condition as a between-subjects factor, as well as a mixed-effects logistic regression including curvature and the curvature-by-condition interaction. Third, exploratory analyses tested whether self-reported prior physics coursework predicted accuracy and whether it moderated the effect of the contextual manipulation; these analyses used both ANCOVA-type models and mixed-effects logistic models including prior coursework as a fixed effect and interaction term. Additional secondary analyses examined response confidence and response time differences between conditions and across curvature levels, using appropriate linear mixed-effects models. All models were estimated using standard software, and model assumptions (e.g., homoscedasticity, normality of residuals for linear models) were checked and met for the linear analyses; logistic models were assessed for overdispersion and convergence. Alpha was set at .05 for significance testing, and effect sizes (Cohen’s d for t-tests, odds ratios for logistic models) were reported to facilitate interpretation. Because the primary hypotheses involved group differences and item-level curvature effects, the mixed-effects models were considered the primary analytic tools due to their flexibility in modeling both participant and item variance.

Results

Descriptive statistics indicated that the overall mean accuracy across all participants and all six items was 0.565, meaning that participants selected Newtonian-consistent tangent responses on approximately 56.5% of trials. For the context condition, the mean participant-level accuracy was 0.58 (SD = 0.20), corresponding to 58% correct on average. For the control condition, the mean participant-level accuracy was 0.55 (SD = 0.21), corresponding to 55% correct on average. These descriptives suggested a modest numerical advantage for the context group, but the magnitude of the difference was small. An independent-samples t-test comparing participant-level accuracy between context and control groups revealed that the difference was not statistically significant, t(98) = 0.86, p = .39, Cohen’s d = 0.17. The 95% confidence interval around the mean difference included zero, indicating uncertainty about any reliable positive effect of context. Because the data were dichotomous at the trial level, a generalized linear mixed-effects logistic regression was also conducted to model item-level accuracy, with random intercepts for participants and items. In that model, condition (context vs control) was entered as a fixed effect. The estimated fixed effect for condition produced an odds ratio of 1.14 (95% CI [0.79, 1.65]), corresponding to a small and non-significant increase in odds of a Newtonian response for the context group relative to control, z = 0.78, p = .44. The mixed-effects model therefore corroborated the t-test result in finding no reliable benefit of the brief contextual passagе on binary accuracy. To examine curvature effects, items were categorized into three curvature levels based on the degree of tube curvature and complexity: low curvature (two items), medium curvature (two items), and high curvature (two items, including the two added for this study). Mean accuracy by curvature level showed modest decline with increased curvature: low curvature items had a mean accuracy of 0.64, medium curvature 0.58, and high curvature 0.50. A repeated-measures ANOVA on item-level accuracy with curvature as a within-subjects factor and condition as a between-subjects factor revealed a main effect of curvature that approached but did not reach conventional significance, F(2,196) = 2.78, p = .064, ηp^2 = .028. The main effect indicated trend-level reduced accuracy for more curved items, consistent with increased difficulty as curvature increased, but statistical evidence was weak. Crucially, there was no significant curvature-by-condition interaction, F(2,196) = 0.47, p = .63, indicating that the pattern of performance across curvature levels did not differ meaningfully between the context and control groups. A mixed-effects logistic regression including curvature as a fixed effect (treated as an ordinal predictor) and condition and their interaction also failed to detect significant effects for curvature or the interaction when modeled simultaneously; the curvature coefficient suggested reduced log-odds of Newtonian responses with higher curvature but was not significant, z = -1.61, p = .11, and the curvature-by-condition interaction term was not significant, z = -0.56, p = .58. Given concerns that the brief contextual passage might have differential effects for participants with some formal physics training, exploratory analyses considered the role of self-reported prior physics coursework. Participants were grouped into three levels: no physics coursework (n = 62), introductory physics only (n = 28), and advanced physics (n = 10). Descriptive inspection showed higher mean accuracy among those with advanced physics (mean = 0.78) relative to those with introductory physics (mean = 0.66) and those with no physics coursework (mean = 0.50). An ANOVA with prior coursework as a between-subjects factor revealed a significant main effect of physics background on accuracy, F(2,97) = 18.9, p < .001, ηp^2 = .28. Post hoc contrasts indicated that participants with any formal physics coursework performed better than those without coursework, and those with advanced coursework performed best. However, when prior coursework was included as a covariate in the primary mixed-effects model assessing context effects, the context fixed effect remained non-significant, z = 0.62, p = .54, and the interaction between context and prior coursework was also non-significant, z = 1.02, p = .31. Thus, although prior physics background predicted better overall performance, it did not appear to moderate the effect of the brief context passage. Secondary analyses examined response confidence ratings and response times. Mean confidence ratings were slightly higher in the context group (M = 4.21, SD = 1.12) compared to the control group (M = 4.01, SD = 1.15), but this difference did not reach significance in an independent-samples t-test, t(98) = 1.01, p = .31, Cohen’s d = 0.20. A linear mixed-effects model predicting confidence with condition, curvature, and their interaction as fixed effects yielded no significant effects of condition, condition-by-curvature interaction, or curvature main effects, indicating that subjective confidence did not vary reliably as a function of the manipulation. Response time data were positively skewed and therefore log-transformed prior to analysis. Median response times were similar across conditions, with geometric mean RTs of 3.8 seconds in the context group and 4.0 seconds in the control group. A mixed-effects linear model of log RTs with condition as a fixed effect produced a non-significant trend favoring slightly faster responses in the context group, t = -1.45, p = .15. This trend was not robust and should be interpreted cautiously. Item-level analyses revealed that two of the original McCloskey items were particularly prone to non-Newtonian responses, with correct rates below 40% across both groups. The two additional high-curvature items introduced for this study had somewhat lower correct rates on average than the original low-curvature items, but variability across items was high. An item analysis indicated that items accounted for approximately 12% of the variance in responses in the mixed-effects models, while participants accounted for 36% of the variance, underscoring the presence of individual differences. To quantify evidence for the null hypothesis regarding condition effects, a Bayesian mixed-effects logistic regression was also estimated with weakly informative priors. The Bayes factor comparing models with and without the condition fixed effect favored the null model by a factor of approximately 3:1, indicating moderate evidence for the absence of a context effect under the employed manipulation. Sensitivity analyses excluding the 10 participants with advanced physics coursework did not qualitatively change the main outcome: the context manipulation remained non-significant, and curvature effects remained modest and non-significant. In sum, the primary hypothesis that a brief contextual presentation of Newtonian mechanics would produce a significant overall improvement in accuracy was not supported by the data. Exploratory analyses reinforced the established link between physics coursework and better performance, but situational activation via a short passage did not produce the hypothesized top-down shift in responses.

Discussion

The present study tested whether a concise contextual activation of Newtonian mechanics could produce immediate top-down effects on judgments about object motion in a classic tube-exit paradigm. Our primary prediction—that participants who read a short passage summarizing Newton’s laws would show higher rates of Newtonian-consistent responses than participants who read an unrelated passage—was not supported. Across both participant-level and item-level analyses, the context manipulation produced only a small and statistically non-significant difference in accuracy, and Bayesian analysis provided moderate support for the null hypothesis that no meaningful context effect was present. Similarly, although mean accuracy declined numerically as curvature increased, curvature effects did not reach conventional significance, and there was no reliable curvature-by-condition interaction. These null results have several implications for theories about the accessibility of domain knowledge and the conditions under which top-down processes influence naive physics judgments. First, the absence of a reliable context benefit suggests that mere exposure to abstract declarative statements about Newton’s laws is insufficient to produce immediate and robust changes in motion judgments for a general undergraduate population. This finding aligns with broader evidence in the learning and cognitive transfer literatures indicating that brief, passive instruction often fails to alter procedural or intuitive reasoning without deeper engagement or practice. Even when declarative knowledge is accurate, its application to perceptual judgments may require explicit mapping between abstract principles and perceptual features, which was not part of the present manipulation. Second, the robust individual differences associated with prior physics coursework underscore that extended formal instruction can cultivate representational resources that support correct Newtonian responses. Participants who reported advanced physics coursework performed substantially better than participants without such coursework, and this effect held even when the brief context manipulation was considered. This pattern suggests that deep, proceduralized knowledge—acquired through cumulative instruction and problem solving—may be necessary for top-down influences to dominate perceptual or intuitive inferences about motion. In other words, knowledge must be not only accurate but also readily accessible and linked to perception in order to produce measurable changes. Third, the lack of a significant curvature effect in the present sample contrasts with the more consistent curvature-related inaccuracy reported by McCloskey and colleagues. There are multiple possible explanations for this divergence. One possibility is that variations in item construction and stimulus properties between the original and the present study altered item difficulty and the salience of diagnostic cues. Our two additional high-curvature items were designed to extend the stimulus range, but the behavior of participants on these items suggests that item-specific features strongly influenced responses. Another possibility is sample composition: university cohorts differ across institutions and semesters, and the present sample may have included a greater proportion of participants with implicit or explicit experience applying physical intuition, thereby attenuating curvature effects. A third possibility is that small methodological differences—such as the mode of presentation (computer display in a lab environment), the availability of response options, or the inclusion of confidence ratings—altered participants’ engagement and decision strategies. Overall, the null curvature effect observed here argues for caution in assuming that curvature-driven inaccuracy is universally robust across variations in materials and samples. The present findings also prompt consideration of processing accounts of naive physics. The original framing that contrasted top-down knowledge-driven processing with bottom-up perceptual extrapolation remains valuable, but our results suggest that top-down activation depends heavily on the form and depth of the knowledge representation and on the elicitation procedure. A short textual prime may activate declarative beliefs about forces in a superficial manner but fail to connect those beliefs to the perceptual heuristics participants apply when interpreting diagrams. Cognitive control and attention allocation also likely play roles; without directed prompts to map text to diagrammatic features or without opportunities to mentally simulate the motion, participants may default to rapid perceptual heuristics that favor curved continuations. Future work that explicitly induces active mapping—such as asking participants to predict and then visualize the path, to physically simulate motion using animations, or to engage in short practice trials with corrective feedback—might be more successful in eliciting top-down corrections. Methodologically, the study highlights the importance of measuring and controlling prior knowledge. Although our exploratory analyses showed predictable advantages for those with formal physics coursework, the context manipulation had no observed differential effect as a function of coursework. This suggests that brief context may not differentially benefit those with less prior knowledge, at least in the absence of scaffolding that helps learners translate declarative principles into perceptual expectations. Designing interventions that bridge this gap—such as worked examples, guided visualizations, or interactive simulations—could be a fruitful direction for future experiments. Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged and can inform future research. One limitation is the reliance on a passive reading manipulation to activate physical theory. Passive reading does not guarantee encoding or application, and without a comprehension check or an active generation task, it is difficult to ensure that participants integrated the information into their reasoning strategies. Future studies could implement short comprehension tasks, require participants to restate or apply the principles before proceeding to the main items, or provide interactive demonstrations that concretely tie Newtonian predictions to diagrammatic cues. A second limitation concerns the restricted stimulus set and the dichotomous scoring scheme. Although binary coding of Newtonian-consistent responses is consistent with prior work, richer response formats—such as continuous trajectory selection, free-hand drawing, or confidence-graded judgments—might provide more sensitive measures of partial knowledge or mixed-mode reasoning. Similarly, including animated trials where participants could view motion pre- and post-exit may better capture dynamic intuitions and could reveal whether context helps interpret motion when temporal information is present. Third, while the sample size was chosen to detect medium effects, it is possible that smaller, yet theoretically meaningful, context effects exist and would require larger samples to detect; however, the Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence favoring the null, which tempers the likelihood that a strong, practically significant effect was missed. Fourth, the self-reported nature of prior physics coursework is a coarse measure of relevant knowledge; future work would benefit from objective assessments of physics understanding or from sampling participants across defined educational strata such as physics majors versus non-majors. Theoretical implications of these results include a more nuanced view of the boundaries of top-down influence in intuitive physics. The data indicate that activation of formal physics principles is not automatically sufficient to override perceptual heuristics, at least when activated in a lightweight manner. This suggests that theories of naive physics must account for the representational and procedural specificity of knowledge: accurate belief content is not equivalent to proceduralized, perception-linked knowledge that can be flexibly applied. The findings also speak to debates about whether intuitive physics is primarily perceptual or conceptual in nature. Our data are consistent with a layered view in which perceptual heuristics often guide immediate judgments, and conceptual knowledge exerts influence only when it is sufficiently entrenched or when situational cues encourage deeper processing. Several concrete avenues for further research follow from this work. One approach would be to compare passive contextual primes with active training protocols that require participants to engage with Newtonian principles through simulations, practice trials, and feedback, to determine the minimal intervention required to produce reliable top-down corrections. A second approach would be to employ process-tracing methods, such as eye-tracking or think-aloud protocols, to observe how participants allocate attention on tube diagrams and whether context changes scanning patterns in ways that reflect more theory-driven analysis. Third, neurocognitive methodologies could be used to explore whether different neural networks are recruited when participants apply declarative knowledge versus perceptual heuristics, thereby elucidating mechanistic underpinnings of the top-down/bottom-up interplay. Fourth, developmental and cross-cultural work could assess the ontogeny and cultural variability of these naive physical beliefs and the conditions under which formal education shifts intuitive reasoning patterns. Fifth, computational modeling—such as Bayesian or connectionist models that formalize how prior beliefs interact with perceptual likelihoods—could provide quantitative frameworks to predict when context will succeed or fail in shifting judgments. In conclusion, the current study found that a brief contextual passage describing Newton’s laws did not produce a significant increase in Newtonian-consistent responses on a tube-exit motion task, even though prior formal physics coursework predicted superior performance. These findings indicate that activating declarative knowledge in a lightweight manner is not sufficient to override perceptual heuristics in intuitive physics judgments. The work therefore emphasizes the importance of proceduralized, deeply encoded knowledge and of instructional strategies that actively link abstract principles to perceptual representations. Future experiments that employ active learning, richer response formats, and process-sensitive measures are needed to map the boundary conditions under which top-down knowledge can reliably influence intuitive physical reasoning.
