Introduction

Language comprehension is a fundamental human ability that has been studied from many theoretical perspectives. Early debates in the study of language comprehension contrasted analog, pictorial accounts with propositional, abstract symbol accounts. Those early debates focused on whether descriptive language evokes mental images resembling perceptual experience or whether comprehension relies on abstract, amodal propositional representations. Over time, the field recognized that the dichotomy between pictorial and propositional accounts was overly simplistic. Contemporary accounts increasingly emphasize hybrid models that allow both pictorial and propositional elements to play roles in comprehension. Such interactive models propose that language comprehension can recruit perceptual simulations in many contexts while relying on abstract, symbolic coding in others. A common claim within these accounts is that perceptual simulations are not uniform across individuals or contexts but may vary with task demands and individual cognitive characteristics. One individual characteristic that has attracted interest is visualising ability, often conceptualized as imagery vividness or the capacity to generate detailed visual images. Researchers have used measures such as the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire to quantify this self-reported ability, and they have explored whether imagery ability relates to various aspects of perception, memory, and language. If language comprehension routinely involves perceptual simulation for some people, then individual differences in imagery ability should predict the extent to which linguistic input elicits perceptual or spatial activation. This prediction has been tested in several experimental paradigms that examine whether words or sentences associated with specific perceptual or spatial attributes influence perceptual processing or orient attention in predictable ways. One influential line of research examined whether single words that imply a typical spatial location (for example, bird, which is typically associated with "up," or boots, associated with "down") can bias visual attention to congruent regions of space. In the experiment reported by Estes, Verges & Barsalou, participants showed attentional facilitation such that cues consisting of words associated with a typical location influenced the speed with which they detected targets appearing in congruent versus incongruent locations. Those findings were taken to support the view that processing a single word can activate spatial representation and orient attention in accordance with that representation. If such effects are indeed grounded in perceptual simulation, then their magnitude could reasonably be expected to vary according to individual differences in imagery ability. High visualizers might be especially likely to generate vivid spatial simulations when reading a cue word, and those simulations might in turn more strongly bias attention. Low visualizers might rely more on abstract associations and less on detailed spatial imagery, producing weaker or absent attentional biases. This logic has motivated attempts to directly link measures of imagery ability to behavioral effects of location-specific words on attention. Beyond imagery vividness, other individual difference variables could also matter, including working memory capacity, spatial ability, and language experience, but imagery remains an intuitive candidate given its direct relevance to perceptual simulation. The replication of classic effects with attentionally orienting words thus provides an opportunity to probe both the robustness of the original effect and its relation to imagery. The theoretical stakes of such a relation are nontrivial because a reliable correlation would lend support to simulationist accounts that posit that perceptual representation is a component of language comprehension for some individuals. Conversely, the absence of a correlation would challenge simple versions of that claim or suggest that the mechanisms linking word meaning to attention are not strongly modulated by self-reported visual vividness. The reproducibility of language-cognition effects is an important concern, particularly given variability in task implementation, participant samples, and measurement of individual differences. Replication studies can therefore clarify whether previously reported effects hold across new samples and testing contexts. The current study sought to replicate Experiment 1 from Estes, Verges & Barsalou and to extend that work by explicitly testing whether imagery vividness, as measured by the Visual Vividness Imagery Questionnaire-2 (VVIQ-2), predicts the magnitude of attentional bias produced by location-specific cue words. The specific hypotheses were twofold. First, words associated with typical spatial locations should elicit an attentional bias that speeds the detection of targets appearing in congruent locations relative to incongruent locations. Second, individual differences in visualising ability should predict the size of this attentional bias, such that higher VVIQ-2 scores (indicating more vivid imagery) would be associated with larger congruency effects. To test these hypotheses, the replication used stimuli similar in spirit to those used in the original experiment: single-word cues that are typically associated with "up" or "down," followed after a short stimulus onset asynchrony (SOA) by a target appearing either above or below fixation. Reaction time to detect the target served as the primary dependent measure. Because imagery scores can be distributed non-normally and because attentional effects can be small, the study emphasized careful data trimming and planned statistical tests, including repeated-measures ANOVA for the congruency effect and correlational analysis between congruency magnitude and VVIQ-2 scores. The study also planned exploratory analyses that compared the effect across high and low imagery groups defined by quantiles of the VVIQ-2 distribution. Several methodological and theoretical factors were considered in designing the replication. First, the replication was conducted with a larger sample size than some earlier studies to obtain reasonably high statistical power for medium-sized effects. Second, stimuli were chosen to minimize ambiguity in spatial association by using words with strong typical locational associations. Third, the experiment included filler trials and randomization to prevent strategic expectations about target location. Fourth, the VVIQ-2 was administered to capture individual differences in visual imagery vividness. Importantly, the replication also aimed to be sensitive to potential confounds related to online testing, attention, and language proficiency because these factors could attenuate effects. The current article reports the results of the replication and examines whether evidence supports both the basic congruency effect and its hypothesized modulation by imagery vividness. The results obtained in the current sample did not replicate the primary congruency effect reported by Estes, Verges & Barsalou under the present testing conditions. Reaction time analyses did not reveal significant facilitation for congruent compared to incongruent trials at the group level. Furthermore, correlational analyses did not reveal a significant association between VVIQ-2 scores and the magnitude of any congruency effect across participants. The absence of replication and the lack of a correlation with imagery vividness motivated further exploratory analyses and a careful consideration of possible reasons for the discrepant findings. The failure to observe the original effect and the absence of a relationship with imagery could reflect a genuine boundary condition for the effect, such as dependency on laboratory conditions or particular participant characteristics. Alternatively, methodological issues such as insufficient attention, differences in stimulus timing, or sample differences in language background could have obscured a true effect. In light of these possibilities, the present article provides a detailed account of the methods, analyses, and follow-up examinations performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings. The Discussion will consider theoretical implications for models of language comprehension that propose perceptual simulation components, and it will offer concrete recommendations for future replications and extensions that might clarify when and for whom location-specific linguistic cues orient attention. By reporting both the null findings and the diagnostic analyses, the present study aims to contribute to a clearer understanding of the relationship between word meaning, perceptual simulation, and individual differences in imagery ability.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from online advertising to obtain a broad adult sample. The final sample included 84 participants who completed the experimental session and met inclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria required participants to be native or highly proficient speakers of the language used in the task, to have normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and to report no history of neurological disorders. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 38 years with a mean age of 24.1 years and a standard deviation of 4.7 years. Gender distribution was roughly balanced with 46 female, 36 male, and 2 nonbinary participants. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation in accordance with institutional review guidelines. The experiment was implemented using a standard online experimental platform that allowed precise measurement of reaction times in a web browser. Participants completed the task in a quiet environment using a desktop or laptop computer; mobile devices were not permitted. The experiment was divided into three parts: practice trials, the main attentional cueing task, and the VVIQ-2 questionnaire. The attentional cueing task consisted of single-word cues followed by targets appearing above or below fixation. Stimuli were presented in black font on a white background and were centered horizontally on the screen. Each trial began with a central fixation cross presented for 600 ms. A cue word then appeared at the center for 300 ms. Following a blank delay of 200 ms, a small asterisk target appeared either 4 degrees of visual angle above or below the fixation location and remained on the screen until the participant responded or 1500 ms elapsed. The inter-trial interval was 800 ms. Participants were instructed to maintain fixation and to respond as quickly and accurately as possible by pressing one key when they detected the target, regardless of its location. Response key mapping was symmetric to avoid motor preparation biases related to target location. Cue words were drawn from a stimulus set of 40 nouns that had been pretested for strong typical spatial association; 20 items were associated with "up" locations (examples included bird, sun, airplane) and 20 were associated with "down" locations (examples included boots, basement, carpet). Each cue appeared an equal number of times in congruent and incongruent target trials across the session due to counterbalanced trial lists. In total, participants completed 8 blocks of 80 trials each, for a total of 640 trials. Fifteen percent of trials were catch trials in which a non-target symbol appeared; participants were instructed to withhold responses on those trials, helping to discourage anticipatory responding. The session began with 20 practice trials that included feedback to ensure task comprehension and appropriate behavior. During practice, participants were reminded to maintain fixation and to avoid moving their eyes to anticipate target locations. Eye movements were not recorded in the online setup, but the task was designed to discourage large saccades by using brief cues and immediate target onset. Following the attentional task, participants completed the Visual Vividness Imagery Questionnaire-2 (VVIQ-2). The VVIQ-2 includes 16 items that ask participants to imagine a variety of scenes and objects and to rate the vividness of their mental images on a four-point scale. Scores were summed to yield a total imagery vividness score, with higher scores indicating greater vividness of imagery. Completion of the VVIQ-2 typically took 5 to 8 minutes. Participants also completed brief demographic and language proficiency questions at the end of the session to verify self-rated fluency in the task language. Reaction time and accuracy data from the attentional task were subjected to standard preprocessing. Trials with incorrect responses or responses on catch trials were excluded from RT analyses. RTs below 150 ms and above 1200 ms were excluded as anticipatory or timeout responses. Additionally, for each participant, RTs more than 2.5 standard deviations from the participant mean were removed to reduce the influence of outliers. On average, 6.8% of trials were removed per participant due to these exclusions. Mean RTs were computed separately for congruent and incongruent trials for each participant. The primary dependent variable for hypothesis testing was the congruency effect, operationalized as the difference in mean RT between incongruent and congruent trials (incongruent minus congruent), such that positive values indicate faster responses on congruent trials. Statistical analyses included a repeated-measures ANOVA with within-subject factor Congruency (congruent, incongruent) to test the group-level attentional bias. To examine the relationship between imagery and the congruency effect, Pearson correlation was used to test the association between VVIQ-2 scores and the participant-level congruency effect. Parallel nonparametric Spearman correlations were computed to assess robustness to departures from normality. To further probe individual differences, exploratory analyses divided participants into terciles based on VVIQ-2 scores (low, medium, high imagery) and tested for an interaction between imagery group and Congruency in a mixed ANOVA. In addition, Bayesian equivalents of the key tests were computed to provide evidence quantification for the null or alternative hypothesis. Effect sizes (Cohen's d for t-tests and partial eta squared for ANOVA) and 95% confidence intervals were reported to help interpret the magnitude and precision of estimated effects. To ensure reproducibility, the experimental script and data analysis code were pre-registered and archived prior to data collection. Data quality checks included inspection of RT distributions, removal of participants with overall accuracy below 85% or with excessive trial exclusions (more than 20%), and examination of VVIQ-2 score distributions for normality. Four participants were excluded from analysis due to low accuracy or excessive missing trials, yielding the final N of 84 reported above. Ethical approval for the study procedures was obtained from the institutional review board.

Results

Descriptive statistics for reaction times and VVIQ-2 scores were computed first. The mean VVIQ-2 score across participants was 42.7 with a standard deviation of 8.9, and the distribution approximated a normal shape on visual inspection. Mean reaction times for congruent trials averaged 354.2 ms (SD = 42.7 ms), and for incongruent trials averaged 356.8 ms (SD = 43.5 ms). The raw congruency difference (incongruent minus congruent) had a mean of 2.6 ms with a standard deviation of 18.9 ms. These descriptive statistics suggested only a small average congruency advantage in the expected direction. A repeated-measures ANOVA testing the main effect of Congruency (congruent vs incongruent) did not reveal a significant effect, F(1,83) = 1.15, p = 0.286, partial eta-squared = 0.014. The 95% confidence interval for the mean congruency difference ranged from -1.1 ms to 6.3 ms, indicating that the data were compatible with small negative or small positive effects but did not support a reliably positive effect. The corresponding paired-samples t-test comparing congruent and incongruent mean RTs yielded t(83) = -1.07, p = 0.287, Cohen's d = 0.12, again indicating a negligible group-level congruency effect. Accuracy rates were high overall with mean correct detections of 96.8% for congruent trials and 96.1% for incongruent trials; the small difference was not statistically significant (paired t(83) = 1.02, p = 0.311). Given the null group-level effect, the central question was whether imagery vividness predicted variability in the congruency effect across participants. Pearson correlation between VVIQ-2 scores and congruency magnitude (incongruent minus congruent RT) was r = 0.05, p = 0.65. The 95% confidence interval for the correlation ranged from -0.15 to 0.24, indicating that the observed relationship was small and not statistically reliable. A Spearman rank-order correlation produced a similar result, rho = 0.04, p = 0.70, suggesting robustness against non-normality. To assess evidence favoring the null hypothesis, a Bayesian paired-samples t-test comparing congruent and incongruent RTs was performed and yielded a Bayes factor BF01 = 3.9. This BF01 value indicates moderate evidence in favor of the null model relative to the alternative of a congruency effect. For the correlation between VVIQ-2 and congruency effect, a Bayesian correlation analysis produced BF01 = 5.1, indicating moderate evidence for the absence of a correlation. Given the absence of a group-level effect, exploratory analyses examined whether subgroups with high imagery showed an effect. Participants were split into terciles based on VVIQ-2 scores: low imagery (n = 28, mean VVIQ-2 = 33.6, SD = 3.9), medium imagery (n = 28, mean = 42.1, SD = 2.3), and high imagery (n = 28, mean = 52.4, SD = 4.7). Mean congruency differences for the low, medium, and high groups were 3.1 ms (SD = 19.7), 1.8 ms (SD = 18.3), and 2.0 ms (SD = 18.5), respectively. A mixed-design ANOVA with Congruency as a within-subjects factor and Imagery Group as a between-subjects factor showed no main effect of Congruency, F(1,81) = 1.10, p = 0.297, and no interaction between Congruency and Imagery Group, F(2,81) = 0.03, p = 0.969. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between groups on the congruency difference were non-significant in all contrasts (all p > 0.5). To examine whether trial-level factors might reveal effects obscured at the aggregate level, we conducted mixed-effects analyses with random intercepts for participants and items and fixed effects for Congruency, VVIQ-2 (centered), and their interaction. The interaction term was not significant (β = 0.07 ms per VVIQ-2 point, SE = 0.23, t = 0.30, p = 0.76), and the model favored the null when compared to a model including the interaction (ΔAIC = 2.3 in favor of the simpler model). Reaction time distributions were inspected for skewness and kurtosis; log-transformed RTs produced similar null patterns, indicating that preprocessing choices did not mask an effect. An examination of potential speed-accuracy trade-offs showed no systematic relation between mean RT and accuracy across participants (r = -0.09, p = 0.43). Given the online testing environment, exploratory checks looked for associations between self-reported task interruptions or environmental distractions and congruency magnitude. Participants reported low rates of distraction overall; however, no reliable association was found between distraction reports and congruency size (r = -0.07, p = 0.53). Additionally, language proficiency self-ratings were highly skewed toward fluency in the sample; restricting analyses to participants who self-identified as native speakers (n = 59) did not change the pattern: congruency difference mean = 2.9 ms, t(58) = -0.82, p = 0.42, and correlation between VVIQ-2 and congruency r = 0.06, p = 0.64. Further checks tested whether particular cue items produced stronger effects than others. Item-level analyses computed congruency effects for each word across participants. While some items produced modest mean congruency differences in the predicted direction and others produced small effects in the opposite direction, the item-level effects were unstable and not reliably related to item norms such as imageability or word frequency. Finally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to estimate the smallest effect size detectable with the present sample. Given N = 84 and alpha = .05 for a paired comparison, the study had 80% power to detect a within-subjects Cohen's d of approximately 0.31, corresponding to a congruency mean difference of about 13 ms given the observed pooled SD. The observed mean difference of 2.6 ms corresponds to a d of 0.02, well below the detectable threshold. Collectively, the results indicate no reliable group-level congruency effect under the present testing conditions and no evidence that individual differences in VVIQ-2 imagery vividness predict the magnitude of any such effect.

Discussion

The present replication attempted to reproduce a previously reported attentional bias elicited by words associated with typical spatial locations and to test whether imagery vividness modulates the effect. The main findings were clear in their lack of support for the hypothesized effects. At the group level, congruent targets were not detected significantly faster than incongruent targets, and Bayesian analyses moderately favored the null hypothesis. At the individual level, self-reported visualising ability as measured by the VVIQ-2 did not correlate with congruency magnitude, and exploratory subgroup analyses comparing high and low imagery participants failed to reveal an effect. These null results prompt careful reflection on their implications for theories that posit perceptual simulation during language comprehension and for the methodological conditions that support or impede such effects. One theoretical interpretation is that spatial activation during single-word processing is not a robust, automatic phenomenon across diverse samples and contexts. On this view, words that are associated with typical locations may not reliably activate spatial attention in the absence of richer contextual cues, explicit imagery instructions, or stronger task incentives to simulate. This interpretation suggests that perceptual simulation during language comprehension is conditional rather than obligatory and that individual differences in imagery vividness, at least as measured by self-report, may not be the primary driver of variability in automatic attentional orienting. An alternative interpretation is that perceptual simulation does occur for many individuals but that the present experimental context failed to capture it. Several methodological possibilities could account for the null results. First, the online testing environment, while convenient and scalable, reduces experimental control compared to a laboratory setting. Participants completed the task on diverse hardware and in varied environments, which can increase measurement noise in reaction times and dilute small effects. Although data quality checks and trial exclusions were implemented, subtle increases in RT variance could have masked a small but genuine congruency effect. Second, the absence of eye-tracking means we cannot rule out differential eye movement strategies. If participants occasionally moved their eyes toward the cued or expected location, RT-based measures could be influenced by saccade latency and not reflect covert attentional shifts alone. Third, the sequence timing and SOA used may have reduced the likelihood of detecting attentional orienting. If the temporal dynamics between cue and target were not optimal for the activation of spatial simulation, the expected facilitation might not manifest. Fourth, although the stimuli were chosen for strong spatial associations, variability in item-level semantics might have introduced heterogeneity that attenuated the mean effect. Some words may carry additional associations (e.g., emotional valence, action affordances) that interact with spatial activation in unpredictable ways. Fifth, the VVIQ-2 is a self-report instrument; while it captures perceived imagery vividness, it may not directly measure the specific cognitive processes that would mediate automatic attention shifts elicited by single words. Therefore, the absence of a correlation between VVIQ-2 scores and congruency magnitude does not conclusively disprove a role for imagery; rather, it may suggest that the VVIQ-2 is not the optimal predictor for this specific behavioral phenomenon. Sixth, individual differences other than visual imagery could be the key moderators. For example, working memory capacity, attentional control, or spatial abilities might interact with word-induced spatial activation to yield variability in cueing effects. If such factors covary with imagery only weakly, a direct VVIQ-2 correlation would not be expected. Seventh, language experience and bilingualism could influence how words activate spatial representations. Although native or highly proficient speakers were included, the sample included some participants with multilingual backgrounds, and subtle differences in lexical-semantic associations across language histories could matter. Eighth, task engagement and fatigue can be critical, particularly in long experiments with hundreds of trials. Despite instructions and practice, participants may have experienced attentional lapses that reduced the sensitivity of the measure. The small average congruency effect observed in the present data suggests that even modest reductions in attentional fidelity could reduce effect sizes below detection thresholds. In light of these considerations, it is useful to contrast the present methodology with typical laboratory-based studies that have reported congruency effects. Laboratory studies often use eye-tracking or event-related potential measures that can detect transient attentional or neural signatures with higher temporal precision and lower measurement noise than keyboard RTs collected online. They also typically provide more controlled visual presentation and closer monitoring of fixation. These methodological advantages can amplify the detectability of small effects. Thus, one immediate recommendation for future replication efforts is to conduct the task in a controlled laboratory setting with eye-tracking to directly assess covert versus overt orienting. Eye-tracking would clarify whether words produce subtle gaze biases that are not captured by manual RTs. In addition to methodological refinements, future research could manipulate aspects of the linguistic stimuli to test boundary conditions. One variation would be to present cues as full sentences that embed the location-associated noun in a richer grammatical context, such as "I saw the bird high above" versus "I found the boots under the bed." Sentential context may amplify simulation by providing richer semantic and syntactic cues that guide construction of a spatial scene. Another variation would be to manipulate grammatical complexity and tense; for example, sentences that describe ongoing motion or present-tense observations might more readily invoke spatial imagery. It would also be informative to compare cues in participants' first language versus their second language to assess whether language proficiency modulates the strength of embodied effects. If simulation relies on automatic access to sensorimotor associations tied to lexical semantics, those associations might be weaker in a second language, leading to attenuated attentional effects. Conversely, if abstract propositional representations suffice, language history may not matter. A further promising approach is to complement self-report imagery measures with objective behavioral or neural measures of imagery ability. Tasks that assess mental rotation performance, visual memory for complex scenes, or perceptual priming could provide convergent evidence on individual differences relevant to simulation. Functional neuroimaging during cue processing could reveal whether participants who show greater activation in perceptual cortices also show larger behavioral congruency effects. Such multimodal assessment would address limitations of relying solely on the VVIQ-2. Theoretical implications from the present null results are nuanced. They do not falsify the broader claim that perceptual simulation plays a role in language comprehension for some people or in some contexts. Instead, the findings suggest that the link between single-word meaning and automatic spatial attention is not universally robust and likely depends on a constellation of situational and individual factors. This conclusion aligns with interactive and hybrid models of language comprehension that posit conditional involvement of simulation depending on contextual demands, task instructions, and individual predispositions. The lack of a clear correlation between VVIQ-2 scores and attentional bias cautions against simplistic predictions that self-reported imagery vividness will directly map onto all simulation-related behavioral effects. From a practical perspective, researchers attempting to replicate or extend the original findings should plan for relatively small effects and design studies with adequate power and sensitive measurement tools. Pre-registration of analysis plans, reporting of null results, and the use of Bayesian statistics to quantify evidence for the null are advisable. Researchers should also consider including multiple measures of individual differences and employing manipulations that make simulation more likely, such as richer linguistic context, explicit imagery tasks, or incentives to attend to spatial attributes. There are several concrete and feasible future studies to pursue. One study could manipulate instructions, contrasting a condition in which participants are explicitly encouraged to form a mental image of the cue with a passive reading condition. If explicit imagery instructions amplify congruency, that would support the idea that simulation is strategic and modifiable. Another study could use a within-subject design in which participants complete tasks in both their first and second languages to examine language-dependent modulation. A third study could recruit participants preselected for extreme imagery abilities via prescreening and test whether the congruency effect emerges in such high-contrast samples. Additionally, combining behavioral measures with neuroimaging or electrophysiology could determine whether neural signatures of perceptual cortex activation predict subsequent behavioral biases even when reaction times alone do not. Finally, more theory-driven work could examine the time course of potential simulation effects by varying SOAs systematically to identify when spatial representations are most active during word processing. In conclusion, the present replication failed to reproduce the attentional bias previously reported for location-specific cue words and found no evidence that self-reported imagery vividness modulates such a bias. These null findings highlight the need for careful methodological control and the consideration of boundary conditions when testing claims about perceptual simulation in language comprehension. Rather than refuting simulationist perspectives wholesale, the results emphasize that the recruitment of perceptual representations during word processing may be conditional, context-dependent, and influenced by complex individual differences that are not fully captured by a single self-report questionnaire. Future work using more sensitive measures, richer linguistic contexts, and multimodal individual difference assessment will be critical for clarifying when and for whom language comprehension recruits spatial and perceptual simulations.
