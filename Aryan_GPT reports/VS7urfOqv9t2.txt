Introduction

Human decision making under risk has been a central topic in psychology, economics, and decision sciences for many decades. Research on risky choice has traditionally focused on monetary gambles in which outcomes are described explicitly as probabilities and magnitudes of gains or losses. Such research has produced important theories and robust empirical findings, but it has also led to a somewhat narrow view of how people evaluate and choose under risk. In real-world settings, many risky decisions involve outcomes of a nonfinancial nature such as health, safety, or social consequences, and people commonly learn about the likelihoods of these outcomes through experience rather than explicit description. The distinction between decisions from description and decisions from experience was highlighted in a series of influential studies that showed systematic differences in choice behavior depending on how probabilities are learned. In those studies, decisions from description referred to choices in which probabilities and outcomes are stated explicitly to the decision maker, whereas decisions from experience referred to choices in which decision makers sample outcomes from repeated play or otherwise learn about probabilities through feedback. Researchers including Hertwig, Barron, Weber, and Erev showed that choices based on experience often produce underweighting of rare events relative to stated probabilities, a pattern that is opposite to some of the traditional findings from described gambles in which rare events are often overweighted. The description–experience gap has been replicated across multiple studies and has been used to explain patterns in domains such as financial decisions, insurance uptake, and consumer choices. However, the generality of the description–experience gap across different domains and outcome types remains an open question. Subsequent work has emphasized that the magnitude and even the direction of differences between experience-based and description-based decisions can depend on a range of boundary conditions including outcome valence (gains versus losses), feedback structure, sampling effort, and the psychological salience of outcomes. One line of research has sought to examine whether these differences extend to decisions with health outcomes, given that medical decisions often involve aversive and personally salient consequences. Pachur and Galesic adapted classic decisions-from-experience paradigms to medical scenarios and demonstrated that domain matters: when outcomes involve health risks, emotional salience and perceived seriousness can alter risk perception and choice in ways that are not fully captured by financial paradigms. This suggests that the description–experience dichotomy may interact with domain-specific factors such that individuals might be less prone to underweight rare but serious events when outcomes are medical as opposed to monetary. This idea rests on several related findings in the literature. First, studies on risk perception and affect show that emotionally charged outcomes draw more attention and are processed differently than abstract monetary outcomes. Emotional responses can amplify sensitivity to salient or vivid events and increase perceived probability or severity of negative outcomes. Second, work on numeracy and cognitive ability indicates that the way probabilities are processed and integrated into choice depends on individual differences in numerical skills; people with higher numeracy are more likely to use probabilistic information as stated and are less prone to biases arising from sampling variability. Third, demographic variables such as age and gender have been shown to correlate with risk preferences in multiple domains, including health and finance, suggesting that individual-level moderators may shape the description–experience effect. Taken together, these strands of research motivate investigation of whether the description–experience gap observed in financial decision contexts generalizes to medical decisions and how individual differences might moderate any such generalization. The present study was designed to explore these questions by adapting classic description-versus-experience paradigms to scenarios that involve medical side effects in addition to financial gambles. We reasoned that the potential seriousness of medical outcomes would increase the salience and vividness of rare adverse events, thereby reducing the common underweighting of rare events observed in experience-based financial choices. Concretely, our primary hypothesis was that participants would not underestimate risk through experience when the outcomes involved medical side effects, in contrast to the underweighting typically observed in financial domains when probabilities are learned by sampling. We further expected that the learning condition (description vs experience) would interact with domain (financial vs medical) such that the description–experience gap would be smaller in medical dilemmas than in financial dilemmas. To enable these comparisons, we adapted medical dilemmas from Pachur and Galesic and constructed parallel financial dilemmas that matched outcome magnitudes and stated probabilities. We also included measures of numeracy as an individual-differences variable to examine whether numeracy would predict the degree to which participants’ choices aligned with stated probabilities versus experience-based sampling outcomes. We collected demographic information including age and gender to explore whether these variables were associated with choice patterns across domains and learning conditions. Previous literature suggests that higher numeracy should be associated with greater adherence to described probabilities and potentially reduced description–experience discrepancies, whereas age and gender differences might manifest as domain-specific shifts in risk aversion. The experimental design combined a between-subjects manipulation of learning condition with a within-subjects manipulation of domain so that each participant made choices in both financial and medical scenarios but only experienced outcomes either from description or from sampling. This mixed design allowed us to test main effects of learning condition and domain as well as their interaction, and to examine how individual differences predicted variation in risk scores across conditions. We designed a repeated-measures ANOVA framework as our primary analysis approach, with follow-up contrasts and correlational analyses to probe potential moderators. We placed an emphasis on predefining our risk score metric as a continuous measure capturing the extent to which a participant favored risky options over safe options across multiple dilemmas, rather than relying on binary choice counts alone. Methodological considerations guided our decisions about task length, sampling opportunities in the experience condition, and the representation of probabilities in the description condition. Specifically, we sought to balance ecological validity—by making medical outcomes vivid and meaningful—with experimental control—by equating probabilities and outcome magnitudes across domains. Despite careful piloting, we recognized that sample size, sampling variability, and individual differences could contribute to variability in outcomes and potentially limit statistical power to detect effects. The literature on description–experience effects contains examples of both robust effects and context-dependent null findings, suggesting that replication attempts must navigate the sensitivity of effect sizes to procedural details. We therefore included exploratory analyses to examine whether procedural factors such as sampling history or perceived familiarity with medical issues influenced risk scores. Our preregistered predictions focused on the primary hypothesis that the underweighting of rare events in experience-based choices would be attenuated or absent for medical dilemmas relative to financial dilemmas. We also predicted numeracy would moderate choices such that higher numeracy participants would exhibit smaller differences between description and experience conditions, consistent with prior work indicating that numerate individuals better utilize stated probabilities. Finally, we anticipated that age and gender might explain some variance in domain-specific risk-taking and included these variables as covariates in our main analyses. The current paper reports on a between- and within-subjects study in which 72 participants completed choice tasks involving financial and medical dilemmas under either description or experience learning conditions. We provide detailed descriptions of the stimuli and procedures, present repeated-measures ANOVA results and follow-up analyses, and discuss the implications of the results for the generality of the description–experience gap and the role of individual differences. We also consider methodological limitations that may account for null findings and suggest avenues for future research that could clarify the boundary conditions of description–experience effects in health-related decision making. The remainder of this introduction elaborates on the specific theoretical considerations that shaped our predictions and explains how our experimental design was intended to adjudicate between competing accounts of the description–experience gap. One theoretical account posits that experience-based underweighting of rare events arises because sampling variability leads to underrepresentation of rare events in limited samples, and because people form expectations based on observed frequencies rather than stated probabilities. Under this account, longer sampling or more systematic sampling strategies should reduce the gap. A competing account emphasizes affect and vividness: rare, emotionally charged outcomes that are highly salient may be overrepresented psychologically even if they are infrequent in sampling, thereby reducing the description–experience gap in domains such as health. By testing medical dilemmas that invoke negative bodily outcomes and side effects, our study sought to evaluate the relative contribution of sampling variability versus affect-driven salience in shaping risk behavior. If sampling variability were the dominant mechanism, we would expect similar patterns of underweighting of rare events across domains when sampling opportunities are matched. If affect-driven salience were more important, we would expect a reduced description–experience gap for medical dilemmas. Furthermore, if numeracy plays a central role in how people integrate described probabilities, individuals with higher numeracy should show less discrepancy between description- and experience-based choices across domains. The interplay of these mechanisms—sampling variability, affect, and numeracy—motivated our inclusion of both domain manipulations and individual differences measures. We also considered potential gender and age effects, given that prior work has documented gender differences in risk aversion and age-related changes in risk perception. For example, women often report higher levels of health-related risk aversion than men, which could affect choices in the medical domain regardless of learning condition. Similarly, older adults might be more sensitive to health-related risks or might use different heuristics when learning from experience, which could interact with the description–experience manipulation. By incorporating these covariates into our analyses, we aimed to explore whether the description–experience effect is moderated by such demographic factors. In sum, this study is positioned at the intersection of decision-making theory on description versus experience, domain-specific research on medical decision making, and individual-differences research on numeracy, age, and gender. The study’s principal goal was to test whether the canonical underweighting of rare events in experience-based decisions extends to choices involving medical side effects, and whether individual differences modulate any observed effects. The following sections detail the experimental methods, report the statistical analyses and outcomes, and offer an interpretation of the findings in the context of the broader literature.

Methods

Participants and recruitment

Seventy-two adults participated in the study. Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from a local community mailing list to obtain a range of ages and backgrounds. The final sample consisted of 72 individuals aged 18 to 68 years, with a mean age of 29.7 years and a standard deviation of 11.4 years. The gender breakdown was 39 women and 33 men, with no participants identifying outside the binary categories in the sample. Participants provided informed consent before taking part in the study and were compensated with either course credit or a small monetary payment for their time. The study protocol was approved by the university’s institutional review board and complied with ethical standards for research with human participants. We screened for basic exclusion criteria including inability to read English fluently and prior participation in similar description–experience experiments, and no participants were excluded for health reasons related to the medical content of the dilemmas.

Design

The experimental design was a mixed factorial design. Learning condition (description vs experience) was manipulated between-subjects and served as the primary independent variable. Domain (financial vs medical) was manipulated within-subjects so that each participant completed an equal number of financial and medical dilemmas. The order of domain presentation was counterbalanced across participants to control for order effects. Within each domain, participants faced a series of binary choice dilemmas that were adapted from canonical paradigms in the description–experience literature and from medical vignettes used by Pachur and Galesic. The dependent variable was a continuous risk score that indexed a participant’s tendency to choose the risky option as opposed to the safe option across dilemmas; higher scores indicated greater risk preference. Additional measures included a standardized numeracy test, demographic information (age, gender, education), and manipulation checks assessing perceived seriousness and familiarity for each dilemma.

Materials

Stimuli consisted of 16 binary choice dilemmas, with eight financial dilemmas and eight medical dilemmas. Each dilemma presented one safe option with a certain but smaller payoff or outcome and one risky option that offered a larger possible payoff but with an associated probability of a negative outcome. For the financial dilemmas, safe options took the form of guaranteed small monetary gains or losses, whereas the risky options were probabilistic gambles involving larger gains or potential losses. For the medical dilemmas, the safe options consisted of standard treatment choices with known but modest effectiveness and low side-effect profiles, while the risky options represented treatments with higher potential benefits but with probabilistic side effects, including rare but serious adverse events. The probabilities and outcome magnitudes were equated as closely as possible across paired financial and medical dilemmas to facilitate domain comparisons. Presented probabilities ranged from 1% to 70% depending on the scenario and were selected to include several low-probability (rare) events for which the description–experience literature predicts underweighting in experience-based learning. The dilemmas were reviewed for clarity and pretested on a small sample to ensure comprehension and comparable perceived severity across scenarios.

Procedure

Participants were randomly assigned to either the description condition (n = 36) or the experience condition (n = 36). In the description condition, each dilemma included an explicit statement of the probabilities associated with each outcome and a textual depiction of the magnitude of potential benefits or side effects. Participants read each dilemma and then selected their preferred option between the safe and risky alternatives. Choices in the description condition were therefore informed by stated probabilities. In the experience condition, probabilities were not explicitly stated. Instead, participants learned about outcome frequencies by sampling: they observed a series of outcome draws that corresponded to the outcome distribution of the risky option, and the sampling sequences were generated according to the intended probabilities for each dilemma. For each risky option, participants were allowed up to 30 sampling draws to see realized outcomes (e.g., occurrence or nonoccurrence of a side effect) before making a decision. The number of samples taken was recorded as a behavioral measure of sampling effort. The safe option outcomes were deterministic and described to participants as certain in the experience condition, in order to equate knowledge about safe options across conditions. To reduce suspicion, sampling outcomes were presented as discrete trials with short time intervals between draws and were labeled as “simulated patient outcomes” in the medical domain and “simulated investment outcomes” in the financial domain. After completing the choice tasks, participants completed a numeracy assessment (the Berlin Numeracy Test short form was used for brevity) and answered self-report measures about perceived seriousness, familiarity, and emotional impact of medical and financial scenarios. Manipulation checks asked participants in the description condition to restate the probabilities for randomly selected dilemmas and in the experience condition to estimate the probabilities based on their sampling experience.

Risk score calculation

We computed a risk score for each participant and for each domain by assigning 1 to risky choices and 0 to safe choices and summing across the eight dilemmas within each domain to yield a domain-specific risk count ranging from 0 to 8. We then normalized these counts to a 0–1 scale by dividing by 8 to facilitate interpretation and comparisons across domains. The primary dependent variable in the repeated-measures ANOVA was thus the normalized risk score with a within-subjects factor of domain and a between-subjects factor of learning condition. We also examined raw counts and performed secondary analyses using alternative coding schemes (e.g., weighting choices by the objective expected value difference) to ensure robustness of findings.

Data quality and exclusions

We had prespecified exclusion criteria including failure of manipulation checks (e.g., inability to restate probabilities correctly in the description condition on more than half of the checked items) and extremely short response times suggesting inattention. Two participants failed the manipulation checks and were excluded from primary analyses, leaving a final analysis sample of 70 participants (n = 35 per learning-condition group). All analyses reported below are based on the 70-participant sample unless otherwise noted. For transparency, we also report sensitivity analyses including the excluded participants and indicate where inclusion altered the results.

Statistical analysis plan

The primary analysis was a mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVA with domain (medical vs financial) as the within-subjects factor and learning condition (description vs experience) as the between-subjects factor. We examined main effects and the interaction between domain and learning condition. We also included numeracy, age, and gender as covariates in separate ANCOVA models to evaluate their potential moderating effects. Follow-up paired t-tests and independent-samples t-tests were performed to probe significant interactions or main effects. Where appropriate, we report effect sizes (partial eta-squared for ANOVAs and Cohen’s d for t-tests) and 95% confidence intervals. Given the theoretical interest in rare-event underweighting, we conducted exploratory analyses focusing on dilemmas with low-probability adverse outcomes to examine whether sampling-based underrepresentation occurred in the experience condition. Finally, we conducted correlational analyses linking numeracy scores and sampling effort to risk scores.

Software and reproducibility

All analyses were conducted in R statistical software. Analysis scripts and anonymized data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request to encourage reproducibility. We pre-registered the core analyses prior to conducting the experiment, and deviations from the preregistered plan are described in the Results section where relevant.

Results

Descriptive statistics and data screening

The final analysis sample consisted of 70 participants after excluding two participants for failing manipulation checks. The mean age was 29.9 years with a standard deviation of 11.6 years, and the gender split was 38 women and 32 men in the final sample. Numeracy test scores ranged from low to high across participants, with a mean standardized score of approximately 0.02 and a standard deviation of 0.95 after z-scoring within the sample. Average sampling effort in the experience condition was 17.2 draws per dilemma (SD = 6.4), indicating that participants generally engaged with the sampling procedure. Manipulation check responses suggested that participants in the description condition could recall stated probabilities at acceptable accuracy levels on the majority of checked items. Perceived seriousness ratings for medical dilemmas were substantially higher than for financial dilemmas on average, with mean seriousness ratings of 4.2 (on a 1–5 scale) for medical dilemmas and 2.8 for financial dilemmas, indicating that the medical domain elicited greater perceived severity as intended. No participants reported distress requiring discontinuation of the study.

Primary repeated-measures ANOVA

We conducted a 2 (domain: medical, financial; within-subjects) × 2 (learning condition: description, experience; between-subjects) repeated-measures ANOVA on the normalized risk scores. The main effect of learning condition (description vs experience) was not statistically significant, F(1,68) = 0.98, p = 0.326, partial eta-squared = 0.014. This result indicates that, on average, participants in the experience condition did not make significantly different risk choices across domains compared to those in the description condition. The main effect of domain (medical vs financial) approached but did not reach conventional statistical significance, F(1,68) = 3.14, p = 0.081, partial eta-squared = 0.044. The domain effect trend suggested slightly lower risk scores in the medical domain (mean normalized risk = 0.41, SD = 0.21) than in the financial domain (mean normalized risk = 0.46, SD = 0.20), consistent with greater aversion to risk in medical scenarios, but this difference did not meet the conventional p < .05 threshold. Importantly, the interaction between domain and learning condition was not significant, F(1,68) = 0.24, p = 0.626, partial eta-squared = 0.004, indicating that the description–experience gap did not differ between medical and financial domains in our sample.

Follow-up contrasts

Given our a priori prediction that experience-based learning would lead to underweighting of rare events particularly in the financial domain, we ran follow-up t-tests comparing risk scores between description and experience groups separately for each domain. For the financial domain, the difference between description (mean = 0.48, SD = 0.19) and experience (mean = 0.44, SD = 0.21) groups was not significant, t(68) = 0.92, p = 0.360, Cohen’s d = 0.22. For the medical domain, the difference between description (mean = 0.43, SD = 0.22) and experience (mean = 0.40, SD = 0.20) groups was also not significant, t(68) = 0.66, p = 0.512, Cohen’s d = 0.16. Thus, there was no evidence of the classic description–experience gap in either domain in this sample, contrary to our primary hypothesis.

Exploratory analyses of low-probability dilemmas

Because the description–experience literature often highlights underweighting of rare events, we conducted focused analyses on the subset of dilemmas where the adverse event probability was 5% or lower. We computed a rare-event risk score by summing risky choices across these low-probability dilemmas. The mixed ANOVA on the rare-event risk score again revealed no significant main effect of learning condition, F(1,68) = 0.77, p = 0.383, and no significant interaction with domain, F(1,68) = 0.61, p = 0.438. Independent-samples t-tests comparing description and experience groups on the rare-event score within domains were not significant either (financial: t(68) = 0.84, p = 0.404; medical: t(68) = 0.51, p = 0.613). These results suggest that underweighting of rare events in experience-based learning did not emerge in a detectable way for low-probability dilemmas in our study.

Numeracy and individual differences

We examined whether numeracy predicted risk scores and whether it moderated the effect of learning condition. A mixed ANCOVA with numeracy entered as a continuous covariate revealed a significant main effect of numeracy on overall risk-taking, F(1,67) = 5.68, p = 0.020, partial eta-squared = 0.078, such that higher numeracy was associated with slightly lower normalized risk scores across domains. However, the interaction between numeracy and learning condition was not significant, F(1,67) = 1.02, p = 0.316, indicating that numeracy did not differentially affect description versus experience choices in this sample. We also ran correlational analyses between numeracy and the rare-event risk score and found a modest negative correlation (r = −0.24, p = 0.046), suggesting that more numerate participants were less likely to select risky options in low-probability dilemmas. Age was modestly correlated with medical domain risk score (r = −0.28, p = 0.018), indicating that older participants tended to choose safer options more often in medical dilemmas. Gender differences were examined with independent-samples t-tests; women had slightly lower medical risk scores than men (women mean = 0.38, men mean = 0.44), but this difference did not reach significance, t(68) = 1.46, p = 0.149. There were no significant gender differences in the financial domain.

Sampling effort and behavior in the experience condition

Within the experience condition, we examined whether sampling effort predicted subsequent risk choice. Number of samples taken per dilemma averaged 17.2 draws and varied considerably across participants and dilemmas. Across participants, greater average sampling effort was associated with slightly higher alignment between perceived probabilities and objective probabilities, as indicated by better performance on the experience manipulation checks (r = 0.42, p = 0.010). However, sampling effort did not significantly predict the normalized risk score overall (r = −0.12, p = 0.47) or within the financial (r = −0.09, p = 0.56) or medical (r = −0.14, p = 0.39) domains. This suggests that while sampling improved participants’ subjective estimates of probabilities, it did not systematically translate into different choices in the expected direction in our dataset.

Robustness checks and sensitivity analyses

We ran a robustness check including the two previously excluded participants and found that the inclusion did not materially change the main pattern of results; the learning-condition main effect remained non-significant, and the domain by learning-condition interaction remained non-significant. We also repeated analyses with alternative risk score codings, including expected-value-weighted scores, which produced qualitatively similar results: no significant main effect of learning condition and no significant domain-by-learning interaction. Given the null primary findings, we computed observed effect sizes for the learning-condition main effect and estimated that our sample size provided power of approximately 0.35 to detect small effects (Cohen’s d = 0.30) and power of approximately 0.78 to detect medium effects (Cohen’s d = 0.50). Thus, the study was powered to detect medium-sized effects but was underpowered for small effects.

Exploratory qualitative observations

Examination of participant comments and open-ended responses revealed that several participants reported treating medical dilemmas differently because they imagined personal consequences or the well-being of loved ones, which they reported made them more cautious. Some participants in the experience condition remarked that simulated sampling sequences felt “artificial” and that they relied on their intuition rather than on observed frequencies when making choices. These qualitative impressions are consistent with the notion that contextual factors—such as perceived realism and personal salience—may have influenced behavior in ways not captured by the primary quantitative measures.

Summary of results

In summary, the primary analyses did not support the hypothesis that experience-based learning would lead to underweighting of rare events in the financial domain nor that the description–experience gap would be attenuated in medical dilemmas. The main effects and the crucial domain-by-learning-condition interaction were not significant, and focused analyses on low-probability dilemmas did not yield evidence of underweighting in the experience condition. Exploratory analyses did suggest modest associations between numeracy and risk-taking as well as between age and medical risk aversion, indicating individual differences that warrant further investigation. The next section interprets these findings and discusses their theoretical and methodological implications.

Discussion

The present study tested whether the well-documented description–experience gap in risky choice, often observed in financial decision paradigms, generalizes to decisions involving medical side effects and whether the gap is moderated by individual-differences variables such as numeracy, age, and gender. Our preregistered prediction was that experience-based underweighting of rare events would be attenuated in medical dilemmas relative to financial dilemmas because medical outcomes are more emotionally salient and personally relevant. Contrary to this prediction, our primary mixed-design repeated-measures analyses found no significant main effect of learning condition, no significant main effect of domain at conventional thresholds, and no significant interaction between domain and learning condition. Focused analyses on low-probability dilemmas also failed to reveal evidence of the classic underweighting-of-rare-events pattern in the experience condition. Nonetheless, exploratory analyses indicated modest relationships between numeracy and risk-taking and between age and medical risk aversion. These results raise several points for interpretation, have implications for the theory of decisions from experience and description, and suggest directions for future research. First, the null finding concerning the absence of a description–experience gap in our sample does not necessarily imply that the phenomenon is invalid or that it does not occur in financial contexts. Rather, the null result likely reflects the sensitivity of the description–experience effect to procedural details and sample characteristics. The original demonstrations of underweighting of rare events in experience tasks often relied on specific sampling constraints, repeated gambles, or particular framing that encourage certain sampling behaviors. In our study, we attempted to create a balanced and ecologically valid set of dilemmas across financial and medical domains, but some procedural choices may have reduced the likelihood of observing the canonical pattern. One important factor is sampling structure and effort. Although participants in the experience condition took, on average, 17 sampling draws per dilemma, which is substantial, the relationship between sampling and choice was not strong in our dataset. It is possible that sampling did not reliably produce the underrepresentation of rare adverse events that drives the underweighting effect in other studies. For example, participants might have sampled until observing at least one adverse event and then adjusted their behavior, or conversely, they might have stopped sampling prematurely if they found the task tedious, leading to variable sampling quality. Moreover, our experience manipulation displayed sampling outcomes as simulated trials rather than providing highly immersive or high-stakes feedback, and several participants commented that they felt the simulations were artificial. This perception could have diminished the psychological impact of rare adverse events, reducing affective salience and thereby weakening the conditions under which underweighting of rare events emerges. Second, the medical dilemmas successfully elicited higher perceived seriousness ratings than financial dilemmas, indicating that participants recognized the differential domain-level severity. Nevertheless, the increased perceived seriousness did not translate into differential learning effects across conditions. One possibility is that medical scenarios, despite being rated as more serious, also evoke stronger risk-avoidance heuristics that dominate both sampling- and description-based decision processes. If participants adopt a precautionary principle for medical choices—preferring safer options irrespective of probability information—this could mask domain-specific differences in how probabilities are used. Under such a heuristic, the mode of learning would be less influential because participants are essentially applying a domain-specific preference that overrides probability weighting differences. Third, individual differences such as numeracy, age, and gender showed modest associations with risk scores, indicating that person-level variables may be crucial moderators of description–experience effects. Our finding that higher numeracy was associated with marginally lower risk-taking, particularly for low-probability dilemmas, suggests that more numerate individuals might be better at integrating probability information even when it is learned through experience. This aligns with prior work showing that numeracy enhances statistical reasoning and attenuates some biases in probabilistic judgment. However, numeracy did not interact with learning condition in a statistically significant way, suggesting that while numeracy contributes to overall risk preferences, it may not fully explain the differences between description- and experience-based decisions in our sample. The age-related finding that older participants were more risk-averse in medical dilemmas may reflect heightened salience of health outcomes with age or increased health-related caution, but the cross-sectional nature of our data precludes strong inferences about causality. Gender differences were in the expected direction (women somewhat more risk-averse in medical scenarios), but the effects were not robust in our sample size. Together, these individual-differences findings underscore the importance of considering heterogeneity in samples when testing description–experience predictions. Fourth, power and effect-size considerations are relevant for interpreting the null findings. Our sample of 70 participants provided reasonable power to detect medium-sized effects but low power for small effects. It is possible that the true description–experience effect size in the domains and with the procedures we used is small, and therefore our study was insufficiently powered to detect it. The observed partial eta-squared for the learning-condition main effect was small, suggesting that if an effect exists under the present conditions, it is likely small. Future work would benefit from larger samples to detect smaller but theoretically meaningful effects and to provide more precise estimates of effect sizes. Fifth, the nature of the dilemmas and their ecological validity may have influenced the patterns of choice. We intentionally matched probabilities and outcome magnitudes across financial and medical scenarios, but perfect equivalence across domains is difficult to achieve because outcomes differ qualitatively. For example, a 5% chance of a minor monetary loss may be perceived and processed differently than a 5% chance of a significant medical side effect, even if objective values are equated. The qualitative difference in framing and the personal relevance of medical outcomes may lead participants to use different cognitive and affective heuristics that are not captured fully by our risk score metric. In future studies, employing more immersive simulations, real incentives, or longitudinal designs that include real health-related decisions could help establish whether domain-specific cognitive and emotional processes modulate the description–experience gap. Sixth, our manipulation of description versus experience had some practical constraints. In the experience condition, participants observed outcomes from simulated draws rather than making repeated consequential choices that produce real monetary or medical consequences. While ethical considerations limit the realism of medical sampling, laboratory-based consequences or monetary incentives can be used in financial paradigms to enhance engagement. Some participants reported relying on intuition or personal beliefs about treatment efficacy rather than on sampled frequencies, which may have attenuated the intended manipulation. Future designs could incorporate richer experiential feedback, such as narrative patient stories or video simulations, to increase experiential salience, particularly in the medical domain. Seventh, the time constraints and setting could have affected how participants processed information. Some participants may have experienced fatigue across 16 dilemmas, which could have reduced attention and reliance on sampling data later in the session. Counterbalancing domain order mitigated systematic order effects, but trial-level fatigue remains a concern. Shorter blocks, incentivized attention checks, and adaptive sampling protocols could reduce fatigue and increase the reliability of experience-based learning. Eighth, the computational mechanisms that underlie the description–experience gap remain to be fully specified, and our null results hint that multiple mechanisms may be operating with opposing effects. For instance, sampling variability tends to produce underrepresentation of rare events and thus underweighting, while affective vividness could elevate the perceived importance of rare but serious events, leading to overweighting of such events in some contexts. If these mechanisms operate simultaneously in medical dilemmas, they may cancel out, producing no net description–experience difference. Modeling approaches that explicitly quantify the contribution of sampling error, affective weighting, and prior beliefs could help disentangle these competing influences. Ninth, we note that some prior studies have shown that the description–experience gap can be attenuated or reversed when sample sizes are sufficiently large or when participants are encouraged to sample systematically. Our finding that sampling effort was not strongly predictive of risk choices suggests that the quality of sampling behavior may be more determinative than raw sample count. Encouraging participants to sample until they observe a prespecified number of adverse events or using algorithms that ensure minimal representativeness of rare events might produce different outcomes. Such methodological refinements could clarify whether procedural differences account for variability across studies. Tenth, the exploratory qualitative comments from participants provide useful clues for refining future research. Several participants described mental simulation of personal consequences for medical dilemmas, indicating that imagined future selves or loved ones influenced their choices. Incorporating measures of imagined future consequences, empathy, or personal experience with medical issues could illuminate how personal relevance shapes risk processing in health domains. Finally, despite the null primary results, our study contributes to the literature by highlighting boundary conditions and the need for careful task design when attempting to generalize decision-from-experience phenomena across domains. Null findings are informative insofar as they clarify where and when theoretical effects may not replicate and prompt examination of moderating variables. In line with open science practices, we encourage replications with larger and more diverse samples, varied sampling protocols, and enhanced ecological validity to delineate the conditions under which the description–experience gap manifests in medical decision making. Future studies might also adopt computational cognitive models to parse the contributions of memory, sampling variance, affective vigilance, and prior beliefs. Collecting process-level data such as eye-tracking, response times, or physiological arousal may help reveal latent differences between conditions that do not surface in choice outcomes. In conclusion, our experiment did not find evidence for the classic description–experience gap in either financial or medical domains under the tested conditions, and our hypothesis that experience-based underweighting of rare events would be attenuated for medical dilemmas was not supported. Individual differences in numeracy and age showed modest associations with risk-taking, suggesting that heterogeneity among participants plays an important role in risky choice behavior. Methodological limitations including sampling behavior, ecological realism, and statistical power likely contributed to the null results and should be addressed in future research. Understanding how people learn about and respond to risk in domains that matter for health and well-being remains an important goal, and further work is needed to map the psychological mechanisms and boundary conditions that determine when decisions from experience diverge from decisions from description.
