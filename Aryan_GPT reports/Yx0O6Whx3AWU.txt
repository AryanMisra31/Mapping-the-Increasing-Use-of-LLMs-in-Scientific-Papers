Introduction

Human social behavior is shaped by a constellation of personality traits, affective experiences, and learned patterns of interaction. Among the traits that have attracted sustained scientific interest are narcissism and aggression, and among affective states, life satisfaction or happiness has been a central focus. Narcissism is commonly characterized by grandiosity, entitlement, a need for admiration, and a defensively regulated self-esteem. Aggression encompasses behaviors and tendencies that harm others, including verbal hostility, physical aggression, and relational manipulation. Happiness or satisfaction with life refers to a cognitive evaluation of one’s overall quality of life and subjective well-being. All three constructs intersect in everyday social functioning and have implications for interpersonal relationships, work performance, and mental health. Research has repeatedly identified links between narcissistic personality traits and aggressive responses in interpersonal contexts. Narcissistic individuals often respond with anger and hostility when their self-image is challenged, and such responses can escalate to overt aggressive actions. The connection between narcissism and aggression has been explained by theories that emphasize threatened egotism and fragile self-regulation. On the other hand, happiness or life satisfaction tends to be negatively associated with aggressive tendencies, suggesting that individuals who feel content and fulfilled are less prone to lash out. People who report high life satisfaction typically show better emotional regulation and fewer impulsive or hostile behaviors. These general patterns suggest an interplay in which narcissism may foster aggressive responses and diminished happiness may remove a protective buffer against aggression. However, empirical findings on the relationship between narcissism and happiness have not been uniformly straightforward. Some lines of research indicate that narcissistic individuals report elevated self-esteem and subjective well-being, at least in the short term or on self-report measures. This apparent paradox raises important questions about the subjective experience of narcissists and whether their self-reports reflect genuine well-being or defensive self-enhancement. The notion that narcissists can report high satisfaction with life while simultaneously engaging in more aggressive behavior complicates a simple linear model. Divergent findings have led some researchers to distinguish between grandiose and vulnerable narcissism, with different correlates and outcomes. Grandiose narcissism is often linked to high overt self-regard and assertive behaviors, whereas vulnerable narcissism is linked to insecurity, hypersensitivity, and reactive aggression. These subtypes may show distinct patterns in relation to happiness and aggression, but many studies have treated narcissism as a unidimensional trait. Measurement choices also influence results, as different narcissism scales and happiness inventories tap into varied aspects of the constructs. Self-report measures can capture perceived life satisfaction and self-image, but they may also be subject to social desirability and self-presentation biases. Aggression has likewise been operationalized in multiple ways, from situational laboratory paradigms to dispositional questionnaires that assess trait-level tendencies. The bulk of correlational research suggests a positive association between narcissism and aggression, but effect sizes vary and context matters. Meanwhile, happiness and aggression typically show a negative relationship, with lower subjective well-being accompanying more hostile behavior. If narcissism is related both to aggression and to life satisfaction, then understanding the pattern of interrelations among all three variables is crucial for theory. A key question is whether narcissism acts as a mediator or moderator of the relationship between life satisfaction and aggression. For instance, lower life satisfaction could lead to increased aggression, but the presence of strong narcissistic traits might amplify or attenuate this effect. Alternatively, narcissism might serve as a common cause that independently predicts higher aggression and lower life satisfaction. Clarifying these possibilities requires carefully designed empirical studies that measure all relevant constructs and examine their interrelations. Moreover, existing studies have sometimes reported conflicting results, with some finding that narcissistic pride is associated with higher reported well-being. Another strand of findings suggests that narcissists’ apparent well-being may be tenuous and contingent on external validation, making them more vulnerable to frustration and aggression if validation is threatened. The temporal dynamics of narcissism and happiness are also important, because short-term self-enhancement may mask longer-term dissatisfaction. Cross-sectional studies that rely on single time-point self-reports are therefore limited in what they can reveal about causality and directionality. Nevertheless, cross-sectional correlations can provide valuable information about associations that warrant further experimental and longitudinal investigation. The present study adopted this approach, using validated self-report instruments to assess levels of narcissism, trait aggression, and satisfaction with life within a community sample. By focusing on dispositional measures, the study aims to capture stable tendencies rather than transient situational reactions. A sample size of approximately one hundred participants provides a reasonable balance between feasibility and statistical power for detecting medium-sized effects. The study’s central aims were to examine the pairwise associations among narcissism, aggression, and life satisfaction, and to determine whether narcissism accounts for the link between aggression and happiness. If narcissism accounts for a substantial portion of the covariance between aggression and life satisfaction, then it could be conceptualized as a key dispositional driver of both outcomes. Conversely, if the aggression-happiness association remains robust when controlling for narcissism, then other mechanisms must be implicated. Based on the weight of prior research, the study hypothesized that narcissism would be positively associated with aggression. It was also hypothesized that narcissism would be negatively associated with life satisfaction when measured in a manner sensitive to stable subjective well-being rather than situational self-enhancement. Additionally, it was expected that aggression and life satisfaction would show a negative correlation. A further hypothesis was that controlling for narcissism would attenuate the negative relationship between aggression and life satisfaction, indicating that narcissism contributes to their association. These predictions are grounded in theoretical perspectives that link defensive self-enhancement and fragile self-esteem with both interpersonal hostility and reduced genuine well-being. At the same time, the study acknowledges the possibility that different dimensions of narcissism could produce diverging patterns, an issue that cannot be fully resolved in a unidimensional design. The current research therefore sought to provide clear empirical evidence regarding the simple correlational structure among narcissism, aggression, and life satisfaction. By doing so, the study aims to inform subsequent theoretical refinement and empirical testing that may distinguish subtypes or temporal processes. The analytic plan emphasized transparency and robust reporting, with tests of internal consistency, descriptive statistics, and correlational and partial correlational analyses. These methods are appropriate for addressing the study’s hypotheses and for establishing groundwork for future, more complex research designs. In summary, the study addressed a theoretically important set of relationships with implications for understanding how personality traits relate to both antisocial behavior and subjective well-being.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from the surrounding community through online advertisements and word of mouth. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old and fluent in the language of the questionnaires. A total of 101 individuals completed the study and provided usable data. The sample included a range of ages from young adulthood into middle age. Participants reported a variety of educational backgrounds and occupational statuses. Gender distribution in the sample was recorded, with both male and female participants represented. Prior to participation, all individuals provided informed consent in accordance with ethical guidelines for human research. The study procedures were reviewed and approved by a departmental ethics committee. Participants completed a demographic questionnaire that collected information on age, gender, education, and other background variables. Three standardized self-report instruments were administered to assess narcissistic traits, trait aggression, and satisfaction with life. Narcissistic traits were measured using a widely used self-report inventory that captures grandiosity, entitlement, and related features. The narcissism inventory consisted of a series of forced-choice and Likert-format items that yield a total score representing narcissistic tendencies. Trait aggression was assessed with a questionnaire designed to capture verbal aggression, physical aggression, anger, and hostility. The aggression instrument provided a total aggression score as well as subscale scores reflecting different manifestations of aggressive tendencies. Satisfaction with life was measured using a brief scale that asks participants to evaluate their overall satisfaction and quality of life. The life satisfaction scale produced a single score representing subjective well-being and global life satisfaction. Participants completed the questionnaires online in a single session that took approximately 20 to 30 minutes. The order of the three core questionnaires was counterbalanced across participants to reduce order effects. All responses were anonymized and stored securely to protect participant confidentiality. Data were screened for completeness and for response patterns that might indicate disengaged responding, such as straight-lining or random answers. A small number of submissions contained incomplete data and were excluded from analyses according to pre-registered criteria. Scale scores were computed according to standard scoring procedures for each instrument. Internal consistency for each scale was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha to ensure acceptable reliability for subsequent analyses. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, ranges, and skewness, were computed for all main variables. Primary analyses involved Pearson correlation coefficients to examine the bivariate associations among narcissism, aggression, and life satisfaction. A partial correlation analysis was conducted to examine the association between aggression and life satisfaction while statistically controlling for narcissism. In addition, an exploratory multiple regression analysis was performed to evaluate the unique contributions of narcissism and aggression to life satisfaction. Assumptions of parametric tests, including linearity, homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals, were assessed prior to conducting correlational and regression analyses. Outliers were examined using standardized scores and leverage statistics, and any influential cases were considered in sensitivity analyses. Statistical significance was evaluated using a two-tailed alpha level of .05. Effect sizes were reported alongside p-values to provide information about the practical significance of findings. Confidence intervals around correlation coefficients were computed to indicate the precision of estimates. All statistical analyses were conducted using contemporary statistical software with standard routines for reliability and correlation analyses. The overall analytic approach prioritized transparency and robustness, with additional sensitivity checks performed to ensure the stability of the findings.

Results

The final sample consisted of 101 participants with complete data on all primary measures. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 62 years with a mean age of 28.6 years and a standard deviation of 9.4 years. The sample included 58 female and 43 male participants, and a small number who selected other or preferred not to say, reflecting a modest gender balance. Educational attainment varied, with participants reporting high school, undergraduate, and postgraduate levels of education. Initial data screening revealed no pervasive missing data patterns after the exclusion of incomplete cases. Inspection of item-level responses did not reveal systematic straight-lining or disengaged responding among retained participants. Descriptive statistics for the three primary scales are presented below. The narcissism inventory yielded a mean total score of 17.2 with a standard deviation of 6.1 and a range from 4 to 34. The aggression questionnaire produced a mean total aggression score of 57.8 with a standard deviation of 12.5 and a range from 30 to 96. The satisfaction with life scale had a mean score of 23.5 with a standard deviation of 6.9 and a range from 5 to 35. Skewness values for the three scales were within acceptable limits, suggesting approximately normal distributions. Internal consistency for each measure was acceptable, with Cronbach’s alpha equal to .84 for the narcissism inventory. The aggression questionnaire demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of .88 for the total score. The satisfaction with life scale showed a Cronbach’s alpha of .86, indicating good reliability. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationships among the three variables. Narcissism and aggression were positively correlated, with a Pearson r of .47, indicating a moderate positive association. This correlation was statistically significant at p < .001, and the 95% confidence interval for the correlation ranged approximately from .30 to .61. Narcissism and satisfaction with life were negatively correlated, with a Pearson r of -.31. The narcissism-life satisfaction correlation was statistically significant at p = .001, with a 95% confidence interval roughly from -.48 to -.13. Aggression and satisfaction with life were also negatively correlated, with a Pearson r of -.40. The aggression-life satisfaction correlation was statistically significant at p < .001, with a 95% confidence interval approximately from -.55 to -.22. These bivariate results were consistent with the study hypotheses predicting positive narcissism-aggression and negative associations involving life satisfaction. To investigate whether narcissism accounted for the association between aggression and life satisfaction, a partial correlation was computed controlling for narcissism. The partial correlation between aggression and life satisfaction controlling for narcissism was r = -.24. This reduced negative correlation remained statistically significant at p = .016, indicating a smaller but still present association after controlling for narcissism. The decrease in magnitude from the zero-order correlation of -.40 to the partial correlation of -.24 suggests that narcissism accounts for a portion of the covariance between aggression and life satisfaction. An exploratory multiple regression analysis was conducted with satisfaction with life as the dependent variable and narcissism and aggression entered as simultaneous predictors. The overall regression model was significant, explaining approximately 23% of the variance in life satisfaction. In the regression model, narcissism had a negative standardized beta coefficient of -.26, which was significant at p = .006, indicating a unique negative association with life satisfaction. Aggression had a negative standardized beta coefficient of -.30 in the model, significant at p = .002, indicating that higher aggression uniquely predicted lower life satisfaction when both predictors were included. Variance inflation factors were computed and were below common thresholds, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern for the regression parameters. Sensitivity analyses that removed potential outliers produced parameter estimates that were substantively similar to the main results, demonstrating robustness. Additional analyses examined the aggression subscales and their correlations with narcissism and life satisfaction. Verbal aggression and hostility subscales showed particularly strong associations with narcissism, whereas physical aggression showed a smaller but still positive association. The pattern of subscale results mirrored the total score findings and reinforced the core pattern that narcissistic traits are associated with greater aggressive tendency and lower reported life satisfaction.

Discussion

The present study examined the interrelations among narcissistic personality traits, trait aggression, and satisfaction with life in a community sample of adults. The primary findings indicated that narcissism was positively associated with aggression and negatively associated with life satisfaction. Additionally, aggression was negatively associated with life satisfaction, and this association was attenuated but not eliminated when controlling for narcissism. These results provide empirical support for the notion that narcissistic traits are linked to both elevated aggressive tendencies and diminished subjective well-being. The moderate positive correlation between narcissism and aggression aligns with theoretical perspectives that highlight defensiveness and reactivity in narcissistic individuals. Such individuals may be prone to hostile responses when their self-image is challenged or when their need for validation is frustrated. The present data suggest that aggression is one behavioral manifestation of the interpersonal difficulties associated with narcissistic traits. The negative correlation between narcissism and life satisfaction is notable because it contrasts with some prior reports that narcissists can exhibit high self-reported well-being. One way to reconcile these findings is to consider the distinction between apparent or contingent self-esteem and genuine, stable well-being. Self-reported life satisfaction may capture a more stable cognitive evaluation of one’s life circumstances, which may be lower among individuals whose interpersonal strategies undermine long-term satisfaction. Narcissistic behaviors such as entitlement and exploitative interactions could erode social relationships and lead to less support and lower life satisfaction over time. Moreover, if narcissistic grandiosity masks underlying insecurity, reported satisfaction that stems from momentary self-enhancement may not reflect enduring well-being. The observed negative association suggests that, at least within this sample and measurement approach, higher narcissism corresponded with lower life satisfaction. The negative relationship between aggression and life satisfaction is consistent with theories that connect lower subjective well-being with poorer emotion regulation and greater hostility. Individuals who are dissatisfied with their lives may experience higher levels of frustration, resentment, and anger, which can manifest as aggressive tendencies. Conversely, repeated aggressive behavior may diminish social support and result in negative social consequences that further reduce life satisfaction. The partial correlation analysis revealed that controlling for narcissism reduced the magnitude of the aggression-life satisfaction relationship. This pattern implies that narcissism accounts for some of the shared variance between aggression and reduced life satisfaction. In practical terms, the partial correlation suggests that part of why aggressive people report lower life satisfaction is because they are more likely to possess narcissistic traits. Yet the persistence of a significant negative association after accounting for narcissism indicates that other factors also connect aggression and life satisfaction. These remaining connections could include personality dimensions such as neuroticism, impulsivity, or external stressors that were not measured in the present study. The regression analyses further showed that both narcissism and aggression uniquely contributed to lower life satisfaction when entered simultaneously. This finding highlights that interventions aiming to improve life satisfaction may need to address both dispositional aggression and narcissistic attitudes. From a theoretical standpoint, the results can be integrated into models that view narcissism as a dispositional vulnerability that predisposes individuals to maladaptive interpersonal strategies. Those strategies, in turn, generate social and intrapersonal consequences that erode subjective well-being. The study’s cross-sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences about the temporal ordering of these constructs. It remains possible that low life satisfaction could increase the likelihood of aggressive responses or that aggression and narcissism mutually reinforce one another over time. Longitudinal data would be required to determine directionality and to test mediation models that posit narcissism as a causal agent. Another important consideration is the heterogeneity of narcissistic manifestations, since grandiose and vulnerable narcissism may relate differently to aggression and life satisfaction. Grandiose narcissism might show higher momentary self-reported well-being under certain conditions, whereas vulnerable narcissism might be more consistently tied to low satisfaction and greater reactivity. The measures used in the present study were designed to capture narcissistic tendencies in a unidimensional way, which limits the ability to examine these nuanced distinctions. Future research should consider using instruments that differentiate narcissistic subtypes to refine understanding of the pathways connecting narcissism, aggression, and life satisfaction. Self-report measures have strengths in accessing subjective evaluations, but they are also subject to social desirability and self-presentation biases. Narcissistic individuals may be particularly prone to presenting themselves in a favorable light, which could inflate certain self-reports. Nevertheless, in the present study, the negative association between narcissism and life satisfaction suggests that self-enhancement did not uniformly inflate reports of well-being. Supplementing self-report with peer reports, behavioral observations, and physiological measures of reactivity would strengthen future investigations. The sample characteristics should also temper generalizations, as the study drew from a convenience sample that included both students and community members. Demographic diversity was moderate, but larger and more representative samples are needed to establish the generalizability of the present findings. Cultural factors may also influence the expression of narcissism and the interpretation of life satisfaction, and cross-cultural research could illuminate these dynamics. From an applied perspective, the findings suggest that personality assessment for individuals who display aggressive tendencies may benefit from screening for narcissistic traits. Therapeutic interventions that target maladaptive entitlement and interpersonal exploitation could potentially reduce aggression and improve life satisfaction. Cognitive-behavioral techniques that challenge grandiose self-beliefs and bolster empathic understanding may be especially relevant. Moreover, interventions that enhance emotion regulation and coping skills could reduce the propensity for reactive aggression among individuals high in narcissistic traits. Prevention efforts in organizational and educational settings might focus on promoting healthy self-esteem and prosocial coping to mitigate the emergence of aggressive behaviors. The study also raises intriguing questions about the dynamic interplay between self-esteem, narcissism, and well-being over different timescales. Short-term boosts in self-esteem derived from external validation may coexist with longer-term declines in life satisfaction due to deteriorating relationships. Longitudinal research that tracks fluctuations in self-esteem, momentary affect, and life satisfaction in relation to narcissistic behaviors could unpack these temporal patterns. Experimental paradigms that manipulate social feedback could test whether narcissistic individuals respond with heightened aggression when threatened and whether that response predicts subsequent declines in life satisfaction. It would also be instructive to examine mediators such as social support, conflict frequency, and perceived rejection to understand how narcissism leads to poorer subjective well-being. Another important avenue is to explore protective factors that buffer narcissistic individuals from engaging in aggression or experiencing decreased life satisfaction. For example, secure attachment relationships or interventions that foster empathy and perspective-taking might attenuate the negative cascade from narcissism to aggression to reduced well-being. The present findings also suggest that simple dichotomies portraying narcissism as uniformly adaptive or maladaptive are inadequate. Instead, narcissism appears to involve a complex mixture of self-enhancing tendencies that can yield short-term gains in esteem but incur interpersonal costs that erode longer-term satisfaction. This complexity underscores the need for nuanced models and measurement approaches that capture intra-individual variation and contextual sensitivity. In conclusion, the study contributes to a growing body of evidence that narcissistic traits are a meaningful correlate of aggressive tendencies and reduced life satisfaction. By demonstrating that narcissism accounts for a portion of the aggression-life satisfaction association while leaving substantive residual effects, the findings highlight multiple pathways linking personality and subjective well-being. Future research employing longitudinal, experimental, and multi-method designs will be essential for clarifying causality and for identifying targets for intervention to reduce aggression and improve life satisfaction among individuals high in narcissistic traits.
