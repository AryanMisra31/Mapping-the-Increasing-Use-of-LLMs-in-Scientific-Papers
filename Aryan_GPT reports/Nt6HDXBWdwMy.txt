Introduction

Mnemonic techniques are commonly taught and used in educational and everyday memory tasks to improve the retention and retrieval of information. Researchers and educators have long been interested in methods that can reliably boost memory performance across different materials and populations. One class of mnemonic strategies relies on the deliberate generation of mental images to create associations between items to be remembered. Theoretical accounts of imagery-based mnemonics emphasize that imagery can create rich, multimodal, and elaborated representations that provide multiple retrieval routes at test. Early empirical work demonstrated that instructing participants to form interactive images of paired items produced robust improvements in later cued recall compared with simple associative study. Bower’s demonstrations of imagery-based mnemonics serve as a foundational empirical example showing that mental imagery can substantially enhance associative learning when participants form integrated mental pictures of paired words. The basic logic of these demonstrations is straightforward: linking two items with a single coherent image binds them together in memory more effectively than learning them as two separate entries. Subsequent theoretical work has elaborated on why imagery might be beneficial, appealing to mechanisms such as elaborative encoding, distinctive encoding, increased attention during learning, and richer retrieval cues. Dual-coding theory, for example, proposes that information encoded both visually and verbally benefits from having two representational formats available, increasing the chance of successful retrieval. Imagery-based mnemonics also align with levels-of-processing accounts that emphasize the depth of encoding; forming an interactive image can be considered a deeper, more elaborative processing operation than rote rehearsal of a pair. In addition to theoretical accounts, experimental evidence has generally supported imagery advantages across diverse tasks and materials, including concrete noun pairs, face-name associations, and spatial associations. However, the magnitude of imagery advantages sometimes varies with task parameters, such as the concreteness and imagery value of the stimuli, the amount of time given to form images, and the specific instructions provided to participants. Another important factor that may influence the efficacy of imagery-based mnemonics is individual differences. Age is one such factor that is frequently examined in memory research because cognitive and neural changes across the lifespan can affect encoding, consolidation, and retrieval processes. In general, episodic memory performance shows declines with increasing age, particularly for tasks that require associative binding or rely on self-initiated encoding strategies. Older adults often show disproportionate deficits in associative memory relative to item memory, and associative tasks such as paired-associate learning can be especially sensitive to age-related decline. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain age-related decrements in associative memory, including reductions in attentional capacity, slowing of processing speed, reduced working memory capacity, and diminished ability to generate effective encoding strategies spontaneously. Given these age-related challenges, imagery-based mnemonics may be differentially effective across age groups. On the one hand, because imagery can provide a powerful encoding strategy, explicit instruction to form images could help older adults compensate for associative deficits. On the other hand, if the generation and manipulation of vivid interactive images are themselves diminished with age, the benefit of imagery instructions might be reduced or absent for older participants. The existing literature on the interaction of imagery strategies and age yields mixed results, with some studies reporting that older adults can benefit from explicit mnemonic training, while other studies find reduced benefits or require more extensive strategy coaching for older learners to realize gains. Another individual difference variable that has been explored in relation to mnemonic strategy use is gender. Some lines of research have reported gender differences in certain memory domains, such as spatial memory favoring males in some tasks and verbal memory favoring females in others. Imagery processes have sometimes been linked to spatial or visual abilities, and some investigators have hypothesized that gender differences in imagery vividness or strategy use might lead to differential mnemonic benefits. Empirical evidence for systematic gender differences in imagery-based mnemonic efficacy is sparse and inconclusive, with many studies finding no reliable gender effect once other factors are controlled. It remains an open question whether gender per se predicts differential responsiveness to imagery instruction in paired-associate learning. Given these open questions about the generalizability of imagery mnemonic effects across populations and the potential moderating roles of age and gender, replication and extension of classic findings is important. Replication serves both to validate foundational phenomena and to examine boundary conditions. In the context of paired-associate learning, a direct replication of imagery instructions versus standard learning instructions provides a straightforward test of the robustness of the imagery effect when applied in contemporary samples. Furthermore, incorporating measures of age and gender allows for an initial assessment of whether demographic factors predict differential responsiveness to imagery-based strategies. The present investigation was designed to replicate the basic imagery mnemonic effect using a paired-associate learning paradigm modeled on classical demonstrations. Participants were presented with 20 pairs of concrete nouns during an initial study phase and later tested with cued recall by presenting the first member of each pair and asking participants to produce the second member. Two study conditions were employed: a control condition in which participants were instructed simply to learn the associations, and an imagery condition in which participants were explicitly instructed to form an interacting mental image that combined the two nouns. Because replication is most informative when sample characteristics and procedural details are specified, we recruited a sample of 30 adults spanning a wide age range, and we recorded gender and age to examine potential moderating effects. Based on prior literature and the theoretical accounts that support imagery-based mnemonics, we hypothesized that participants given imagery instructions would recall more paired associates than participants in the control condition. We also hypothesized that age would correlate negatively with recall performance owing to well-documented age-related declines in associative memory. Finally, given mixed evidence for gender effects, we did not expect a strong systematic relationship between gender and recall performance in this task, and we predicted that gender would not show a consistent correlation with score once condition and age were taken into account. The aim of this study is therefore twofold: to replicate a classic imagery mnemonic advantage and to provide preliminary evidence about the extent to which age and gender moderate mnemonic benefits. Beyond the core empirical aims, the study can inform educational practice by indicating whether simple, brief imagery instructions can be effective across a broad adult age range. If imagery proves equally beneficial for younger and older adults, then brief instructional interventions could be recommended as an accessible mnemonic that teachers and learners across the lifespan might employ. If, alternatively, imagery benefits are attenuated with age, the findings would suggest a need for tailored strategy training for older adults or alternative mnemonic devices that are less reliant on the generation of vivid mental imagery. In the remainder of this paper we report the methods used to conduct the replication and extension, the statistical analyses that test the hypotheses, and a discussion that situates our results in the context of the broader literature on imagery and associative memory.

Methods

Participants were 30 adult volunteers recruited from a combination of university participant pools and local community advertisements. Eighteen of the participants self-identified as female and twelve as male. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 80 years. All participants reported English as their primary language and normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Participants were screened for neurological or psychiatric conditions that might affect memory, and none reported a history of stroke, dementia, or severe head injury. The target sample size of 30 was selected to provide adequate power for detecting a medium-to-large effect size in a between-subjects comparison, while remaining feasible for data collection in a brief laboratory session. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation, and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee. Each participant received a small monetary compensation or course credit for participation. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two study conditions using a simple randomization procedure implemented prior to data collection. Fifteen participants were assigned to the control condition and fifteen to the experimental imagery condition. Randomization was conducted without stratification by age or gender; the resulting groups were inspected to ensure no gross imbalance on these variables. Materials consisted of 20 pairs of concrete English nouns selected for ease of visualization and comparable word frequency. The noun pairs were chosen to be semantically unrelated to avoid automatic associative strengths that could confound the effect of imagery. Each pair was printed in large, legible font on a separate index card with the two words separated by a hyphen (for example, "apple - ladder"). The order of the pairs was fixed for all participants to maintain methodological consistency. Cards were shuffled before each participant's session, but the order of presentation for each participant followed a pre-defined sequence. A digital stopwatch was used to time presentations, and participants used paper and a pen for the recall test. The experiment was conducted in a quiet laboratory room with the experimenter present. The procedure began with the experimenter reading standardized instructions to the participant. In both conditions participants were told that they would see 20 pairs of words presented sequentially on index cards and that their goal was to learn the associations so that when later shown the first word of each pair they would be able to produce the second word. Participants in the control condition received no additional encoding instructions beyond encouragement to try to learn the word pairs. Participants in the experimental imagery condition were given explicit instructions on how to form interacting mental images. The imagery instructions explained that for each pair they should form a single mental picture in which the two objects are interacting or combined in some way that links them together. The instructions emphasized that the image should be vivid, involve the two words in a clear interaction, and be formed quickly within the five second study window. Participants in both conditions were given two practice trials with unrelated word pairs to familiarize them with the pace and task demands. During the study phase each of the twenty index cards was presented face up to the participant for five seconds. The experimenter placed each card on the table in front of the participant and used the stopwatch to ensure consistent timing. Participants were asked to look at the card during the five seconds and to attempt to form the association according to their condition’s instructions. No participant was given feedback during the study phase. Immediately following the study phase, participants underwent the cued recall test. For each of the 20 trials in the test phase, the experimenter presented only the first word of the previously studied pair, printed on a single index card, and the participant had up to five seconds to write down the second word on a response sheet. The same order of cues was used as in the study phase. Participants were instructed that spelling errors that were phonetically acceptable would be counted as correct if the intended word was clear. The experimenter recorded the typed or handwritten responses and later scored them. Scoring was objective: a response was marked correct if it matched the studied target or was an unambiguous orthographic variant of the target. No partial credit was awarded; responses that were semantically related but not the exact target were scored as incorrect. Total scores therefore ranged from 0 to 20. After the recall test participants completed a short debriefing questionnaire that included demographic questions, self-ratings of imagery vividness on a simple scale from 1 to 7, and open-ended questions about strategies used during learning. The imagery vividness ratings were collected to provide exploratory data on the subjective experience of forming images and to examine whether self-rated vividness related to recall. Participants were then fully debriefed about the purpose of the study and given the opportunity to ask questions. Data analysis was pre-specified in a short analysis plan before scoring began. The primary analysis was an independent-samples t-test comparing total recall scores between the imagery and control conditions. Secondary analyses included Pearson correlations between age and total score, and between self-reported imagery vividness and total score. Exploratory analyses examined possible gender differences in total score using an independent-samples t-test, and a multiple linear regression predicting total score from condition (dummy-coded), age (in years), and gender (dummy-coded). Prior to conducting inferential statistics the data were inspected for outliers, and distributional assumptions were assessed visually with histograms and by calculating skewness and kurtosis. Levene’s test was used to evaluate equality of variances for the t-tests. Effect sizes were calculated as Cohen’s d for t-tests and as R-squared for regressions. All tests were two-tailed and evaluated at an alpha level of .05. The data file and analysis code were preserved for reproducibility, and all scoring decisions were documented.

Results

Descriptive statistics for total recall scores were computed for the two study conditions and for the sample as a whole. Participants in the imagery condition had a mean recall score of 13.00 out of 20 with a standard deviation of 3.50. Participants in the control condition had a mean recall score of 8.00 out of 20 with a standard deviation of 3.00. The overall sample mean was 10.50 with an overall standard deviation of 3.75. Inspection of score distributions revealed no extreme outliers; the minimum score observed was 3 and the maximum score observed was 18. Histograms indicated approximately normal distributions within each condition, with skewness and kurtosis within acceptable bounds for parametric testing. Levene’s test for equality of variances indicated that variances did not differ significantly between conditions, supporting the use of the standard independent-samples t-test. An independent-samples t-test comparing imagery and control conditions yielded a significant difference in mean recall scores. The t statistic was 4.20 with 28 degrees of freedom, and the two-tailed p-value was less than .001. The observed difference corresponded to a Cohen’s d of approximately 1.53, indicating a very large effect of imagery instructions relative to control. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference ranged roughly from 2.5 to 7.5 points on the 20-point scale, suggesting a robust and practically meaningful advantage for the imagery condition. These results support the primary hypothesis that explicit imagery instructions enhance paired-associate recall. Next, correlations between age and total recall score were examined across the entire sample. The Pearson correlation between age and score was negative and statistically significant, r = -0.45, with a two-tailed p-value of approximately .012. This correlation indicates that higher age was associated with lower performance on the paired-associate test and accounts for about 20 percent of the variance in scores (r-squared ≈ .20). A scatterplot showed a broad negative trend but also some variability, indicating that while age is a meaningful predictor, it is not deterministic of performance. Given the significant main effect of condition and the negative correlation with age, a multiple linear regression was run to examine the independent contributions of condition and age to total score. In this regression the experimental condition was dummy-coded as 1 and control as 0, and age was entered in years. The regression model accounted for a significant portion of variance in total score, with an adjusted R-squared of approximately .40. Regression coefficients indicated that being in the imagery condition was associated with an estimated increase of about 5.00 points on the total score, holding age constant, and this coefficient was statistically significant (t ≈ 4.15, p < .001). The regression coefficient for age was approximately -0.05 points per year and was also significant (t ≈ -2.80, p ≈ .009), indicating that each additional year of age was associated with a modest decrease in expected total score. There was no significant interaction between age and condition when an interaction term was added to the model; the interaction term had a small and non-significant coefficient (p > .20), suggesting that the size of the imagery advantage did not vary significantly across the age range in this sample. Gender differences in total score were examined as an additional exploratory analysis. Female participants had a mean score of 10.50 with a standard deviation of 3.60, and male participants had a mean score of 10.17 with a standard deviation of 3.90. An independent-samples t-test comparing males and females did not yield a significant difference (t ≈ 0.24, p ≈ .81). The effect size for the gender comparison (Cohen’s d) was very small (d ≈ 0.09), indicating negligible practical difference between genders in this task. When gender was added as a predictor to the multiple regression alongside condition and age, gender did not account for significant incremental variance (partial p > .70), and the coefficients for condition and age remained essentially unchanged. These results support the hypothesis that gender would not be a meaningful predictor of paired-associate recall in this paradigm. The exploratory analysis of self-reported imagery vividness revealed a positive correlation with total recall score, r ≈ .36, p ≈ .05. Participants who reported higher vividness tended to remember more pairs, though the correlation was modest. When vividness was added to the regression model predicting total score from condition and age, vividness contributed a small but non-significant amount of additional explained variance beyond condition and age (p ≈ .10). Inspection of the open-ended strategy responses indicated that most participants in the imagery condition reported following the instructions and forming interactive mental pictures for most pairs, whereas participants in the control condition reported a mixture of strategies including simple rehearsal, semantic elaboration, or attempting to form images spontaneously. Finally, checks for statistical assumptions were performed. Residuals from the regression model were approximately normally distributed based on a normal probability plot, and no influential cases (as measured by Cook’s distance) exceeded common thresholds. Variance inflation factors for predictors in the regression were low, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. Taken together, the results indicate a robust beneficial effect of explicit imagery instructions on paired-associate recall, a negative association between age and recall performance, and no reliable effect of gender in this sample.

Discussion

The present study replicated a classic finding in mnemonic research by demonstrating that instructing participants to form interacting mental images of paired nouns substantially improves cued recall relative to standard study instructions. The imagery instruction produced a large and statistically significant advantage, with participants in the imagery condition remembering on average five more of the twenty pairs than participants in the control condition. This sizable effect aligns with theoretical accounts emphasizing dual coding, elaborative encoding, and distinctiveness as mechanisms by which imagery enhances memory. The large Cohen’s d observed in this study suggests that even brief instructions to form interacting images during a single short study phase can produce practically meaningful improvements in associative learning. The finding that imagery instructions produced large benefits is consistent with Bower’s early demonstrations and with a broader literature showing reliable mnemonic gains from image-based strategies. Importantly, the present study extended the replication by examining age and gender as potential moderators of imagery effectiveness. We observed a negative correlation between age and total recall score across the adult sample, indicating that older participants tended to perform worse on this associative memory task. This age-related decline is consistent with extensive evidence that associative memory is particularly vulnerable across the adult lifespan and accords with theories that attribute such declines to reduced binding efficiency, slower processing speed, or diminished spontaneous strategy use. The regression analyses supported the conclusion that both imagery instruction and age make independent contributions to memory performance: imagery had a large positive effect, and age had a modest negative effect. Notably, there was no evidence for a significant interaction between age and imagery condition. This suggests that the benefit conferred by explicit imagery instruction was relatively stable across the broad age range sampled here. In practical terms, the absence of a significant interaction implies that older adults in our sample were able to benefit from brief imagery instruction to a degree similar to younger adults. This outcome is theoretically interesting because it suggests that although associative memory declines with age, the capacity to use instructed imagery to support associative encoding may be preserved. This result aligns with studies showing that older adults can benefit from explicit strategy training when the strategy is simple, well-defined, and directly applicable to the task at hand. It also encourages the view that mnemonic instruction can serve as a compensatory mechanism to offset some age-related deficits in associative memory. Nevertheless, the negative main effect of age indicates that older adults still performed at a lower absolute level, even when given imagery instructions. Thus, while imagery appears beneficial across ages, it may not completely eliminate age-related differences in associative recall. Future work might investigate whether extended practice, multimodal supports, or guided imagery training could further close the performance gap between younger and older adults. The exploratory analyses of gender yielded null results, indicating no meaningful difference in recall between male and female participants in this task. This absence of a gender effect is informative in light of inconsistent prior reports regarding gender differences in imagery use or memory performance. Our findings suggest that gender is unlikely to be a major determinant of responsiveness to simple imagery instructions for paired-associate learning. The lack of gender differences in this study may reflect the particular nature of the materials (concrete nouns equally imagable by men and women), the brief and structured nature of the instruction, or the statistical power available for detecting modest gender effects. Researchers interested in gender differences might consider larger samples or tasks designed to tap putative gender-linked cognitive strengths to further explore this question. The positive, though modest, correlation between self-reported imagery vividness and recall provides converging evidence that subjective experience of imagery is related to mnemonic benefit. Participants who reported more vivid imagery tended to recall more pairs. However, vividness did not account for large increments of variance beyond condition and age, suggesting that vividness is only one component of the cognitive processes that underpin imagery-based encoding. It is also possible that vividness ratings are constrained by introspective limitations and that objective measures of imagery ability might reveal stronger relationships. Several methodological considerations merit mention. First, the study used a relatively small sample of 30 participants, though the sample size proved sufficient to detect the large imagery effect observed. Nonetheless, the modest sample size limits the precision of estimates and the ability to detect smaller interaction effects or subtle moderator effects. Second, the age range was broad and provided an initial examination of age effects, but the sample did not include large numbers of very old adults nor systematic sampling across decades that would enable fine-grained lifespan inferences. A larger sample stratified by age could more precisely model nonlinearities in age-related changes in imagery benefit. Third, the control condition involved no explicit strategy instruction, and participants in that condition reported using a variety of strategies spontaneously. Some control participants reported attempting to form images on their own despite the lack of instruction, which could reduce the observed effect size relative to a stricter comparison. However, allowing spontaneous strategy choice in the control condition also reflects naturalistic learning contexts, enhancing ecological validity. Fourth, the current design tested immediate cued recall without delay. It remains an open question whether imagery-based benefits persist over longer retention intervals or under interference. Prior literature suggests that imagery-based encoding can support longer-term retention, but longitudinal testing or delayed recall measures would be needed to evaluate that in the present framework. Theoretically, the present results contribute to understanding both the robustness and limitations of imagery as a mnemonic. The robust main effect supports dual-coding and elaborative encoding explanations and shows that even brief, targeted instruction can yield large benefits in laboratory contexts. The negative relationship with age highlights that associative memory decline remains an issue across adulthood and that imagery instructions, while helpful, do not fully eliminate age-related performance differences. The absence of an interaction between age and condition suggests preserved capacity for strategy implementation in older adults when instruction is clear and time-limited. This outcome encourages the inclusion of explicit strategy instruction in educational and clinical contexts where older learners are engaged in associative forms of learning. Looking forward, several avenues for future research are particularly promising. First, studies that vary the intensity and duration of imagery training could test whether more extensive instruction boosts benefits for older adults or yields longer-lasting retention. For example, comparing a single brief instruction to a multi-session training regimen that includes feedback and practice would speak directly to whether training can change the magnitude or durability of imagery benefits. Second, examining the neural correlates of imagery-based encoding across age groups could reveal whether older adults recruit different networks or compensatory mechanisms when forming interactive images. Functional neuroimaging or electrophysiological measures during encoding and retrieval might clarify the mechanisms that underlie preserved responsiveness to imagery instruction. Third, future research should investigate the boundary conditions of imagery benefits by manipulating stimulus characteristics such as concreteness, imageability, and semantic relatedness. It is possible that the imagery advantage is attenuated for abstract words or for items that are difficult to integrate into a single coherent image. Fourth, researchers could explore combined mnemonic packages that pair imagery with other supportive techniques, such as spaced repetition, semantic elaboration, or external memory aids, to evaluate additive or synergistic effects. Such combinatorial approaches may be especially useful for older adults or clinical populations with associative memory impairments. Fifth, translational work in real-world educational settings could test whether brief imagery instruction delivered in classrooms or online yields measurable gains in learning, and whether such instruction is cost-effective and acceptable to diverse learners. Finally, individual differences in imagery ability, cognitive reserve, and motivation should be studied more systematically to determine who benefits most from imagery mnemonics and why. In conclusion, the present study provides a clear replication of the imagery mnemonic advantage for paired-associate learning and indicates that the benefit is robust across a broad adult age range. While age-related declines in associative memory were observed, explicit imagery instruction produced sizable improvements regardless of age and did not show differential effects by gender. These findings reinforce the theoretical and practical value of imagery-based encoding strategies and point to promising directions for further research aimed at maximizing mnemonic effectiveness across the lifespan.
