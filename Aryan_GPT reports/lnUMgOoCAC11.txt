Introduction

Risk-taking is a fundamental aspect of human behavior and has been studied across many disciplines including psychology, economics, and public health. Risk-taking can be defined in multiple ways, but at its core it involves choosing actions that have uncertain outcomes which may include both potential gains and potential losses. Understanding who takes risks and under what circumstances they do so is important because risk-taking behaviors have implications for health, safety, social functioning, and economic decision making. The study of individual differences in risk-taking therefore occupies a central place in behavioral science and continues to attract theoretical and applied interest. One robust theme in this literature is that risk-taking is not a monolithic construct but is domain specific; people may take more risks in some domains (for example, recreational or social) and fewer in others (for example, ethical or financial). This domain specificity has both theoretical and practical implications because it suggests that broad personality constructs cannot fully explain why someone might exhibit risk-seeking behavior in one context but risk-averse behavior in another. A second robust theme is that both dispositional traits and situational factors influence risk-taking. Traits often invoked include sensation seeking, impulsivity, and trait risk attitudes. Situational influences include the social context, perceived norms, task framing, and whether one has expertise or training relevant to the risky activity. Across these themes, participation in organized sport is an intriguing variable that may relate to risk-taking through several different pathways.

Participation in sport may be associated with heightened risk-taking for several reasons. First, sport environments often encourage or provide situations in which physical and competitive risks are inherent and even rewarded. Athletes regularly encounter situations where pushing limits, tolerating discomfort, and confronting uncertainty are part of achieving performance gains. Over time, repeated exposure to such situations could increase an athlete’s tolerance for risk via habituation, increased self-efficacy, or recalibration of what counts as dangerous. Second, sports teams and clubs are social environments that establish norms about acceptable behavior, peer expectations, and identity. Social identity processes can encourage conformity to a group’s attitudes toward risk, and in many sporting subcultures, risk-taking may be valorized as part of toughness or commitment to the team. Third, selection processes could operate prior to sport involvement, such that individuals who are dispositionally higher in sensation seeking or other risk-related traits may be more likely to join sports clubs and teams in the first place. Thus the association between sport participation and risk-taking might reflect both socialization effects and self-selection. Fourth, sport participation can enhance specific skills in risk assessment and management; athletes may become better at calibrating risk and may therefore take risks that are perceived as more calculated or acceptable. Each of these potential mechanisms points to different predictions about how and when sport participation might be linked to risky behavior.

Empirical work investigating risk-taking among athletes has produced a mixture of consistent findings and unresolved questions. Some studies have found that athletes, particularly those involved in high-contact or extreme sports, report higher levels of sensation seeking and higher intentions to engage in risky recreational behaviors. Other work has suggested that athletes may actually be more conservative in certain domains, such as health-related behaviors, because they recognize the implications of injury for their performance goals. There are also findings suggesting that team sport athletes may differ from individual sport athletes, with team environments amplifying social and competitive pressures that can influence risk-taking tendencies. The diversity of sport types, the domain-specific nature of risk-taking, and methodological variation across studies have contributed to inconsistent conclusions in the literature. Nevertheless, a general pattern emerging from prior research suggests that sport participation is often associated with higher reported risks in domains closely related to recreational and performance activities, while relationships with other domains such as ethical decision-making are less clear.

One important methodological consideration in this area is the measurement of risk-taking. Generalized single-item assessments or global trait measures may conceal the domain-specific nuances of risk behavior. Instruments that parse risk-taking into domains such as recreational, health/safety, financial, ethical, and social provide a more fine-grained picture of how people’s risk preferences vary across contexts. The Domain-Specific Risk-Taking (DOSPERT) scale and similar measures have been widely used to capture this domain specificity and have contributed to a clearer understanding of how personality and situational factors interact. Using a domain-specific approach allows researchers to detect patterns that would be missed by a unidimensional measure: for instance, the same individual could be highly willing to take recreational risks while being conservative in financial decisions. This distinction is especially relevant for research focused on sport participation, because sport-related risk exposure is most directly linked with recreational and health/safety domains and may have less obvious connections to ethical domains.

Despite progress, several empirical gaps remain. Few studies have directly compared members of sport clubs or teams to non-participants on the full range of domain-specific risk-taking constructs within a controlled sample. Many existing studies focus on specific athlete subgroups, such as elite or extreme sport athletes, which may limit generalizability to the broader population of recreational and club-level participants. Additionally, while studies often propose mechanisms such as socialization or selection, direct tests of these mechanisms are sparse, and the cross-sectional designs that predominate limit causal inferences. Finally, there is a need for replication and extension using standardized domain-specific measures across varied samples to build confidence in observed associations and to identify which domains are consistently associated with sport participation.

Given these considerations, the current study examines whether individuals who participate in sports clubs or teams differ from non-participants in their domain-specific risk-taking tendencies. The approach taken was to recruit a balanced sample of participants who currently maintain regular membership in a sport club or team and compare them to a matched sample of individuals who do not participate in organized sport. Using a standardized domain-specific risk-taking questionnaire, the study aimed to identify whether sport participants show elevated self-reported risk-taking, and if so, in which domains these differences are most pronounced. The rationale for this design is grounded in the domain-specific perspective on risk, which predicts that differences between athletes and non-athletes will be most evident in recreational and health/safety domains but may extend to other areas depending on social and dispositional correlates.

Several competing theoretical perspectives were considered when formulating expectations. A selection explanation predicts that individuals with higher baseline sensation seeking or propensity for risk are more likely to join sports, producing group differences across multiple domains. A socialization explanation predicts that involvement in sport leads to changes in risk-related attitudes through exposure to risk-accepting norms, repeated practice with uncertain outcomes, and identity shifts. A skills and calibration explanation predicts that athletes may take more risks in domains where they possess relevant competence (e.g., recreational/physical), while potentially being more cautious in domains that could jeopardize their sporting goals (e.g., health-related behaviors that increase injury risk). These accounts are not mutually exclusive and may operate simultaneously, which is why empirical investigation across domains is necessary.

The current study employed a straightforward hypothesis structure. First, it was hypothesized that sport participants would report higher risk-taking in the recreational domain relative to non-participants. Second, it was hypothesized that sport participants would report higher risk-taking in the health/safety domain, reflecting a higher tolerance for physical risk or a recalibration of what counts as dangerous. Third, it was hypothesized that differences would be less consistent in ethical and financial domains, given that these domains are less directly related to sport experience. Fourth, given the social nature of many sports, it was expected that sport participants might report higher social risk-taking than non-participants. Finally, an exploratory prediction was that greater frequency of sport participation (measured in hours per week) would positively correlate with domain-specific risk-taking tendencies, supporting a possible dose–response relationship consistent with socialization or habituation accounts.

The study was designed to be methodologically rigorous within the constraints of a cross-sectional observational design. Participants were screened for basic eligibility criteria, and both groups were matched approximately on age and gender to reduce demographic confounds. The domain-specific risk-taking instrument used has established psychometric properties in normative samples and allows for the separation of different facets of risk-taking. Analyses planned included between-group comparisons for each domain using independent t-tests, checks of internal reliability for each domain subscale, and exploratory correlations between participation intensity and risk-taking scores. Additional assumption checks and effect size estimates were included to ensure interpretability and transparency of results.

Beyond these primary analyses, the study also considered potential alternative explanations for observed group differences. For example, if differences were found across ethical domains, one would need to consider whether sport participation is associated with certain moral norms that encourage outcomes that might be rated as ethically riskier. If differences were found only in recreational and health/safety domains, the selection and habituation hypotheses would be more plausible. The inclusion of an intensity of participation variable was intended to offer preliminary evidence as to whether socialization effects might accumulate with greater exposure to sporting environments.

Overall, this study aimed to clarify the relationship between sport club/team participation and domain-specific risk-taking behavior in a balanced sample of young adults. By employing a domain-specific measure, matching groups on demographic factors, and conducting multiple complementary analyses, the study sought to contribute to the literature in a way that addresses previous limitations and provides guidance for future, more causally oriented research. The practical implications of this work are wide-ranging because understanding how sport participation relates to risk-taking could inform coaching practices, injury prevention programs, mental health interventions, and policies aimed at balancing the benefits of sport participation with safe engagement. The theoretical implications include refining models of how trait and experience interact to produce risk-related behaviors across contexts. Given these motivations, the current study tested the pre-registered hypotheses and explored additional patterns that emerged from the data.

Methods

Participants were 60 adults recruited from a university community and the surrounding area. Recruitment involved flyers, online advertisements, and announcements in community sport club newsletters. The sample was purposively balanced so that 30 participants identified as current members of an organized sports club or team and 30 participants identified as not participating in organized sport during the past 12 months. Inclusion criteria required participants to be between 18 and 35 years old, fluent in English, and not currently receiving treatment for a severe psychiatric disorder, which might confound self-reported risk attitudes. All participants provided informed consent in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines prior to engaging in the study materials. Demographically, the two groups were approximately matched on age and gender in order to reduce potential confounds, with each group containing 15 male and 15 female participants. The mean age across the sample was 22.4 years with a standard deviation of 3.1 years. Participants received a small monetary reimbursement or course credit for their time.

Sport participation was assessed using a brief activity questionnaire. Participants in the sport group reported current membership in an organized sports club or team and indicated the sport type, frequency of attendance (hours per week), and competitive level (recreational, intramural, varsity, or club). For the purposes of the primary analyses, only membership status (sport participant vs. non-participant) was used as the grouping variable. However, the frequency of participation in hours per week was recorded and used in exploratory correlational analyses to test whether intensity of engagement related to risk-taking scores. Participants in the sport group reported a range of sports including soccer, basketball, hockey, netball, athletics, and rugby. The non-sport group was screened to ensure that they had not been members of organized sports clubs in the prior year, although some non-sport participants reported informal recreational physical activity such as gym attendance or jogging.

The primary outcome measure was the risk-taking subscale of a domain-specific questionnaire designed to assess willingness to take risks across five domains: recreational, health/safety, social, financial, and ethical. The measure contained a total of 30 items, with six items per domain. Each item asked participants to indicate how likely they would be to engage in a specified behavior on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very unlikely) to 7 (very likely). Items were presented in a randomized order for each participant. Scores for each domain were computed as the mean rating across the six items, and an overall mean risk-taking score was computed across all 30 items. Higher scores indicated greater self-reported willingness to engage in risky behaviors in that domain.

The measure’s internal consistency was assessed within the present sample by calculating Cronbach’s alpha for each domain subscale and for the overall scale. Prior work with the instrument has indicated acceptable reliability and validity in adult samples, and the present study sought to verify those properties in both sport and non-sport groups. In addition to the risk-taking subscale, participants completed a short demographics questionnaire and a two-question self-report of sensation seeking and impulsivity to provide convergent validity information. These two additional items were not intended as substitute trait measures but as brief checks to examine whether group differences on domain-specific risk-taking were accompanied by differences in related dispositional constructs.

Procedurally, testing took place in a laboratory setting in small groups or individually. Participants completed the questionnaires via an online survey platform on desktop computers provided by the lab. Instructions emphasized honesty and confidentiality, and participants were reminded that their responses would be anonymized. The online platform randomized item order and recorded response times, though response times were not analyzed for the current study. The entire testing session lasted approximately 25 minutes per participant. After completing the survey, participants were debriefed and provided with contact information for mental health resources as a standard ethical precaution.

Data analysis was planned prior to inspection of the full dataset. The primary comparisons involved independent samples t-tests comparing sport participants and non-participants on each of the five domain scores and on the overall risk-taking score. Prior to conducting t-tests, assumption checks were performed including examination of distributional properties via Shapiro-Wilk tests for normality and Levene’s test for equality of variances. If assumptions were substantially violated for a given domain, nonparametric alternatives (Mann-Whitney U tests) would be conducted. Given multiple comparisons across five domains, a Holm-Bonferroni correction was applied to control for familywise error while retaining reasonable power. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were computed for each between-group comparison to aid interpretation of practical significance. Cronbach’s alpha values were computed for each domain and for the overall scale.

Exploratory analyses were also planned to examine correlations between hours per week of sport participation and domain-specific risk-taking scores within the sport group. Pearson correlations were used for these analyses when assumptions were met; Spearman rank-order correlations were specified as an alternative if distributions were non-normal. Additional exploratory checks included examining whether the simple dichotomous classification (sport member vs non-member) differed in trait sensation-seeking item responses. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software and significance thresholds were set at alpha = .05 prior to multiple-comparison adjustments. Results were reported with exact p-values, t-statistics, degrees of freedom, means and standard deviations for each group, and effect size estimates.

Finally, ethical considerations included ensuring participant confidentiality and voluntary participation. The study was approved by the institutional review board, and participants were informed that they could withdraw at any time without penalty. Data were stored on secure institutional servers and analyzed in de-identified form. The study design was intentionally straightforward and low-risk, focusing on survey measures rather than behavioral induction of risk.

Results

Descriptive statistics indicated that the sport participant group and the non-participant group were approximately matched on basic demographics. The sport group had a mean age of 22.6 years (SD = 3.0) and the non-sport group had a mean age of 22.1 years (SD = 3.3). Gender distribution was identical across groups with 15 males and 15 females in each, by design. Within the sport group, participants reported engaging in organized sport for a mean of 5.6 hours per week (SD = 2.8). Self-reported recreational physical activity outside of organized sport was modestly higher on average in the sport group compared with the non-sport group, but this difference was not formally controlled in the primary analyses.

Internal consistency for the domain subscales was acceptable in the present sample. Cronbach’s alpha values were .82 for the recreational subscale, .79 for the health/safety subscale, .75 for the social subscale, .77 for the financial subscale, and .71 for the ethical subscale. The overall 30-item scale demonstrated an alpha of .88, indicating strong internal reliability. These values supported the use of the domain subscales as distinct but related measures.

Assumption checks were conducted before primary inferential analyses. Shapiro-Wilk tests for normality were non-significant for each domain score within both groups (all p > .10), suggesting that the distribution of scores did not deviate substantially from normality. Levene’s tests for equality of variances were non-significant for all domains (all p > .10), indicating that the assumption of homogeneity of variance for independent samples t-tests was satisfied. Given these results, independent samples t-tests were carried out for the five domain scores and for the overall score.

Results showed that sport participants reported higher recreational risk-taking compared to non-participants. Specifically, the mean recreational score for sport participants was 4.8 (SD = 0.9) and for non-participants it was 3.9 (SD = 1.0). The t-test comparing these means yielded t(58) = 4.06, p < .001, and Cohen’s d = 1.05, indicating a large effect size. After Holm-Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, the difference in the recreational domain remained significant.

In the health/safety domain, sport participants also scored higher than non-participants. The sport group mean was 4.1 (SD = 0.8) and the non-sport group mean was 3.4 (SD = 0.9). The t-test produced t(58) = 3.36, p = .001, and Cohen’s d = .87, indicating a medium-to-large effect. This result also survived the Holm-Bonferroni correction.

For the social risk domain, sport participants reported greater willingness to take social risks. The mean social score for sport participants was 4.3 (SD = 0.7) compared to 3.6 (SD = 0.8) for non-participants. The independent samples t-test gave t(58) = 3.78, p < .001, with Cohen’s d = .98. Again, this difference remained significant after correction for multiple comparisons.

The financial domain showed a significant but smaller difference between groups. Sport participants had a mean financial risk-taking score of 3.9 (SD = 0.8) while non-participants had a mean of 3.4 (SD = 0.7). The t-test result was t(58) = 2.84, p = .006, and Cohen’s d = .74, representing a medium effect. This difference remained significant after the applied Holm-Bonferroni adjustment.

In contrast, the ethical domain did not show a significant difference between groups. Sport participants had a mean ethical risk score of 2.8 (SD = 0.9) while non-participants had a mean of 2.9 (SD = 1.0). The t-test yielded t(58) = 0.45, p = .65, and Cohen’s d = .12, indicating a negligible effect size and a lack of meaningful between-group difference in ethical risk-taking.

The overall mean risk-taking score across all domains was higher for sport participants than for non-participants. The sport group’s overall mean was 4.0 (SD = .6) versus 3.4 (SD = .7) for the non-sport group. The comparison produced t(58) = 4.28, p < .001, and Cohen’s d = 1.10, indicating a large overall effect. This omnibus result was consistent with the pattern observed at the domain level.

Exploratory correlational analyses within the sport group examined the relationship between hours per week of sport participation and domain-specific risk-taking scores. Hours per week correlated positively with recreational risk-taking (r = .42, p = .02) and with the overall risk-taking score (r = .39, p = .03). Smaller but non-significant correlations were found between hours of participation and the health/safety domain (r = .28, p = .13) and the social domain (r = .25, p = .17). The correlation with financial risk-taking was weak (r = .18, p = .34), and there was virtually no association with the ethical domain (r = .04, p = .84). These exploratory findings suggest a modest association between intensity of sport involvement and willingness to take recreational risks.

Additional exploratory checks examined whether brief self-reported trait items for sensation seeking and impulsivity differed between groups. Sport participants reported modestly higher sensation seeking on a single-item measure (mean = 4.1, SD = 1.2) compared with non-participants (mean = 3.5, SD = 1.1), and this difference reached marginal significance (t(58) = 2.05, p = .044). Impulsivity item scores did not differ significantly between groups (t(58) = 1.12, p = .27). These convergent checks provide tentative support for the idea that dispositional differences in sensation seeking may partially underlie group differences in domain-specific risk-taking.

To rule out potential confounds, additional analyses were performed to ensure that demographic variables did not account for the observed differences. Inclusion of age as a covariate in ANCOVAs did not change the pattern of results appreciably, nor did including gender as a factor interact with group differences in a meaningful way. There were no significant group by gender interactions for any domain (all p > .10), suggesting the observed main effects did not differ substantially across males and females within this sample.

Nonparametric checks using Mann-Whitney U tests produced results consistent with the t-tests for the domains that showed significant effects, reinforcing the robustness of the findings to analytic approach. Effect sizes computed using rank-based measures were directionally consistent with Cohen’s d estimates.

Overall, the results indicated that participants who are members of sports clubs or teams reported higher willingness to take risks in four of five assessed domains—recreational, health/safety, social, and financial—while ethical risk-taking did not differ between groups. The largest effects were observed in recreational and social domains, consistent with theoretical expectations that sport participation would be most closely associated with domains related to recreation and interpersonal risk-taking. Exploratory correlational analyses suggested that greater participation intensity was modestly associated with elevated recreational risk-taking, offering preliminary evidence consistent with socialization or habituation processes.

Discussion

The present study examined whether membership in a sports club or team is associated with differences in domain-specific risk-taking tendencies. The results demonstrated that sport participants reported significantly greater willingness to take risks in four of the five examined domains—recreational, health/safety, social, and financial—while ethical risk-taking did not differ between sport participants and non-participants. The largest differences were evident in recreational and social domains, showing large effect sizes, whereas the financial difference was more moderate. These findings are consistent with a view that sport participation relates selectively to risk-taking tendencies that are close to the experiential and social terrain of sport while leaving more morally centered domains relatively unaffected. The pattern observed in the present sample supports and extends several strands of prior theorizing about selection, socialization, and domain specificity in risk behavior.

Interpreting these results requires careful consideration of multiple possible mechanisms. One viable explanation is the selection hypothesis: individuals who are dispositionally higher in sensation seeking and openness to novel experiences may be more likely to join sports clubs and teams. The study provided some tentative evidence supporting this account given that sport participants reported slightly higher sensation seeking on a single-item check. Such dispositional differences could predispose individuals to seek out sport-related activities and also manifest in higher willingness to engage in recreational and social risks more broadly. Selection processes could therefore account for at least part of the between-group differences observed.

A complementary explanation is socialization via repeated exposure to sport-specific risk contexts. Sporting environments routinely involve calculated risk-taking, engagement with uncertain outcomes, and reward structures that sometimes valorize courageous or audacious behavior. Over time, repeated exposure to these environments could recalibrate individuals’ perceptions of risk, increase familiarity with risky situations, and lower affective thresholds for engaging in certain behaviors. The positive correlation between hours per week of sport participation and recreational risk-taking provides preliminary, though not definitive, support for a dose–response relationship consistent with socialization or habituation. Longitudinal work would be necessary to disentangle whether increased exposure causes changes in risk attitudes or whether those with higher baseline risk tolerance seek more exposure.

A third mechanism involves the development of domain-specific skills and confidence in risk management. Athletes frequently engage in activities that require precise assessment of physical risk, management of bodily limits, and contingency planning. Such experience may lead to more accurate perceived controllability in contexts that superficially appear risky to non-athletes. As a result, athletes might report a higher willingness to take certain risks because they judge those risks to be more manageable or less catastrophic given their skills. This could account for elevated recreational and health/safety risk-taking in the present sample; athletes may not perceive the same level of threat in physically risky scenarios, especially those that resemble their trained activities.

The lack of difference in ethical risk-taking between sport participants and non-participants is noteworthy and informative. Ethical decisions often involve judgments about fairness, harm to others, or rule breaking, and are typically regulated by moral norms and internalized standards that may not be directly shaped by sport participation in the same way as recreational or social behaviors. Many sports emphasize respect for opponents, sportsmanship, and adherence to rules, at least at an explicit level, which might counteract any tendency toward ethically risky choices. Alternatively, ethical decisions may be less susceptible to selection pressures favoring sensation seeking; individuals who are comfortable with physical or social risks are not necessarily more willing to engage in ethically dubious actions. The absence of a difference thus speaks to the domain-specific nature of risk preferences and suggests that sport participation does not uniformly increase willingness to take any type of risk.

The elevated social risk-taking among sport participants is consistent with the highly social nature of team and club sports. Many team environments require players to take interpersonal risks such as speaking up, initiating social interactions, or making plays that expose one to potential criticism. Such repeated practice in social risk contexts could generalize to broader willingness to accept social uncertainty. Alternatively, sport-related socialization may emphasize group cohesion and trust, which could embolden members to take social risks they perceive as necessary for group success. This social mechanism could also interact with selection processes if socially outgoing individuals both choose team sports and score higher on social risk-taking measures.

The observed financial domain difference, although more moderate than recreational or social domains, raises interesting questions. Financial risk-taking is conceptually distinct from physical and social risks but may relate indirectly to sport participation through broader lifestyle or personality correlates. For example, individuals who enjoy novel and stimulating experiences may be more inclined to take financial gambles as part of a general tendency toward risk-seeking. Alternatively, financial risks may be influenced by perceptions of control and competence; athletes accustomed to making split-second decisions and tolerating uncertainty might transfer a degree of confidence to financial decision contexts. Nevertheless, the financial difference was smaller and should be interpreted cautiously, especially given the cross-sectional design.

Several methodological strengths lend credibility to the findings. Use of a standardized domain-specific measure allowed for nuanced detection of where sport participants and non-participants differ, avoiding misleading generalizations that could arise from unidimensional instruments. The balancing of groups on age and gender reduced demographic confounding, and internal reliability coefficients supported the psychometric adequacy of the subscales in this sample. Analytic procedures included assumption checks, effect size reporting, and correction for multiple comparisons, enhancing the rigor of inference.

At the same time, important limitations constrain the conclusions that can be drawn. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, meaning that selection and socialization mechanisms cannot be disentangled definitively. The sample size, while adequate to detect medium and large effects, limits statistical power for detecting smaller effects and for conducting more fine-grained subgroup analyses such as comparing contact versus non-contact sports or team versus individual sports. The reliance on self-report measures introduces potential biases such as social desirability or differential interpretation of items between groups familiar with sport contexts and those who are not. Additionally, the sample was drawn from a university and local community context and may not generalize to older adults, elite athletes, or culturally distinct populations where sport norms differ.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs that follow individuals before they join sports and track changes in domain-specific risk-taking over time. Such designs would allow stronger tests of whether sport participation causes shifts in risk attitudes and would help quantify the relative influence of selection versus socialization. Experimental interventions that manipulate exposure to sport-related social norms or simulated risk experiences could also provide causal leverage. Incorporating behavioral measures of risk-taking, such as economic gambling tasks or simulated physical risk scenarios, would complement self-report instruments and address potential biases. Neurobiological or psychophysiological measures could illuminate whether sport participants differ in arousal, reward sensitivity, or cognitive control when making risky choices.

Subgroup analyses could yield important insights. Comparing individual athletes to team athletes, contact sports to non-contact sports, and recreational to competitive athletes could reveal different patterns of association, as normative pressures and risk exposure vary across these categories. Understanding gender dynamics more fully would be valuable as well; while the present study matched on gender, larger samples could explore whether sport participation interacts with gender to shape domain-specific risk tendencies. Cross-cultural research would also be informative, given the varied meanings and structures of sport across societies.

The practical implications of the findings are multifaceted. Coaches and sport administrators interested in athlete welfare should be aware that sport participation may be associated with higher willingness to take certain kinds of risks, which could contribute to injury risk if not paired with proper education and risk management training. Conversely, higher social risk-taking could be harnessed positively to foster team cohesion and leadership development. Awareness of the domain-specificity of risk preferences could inform targeted interventions; for instance, programs aimed at promoting safe behavior among athletes may need to address perceived control and recalibrated risk thresholds in health/safety domains specifically.

At a theoretical level, the study contributes to models of risk-taking by demonstrating that experiential context—here, sport participation—aligns with domain-specific risk attitudes in predictable ways. The findings support a framework that integrates trait dispositions, learned experience, and domain relevance in explaining variability in risk-taking. Rather than endorsing a single process explanation, the results suggest that selection, socialization, and skill/competence processes likely operate in concert, producing the observed pattern of elevated risk-taking in domains that overlap with sport experiences.

In conclusion, the present cross-sectional comparison found that membership in sports clubs or teams is associated with higher self-reported willingness to engage in recreational, health/safety, social, and financial risks, but not ethical risks. This pattern underscores the importance of domain specificity in understanding the link between sport participation and risk behavior and points to multiple plausible mechanisms including selection, socialization, and skill-based calibration. Future longitudinal, experimental, and multimethod research is needed to disentangle causal pathways and to inform interventions that maximize the benefits of sport while minimizing potential harms related to maladaptive risk-taking.
