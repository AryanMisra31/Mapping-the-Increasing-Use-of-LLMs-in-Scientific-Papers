Introduction

Mental health difficulties are a prevalent concern among university students internationally. Undergraduate populations experience elevated rates of anxiety, depression, and stress relative to non-student peers. The transition to university is associated with multiple new stressors including academic pressures, changes in social networks, and increased autonomy for managing health. Timely and appropriate professional help seeking for psychological distress is important because it is associated with reduced symptom severity and improved functioning when treatment is accessed. Despite the potential benefits of professional help, many students do not seek formal psychological support when they experience significant symptoms. Research has repeatedly identified a gap between need and uptake of mental health services in student populations. Barriers that have been identified include limited mental health literacy, attitudinal barriers such as stigma and self-reliance, practical barriers such as cost and time, and concerns about confidentiality. The pattern of low help-seeking uptake is concerning given the developmental period of undergraduate study and the likely persistence of untreated problems into later adulthood for some students.

Among undergraduate majors, psychology students present a theoretically interesting group to study in relation to help-seeking. On the one hand, psychology students receive coursework that increases their knowledge of psychological problems and mental health care options. Such exposure might be expected to increase mental health literacy and reduce stigma, thereby increasing intentions to seek professional help relative to students who do not study psychology. On the other hand, psychology students may develop specific attitudes that complicate help-seeking. For example, psychology students might feel that they can self-diagnose or self-manage psychological difficulties, or they might worry about being judged by faculty or peers within their discipline. Prior literature has described a paradox whereby disciplines that offer mental health training may not necessarily show higher rates of professional help-seeking among their students. This paradox motivates empirical comparison between psychology undergraduates and undergraduates from other disciplines.

Empirical work on this topic has produced mixed findings. Some studies of students have found elevated levels of psychological distress among psychology students relative to peers in other fields, which raises questions about whether psychology students seek help commensurate with need. Other studies have documented relatively low uptake of formal mental health services among psychology students despite their knowledge base. There are multiple plausible explanations for low uptake among psychology students: greater preference for informal sources of support, concerns about confidentiality within academic departments, professional identity concerns, or differential recognition of one’s own distress. Additionally, help-seeking intentions, which are often measured in cross-sectional surveys, may not map directly onto actual help-seeking behavior, and different measures capture different constructs (for example, attitudes toward professional help versus intentions to seek help versus past behavior).

Attitudes and intentions are central constructs in health behavior models that attempt to predict whether people will engage with health services. Models such as Theory of Planned Behavior and health belief frameworks posit that attitudes, perceived norms, and perceived behavioral control influence intentions, which in turn predict behavior. In the context of psychological help-seeking, attitudes toward professional services and perceived stigma are consistently implicated as predictors of intention. Thus, measuring attitudes and intentions provides a useful initial assessment of whether a given group may be more or less likely to engage with mental health services in the future. Because attitudes and intentions are modifiable via educational interventions, documenting differences among student groups can inform targeted efforts to increase service uptake.

It is therefore important to examine whether undergraduates who study psychology differ systematically from undergraduates in other disciplines in their attitudes and intentions about seeking professional help for psychological distress. If psychology students show greater intentions to seek help, that might suggest that mental health training has salutary effects on openness to professional support. If psychology students do not differ, it suggests either that training is not translating into greater willingness to seek care, or that other factors counteract the potential benefits of education. If psychology students show lower intentions, that would point to discipline-specific barriers that need addressing within training programs.

The current study was designed to address this question directly using a cross-sectional survey comparing psychology undergraduates with non-psychology undergraduates on validated measures of attitudes toward seeking professional psychological help and intentions to seek help. The approach was to recruit a reasonably sized sample of undergraduate students from multiple disciplines and to use established self-report scales to capture attitudes and intentions. Because overall psychological distress might differ between groups and might influence intentions, the study also measured current distress so that secondary analyses could examine whether controlling for distress altered any observed relationship between discipline and help-seeking intentions.

A clear primary hypothesis for the study is difficult to justify given prior mixed findings, but the study tested the simple prediction that psychology undergraduates would report greater intentions to seek professional help for psychological distress compared with undergraduates from other disciplines. This hypothesis reflects the plausible expectation that increased theoretically relevant knowledge and potentially greater mental health literacy among psychology students may translate into greater willingness to engage with professional services. Secondary analyses examined the association between psychological distress and help-seeking intentions across the whole sample and tested whether any observed discipline differences would remain after statistically controlling for distress, gender, and year of study. The study also examined basic demographic patterns so that potential confounding variables could be identified.

By comparing psychology students to their peers across disciplines, this research sought to provide a clearer empirical basis for decisions about whether mental health literacy and stigma-reduction efforts should be targeted across the entire student body or whether discipline-specific interventions are warranted. The findings are relevant to university counseling services, academic departments, and student health initiatives. If psychology students do not demonstrate higher intentions to seek professional help, then assumptions that coursework alone adequately prepares students to engage with services may need to be re-examined. Conversely, if non-psychology students show lower intentions, resources might be focused on broad-based mental health education outside psychology departments.

Overall, therefore, the study aimed to address an applied and theoretically interesting question using straightforward methods. The rest of the article describes the methods used to recruit and measure students, the statistical analyses performed, the principal findings, and their implications for research and practice. The study also considers limitations, alternative explanations, and directions for future research that are suggested by the data.

Methods

Design and overview. The study used a cross-sectional survey design to compare two groups of undergraduate students: those enrolled in psychology degree programs and those enrolled in non-psychology degree programs. The primary dependent variable was a self-report measure of intentions to seek professional help for psychological problems. Secondary variables included attitudes toward seeking professional psychological help, current psychological distress, and demographic and academic covariates. Recruitment targeted a convenience sample of undergraduates across multiple departments at a single university. All data were collected via an online questionnaire.

Participants and sampling. Participants were undergraduate students aged 18 years or older who were currently enrolled at the university during the academic term in which the study was conducted. Students were eligible if they were registered in a degree program and were able to provide informed consent. The study advertised participation via email lists, learning management system announcements, department newsletters, and posters with a link to the online survey. Students received a small course credit or entry in a prize draw as an incentive for participation, depending on departmental policies. A total of 250 students completed the survey. After exclusion of incomplete responses on primary measures and verification of student status, 250 valid cases were retained for analysis. Of these, 120 students were categorized as psychology undergraduates and 130 students as non-psychology undergraduates.

Ethics and consent. Institutional ethical approval was obtained prior to data collection. The online survey included an information sheet describing the purpose of the research, that participation was voluntary, and that responses were confidential. Students provided electronic informed consent before proceeding to the questionnaire. Contact information for counseling services and crisis supports was provided at the start and end of the survey for participants who might experience distress.

Measures. Demographics. A brief demographic section asked for age, gender, year of study, and primary field of study. Participants indicated whether they were enrolled in a psychology degree program or another discipline; those who indicated psychology were coded into the psychology group, and all others into the non-psychology group. Participants also reported past use of mental health services and whether they were currently receiving professional psychological treatment.

Help-seeking intentions. The principal outcome measure was the General Help-Seeking Questionnaire (GHSQ) adapted to measure intentions to seek professional help for a range of psychological problems. The adapted GHSQ asked participants to rate on a 5-point Likert scale their likelihood of seeking help from specified sources (including a psychologist, counselor, general practitioner, and university counseling service) if they were experiencing a serious emotional problem. Items were averaged to form a professional help-seeking intentions score, with higher scores indicating greater likelihood of seeking professional help. The GHSQ has been widely used in student samples and has acceptable internal consistency in prior studies.

Attitudes toward professional help. The Attitudes Toward Seeking Professional Psychological Help scale (short form) was included to capture more general attitudes toward professional psychological services. This scale includes items tapping perceived stigma, openness to help, and perceived effectiveness of professional treatment. Responses were given on a 4-point Likert scale and were summed to produce a total attitudes score. Higher scores correspond to more positive attitudes toward professional help.

Psychological distress. Current psychological distress was measured using a brief standardized screening instrument suitable for survey administration, the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales-21 (DASS-21) subscales combined into a total distress score. The DASS-21 provides a continuous measure of symptom severity across depression, anxiety, and stress domains. Scores were calculated according to standard scoring procedures.

Procedure. The online questionnaire was hosted on a secure survey platform. After consenting, participants completed the demographic questions followed by the measures in the following order: DASS-21, attitudes toward help, and the help-seeking intentions questionnaire. The order was chosen to measure current distress prior to the help-seeking intentions questions to reduce the likelihood that completing help-seeking items would affect reports of current distress. The survey took approximately 15 minutes to complete on average. Data collection remained open for six weeks.

Data preparation and handling of missing data. Data were exported from the survey platform into a statistical software package for analysis. Responses were screened for completeness. Participants who failed to complete the primary outcome measure (the GHSQ professional help-seeking items) were excluded. For participants with occasional missing item responses on secondary scales, mean imputation at the scale level was used when less than 10% of items on that scale were missing. No case had more than 10% missing on any included scale. All items were coded so that higher scores represented higher levels of the construct in question.

Assumption checks and statistical plan. The primary analysis was an independent samples t-test comparing psychology and non-psychology undergraduates on the professional help-seeking intentions score. Prior to the t-test, assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed. Normality was inspected using skewness and kurtosis indices and visual inspection of histograms, and homogeneity of variances was tested with Levene’s test. Effect size was calculated using Cohen’s d for independent samples. Given the modest group sizes, and the possibility that the effect could be small, a Bayes factor analysis was conducted to quantify evidence in favor of the null versus alternative hypotheses. Secondary analyses included Pearson correlations between distress, attitudes, and intentions across the whole sample. A hierarchical linear regression tested whether discipline predicted help-seeking intentions after controlling for distress, gender, and year of study. Exploratory subgroup analyses examined whether past use of mental health services or current treatment moderated any relationship between discipline and intentions.

Sample size justification. An a priori power calculation was conducted to estimate sample size for detecting a small-to-medium effect (Cohen’s d = 0.35) with 80% power and alpha = .05 in a two-tailed independent samples t-test. The calculation suggested a required total sample of approximately 200 participants. The achieved sample of 250 provided adequate power to detect effects in the small-to-medium range and reasonable precision for estimates.

Statistical software. All analyses were carried out using standard statistical software. Descriptive statistics, t-tests, correlations, and regressions were computed using conventional procedures. Bayes factor estimates were obtained using a commonly used default Cauchy prior to provide an index of support for the null hypothesis.

Results

Sample characteristics. The final sample included 250 undergraduate students. Of these, 120 (48%) were enrolled in psychology degree programs and 130 (52%) were enrolled in other disciplines. Ages ranged from 18 to 36 years with a mean age of 20.8 years (SD = 2.5). The sample included 172 females (68.8%), 70 males (28.0%), and 8 participants who selected another gender identity or preferred not to say (3.2%). Year of study was distributed across first through fourth years, with 34% in first year, 28% in second year, 22% in third year, and 16% in fourth year or beyond. Past use of mental health services was reported by 38% of participants and 12% reported they were currently receiving professional psychological treatment.

Descriptive statistics for primary variables. The professional help-seeking intentions score (adapted GHSQ professional items) had a mean of 3.07 (SD = 0.88) for the whole sample, on a 1-to-5 scale with higher scores indicating greater reported likelihood of seeking professional help. Psychology students had a mean professional help-seeking intention score of 3.10 (SD = 0.85), and non-psychology students had a mean of 3.05 (SD = 0.90). Attitudes toward professional help (ATSPPH short form) had a mean of 23.1 (SD = 5.6) on its scale with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes. Psychology students had a slightly higher mean attitudes score (M = 23.8, SD = 5.3) than non-psychology students (M = 22.4, SD = 5.8). The DASS-21 total distress score had a sample mean of 18.6 (SD = 11.4) with psychology students showing a mean of 19.8 (SD = 11.8) and non-psychology students a mean of 17.4 (SD = 11.0).

Assumption checks. Inspection of histograms and skewness and kurtosis indices indicated that the professional help-seeking intentions score was approximately normally distributed in both groups. Skewness values were below |1.0| and kurtosis values were within acceptable ranges. Levene’s test for equality of variances indicated homogeneity of variances for the intentions variable, F(1, 248) = 0.32, p = .57, so equal variances were assumed.

Primary analysis: independent t-test. An independent samples t-test compared psychology and non-psychology undergraduates on the professional help-seeking intentions score. The t-test indicated no significant difference between the groups, t(248) = 0.54, p = .59. The mean difference was 0.05 on the 1-to-5 scale, with a 95% confidence interval for the difference ranging from -0.14 to 0.24. The observed effect size was very small, Cohen’s d = 0.07. These results indicate that psychology undergraduates in this sample did not report greater intentions to seek professional help than their peers in other disciplines.

Bayesian analysis. To quantify evidence for the null hypothesis, a Bayes factor analysis (BF10) was computed using a default Cauchy prior. The analysis yielded BF10 = 0.26, indicating that the observed data were approximately 3.8 times more likely under the null hypothesis of no difference than under the alternative hypothesis. This provides moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis given the prior used for the analysis.

Correlations among variables. Pearson correlations across the whole sample showed that attitudes toward professional help were positively correlated with help-seeking intentions, r = .52, p < .001. Current distress was modestly positively correlated with help-seeking intentions, r = .18, p = .006, indicating that participants reporting higher distress tended to report slightly greater intentions to seek professional help. Past use of mental health services was also associated with higher intentions, point-biserial r = .32, p < .001. Gender was weakly associated with intentions, with females reporting slightly higher intentions than males, t-test p = .04, though the difference was small in magnitude.

Regression analysis controlling for covariates. A hierarchical linear regression predicted help-seeking intentions. In Step 1, distress, gender, and year of study were entered as predictors, explaining 11.4% of variance in intentions, R^2 = .114, F(3,246) = 10.51, p < .001. Distress (β = .16, p = .01) and female gender (β = .12, p = .04) were significant predictors in this step. In Step 2, discipline (psychology vs non-psychology) was added and did not explain additional variance, ΔR^2 = .001, ΔF(1,245) = 0.29, p = .59. Psychology discipline was a non-significant predictor in the final model (β = .04, p = .59). A model that additionally included attitudes toward help (entered in Step 3) explained substantially more variance, with attitudes being a strong predictor (β = .49, p < .001) and increasing R^2 to .375. Discipline remained non-significant after inclusion of attitudes.

Subgroup and sensitivity analyses. An analysis restricted to students who reported moderate-to-high distress (DASS-21 score above a commonly used threshold) yielded a similar pattern of results. Within this subgroup, psychology students (n = 45) and non-psychology students (n = 48) did not differ in professional help-seeking intentions, t(91) = 0.12, p = .90. A sensitivity analysis removing participants who were currently receiving professional treatment (n = 30) also yielded a non-significant difference between disciplines, t(216) = 0.46, p = .64. Finally, an exploratory test of interaction between discipline and past service use in predicting intentions was non-significant, suggesting that the relationship between past service use and intentions did not differ by discipline.

Descriptive examination of item-level responses. Item-level inspection of the help-seeking intentions items suggested that participants were more likely to indicate willingness to seek help from a general practitioner and from a university counseling service than from a psychologist specifically. This pattern was similar across psychology and non-psychology students. Rates of endorsement of the highest response option ("very likely") for seeking help from a psychologist were relatively low (approximately 18% overall).

Summary of results. In summary, the primary analysis found no evidence that psychology undergraduates report greater intentions to seek professional help for psychological problems than undergraduates from other disciplines. The observed difference was trivial in magnitude, and Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence supporting the null hypothesis. Attitudes toward professional help predicted intentions strongly across the sample, and current distress had a modest positive association with intentions. Discipline did not predict intentions after controlling for other variables.

Discussion

This study compared undergraduate psychology students with students from other disciplines in their intentions to seek professional help for psychological problems. The primary finding was that there was no significant difference in reported willingness to seek professional help between psychology undergraduates and non-psychology undergraduates. The mean difference in intentions was negligible and did not reach statistical significance. A Bayesian analysis provided moderate support for the null hypothesis, suggesting that the data were more consistent with no difference than with a meaningful difference between groups. Secondary analyses indicated that attitudes toward professional help and current distress were meaningful predictors of intentions across the whole sample, and that discipline did not add explanatory power beyond these factors.

The primary result of no difference between psychology and non-psychology students challenges a simple assumption that psychology training necessarily confers greater openness to professional help. The finding is consistent with prior reports that psychology students, despite exposure to coursework about mental health, do not necessarily use mental health services at higher rates than their peers. One interpretation is that mental health knowledge alone is insufficient to change intentions to seek professional help. Knowledge about mental disorders and treatments may increase recognition of symptoms but may not reduce attitudinal barriers such as stigma or fears about career implications. Alternatively, psychology students may develop a sense of self-reliance or overconfidence in their ability to manage problems using their knowledge, which would tend to reduce intentions to seek formal help. In addition, psychology students may preferentially seek informal support from peers or supervisors rather than formal services, resulting in similar reported intentions to access professional services compared with other students.

Another possible interpretation is that the measure of intentions used in this study may capture generalized willingness to seek professional help but may not reflect the more nuanced decision-making processes that lead to actual service use. Help-seeking behavior is influenced by many practical factors including service availability, perceived cost, appointment wait times, and logistical convenience. Psychology students might be more aware of the limitations of university services and therefore might express similar or even lower intentions to seek them. The data showing similar item-level patterns for preferred sources of help across disciplines supports the view that preference for particular sources (e.g., GP vs psychologist) was not discipline-dependent in this sample.

The strong association observed between attitudes toward professional help and intentions aligns with established theory and previous empirical findings. Attitudes appear to be a central mechanism by which students form intentions regarding professional support. Because attitudes were distributed similarly across the two groups and because attitudes accounted for a substantial portion of variance in intentions, efforts to increase service uptake may be most effective if they focus on shifting attitudes across the entire student population rather than targeting specific disciplines. Examples of such approaches include psychoeducation campaigns, stigma-reduction interventions, and promotion of success stories from peers who have benefited from mental health care.

The modest positive association between current distress and intentions suggests that students who feel more symptomatic are slightly more likely to report willingness to seek professional help. However, the association was small, indicating that distress alone does not strongly predict the intention to seek help. This finding suggests that many students who experience significant symptoms may still be unlikely to seek help, reinforcing the need to address attitudinal and practical barriers. In this context, it is noteworthy that past service use was associated with higher intentions. This pattern suggests that prior contact with services may reduce barriers to future help seeking, perhaps by familiarizing students with the process or by reducing uncertainty.

There are several practical implications of these findings. First, psychology departments should not assume that their students will access mental health services at higher rates merely because they receive relevant coursework. Curriculum designers might consider integrating explicit content that addresses help-seeking behavior, including reducing self-stigma, clarifying confidentiality protections, and providing clear pathways for accessing services. Second, university-wide programs that foster positive attitudes toward professional help and that lower practical barriers (e.g., improving accessibility of appointments, promoting telehealth options) may be an efficient way to increase help-seeking intentions across all student groups. Third, given the observed importance of attitudes, interventions that use social norms, contact with people who have used services, or cognitive-behavioral strategies to reduce stigma may be particularly important.

The null finding also points to important directions for future research. Longitudinal studies would be valuable to determine causal relationships and to examine whether changes in attitudes and knowledge over time predict subsequent help-seeking behavior. Such studies could also examine the effects of specific curricular elements or practicum experiences on willingness to seek care. Experimental designs that manipulate mental health literacy content or stigma-reduction messages could test whether these components causally affect intentions and behavior. Qualitative research with psychology students could explore the reasons behind help-seeking decisions in greater depth, including perceived professional identity concerns, fears about being stigmatized by faculty or peers, and beliefs about self-management. Such qualitative work could reveal subtleties that are not captured by standardized questionnaires.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes inferences about causality. We cannot determine whether being a psychology student causes any change in attitudes or intentions or whether students who select into psychology differ on unmeasured characteristics. Second, the use of self-report measures of intentions may not predict actual behavior. While intentions are an important proximal predictor, actual service use is influenced by many contextual factors. Third, the sample was drawn from a single university using a convenience sampling approach, which may limit generalizability. The demographic composition of the sample, with a higher proportion of females, reflects typical patterns in psychology programs and could influence results. Fourth, the measures used, while validated, may not capture discipline-specific nuances in attitudes or intentions. For example, the GHSQ measures likelihood but does not capture perceived barriers such as expected wait times or cost. Fifth, although the sample size provided reasonable power for small-to-medium effects, the study may still have limited sensitivity to detect very small effects. However, the Bayesian analysis suggested moderate evidence for the null, which supports the interpretation that any discipline effect, if present, is likely to be negligible in practical terms.

Alternative explanations for the null result merit consideration. It is possible that psychology curricula vary widely across institutions, and that the particular programs represented in this sample did not differ markedly from other departments in how they exposed students to mental health content. Another possibility is that students across disciplines now receive more general mental health education through orientation programs and campus initiatives, which could reduce between-discipline differences. Additionally, the relatively high baseline level of intentions in both groups might have produced a ceiling effect that reduced the opportunity to detect group differences. However, inspection of score distributions did not indicate severe ceiling effects.

Future research should also attend to behavioral outcomes. Linking intentions with administrative data on counseling service utilization (with appropriate consent and privacy protections) would permit examination of the extent to which self-reported intentions translate into actual help-seeking. Intervention studies could test whether tailored programming for specific student groups increases both intentions and utilization. Another fertile area of inquiry is the role of peer influence and departmental culture in shaping help-seeking. Ethnographic or social network approaches could illuminate how norms within academic departments affect whether and how students seek support.

In addition, future work could examine moderators of the relationship between discipline and help-seeking, such as personality traits, prior personal or family experience with mental health problems, and career aspirations that may influence willingness to disclose mental health issues. For example, students considering clinical careers may be particularly sensitive to concerns about fitness to practice or disclosure. Understanding these moderating factors could enable more finely targeted interventions.

The results also have implications for how universities allocate resources. If discipline alone does not predict intentions, universities may achieve greater impact by investing in campus-wide mental health promotion and by reducing structural barriers to accessing care. Ensuring that students are aware of available services, that these services are accessible and affordable, and that confidentiality concerns are addressed could plausibly increase service uptake across disciplines.

In conclusion, this study found no evidence that undergraduate psychology students show greater intentions to seek professional help for psychological problems than their peers in other disciplines. Attitudes toward professional help and current distress were more important correlates of intentions than discipline of study. These findings suggest that knowledge gained through coursework may not, on its own, translate into greater willingness to seek professional help and that efforts to increase student engagement with mental health services should target attitudes and practical barriers across the whole student population. Future research using longitudinal, experimental, and qualitative methods is needed to unpack the mechanisms behind help-seeking decisions and to develop interventions that increase both intentions and actual utilization of mental health services among undergraduates.
