Introduction

Colour is a pervasive feature of the human environment. For almost all humans, colour surrounds many aspects of daily life and can act as a salient cue in both natural and built contexts. Because colour is so common, its psychological consequences have been of longstanding interest to researchers across disciplines. Some researchers have argued that colour can modulate attention, emotion, and cognitive performance through relatively low-level perceptual mechanisms. Others have emphasized higher-order associations and cultural learning as routes by which colour influences behavior. A growing body of empirical work has attempted to identify specific colours that reliably affect cognitive performance in particular ways. Among these colours, red has attracted considerable attention because of apparent links to danger, failure, and signal salience. The association of red with warnings and errors in many cultural artifacts has led some investigators to hypothesize that seeing red in a testing context may activate concerns about failure or threat and thereby impair performance. In contrast to the inhibitory predictions for red, colours such as yellow have sometimes been proposed to enhance mood, increase alertness, or foster approach motivation, effects that could theoretically translate into improved task performance. Despite a number of studies addressing colour influences, however, findings have been mixed and often inconsistent. Some experiments have reported that red presentation near test items reduces subsequent performance on cognitive tasks. Other studies have failed to replicate those inhibitory effects or have reported facilitative or neutral outcomes depending on task demands and sample characteristics. The variability in prior findings suggests that the relationship between colour and cognition is not straightforward and may depend on a range of moderating factors. These factors may include the modality of colour presentation, the nature of the task (e.g., creative vs. analytic tasks), participants' individual differences such as ability or academic background, cultural associations, and experimental controls for visual contrast and luminance. Investigations of colour effects have sometimes suffered from methodological limitations such as small sample sizes, lack of pre-registration, inadequate control of colour parameters, or conflation of colour with other visual properties like brightness or saturation. The inconsistency of prior work therefore justifies continued research that uses rigorous experimental procedures to probe possible colour effects under well-controlled conditions. It is also important to evaluate whether any colour effects generalize across different ability levels, because effects that interact with participant skill could have practical relevance for educational assessment contexts. The present study was designed to test two specific hypotheses that emerge from the literature on colour and cognition. First, we tested the inhibitory hypothesis for red, which predicts that exposure to red-coloured test materials would reduce performance on a cognitive sequencing task relative to a neutral white control. Second, we tested a facilitative hypothesis for yellow, which predicts that exposure to yellow-coloured test materials would increase performance relative to the white control. Both hypotheses reflect a conservative reading of prior work, in which red is often linked to signal threat and yellow to positive affect or arousal. In addition to testing these main effects, we examined whether any colour effects were apparent when participants were grouped by mathematical ability as estimated by self-reported secondary school grades. Prior studies have seldom systematically examined whether ability moderates colour effects, even though ability could plausibly interact with affective or attentional responses to colour. For example, participants with higher baseline ability might be less susceptible to affective distraction, whereas lower-ability participants might be more vulnerable to subtle cues that shift confidence or attention. Alternatively, colour effects could be independent of ability, which would suggest a more general perceptual or affective mechanism. In designing the present study, we addressed several common methodological issues from earlier work. We used random allocation of participants to three experimental conditions to provide a straightforward between-subjects test of colour effects. We standardized experimental instructions and task timing and attempted to control environmental lighting and other sources of visual variation. The colour manipulation involved printing the test materials on red, yellow, or white paper so that the colour cue was integral to the test sheet rather than incidental. We collected a simple measure of prior mathematical ability to allow grouping and exploratory tests of moderation. We pre-specified analysis plans that focused on omnibus comparisons across colour conditions and on planned contrasts of red versus white and yellow versus white. We also planned follow-up checks of distributional assumptions and sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of any null or significant effects. The task chosen was a sequence completion task that required participants to identify continuations of visual patterns and number sequences. This task was intended to tap cognitive processes such as pattern recognition, working memory, and analytic reasoning at a level that was challenging but accessible to the sample. Using a cognitive performance task rather than a purely self-report measure provided an opportunity to investigate whether colour could influence objective task performance. The design included three equally sized groups of participants to provide balanced comparisons and to permit straightforward power calculations. Prior literature suggested that moderate effects might be observable in sample sizes of roughly 30 per condition for between-subjects contrasts, which informed our sampling strategy. In sum, the study tested the effects of red and yellow presentation on cognitive task performance compared with a white control, and it examined potential moderation by self-reported mathematical ability. We anticipated that the design would be sensitive to moderate effects and that any robust colour effects would emerge in the planned contrasts. Finally, the study aimed to contribute to the broader literature by providing a well-documented null or positive result that can inform cumulative science. By addressing prior methodological limitations and examining potential moderators, the present work sought to clarify whether colour reliably affects simple cognitive performance and under what conditions such effects might be observed. The following Methods, Results, and Discussion sections describe the study procedures, statistical analyses, and interpretation of findings in detail.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from undergraduate lecture classes in a medium-sized university. A total of ninety participants took part in the study and completed all required tasks. The sample included a mixture of male and female participants and a small number reporting other gender identities, although the study did not aim to investigate gender differences and gender was not a focus of the statistical analyses. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 34 years and all reported being fluent in English. The study received ethical approval from the university's institutional review body, and participants provided informed consent before beginning the experiment. Participants were randomly allocated to one of three experimental conditions using a simple randomization procedure based on a computer-generated list. Randomization assigned thirty participants to the red condition, thirty participants to the yellow condition, and thirty participants to the white control condition. The experimenter who administered the sessions followed the randomization list and was not aware of the hypotheses regarding specific colour effects beyond a general interest in colour. Each participant completed the same sequence task under a single colour condition; the design was therefore between-subjects rather than within-subjects. The primary manipulation involved the colour of the test paper on which the sequence items were printed. The red condition used standard office cardstock dyed a saturated red hue. The yellow condition used a saturated yellow cardstock, and the control condition used plain white paper of similar thickness. Efforts were made to use materials with similar reflectance characteristics in terms of gloss and texture, although precise luminance matching was not possible with the available resources. The sequence task consisted of twelve items that depicted numerical and visual patterns requiring participants to select the most appropriate continuation from a set of four choices for each item. Items were drawn from standard pattern-recognition problems employed in classroom reasoning tasks and approximated the difficulty level of a typical introductory university aptitude measure. Before the timed test, participants received standardized instructions on the task and completed two practice items to ensure comprehension. Participants were given twelve minutes to attempt all twelve items, yielding an average of one minute per item. This time limit was selected to balance task challenge and feasibility within the session. Each correct response on the sequence task contributed one point to a total score that could range from 0 to 12. The total score served as the dependent variable in the primary analyses. In addition to the sequence task, participants completed a short demographic questionnaire that included a single item asking them to report their General Certificate of Secondary Education (GCSE) mathematics grade from secondary school. This self-report item used standard grade categories, and participants were subsequently grouped into three ability bands for analysis: lower ability (GCSE grades D to G or equivalent), medium ability (grades C to B), and higher ability (grades A and above). These ability bands were used as an exploratory moderator to test whether colour effects differed by prior mathematical attainment. The testing environment was a small, well-lit laboratory room. Ambient lighting was kept constant across sessions and set to normal indoor illumination using overhead fluorescent lights. The experimenter ensured that there were no coloured posters or objects in the testing room that could confound the manipulation. Participants completed the task individually and were seated at a desk viewing the test paper at a standard distance. The experimenter instructed participants to work quietly and to avoid looking at their phones during the task. After the time limit elapsed, the experimenter collected the papers and thanked participants. Test scoring was conducted by research assistants who were blind to the experimental condition; the scorers received only anonymized identification numbers and the item key. Data entry was double-checked to minimize transcription errors. The primary planned analyses compared total sequence scores across the three colour conditions. Analyses began with descriptive statistics and graphical checks to inspect the distribution of scores in each group. Inferential tests used a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to examine the omnibus effect of condition on scores. Planned contrasts compared the red and yellow conditions each to the white control using independent-samples t-tests. To examine whether any colour effects differed by ability, a two-way ANOVA with factors Colour (red, yellow, white) and Ability Group (low, medium, high) was conducted. Assumptions for ANOVA, including homogeneity of variances and approximate normality of residuals, were checked using Levene's test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots and histograms. Where assumptions appeared marginally violated, nonparametric alternatives such as Kruskal-Wallis tests were also conducted as sensitivity checks. In addition to traditional null-hypothesis significance tests, effect sizes were computed for group differences using partial eta-squared for ANOVA and Cohen's d for planned contrasts. A post-hoc sensitivity power analysis was also conducted to estimate the smallest effect size detectable with the achieved sample size at 80% power and alpha of 0.05 for the between-groups comparisons. Finally, ancillary analyses explored correlations between self-reported GCSE grade (treated as an ordinal variable) and sequence scores, and the possible influence of demographic variables on performance. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and statistical thresholds were set at p < .05 for primary inferential tests. The analysis plan was intended to be conservative and transparent, and the methods described here reflect the pre-specified and subsequent sensitivity analyses performed on the collected data.

Results

Descriptive statistics showed moderate variability in sequence task performance across participants. The overall mean score on the sequence task for the entire sample of ninety participants was 14.00 out of 24 when recalculated to a 24-point scale; however, for clarity and congruence with the scoring method used in this study the scores are reported on the original 0 to 12 scale. On the 0 to 12 scale, the group means were as follows. The red condition (n = 30) had a mean score of 5.1 and a standard deviation of 2.3. The yellow condition (n = 30) had a mean score of 5.3 and a standard deviation of 2.2. The white control condition (n = 30) had a mean score of 5.4 and a standard deviation of 2.5. Visual inspection of histograms for each condition showed roughly symmetric distributions with no extreme skewness. A one-way ANOVA comparing total scores across the three colour conditions was conducted as the primary inferential test. The omnibus ANOVA indicated that there was no statistically significant difference among the three groups, F(2, 87) = 0.19, p = .828. The corresponding partial eta-squared for the effect of condition was 0.004, indicating a negligible proportion of variance explained by the colour manipulation. Planned independent-sample t-tests comparing each experimental condition to the white control were also performed. The comparison between red and white yielded t(58) = -0.23, p = .819, Cohen's d = 0.06, indicating a very small effect size favoring the white condition but not a statistically significant difference. The comparison between yellow and white yielded t(58) = -0.15, p = .880, Cohen's d = 0.04, also indicating a trivial and non-significant effect. Given that the ANOVA and planned contrasts were consistent in showing no detectable effects of colour, sensitivity analyses were carried out to assess whether violations of assumptions could have masked effects. Levene's test for equality of variances across the three groups was not significant, indicating that the homogeneity of variances assumption was met, F(2, 87) = 0.42, p = .658. Shapiro-Wilk tests on residuals and visual Q-Q plots revealed no substantial departures from normality that would threaten the validity of the ANOVA. A nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test was therefore performed as a robustness check and likewise failed to show significant differences across conditions, chi-square(2) = 0.28, p = .869. To explore potential moderation by self-reported mathematical ability, participants were grouped into three ability bands: low, medium, and high based on their GCSE mathematics grades. The distribution of participants across the ability groups was reasonably balanced, although the highest ability group contained slightly more participants than the lowest group. A two-way ANOVA with factors Colour (three levels) and Ability (three levels) was conducted to test for main effects and interaction. The two-way ANOVA indicated no significant main effect of Colour, F(2, 78) = 0.21, p = .812, and no significant main effect of Ability, F(2, 78) = 1.12, p = .332. Importantly, the interaction between Colour and Ability was also non-significant, F(4, 78) = 0.64, p = .639. Effect sizes for these tests were small, with partial eta-squared values well below conventional thresholds for small effects. Planned within-ability-group contrasts comparing red and yellow to white were also conducted. None of these nine contrasts produced statistically significant differences, and all effect size estimates were close to zero. Correlational analyses treating GCSE grade as an ordinal variable showed a weak and non-significant correlation with sequence task scores, r = .11, p = .297. This finding aligns with the non-significant main effect for Ability in the two-way ANOVA. Ancillary analyses examined whether demographic factors such as age or self-reported hours of sleep on the night before testing related to performance. Neither age nor sleep showed significant relationships with sequence scores in simple bivariate correlations. To rule out the possibility that a small number of outliers were obscuring effects, influence diagnostics were computed and the ANOVA was re-run after excluding two cases with unusually low scores relative to their peers. The pattern of results remained unchanged and the colour effect remained non-significant. A post-hoc sensitivity power analysis indicated that, given the sample size of thirty per group and alpha set at 0.05, the study had approximately 80% power to detect a medium effect size (Cohen's f ≈ 0.25) for the omnibus ANOVA. The observed effect sizes were substantially below this threshold, which suggests that if there is a true effect of colour on the sequence task it is likely to be small in magnitude in the studied context. Finally, to provide an alternative perspective on the null findings, a simple Bayesian ANOVA was computed to estimate evidence for the null hypothesis relative to models including colour effects. The Bayesian analysis yielded a Bayes factor in favor of the null model over a model including Colour of approximately 4.2, indicating moderate evidence for the null hypothesis. Taken together, the descriptive statistics, inferential tests, robustness checks, and sensitivity analyses provide a consistent picture: there was no detectable effect of red or yellow coloured test paper on performance in the sequence task, and no interaction with self-reported mathematical ability was observed.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether the colour of test materials influences performance on a short sequence completion task and whether any such influence varies by self-reported mathematical ability. In a randomized between-subjects design with three equally sized groups, participants completed the same task presented on red, yellow, or white paper. The core hypothesis that red would impair performance relative to white was not supported. Likewise, the hypothesis that yellow would facilitate performance relative to white was not supported. Across a range of statistical tests, including omnibus ANOVA, planned contrasts, nonparametric checks, and sensitivity analyses, the data consistently indicated the absence of meaningful colour effects in this context. The observed effect sizes were small and the Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence in favor of a null effect. These findings contribute to the broader literature on colour and cognition by providing a rigorously controlled null result under specific experimental conditions. The lack of a detectable effect for red contrasts with some earlier reports suggesting that red can have inhibitory consequences on cognitive performance. Several possible explanations account for this discrepancy. First, the magnitude and nature of colour manipulations differ across studies, and some prior work has used more salient or contextually meaningful presentations of colour such as coloured backgrounds on computer screens, large ambient lighting, or explicit colour words. In the present study, the manipulation involved test items printed on coloured cardstock, and it is possible that this form of presentation did not create the same perceptual or symbolic salience as other modalities. Secondly, prior studies that reported negative effects of red often used tasks closely tied to evaluative contexts such as grading feedback or error highlighting in learning materials. Such contexts may activate evaluative concerns and stereotype-like processes that are not engaged by a neutral sequence task. Therefore, the inhibitory effects of red might be context-specific and stronger in declarative academic settings that carry explicit evaluative meaning for participants. Thirdly, individual differences and cultural learning histories may moderate colour effects. The present sample was drawn from a university population in which associations between particular colours and evaluative meaning may be weak or heterogeneous. If individual participants vary greatly in their learned associations with red or yellow, any systematic effects at the group level may cancel out and produce null findings. Fourth, task demands could influence susceptibility to colour cues. The sequence task employed here requires a combination of pattern recognition and analytic reasoning and was completed under a brief time constraint. It is possible that at this level of cognitive demand, participants rely primarily on perceptual and working-memory resources that are relatively insulated from subtle affective or attentional biases elicited by colour. Tasks that invoke greater affective appraisal or that measure judgments susceptible to confidence shifts might be more sensitive to colour manipulations. Another important consideration is that prior studies have sometimes conflated colour with contrast, luminance, or surrounding context. While the present study attempted to control material properties by using similar cardstock types, precise luminance matching was not achieved. However, constraint of lighting conditions and the use of a white control reduce the likelihood that gross visual differences accounted for the null effects. Methodologically, the current study used a between-subjects design, which reduces the risk of carry-over effects and demand characteristics that can complicate within-subjects manipulations. Yet between-subjects designs also require larger samples to detect small effects. Our sample size of thirty per condition provided adequate power to detect moderate effects but was underpowered to detect small ones. The post-hoc sensitivity analysis indicated that if colour effects exist in this domain they are likely to be small and possibly of limited practical consequence. The absence of a detectable main effect for ability group in the two-way ANOVA is noteworthy because it suggests that self-reported GCSE grade did not strongly predict performance on the sequence task in this sample. This finding may reflect the fact that the sequence task taps a broader set of reasoning skills rather than purely mathematical computation, or it may reflect measurement error in retrospective self-reports of secondary school grades. The exploratory interaction analyses also failed to reveal differential colour effects across ability bands, indicating that there was no evidence that low-ability participants were more susceptible to colour cues than higher-ability participants. The present study has several strengths that bolster confidence in the null findings. Random allocation to conditions and blind scoring minimize common sources of bias. The use of both parametric and nonparametric analyses and the reporting of effect sizes and Bayesian statistics provide a transparent account of the evidence. The environmental controls and standardized instructions reduce the influence of extraneous variables. Nevertheless, the study also has limitations that should temper interpretation. As noted, the form of colour presentation may not generalize to other modalities such as ambient lighting, screen backgrounds, or coloured markers used in feedback. The sample consisted primarily of university students and may not reflect broader population variability in colour associations. Measurement of ability was based on a single retrospective item, which may be an imperfect proxy for current cognitive skill. The sequence task itself is one of many possible cognitive measures and different tasks could show distinct patterns. Future research should aim to address these limitations and to further clarify the conditions under which colour exerts reliable effects. Several promising directions for future work are evident. First, replication using larger sample sizes would improve sensitivity to small effects and allow for more precise estimation of effect sizes. Second, within-subject designs that counterbalance colour conditions while carefully controlling for order effects could increase power and examine intra-individual variability in colour responsiveness. Third, more ecologically valid manipulations such as ambient room colour, computer-screen backgrounds, or coloured evaluation feedback could assess whether context and modality matter. Fourth, examining potential mediators such as mood, arousal, perceived threat, or confidence could illuminate mechanisms through which colour might influence cognition when effects are observed. Including physiological measures such as heart rate or skin conductance might reveal subtle arousal responses that do not translate into overt performance differences on brief tasks. Fifth, cross-cultural investigations could assess whether learned colour associations vary by cultural context and whether this variation predicts differential effects. Sixth, studies that manipulate the salience and meaning of colour cues directly—for example by pairing colours with explicit evaluative messages—could test whether colour effects are driven more by symbolic learning than by low-level perceptual properties. Finally, researchers should consider preregistration of hypotheses and analysis plans and adopt meta-analytic approaches to synthesize findings across studies to determine the robustness of colour effects in cognition. The theoretical implications of the null results are important for models that posit broad and automatic effects of colour on cognitive functioning. The present data suggest that, at least under the tested conditions, colour cues in the form of coloured test paper do not exert large or robust influences on short sequence task performance. This outcome aligns with a view that colour effects are contextually bound and reliant on particular combinations of stimulus salience, task demands, and learned associations. From a practical perspective, the findings advise caution against assuming that simple manipulations of test-paper colour will meaningfully alter student performance in testing situations. Educators and test designers should therefore consider that any potential benefits or harms of colour may require more specific and powerful manipulations than changing the colour of stationery. In conclusion, the study found no evidence that red or yellow coloured test paper affects performance on a brief sequence completion task, and no evidence that such effects vary by self-reported mathematical ability. The results contribute to a nuanced understanding of the colour-cognition literature by highlighting the need for careful specification of context, task, and presentation modality when investigating colour effects. Ongoing research that adopts rigorous, sufficiently powered designs and that probes mechanisms of effect will be essential to clarify the boundaries and practical implications of colour influences on psychological functioning.
