Introduction

Sleep and anxiety are two constructs that are increasingly recognized as central to mental health across the lifespan. There is a growing consensus in the psychological literature that sleep quality exerts a powerful influence on emotional regulation and vulnerability to psychopathology. Sleep loss and poor sleep quality have been linked to disturbances in mood, cognitive functioning, and physiological stress systems. The interplay between sleep and anxiety is particularly salient given that insomnia and subclinical sleep disturbances often co-occur with anxiety disorders. In student populations, the prevalence of sleep complaints and anxiety symptoms is notably high relative to the general population. University life brings distinctive stressors including academic workload, social adjustment, and irregular schedules that can compromise sleep and increase anxiety. Given the public health and educational implications, understanding the relationship between sleep quality and anxiety in students is an important research priority.

Early empirical work suggested robust associations between sleep loss and decrements in daytime mood and functioning. Classic laboratory and field studies have demonstrated that acute sleep restriction leads to negative mood changes and increased irritability. Pilcher and Huffcutt, among others, reviewed the literature on the effects of sleep deprivation on mood and cognitive performance and argued that reduced sleep quantity can substantially impair affective functioning. Their synthesis emphasized that both acute and chronic sleep disturbances are detrimental to mood regulation. More recent work has extended these findings to focus on sleep quality as distinct from sleep duration. Sleep quality captures subjective aspects of sleep continuity, latency, perceived depth, and restorative value, and has been shown to predict emotional outcomes above and beyond hours slept.

Anxiety and sleep are bi-directionally related in theoretical models, with anxiety increasing the likelihood of sleep disruption and disturbed sleep heightening vulnerability to anxiety. Cognitive models of insomnia highlight how worry and rumination maintain sleep problems, while physiological models emphasize hyperarousal as a shared mechanism in both insomnia and anxiety disorders. Neurobiological research suggests overlapping circuits including the limbic system and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis that modulate both sleep and anxiety. Longitudinal studies have provided evidence that poor sleep predicts increases in anxiety symptoms over time, and conversely, baseline anxiety predicts later sleep problems, supporting the notion of a reciprocal relationship.

Within higher education, survey work and epidemiological studies have documented elevated rates of anxiety symptoms and sleep problems in student samples. National student surveys have reported that sizable proportions of students endorse difficulties with mental health during their university years, and sleep disturbance is commonly cited as a concern. Given the potential for sleep interventions to ameliorate daytime mood and academic functioning, research that clarifies the strength and nature of the sleep–anxiety association in student populations can inform prevention and intervention strategies.

Several studies using self-report measures have examined associations between indices of sleep quality and anxiety severity. Many of these studies report moderate to strong correlations between poor sleep quality and higher anxiety scores. However, the heterogeneity of measures, sample characteristics, and analytic approaches in the literature leaves open questions about effect sizes and potential moderating variables such as gender, academic level, and concurrent depression. Gender differences have been observed in both sleep and anxiety research, with females commonly reporting higher rates of sleep problems and greater anxiety symptomatology in many samples. The mechanisms underlying these gender differences are multifactorial and may include hormonal influences, differential stress exposure, and socialization effects.

In student populations, gender differences in mental health are of particular interest because they may guide targeted support services. Some studies in university samples have found that female students report higher subjective sleep disturbance and higher anxiety than male students, but other investigations have produced mixed results. It remains important to test for gender effects when examining the sleep–anxiety relationship, both to document mean differences and to examine whether the strength of the association differs by gender.

Measurement choices matter when assessing the sleep–anxiety link. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is a widely used self-report instrument that yields a global score reflecting overall sleep quality and multiple component scores indexing different aspects of sleep. The Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) is a commonly used measure of anxiety severity that focuses on somatic and cognitive symptoms. Both measures have strong psychometric support in adult and student samples and are convenient for large-scale survey studies. Using validated measures permits comparability across studies and more confident interpretation of effect sizes.

Correlational designs using these self-report measures are well-suited to explore associations in naturalistic samples, particularly when the intent is to estimate population-level relationships rather than test causal mechanisms in experimental contexts. However, correlational work must be interpreted with caution due to the possibility of confounding variables and the inability to determine directionality. Despite these limitations, establishing robust cross-sectional associations is a necessary step toward designing longitudinal and intervention research.

The present study was designed to examine the relationship between sleep quality and anxiety in a sample of university students. The core aims were threefold: first, to estimate the strength of the association between PSQI global scores and BAI scores; second, to test for gender differences in anxiety; and third, to evaluate whether sleep quality predicts anxiety after controlling for gender. The study focused on university students to attend to a population with reported high prevalence of both sleep problems and anxiety and to inform university-level mental health initiatives.

Previous literature suggests that we should expect a positive association between poorer sleep quality (higher PSQI scores) and higher anxiety (higher BAI scores), and that female students may report higher anxiety than male students. Based on this prior work, we hypothesized that PSQI global scores would be positively correlated with BAI scores. We also hypothesized that female students would report significantly higher anxiety than male students and that PSQI scores would remain a significant predictor of anxiety when gender was included in a regression model.

By exploring these associations using well-validated measures in a modestly sized student sample, the study sought to provide actionable evidence relevant to university mental health services and to guide future longitudinal or experimental research. The findings were intended to inform whether interventions targeting sleep quality could be a plausible avenue for reducing anxiety symptoms among students. In what follows, we present a detailed account of the method, analytic strategy, results, and implications of the study for theory, practice, and future research.

Methods

This section describes a cross-sectional survey study conducted with university students. Participants were recruited from a mid-sized university campus through psychology department participation pools and campus-wide email announcements. Inclusion criteria required participants to be currently enrolled as undergraduate or postgraduate students and to be at least 18 years of age. Exclusion criteria included self-reported diagnosis of a primary sleep disorder other than insomnia (e.g., sleep apnea) if the student indicated active medical treatment for that disorder, because such conditions can create distinct patterns of sleep disturbance. All participants provided informed consent before completing the survey. The study protocol and recruitment materials were approved by the university's institutional review board.

A convenience sample of 200 students completed the survey. Gender and demographic information were collected as part of the intake. Among the 200 participants, 130 self-identified as female and 70 self-identified as male. Age was recorded in years and ranged from 18 to 32 with a mean age of 20.8 years; age was treated as a descriptive variable and was not used as a covariate in primary analyses given the relatively narrow age distribution. Academic status (undergraduate or postgraduate) was recorded and the majority of participants were undergraduates. Participants reported their current year of study, course of study, and self-rated general health in single-item questions.

Sleep quality was assessed using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). The PSQI contains multiple items that are combined to produce a global score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating poorer sleep quality. The PSQI has been widely used in student samples and provides a clinically relevant cutoff score of greater than 5 to indicate poor sleep quality. In this study the PSQI global score was the primary sleep variable included in analyses. Component scores were retained for exploratory analyses but are not the focus of the primary hypotheses.

Anxiety was assessed using the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI). The BAI is a 21-item self-report scale that measures common symptoms of anxiety over the past week and yields a total score. The BAI has a possible range from 0 to 63, with higher scores indicating greater anxiety symptom severity. The BAI emphasizes somatic symptoms and has been validated in student samples. For descriptive purposes, standard severity bands were used to categorize anxiety levels, but continuous scores were used in all inferential analyses.

Data collection was performed online using a secure survey platform. The survey included demographic items, the PSQI, the BAI, and attention check items to ensure data quality. The entire survey took approximately 15 to 20 minutes to complete. Participants received course credit or a small monetary voucher as compensation, in accordance with institutional guidelines. All responses were anonymized for analysis.

Data screening procedures were implemented prior to conducting primary analyses. Cases with incomplete PSQI or BAI scales were omitted from analyses to ensure valid scale scoring; this resulted in a final analytic sample of 200 participants because completion rates were high. The data were inspected for outliers and normality of key variables. PSQI and BAI total scores were examined for skewness and kurtosis and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots was performed. Transformations were considered if substantial deviations from normality were observed, but given acceptable distributional characteristics no transformations were applied. Levene's test of homogeneity of variance was used before conducting group comparisons by gender.

Primary analyses consisted of Pearson correlation to estimate the association between PSQI global scores and BAI total scores. A two-tailed test of significance was used with an alpha level set at .05. Given theoretical interest in gender, an independent-samples t-test compared mean BAI scores between female and male participants. Effect sizes were calculated for all major tests; Cohen's d was computed for the gender comparison and r was reported for correlation coefficients. To evaluate the unique predictive contribution of sleep quality, a hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted. In the first step gender was entered as a categorical predictor (coded 0 = male, 1 = female), and in the second step PSQI global score was added. The outcome variable was BAI total score. Standardized beta coefficients, unstandardized regression coefficients, standard errors, t-values, and significance values were reported. Model R-squared and change in R-squared were also reported.

Assumptions for regression were evaluated, including linearity, independence of errors, homoscedasticity, and absence of multicollinearity. Residual plots and scatterplots of predicted versus observed values were inspected. Variance inflation factors were computed to assess multicollinearity among predictors. In addition to the main analyses, exploratory analyses examined PSQI component scores in relation to BAI scores and tested whether the PSQI–BAI association differed by gender using interaction terms.

All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Results are reported with point estimates, 95% confidence intervals where relevant, and p-values. Given the cross-sectional design, results were interpreted with caution regarding causal inferences. The study adhered to ethical standards and participants were provided with information on campus counseling resources at the conclusion of the survey.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the main variables are provided first. The final analytic sample included 200 university students. The mean age of participants was 20.8 years (SD = 2.6). The sample contained 130 female participants and 70 male participants. The mean PSQI global score for the sample was 8.2 with a standard deviation of 3.4. The PSQI distribution indicated that a majority of participants scored above the conventional cutoff of 5, consistent with a high prevalence of self-reported sleep problems in university samples. The mean BAI total score was 16.5 with a standard deviation of 10.2, reflecting a range of anxiety severity levels from minimal to severe across participants.

The Pearson correlation between PSQI global score and BAI total score was computed to test the primary hypothesis regarding the association between sleep quality and anxiety. There was a strong, positive correlation between PSQI and BAI scores, r(198) = .58, p < .001. This correlation corresponded to an r-squared of approximately .34, indicating that about 34% of the variance in anxiety scores was shared with sleep quality scores. Scatterplot inspection suggested a linear relationship with higher PSQI scores associated with higher BAI scores. The 95% confidence interval for the correlation coefficient was approximately .48 to .66, indicating a reliable positive association in the sample.

Given theoretical interest in gender differences in anxiety, an independent-samples t-test compared BAI scores for female and male participants. Female participants had a mean BAI score of 18.1 (SD = 10.0) while male participants had a mean BAI score of 13.2 (SD = 9.8). The t-test revealed a significant difference, t(198) = 3.31, p = .001, indicating that female students reported higher anxiety than male students. The mean difference of 4.9 points corresponded to a Cohen's d of approximately 0.49, reflecting a medium-sized effect. Levene's test indicated that variances were homogeneous for the two groups.

To determine whether sleep quality predicted anxiety above and beyond gender, a hierarchical multiple regression was conducted with BAI total score as the outcome. In the first step gender was entered and accounted for a modest proportion of variance in BAI scores, R^2 = .052, F(1,198) = 10.95, p = .001. The unstandardized coefficient for gender indicated that, on average, female participants scored 3.8 points higher on the BAI than male participants (b = 3.8, SE = 1.15, p = .001).

In the second step PSQI global score was entered and significantly improved the model fit. The change in R^2 was significant, ΔR^2 = .303, F change(1,197) = 105.2, p < .001. The full model including both gender and PSQI explained R^2 = .355 of the variance in BAI scores, F(2,197) = 54.3, p < .001. The unstandardized coefficient for PSQI was b = 1.80 (SE = .18), indicating that each one-point increase in PSQI global score was associated with an average increase of 1.80 points on the BAI, holding gender constant. The standardized beta for PSQI was β = .56, p < .001, showing a strong standardized effect.

Diagnostic checks of regression assumptions indicated that linearity between predictors and outcome was acceptable and residuals approximated normality. Plots of residuals versus predicted values showed no clear pattern that would suggest heteroscedasticity. Variance inflation factors for the predictors were below 1.5, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. An interaction term between gender and PSQI was tested in an additional regression model to determine whether the strength of the PSQI–BAI association differed by gender. The interaction was not significant, b = .12 (SE = .22), t(196) = .55, p = .58, and did not meaningfully improve model fit, indicating that the relationship between sleep quality and anxiety was similar in male and female participants.

Exploratory analyses examined PSQI component scores in relation to BAI scores. Components capturing subjective sleep quality and sleep disturbances showed the strongest bivariate correlations with BAI scores, while component scores reflecting daytime dysfunction also correlated moderately with anxiety. These patterns align with the notion that subjective perceptions of poor sleep and daytime impairment are closely linked to anxiety symptomatology. Correlations for the PSQI components ranged from small to moderate, with the largest correlations falling in the .30 to .45 range.

Additional descriptive analyses categorized participants according to PSQI cutoff scores. Using the conventional cutoff of greater than 5 to indicate poor sleep quality, 72% of the sample met criteria for poor sleep. Participants with poor sleep had a mean BAI score notably higher than those with PSQI ≤ 5. A between-groups comparison yielded a significant difference, t(198) = 8.9, p < .001, demonstrating that students with clinically meaningful sleep problems reported substantially greater anxiety.

All reported p-values are two-tailed. Confidence intervals were calculated for the primary regression coefficients and correlation; the interval for the PSQI coefficient did not include zero, supporting the inference that sleep quality is a reliable predictor of anxiety in this sample. Taken together, the results support the hypotheses that poorer sleep quality is strongly associated with higher anxiety and that female students report higher anxiety than male students. Despite the cross-sectional nature of the data, the magnitude of the association suggests that sleep quality is closely related to anxiety severity among university students.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between sleep quality and anxiety in a sample of university students using established self-report measures. The principal finding was a strong, positive association between PSQI global scores and BAI total scores, indicating that poorer self-reported sleep quality was associated with greater anxiety symptom severity. This association accounted for a substantial portion of variance in anxiety scores and remained robust when gender was included as a covariate in regression analyses. Female students reported higher mean anxiety than male students, which is consistent with a body of literature documenting gender differences in anxiety prevalence and severity. However, the interaction between gender and sleep quality was not significant, suggesting that the association between sleep quality and anxiety was similar in magnitude for both genders in this sample.

The observed correlation between PSQI and BAI (r = .58) is in line with prior studies that have documented moderate to strong associations between subjective sleep disturbance and anxiety symptoms. The fact that PSQI explained approximately 34% of variance in BAI scores underscores the clinical relevance of sleep in relation to anxiety among students. Although the cross-sectional design precludes inferences about causality, the size of the association indicates that sleep quality is an important correlate of anxiety and a promising target for interventions aimed at reducing anxiety-related distress in higher education contexts.

The gender difference in anxiety identified in the present sample also aligns with existing epidemiological and student-focused research. Female students' higher mean BAI scores suggest that they may be at increased risk for anxiety-related impairment, though the mechanisms underlying gender disparities are complex and multifaceted. Physiological, psychological, and sociocultural factors likely contribute to observed differences, and further research is warranted to unpack the specific pathways that explain why female students report higher anxiety on average.

Importantly, the regression analysis demonstrated that sleep quality predicted anxiety above and beyond gender, and that adding PSQI to a model that already included gender substantially increased explained variance. This finding highlights the potential value of sleep-focused assessment and intervention in university mental health services. Interventions that aim to improve sleep quality—such as cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), sleep hygiene education, and brief behavioral programs—may have downstream benefits for anxiety symptoms. While causal conclusions cannot be drawn from cross-sectional data, the strong association between sleep and anxiety supports the rationale for experimental and longitudinal studies to test whether improving sleep leads to reductions in anxiety among students.

The lack of a significant interaction between gender and PSQI suggests that the relationship between sleep quality and anxiety is comparable for male and female students in this dataset. This result implies that sleep interventions could be beneficial across genders, though targeted strategies may still be warranted given baseline differences in anxiety levels. Future research could examine whether sleep interventions yield similar effect sizes for anxiety reduction in male and female students and whether tailoring interventions to gender-specific needs enhances outcomes.

Exploratory analyses of PSQI component scores indicated that subjective sleep quality, sleep disturbances, and daytime dysfunction were particularly associated with anxiety. This pattern suggests that not all aspects of sleep disturbance are equally related to anxiety, and that perceived sleep quality and functional daytime impairment may be especially salient. Clinically, this points to the utility of assessing specific sleep dimensions when designing interventions; for example, strategies that target sleep continuity and reduce nighttime arousals could be prioritized when anxiety is prominent.

The high prevalence of poor sleep quality in the sample—approximately 72% scoring above the PSQI cutoff—mirrors concerns raised by national surveys documenting widespread sleep problems among students. This prevalence underscores the public health significance of sleep disturbance on campus and supports initiatives to integrate sleep education, screening, and treatment into student health services. Universities may consider proactive screening for sleep problems and anxiety, and providing accessible referrals or brief interventions that can be delivered within campus counseling centers or through digital platforms.

Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation, and longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to test directional hypotheses. Self-report measures introduce the potential for shared method variance and reporting bias; objective measures of sleep such as actigraphy or polysomnography and clinician-administered diagnostic interviews for anxiety would complement self-report data in future work. The convenience sample drawn from a single university may limit generalizability, and replication across diverse institutions and cultural contexts is important. The sample was relatively young and predominantly undergraduate; findings may not generalize to older students or non-student populations.

Another limitation is the absence of measures for related constructs such as depression, substance use, academic stress, and medication use, which might confound the observed association between sleep and anxiety. Future research would benefit from more comprehensive assessment batteries that permit the testing of mediation and moderation models, including whether depressive symptoms account for portions of the sleep–anxiety association or whether stress and lifestyle factors moderate the strength of the relationship.

Despite these limitations, the study makes several contributions. It reinforces and quantifies the strong association between subjective sleep quality and anxiety severity in a university sample using well-validated instruments. The findings add to the accumulating evidence that sleep quality is a significant correlate of anxiety and that sleep-related interventions may represent an important avenue for mental health support in higher education settings. By documenting both the magnitude of the association and gender differences in anxiety, the study provides actionable information for campus mental health practitioners and policymakers.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs that can assess temporal precedence and potential bidirectional effects between sleep and anxiety. Randomized controlled trials of sleep interventions in student populations that include anxiety as an outcome would provide stronger tests of causality and inform clinical practice. Additionally, mechanistic studies exploring physiological markers of hyperarousal, stress reactivity, and cognitive processes such as worry and rumination could elucidate pathways linking sleep disturbance and anxiety. Understanding whether particular sleep components or patterns (e.g., sleep latency, fragmentation, circadian misalignment) are more etiologically important for anxiety could refine intervention targets.

Research should also explore heterogeneity in the sleep–anxiety relationship. Subgroups of students—such as international students, students with caregiving responsibilities, and those with medical comorbidities—may experience different patterns of risk and resilience. Tailored interventions that account for these contextual factors may yield greater benefit than one-size-fits-all approaches. Evaluating digital and stepped-care models for delivering sleep and anxiety interventions could enhance scalability and accessibility on campus.

In sum, the present study highlights a robust association between poorer sleep quality and higher anxiety in university students, together with gender differences in mean anxiety levels. These findings underscore the importance of integrating sleep assessment and evidence-based sleep interventions into campus mental health strategies. While causal claims await longitudinal and experimental confirmation, improving sleep quality remains a promising and potentially impactful component of comprehensive efforts to reduce anxiety and promote well-being among students.
