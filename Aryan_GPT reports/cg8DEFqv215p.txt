Introduction

Social networking sites have become an integral part of modern social life. Many people use these platforms to keep in touch with friends and family. Many also use them to meet new people and to present curated images of themselves. The popularity of different platforms has varied over time and across demographic groups. Research in personality psychology has frequently examined how individual differences predict the ways people use these platforms. Extraversion is one of the traits that has received considerable attention in this area. Extraversion is generally characterized by sociability, high energy, and a preference for engaging with others. People higher in extraversion tend to seek out social stimulation in offline contexts. It therefore seems plausible that extraverted individuals would also seek out social stimulation online. A number of empirical studies have reported positive associations between extraversion and use of particular social networking sites. For example, some studies have reported that extraversion is linked to more frequent posting on Facebook. Other work has linked extraversion to larger online social networks and greater engagement on Twitter. These findings have led to a common expectation in the literature that extraversion should predict increased social media activity more generally. However, important nuances have also been documented. First, not all studies report consistent results. Some investigations have produced null or small effects. Second, associations sometimes differ by the dependent measure used. For example, extraversion may predict the number of contacts a person has but not the time they spend on the site. Third, platform-specific features and affordances can moderate personality-behavior relationships. The functions of Facebook, Twitter, and Instagram are not identical. Each platform affords different modes of communication, presentation, and audience reach. The visual focus of Instagram and its emphasis on image curation could lead to different relationships with personality traits than text-based platforms. Instagram users often focus on photographs and visual narratives. This emphasis on visual content may make self-presentation and aesthetic considerations more salient. It is plausible that people who enjoy social interaction would nonetheless not engage with Instagram in the same way they engage with more conversational platforms. Prior literature has begun to explore Instagram specifically. Some studies have found that extraversion correlates with certain Instagram behaviors, such as posting frequency or number of followers. Other studies have suggested that visual self-presentation and image-centric motivations may be more closely linked to traits such as narcissism or openness to experience than to extraversion. There is therefore not a simple or settled expectation regarding extraversion and Instagram use. In addition to platform differences, measurement differences complicate interpretation. Researchers have measured social media usage in many ways, including self-reported frequency, number of posts, time spent online, and objective server logs. These operational differences can lead to different conclusions. Self-report measures may be influenced by recall biases or social desirability. Objective measures from platform logs are more precise but are not always available. Many studies also vary in their operationalization of extraversion. Some use short screening scales while others use full trait inventories. The use of subscales or single items can affect reliability. Reliability in turn affects the observed associations with behavioral measures. Another important consideration is sample composition. Age, gender, and cultural background influence both personality distributions and patterns of social media use. Younger users tend to be heavier users of Instagram than older users. Gender differences have also been reported for certain types of social media engagement. Studies that fail to account for these demographic influences may report associations that are confounded. There is also temporal change. Social media platforms change their features over time, and user norms evolve. This means that findings from older studies may not generalize to the current environment. Furthermore, psychological theories about extraversion and online behavior have become more nuanced. Researchers now consider that extraversion may influence motivations for online behavior rather than raw usage metrics. For example, extraversion might predict the extent to which a person uses Instagram to connect with friends, but not the total time they spend editing an image. The distinction between motivation and behavior is therefore important. Methodological considerations such as sample size and statistical power also matter. Some prior studies that reported significant associations may have been underpowered or may have capitalized on specific samples. Conversely, null findings can be difficult to interpret when sample sizes are small. Given the mixed evidence, replication across platforms and measures is valuable. The present study was motivated by these considerations. We aimed to examine whether previously documented associations between extraversion and social networking behaviors would replicate on Instagram. Specifically, we selected a set of usage metrics that have been commonly discussed in the literature. These included self-reported frequency of Instagram use, self-reported number of photos posted, the amount of time spent editing photos prior to posting, and the number of followers and people followed. Together, these measures capture different aspects of Instagram engagement, including activity level, content production, and social reach. We also aimed to use a widely used measure of extraversion drawn from a well-established trait inventory. This allowed for comparability with prior work. We preregistered a straightforward hypothesis based on extant literature. We predicted that individuals scoring higher on extraversion would report using Instagram more frequently. We also predicted that higher extraversion would be associated with posting more photos, spending more time editing photos, and having more followers and followees. These predictions were grounded in the general notion that extraverted individuals seek social interaction and would therefore use social platforms more intensively. At the same time, we recognized the potential for divergence given Instagram’s visual focus and the mixed prior evidence. The study was designed to be a direct, focused test of the hypothesized relationships using an online questionnaire. We collected data from a broad sample of adult Instagram users using standardized measures. We planned to assess the reliability of the extraversion measure, inspect the distributions of the usage variables, and conduct correlational and regression analyses to test the hypotheses. We also planned exploratory checks for potential moderators such as age and gender. This approach was intended to provide a clear answer to whether extraversion predicts the selected Instagram behaviors in this sample. The present paper reports the study’s methods and results and discusses their implications. We present the findings in the context of the broader literature on personality and social media use. We consider alternative explanations and suggest directions for future research that could clarify the role of personality in digital social behavior.

Methods

Participants were recruited through online advertisements placed on social media and university mailing lists. A screening question asked respondents whether they had an active Instagram account. Only participants who indicated they used Instagram at least occasionally were allowed to continue. A total of 213 participants completed the survey. Of these, 7 cases were removed for failing attention checks, leaving a final sample of 206. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 64 years. The mean age was 28.7 years with a standard deviation of 8.9 years. The sample was 62% female, 36% male, and 2% non-binary or preferred not to say. Participants were primarily from English-speaking countries. Recruitment materials advertised the study as research on social media habits and personality. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the author’s institutional ethics board prior to data collection. Participants provided informed consent online before proceeding. The survey was delivered via a secure web-based survey platform. The questionnaire took approximately 12 to 18 minutes to complete. Participants were not compensated with monetary payment but were entered into a prize draw for one of five gift vouchers. Measures included a short extraversion subscale drawn from the Big Five Inventory. The extraversion subscale contained eight items that capture sociability, assertiveness, and positive affectivity. Items were rated on a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (disagree strongly) to 5 (agree strongly). Item scores were averaged to create an extraversion score for each participant. Higher scores indicated greater extraversion. The extraversion subscale used the same phrasing as the standard inventory’s extraversion items to maintain comparability with prior work. Cronbach’s alpha was computed to assess internal consistency of the extraversion scale. Instagram usage variables were measured via self-report items. Frequency of Instagram use was assessed with a single item that asked participants how often they used the platform, with response options ranging from 1 (less than once per week) to 7 (multiple times per day). Participants were asked to estimate the number of photos they posted on Instagram in a typical month. For this item participants entered an integer value. Time spent editing photos before posting was assessed with a single item asking participants to estimate how many minutes they typically spent editing a photo prior to posting. Responses were provided in open numeric format. Participants were also asked to report their number of followers and the number of people they followed on Instagram at the time of the survey. These two social network variables were entered as integer counts. To reduce the influence of outliers, values for photos posted, editing time, followers, and following were winsorized at the 99th percentile prior to analysis. Participants also provided demographic information including age, gender, country of residence, and whether their Instagram account was public or private. Account privacy status was included because it could plausibly influence posting behavior and follower counts. Several attention-check items were embedded in the survey to detect random or inattentive responding. Cases that failed two or more attention checks were excluded from analyses. Missing data were minimal; less than 2% of responses were missing on any given item. Listwise deletion was used for analyses requiring complete data because the amount of missingness was small. Prior to hypothesis testing, the distributions of the usage variables were inspected. Frequency of use was approximately normally distributed across the seven-point scale. Photos posted per month, editing time, followers, and following counts were positively skewed. To approximate normality for inferential analyses, those variables were log-transformed after adding a small constant to account for zero values. Both raw and transformed descriptive statistics are reported below. The primary analytic approach consisted of Pearson correlation coefficients between extraversion and the five Instagram measures: frequency of use, photos posted, editing time, followers, and following. Correlation analyses were supplemented by multiple linear regression models predicting each Instagram variable from extraversion while controlling for age, gender, and account privacy status. Age was entered as a continuous covariate. Gender was dummy coded with male as the reference category. Account privacy was coded as public versus private. Model residuals were inspected for normality and homoscedasticity. Robust standard errors were computed when indications of heteroscedasticity were found. An alpha level of .05 was used to determine statistical significance for primary hypotheses. Given the multiple tests conducted, we also report false discovery rate adjusted p-values for the set of five correlations. Effect sizes are reported as Pearson r for correlations and as standardized beta coefficients for regression models. Confidence intervals at the 95% level are reported for primary effect estimates. A post-hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted to estimate the smallest effect size detectable with the achieved sample size at 80% power for two-tailed tests at alpha .05. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.x) using base functions and the sandwich package for robust standard errors. Exploratory analyses included tests for gender moderation via interaction terms in regression models and comparisons of correlations across age subgroups. These exploratory tests were interpreted cautiously and are reported as such.

Results

Participant characteristics and descriptive statistics are presented first. The final sample consisted of 206 participants after exclusions. The mean extraversion score was 3.21 on the five-point scale, with a standard deviation of 0.68. Cronbach’s alpha for the extraversion subscale was .82, indicating acceptable internal consistency. Frequency of Instagram use had a mean of 5.1 on the seven-point scale, corresponding to approximately daily use for the average respondent. The median number of photos posted per month was 4 with a range from 0 to 240 before winsorization. After winsorization and log transformation, the mean log(photos+1) was 1.05 with a standard deviation of 0.92. Median editing time per photo was 5 minutes with a range from 0 to 180 minutes. After log transformation, the mean log(editing minutes+1) was 1.58 with a standard deviation of 0.68. Median follower count prior to winsorization was 482 with a range from 1 to 92,000. Following winsorization at the 99th percentile and log transformation, the mean log(followers+1) was 6.01 with a standard deviation of 1.32. Median following count was 312 before winsorization. After processing, the mean log(following+1) was 5.70 with a standard deviation of 1.11. Account privacy status was reported as public by 64% of participants and private by 36%. The distribution of gender and age described earlier was used as covariates in regression models. The primary correlational analyses tested associations between extraversion and the five Instagram variables. Extraversion and frequency of Instagram use showed a Pearson correlation of r = .07, p = .34. The 95% confidence interval for this correlation ranged from −.07 to .20. Extraversion and log-transformed photos posted per month were correlated at r = .03, p = .65, 95% CI [−.10, .16]. Extraversion and log-transformed editing time had r = −.02, p = .78, 95% CI [−.15, .12]. Extraversion and log-transformed followers produced r = .05, p = .46, 95% CI [−.08, .18]. Finally, extraversion and log-transformed following yielded r = .06, p = .38, 95% CI [−.07, .19]. None of the five correlations reached statistical significance at the .05 level. After applying a false discovery rate correction across the five correlations, all adjusted p-values remained above .30. Effect sizes were small in magnitude across measures. To examine whether demographic variables might obscure relationships, we ran multiple linear regressions predicting each Instagram outcome from extraversion while controlling for age, gender, and account privacy. In the model predicting frequency of use, extraversion’s standardized beta was .06, t(201) = 1.02, p = .31. Age was a significant negative predictor of frequency (beta = −.28, p < .001), indicating that younger participants used Instagram more frequently. Gender and privacy status were not significant predictors of frequency in that model. In the model predicting log-transformed photos posted per month, extraversion’s beta was .02, t(201) = .44, p = .66. Age and privacy were significant predictors in this model; younger participants and those with public accounts posted more photos. Gender was not significant. For the model of log-transformed editing time, extraversion’s beta was −.03, t(201) = −.51, p = .61. None of the covariates accounted for substantial variance in editing time. For the model predicting log-transformed followers, extraversion’s beta was .05, t(201) = 1.08, p = .28. Account privacy was a strong predictor of follower count, with public accounts showing higher numbers of followers on average (beta = .42, p < .001). Age also predicted follower counts with younger users having more followers on average. In the model predicting log-transformed following, extraversion’s beta was .06, t(201) = 1.12, p = .26. The regression models explained significant variance only for follower count and photos posted, largely due to age and privacy status. For example, the model for followers had an adjusted R^2 of .31, while the model for editing time had an adjusted R^2 of .03. Residual diagnostics suggested approximate normality for the transformed outcomes and no severe heteroscedasticity, although the follower and following models showed mild deviations from homoscedasticity which were addressed with robust standard errors. An exploratory moderation analysis tested whether gender moderated the relationship between extraversion and frequency of use. The interaction term between extraversion and gender was not significant (beta = .01, p = .86). Similarly, age did not moderate the extraversion-frequency association in an interaction model (beta = −.01, p = .72). We also explored non-linear associations by including quadratic terms for extraversion in models, but the quadratic terms were non-significant across outcomes. To evaluate statistical power, a post-hoc sensitivity analysis indicated that with N = 206 we had 80% power to detect correlations of approximately r = .19 or larger at alpha .05. Observed correlations were well below this threshold. We therefore cannot rule out very small effects (r < .19) but we can say that no moderate or large associations were present in this sample. Given the null findings in primary analyses, we conducted Bayesian analyses as a complementary approach. Bayes factor computations comparing the null model (no association) to the alternative indicated Bayes factors ranging from 3.1 to 4.8 in favor of the null for the different outcomes. These Bayes factors provide moderate evidence supporting the absence of a meaningful association between extraversion and the Instagram measures in this dataset. Finally, we examined descriptive patterns by extraversion tertiles to offer a nonparametric perspective. Mean frequency, photos posted, editing time, followers, and following did not differ significantly across low, medium, and high extraversion groups in ANOVA tests. In sum, across correlational, regression, and supplementary Bayesian analyses, extraversion was not significantly associated with frequency of Instagram use, number of photos posted, time spent editing photos, or social network size on Instagram in this sample.

Discussion

The present study tested the hypothesis that extraversion would predict greater Instagram engagement across several behavioral indicators. The analyses revealed no significant associations between extraversion and any of the Instagram usage variables measured. These null results were consistent across correlational tests, regression models controlling for demographics and account privacy, non-linear specifications, and exploratory moderation tests. Bayesian analyses afforded moderate support for the null hypothesis in this sample. Taken together, the findings indicate that extraversion, as measured here, was not a meaningful predictor of self-reported Instagram frequency, photo production, photo editing time, or follower/following counts for the participants in this study. These results are notable given the prior literature that has often linked extraversion to greater social media use on platforms such as Facebook and Twitter. Several possible interpretations can account for the divergence between our results and studies that have reported positive associations. First, platform differences likely matter. Instagram is a primarily visual platform where aesthetic presentation and curation can play a central role. Such functions may attract users motivated by visual self-presentation rather than by social interaction per se. If Instagram use is driven more by image-focused motives or by concerns about appearance and visual self-branding, traits other than extraversion — such as narcissism, openness to aesthetics, or conscientiousness related to curation — may be stronger predictors. Thus the lack of association with extraversion may reflect the specific affordances and normative uses of Instagram. Second, the operationalization of usage in previous studies has varied, and simple analogies across platforms can be misleading. For example, a higher number of Facebook friends may reflect different behaviors than a higher number of Instagram followers. Facebook networks often index existing offline relationships, whereas Instagram follower counts can include many distant or asymmetrical ties including celebrities and fandom relationships. Thus, follower counts on Instagram may not be a direct analog to friend counts on other platforms. Third, measurement modality likely contributed to the null findings. This study relied on self-report measures of both personality and Instagram behaviors. Self-report estimates of posting frequency and editing time can be noisy and subject to recall bias. Objective behavioral logs from Instagram would provide more precise measurement and might reveal associations that are attenuated by self-report error. However, obtaining such objective data raises privacy and technical challenges. Fourth, the demographic composition of the sample matters. Our sample had a mean age of 28.7 years and was recruited online through convenience methods. If the relationship between extraversion and Instagram use depends on life stage or cultural context, our sample composition may have attenuated or obscured effects present in other populations. For instance, younger adolescents may show different patterns than the adults sampled here. Fifth, extraversion itself is a multifaceted trait. The subscale used in this study captured general sociability and assertiveness. Prior work suggests that specific extraversion facets, such as gregariousness versus excitement-seeking, may differentially predict online behaviors. It is possible that a more fine-grained measurement of extraversion facets would uncover associations that the overall subscale did not. Sixth, temporal changes in platform norms and features can modulate personality-behavior associations. Instagram introduced features such as Stories, Reels, and algorithmic feeds that have altered how users interact with the platform since earlier studies. Users who once posted infrequently may now engage more through ephemeral formats or through lighter-weight interactions that were not captured by our measures. Seventh, social motives for using Instagram may be more heterogeneous than motives for using other platforms. People may use Instagram for information seeking, inspiration, shopping, hobby expression, or community belonging in ways that do not map strongly onto extraversion. Consequently, variance in Instagram usage may be more related to interest domains than to broad social traits. Eighth, the range and variance of the Instagram usage variables may have constrained the ability to detect associations. Although we had a reasonably sized sample, the variability in some measures was limited for certain subgroups, particularly for editing time which showed lower between-person variability. Measurement ceiling or floor effects can reduce correlations with personality. Ninth, social desirability and self-presentational concerns might differentially influence responses. Extraverted individuals might be more or less inclined to report certain behaviors in self-report formats, introducing measurement bias. We took steps to reduce these biases by ensuring anonymity, but residual effects are possible. The null findings have theoretical implications. They suggest that the assumption that extraversion straightforwardly predicts greater social media engagement across platforms may be oversimplified. Platform affordances and user motives need to be accounted for when theorizing about personality and digital behavior. The results align with a growing view in the literature that personality influences online behavior through specific motivational pathways, and that platform-specific characteristics shape the expression of personality traits. The absence of an effect in this case underscores the importance of avoiding generalizations from one platform to another without empirical verification. From a methodological standpoint, the study highlights the importance of measurement choices. Using multiple, objective indicators of Instagram behavior in future research would strengthen inferences. For example, combining self-report with logged data on posting frequency, time spent in-app, and interaction patterns (likes, comments, direct messages) would reduce measurement error and provide a richer behavioral picture. Additionally, longitudinal designs would clarify the directionality of relations and whether personality predicts changes in use over time or whether social media use influences trait-related outcomes. There are several concrete directions for future research prompted by these results. First, researchers should investigate facet-level extraversion measures to determine whether specific components predict visual-social behaviors. Second, studies could include other relevant traits such as narcissism, openness to experience, and aesthetic sensitivity to examine whether they outperform extraversion as predictors of Instagram usage. Third, research employing objective usage logs or digital trace data would help verify whether null effects are due to measurement limitations. Fourth, experimental or quasi-experimental designs that manipulate social affordances or feedback on Instagram-type interfaces could reveal causal mechanisms linking social motivation and posting behavior. Fifth, cross-cultural research could determine whether cultural norms around self-presentation and image-sharing influence the role of personality in platform use. Sixth, sampling across a wider age range, including adolescents, could test developmental moderation of effects. Seventh, qualitative or mixed-methods studies could explore users’ motivations for editing photos and posting patterns to enrich quantitative models. The study has limitations that should be acknowledged. The sample was convenience-based and not nationally representative. The reliance on self-report for behavior introduces potential recall and social desirability biases. Although we winsorized and transformed skewed variables, these procedures cannot fully substitute for precise objective measures. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. We also focused on a limited set of Instagram behaviors; other outcomes such as commenting frequency, direct messaging, use of Stories versus feed posts, and engagement with visual aesthetics were not assessed. Despite these limitations, the study contributes useful information by providing a clear, preregistered test of hypothesized relationships between extraversion and common Instagram behaviors in a reasonably sized adult sample. The lack of significant associations suggests that researchers and practitioners should be cautious in assuming that extraversion predicts Instagram engagement in the same way it may predict use of other social networking platforms. In applied contexts such as marketing or mental health interventions, these findings suggest that targeting users on the basis of extraversion alone may not be effective for predicting Instagram activity. Instead, a more nuanced approach that considers platform-specific motivations and other individual differences would likely perform better. In conclusion, the present study finds no evidence that extraversion, as measured by a widely used trait subscale, predicts frequency of Instagram use, number of photos posted, time spent editing photos, or follower/following counts in this sample. These null results underscore the importance of considering platform affordances, measurement precision, and motivational specificity when studying personality and social media behavior. Future research employing objective usage data, facet-level personality measures, and diverse samples will be necessary to further clarify the role of extraversion and other traits in shaping Instagram engagement.
