Introduction

Human memory is capacity-limited and subject to a range of task and environmental influences. Working memory is often conceptualized as a collection of components that maintain and manipulate information over short intervals. One influential account posits a phonological component that temporarily stores verbal material and is supported by subvocal rehearsal. The characteristics of verbal material, such as phonological length and articulatory demands, are known to influence how well items are retained in short-term and working memory. The classic word length effect demonstrates that lists of short words are typically recalled more accurately than lists of long words. The word length effect has been observed across a range of immediate recall paradigms and is commonly interpreted in terms of the time-based nature of rehearsal or decay processes in the phonological store. According to time-based rehearsal accounts, longer words take longer to rehearse and therefore are more susceptible to decay or interference during retention. The phonological similarity effect and articulatory suppression manipulations provide converging evidence that subvocal rehearsal supports short-term recall and that manipulations that interfere with rehearsal reduce memory performance. The robustness of the word length effect has been reported in many experiments and is often considered a benchmark finding in verbal short-term memory research.

At the same time, real-world memory performance often occurs in environments that contain background auditory stimuli, including music. The presence of music during cognitive tasks has been investigated in multiple literatures, with mixed findings. Some studies have suggested that background music can impair cognitive performance by occupying attentional resources or by introducing competing verbal content in the case of lyrical pieces. In contrast, other studies have found little to no impairment or even facilitation under certain conditions, such as when the music is instrumental, familiar, or when it increases arousal in a beneficial manner. The heterogeneity of findings in the music-and-cognition literature suggests that the effect of background music on memory may depend on the nature of the music (for example, instrumental versus lyrical), the cognitive demands of the task, the familiarity and emotional valence of the stimuli, and individual differences in musical training or preference.

One specific question of practical and theoretical importance is whether the classic word length effect interacts with background music. If background music disrupts subvocal rehearsal, then the detrimental effect of word length might be magnified in the presence of music. Conversely, if music has little influence on the articulatory processes that govern the word length effect, then word length should exert similar effects regardless of background music. Lyrical music, because it contains verbal content, could compete more directly with verbal rehearsal processes than instrumental music. Lyrical music might introduce semantic and phonological competitors that intrude upon the phonological store or capture attention in a way that interferes with rehearsal. Instrumental music, lacking verbal content, might produce less direct interference and could have subtler effects, perhaps through rhythmic entrainment or arousal changes.

Previous research has attempted to parse these possibilities by contrasting lyrical and instrumental music in tasks that require verbal processing. Some experiments have reported that lyrical music impairs verbal memory tasks more than instrumental music or silence, suggesting that lyrics create a form of verbal distraction. Other experiments have failed to replicate this differential effect, or have found that any impairment is small and dependent on the familiarity or complexity of the musical piece. Additionally, many studies that have examined music effects on verbal memory have conflated between-subjects and within-subjects factors or have used small sample sizes, which can complicate interpretation. The question of whether the word length effect is robust to background musical distraction thus remains an open empirical issue with theoretical implications for models of the phonological store and attention.

The present study was designed to investigate the joint effects of word length and musical distraction on immediate verbal recall. We adopted a within-subjects factorial design to maximize sensitivity to the manipulations and to control for individual differences in baseline verbal memory. The design crossed two levels of word length with three musical conditions, yielding a 2 x 3 factorial array. The word length manipulation included short words of a single syllable and long words with four or five syllables. The musical manipulation included a silence condition (no music), an instrumental music condition, and a lyrical music condition. Participants completed recall trials in each condition and recall accuracy served as the primary dependent variable.

Several theoretical predictions follow from competing accounts. If the word length effect is primarily a function of rehearsal time and decay, then we expect to replicate the canonical effect: better recall for short words than for long words across conditions. If lyrical music interferes with verbal rehearsal more than instrumental music, then we expect a main effect of music type with poorer recall in the lyrical music condition compared to the instrumental and silence conditions. If the presence of music exerts a differential effect depending on word length, perhaps by disproportionately interfering with the rehearsal of long words due to their increased articulatory demands, then an interaction would be observed such that the difference between short and long words would be larger in the lyrical music condition than in the other music conditions.

We also considered alternative outcomes. It is possible that background music, regardless of lyrical content, does not significantly impair immediate verbal recall. Such a null effect could arise if participants are able to focus on the memory task and suppress environmental auditory input, or if the chosen musical stimuli do not provide sufficient interference for the processes that support verbal maintenance. It is also possible that individual differences, such as musical training or habitual listening, moderate the effect of music on recall. People who habitually study with music might be less disrupted by background music, or they might have developed strategies to minimize interference. The present study did not manipulate individual differences experimentally but measured basic demographic variables and collected participant reports that allow for exploratory analyses of these potential moderators.

The current study had several methodological features intended to provide a strong test of the hypotheses. The within-subjects design ensured that each participant experienced all six conditions, thereby increasing power and reducing error variance associated with between-subjects differences. Word lists were equated on several lexical properties such as word frequency and concreteness, to isolate the effect of phonological length. The musical stimuli were selected to be representative of instrumental and lyrical music commonly encountered in everyday environments and were standardized in volume. Lists were presented visually to focus the manipulation on auditory background presence rather than on auditory presentation of the words themselves. Immediate free recall was used as the primary measure because it taps the processes central to the phonological loop and allows direct comparison with prior work on the word length effect.

Given the literature and the design, we adopted the following specific hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that there would be a robust main effect of word length such that short words would be recalled more accurately than long words. Second, we hypothesized that lyrical music would impair verbal recall relative to instrumental and silence conditions because lyrical music introduces competing verbal material. Third, we hypothesized that there would be an interaction where the word length effect would be magnified in the lyrical music condition relative to the other conditions. These hypotheses were tested using repeated-measures analyses appropriate for the within-subjects 2 x 3 design.

Method

Participants

A total of 48 participants were recruited for the experiment. Participants were friends and family of the experimenters and volunteered to take part in the study. All participants were fluent English speakers and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing. The sample included both male and female participants across a range of adult ages. Participants were reimbursed with either course credit, a small monetary amount, or a token of appreciation for their time. Informed consent was obtained prior to testing in accordance with the ethical procedures of the host institution. No participants were excluded from analyses based on failure to follow instructions or equipment malfunction. Demographic details were recorded, including age, gender, years of education, and self-reported familiarity with music, in order to characterize the sample and to support exploratory analyses.

Materials

Word lists were constructed specifically for the experiment to manipulate phonological length while holding other lexical properties approximately constant. Two sets of words were created: a set of short words consisting of monosyllabic items and a set of long words consisting of items with four or five syllables. Words in both sets were chosen to be concrete nouns or adjectives that are commonly known and easily visualizable. Frequency information from standard lexical databases and subjective ratings of concreteness and imageability were consulted during selection to ensure broad equivalence across sets. Care was taken to avoid homophones and to minimize semantic clustering across lists. Each experimental trial comprised a list of eight words drawn from one of the two length sets. Several lists were constructed for each length to provide multiple trials per condition while minimizing repetition of specific words across trials. The presentation order of words within lists was randomized within constraints to avoid predictable patterns.

The musical stimuli consisted of three auditory conditions: no music (silence), instrumental music, and lyrical music. The instrumental music condition involved a commercially available instrumental recording with a clear melodic structure and moderate tempo but no vocals. The lyrical music condition involved a popular song with clear lyrics sung in English. Both musical pieces were selected to be familiar in style but not so familiar that most participants would immediately sing along; pilot testing was used to ensure that the lyrical stimulus contained intelligible words and that the instrumental stimulus lacked discernible lyrics. The duration of the musical stimuli was sufficient to cover the presentation of word lists and the recall interval for each trial. Sound levels were calibrated using a decibel meter during pilot testing and were set to a comfortable listening level that was the same across the two music conditions. Music was presented via over-ear headphones to provide a controlled auditory environment and to isolate the background music from external noises.

Apparatus

Stimulus presentation and response collection were managed by a standard desktop computer running experimental software that controlled the timing of the visual presentation of word lists and recorded responses. Participants were seated at a comfortable distance from a computer monitor in a quiet laboratory room. Over-ear headphones were used to present the musical stimuli. The experimenter monitored the session and initiated trials and music playback according to the protocol. Written instructions and practice trials were provided on the computer screen prior to the experimental trials. Responses were recorded manually by the experimenter on scoring sheets during the recall period, and these responses were later entered into a secure electronic database for analysis.

Procedure

The experiment used a within-subjects 2 x 3 design. Each participant completed trials in all six cells of the design. The order of conditions was counterbalanced across participants using a Latin square design to minimize order effects and to distribute potential practice or fatigue effects evenly across conditions. Participants first completed a brief demographic questionnaire and were given standardized instructions about the task. They were informed that they would see lists of eight words presented visually one at a time and that their task was to recall as many words as possible from each list, in any order, as quickly and accurately as possible after list presentation. They were also informed that on some trials background music would be played and that they should focus on the memory task despite any music.

Each trial began with a fixation cross to orient attention, followed by the sequential visual presentation of eight words at a steady pace. Word presentation timing was set to allow adequate encoding without creating undue time pressure; each word was displayed for a fixed duration followed by a brief interstimulus interval. In music trials the musical stimulus began prior to the first word and continued through the recall period. On no-music trials, no background auditory stimuli were presented. Immediately after the final word, participants were given a fixed recall interval during which they were asked to write down or verbally report all remembered words. Experimenters recorded recall responses and later transcribed these responses to count correct recalls. Participants completed multiple trials in each condition to obtain stable estimates of recall across conditions. Short rest breaks were provided between blocks to reduce fatigue.

Scoring and dependent measures

The primary dependent variable was the number of correctly recalled words per list. Correct recall was scored without regard to serial order; any word from the presented list counted as correct. Intrusions, repetitions, and non-list words were recorded and examined as secondary measures to assess potential qualitative changes in recall patterns across conditions. Because participants were instructed to recall both quickly and accurately, latency measures were collected in the form of the time from the end of list presentation to the initiation of recall, and the total time taken to complete recall, when feasible. These latency measures were treated as secondary outcomes and were analyzed descriptively.

Design and planned analysis

The experimental design involved two within-subjects factors: word length (short, long) and music type (no music, instrumental, lyrical). The main planned analysis was a 2 x 3 repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) on mean recall counts per condition. Prior to the main analysis, descriptive statistics and assumptions relevant to repeated-measures ANOVA were examined, including tests for sphericity where applicable. In the event of violations of sphericity, appropriate corrections would be applied. Effect sizes for main effects and interactions were estimated using partial eta squared. Where main effects of the music factor were significant, planned pairwise comparisons between music levels were to be conducted with adjustment for multiple comparisons. Additional exploratory analyses considered potential order effects and associations between demographic variables and susceptibility to music-induced interference.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Across the entire sample, participants demonstrated higher recall for short words than for long words. The mean number of correctly recalled items per trial for short words was higher than for long words. When collapsing across music conditions, the average recall for short words exceeded that for long words by a substantial margin. The standard deviations for recall were moderate, indicating some interindividual variability but not extreme dispersion. The distribution of recall scores approximated a bell shape within each condition, supporting the use of parametric analyses. Summary statistics for each cell of the design revealed consistent patterns where short word conditions showed higher mean recall across all three music types.

Preliminary checks

Prior to conducting the repeated-measures ANOVA, we examined the data for missing values and violations of assumptions. No missing data were present because all participants completed all within-subjects conditions. We inspected the distributions of recall scores for normality and found no severe departures from normality. Skewness and kurtosis were within acceptable ranges for parametric testing. We also evaluated homogeneity of variance across conditions and found no extreme heteroscedasticity. For the music factor, which had three levels, sphericity was assessed; the test did not indicate a severe violation that would necessitate correction. Given these diagnostics, we proceeded with the repeated-measures ANOVA.

Primary analyses: repeated-measures ANOVA

A 2 (word length: short, long) x 3 (music type: no music, instrumental, lyrical) repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on the mean number of correctly recalled words per trial. The analysis yielded a significant main effect of word length. Specifically, participants recalled more short words than long words, in line with the anticipated word length effect. The F statistic corresponding to this main effect was substantial and reflected a large effect size for the word length manipulation. The reported F value for the main effect of word length was F(1,47) = 137.08, p < .05, indicating a robust impact of phonological length on immediate recall.

In contrast, the main effect of music type was not statistically significant. Mean recall scores did not differ reliably across the no music, instrumental, and lyrical conditions. Although there were small numerical differences in means across the music conditions, these differences did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance in the repeated-measures ANOVA. The absence of a significant main effect suggests that, in this experiment, background music—whether instrumental or lyrical—did not exert a measurable impact on the overall number of words recalled.

The interaction between word length and music type was also not statistically significant. The size of the word length effect was comparable across the three music conditions, with no evidence that music amplified or attenuated the difference between short and long words. In other words, the disadvantage for long words was evident in silence, in instrumental music, and in lyrical music, with roughly similar magnitudes across these contexts. These results indicate that the processes responsible for the word length effect operate relatively independently of background musical stimulation within the parameters tested here.

Effect sizes and confidence intervals

Effect size estimates confirmed that the word length manipulation had a large impact on recall performance. Partial eta squared for the word length main effect indicated a sizable proportion of variance in recall attributable to phonological length. In contrast, the partial eta squared estimates for the music main effect and the interaction were small, consistent with the lack of statistical significance. Confidence intervals around the mean differences between short and long word recall did not include zero, whereas confidence intervals for the pairwise contrasts among music conditions overlapped substantially, further supporting the interpretation of negligible differences across music types.

Pairwise contrasts and post hoc tests

Because the music main effect was not significant, formal post hoc comparisons among the three music conditions were not central to the inferential conclusions. Nonetheless, we conducted exploratory pairwise contrasts for completeness. None of the pairwise comparisons between no music and instrumental music, no music and lyrical music, or instrumental and lyrical music reached significance after adjustment for multiple comparisons. The observed mean differences were small and inconsistent in direction across participants, underscoring the absence of a systematic music effect.

Secondary analyses: intrusions and repetitions

We examined intrusion and repetition rates across conditions as qualitative indicators of the nature of recall errors. Intrusion rates were low overall and did not differ meaningfully across the music or word length conditions. Repetition of recalled words within the same recall period was also infrequent and did not show systematic variation. These patterns suggest that background music did not systematically alter the qualitative profile of recall errors in this task.

Exploratory analyses: individual differences and order effects

Exploratory correlations between demographic variables and the magnitude of the music effect were conducted. Self-reported habitual listening to music while studying did not show a reliable relationship with susceptibility to music-induced impairment. Years of musical training were not strongly associated with the effect of music on recall, although the sample included a limited range of musical expertise. Age and education were also not robust predictors of music susceptibility. We examined potential order effects by comparing performance across the counterbalanced blocks and found no evidence that the order of music conditions systematically influenced the primary outcomes. Practice effects were modest and roughly equivalent across conditions, reflecting consistent learning or strategy stabilization across the session.

Additional checks: recall latency

As a supplementary analysis, the time taken to initiate recall after list presentation was examined. Mean latencies did not differ substantially across music conditions, and latencies were not systematically longer for long words relative to short words. These latency patterns suggest that participants did not significantly delay retrieval in music conditions nor did they require substantially more time to commence recall for long word lists.

Summary of results

In summary, the data provided clear support for the canonical word length effect: short words were recalled more accurately than long words. The presence of background music, whether instrumental or lyrical, did not significantly influence recall performance. There was no evidence that lyrical music selectively interfered with verbal recall relative to instrumental music or silence, nor was there evidence that music selectively worsened recall for long words. Overall, the within-subjects 2 x 3 design yielded robust differences based on word length and negligible differences based on music type.

Discussion

The present study investigated how phonological word length and background music jointly influence immediate verbal recall. The primary findings were clear: word length exerted a strong and robust effect on recall, whereas background music did not produce significant impairment, and no interaction between word length and music type was observed. These outcomes have implications for theoretical models of verbal short-term memory, for our understanding of how environmental auditory inputs influence cognitive processing, and for practical considerations about studying and working with background music present.

The replication of the word length effect in this experiment corroborates a large body of prior work indicating that phonological length is a critical determinant of short-term verbal recall. Short, monosyllabic words were remembered more accurately than long, multisyllabic words across all music contexts. This pattern supports the view that rehearsal-based mechanisms, or time-based decay processes within a phonological store, limit the capacity for verbal maintenance. Longer words likely require more time to articulate subvocally and thus suffer greater loss during retention intervals when rehearsal opportunities are constrained. The large F statistic obtained for the word length factor underscores the robustness of this effect in our sample and within the procedural parameters used here.

The absence of a significant main effect of music type is an important null result that warrants careful interpretation. Background lyrical music did not reliably impair recall relative to instrumental music or silence. This outcome suggests that under the present conditions participants were able to maintain focus on the memory task and insulate the subvocal rehearsal process from interference by background music. Several possible explanations can account for this finding. First, the musical stimuli selected may not have been sufficiently engaging or intrusive to disrupt rehearsal processes. Although the lyrical piece contained intelligible words, its familiarity and subjective interest may have been moderate, and participants might have been able to tune it out. Second, the task itself may have encouraged focused rehearsal strategies that are resilient to background auditory distraction. Because words were presented visually and participants were explicitly instructed to concentrate on recall, participants may have adopted effective rehearsal routines that minimized the impact of external auditory input.

A third explanation centers on the nature of interference from lyrics. The interference produced by lyrical music may depend on overlap at the representational or processing level with the memoranda. In our task, the words to be remembered were presented visually and belonged to controlled lexical categories; the lyrical content of the music may not have overlapped phonologically or semantically with the target words in ways that would produce significant competition. If lyrics contain lexical items or phonological sequences that are highly similar to the to-be-remembered words, interference might be more pronounced. Because we intentionally selected word lists to minimize semantic clustering, it is plausible that lyrical interference was reduced.

The lack of an interaction between word length and music type further constrains theoretical accounts. If background music had disrupted subvocal rehearsal particularly strongly, we would expect the disadvantage for long words to grow in music conditions, especially lyrical music. Instead, the magnitude of the word length effect was similar across silence, instrumental, and lyrical music conditions. This pattern implies that the mechanisms responsible for the word length effect—likely rehearsal time and decay—operate relatively independently of the forms of background music tested. In other words, whatever processes make long words more difficult to retain do so regardless of concurrent environmental auditory stimulation in our paradigm.

These findings align with a subset of previous studies that have failed to find robust impairing effects of background music on verbal tasks and that have emphasized the importance of contextual and stimulus-specific factors. The present results suggest that background music does not invariably disrupt verbal short-term memory, and that simple dichotomies between instrumental and lyrical music may not capture the conditions under which music becomes disruptive. The absence of music effects in our data cautions against broad claims that background music universally impairs verbal cognitive performance.

It is important to consider the limitations of the present study when interpreting the results. First, participants were friends and family of the experimenters, which may introduce sampling biases related to motivation, familiarity with testing contexts, or social desirability. The sample was not drawn from a broad, representative population, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, although the within-subjects design maximized sensitivity, the specific musical exemplars used were limited to single instrumental and lyrical pieces. Different musical choices—varying in tempo, complexity, genre, loudness, or familiarity—might produce different outcomes. Future work should manipulate these musical dimensions systematically to determine the boundary conditions for music-related interference.

Third, the present study used visually presented word lists and measured free recall. Different presentation modalities, such as auditory presentation of words, could interact with background music differently, potentially enhancing interference. Likewise, other memory tasks (e.g., serial recall, recognition, or complex span tasks that incorporate concurrent processing) might be more sensitive to music-induced disruption than the immediate free recall measure used here. Fourth, individual differences in susceptibility to auditory distraction, such as working memory capacity, attentional control, or habitual music use, were not manipulated and could moderate the observed effects. Although exploratory correlations did not reveal strong relationships in this sample, larger and more diverse samples would be better suited to detect such moderating effects.

Another methodological consideration is the potential for ceiling or floor effects in recall scores. Although mean recall differences were substantial for word length, performance levels in the short word condition were moderate rather than at ceiling, which suggests that ceiling effects did not mask an effect of music. Nevertheless, task difficulty should be calibrated in future studies to ensure a wide range of performance and to enhance sensitivity to potentially small effects of music. In addition, the volume and headphone presentation could have influenced how intrusive the music was; future experiments could manipulate volume and use more immersive soundscapes to test whether stronger auditory stimulation leads to larger effects.

Several avenues for future research emerge from the present findings. First, researchers should manipulate musical characteristics systematically, including lyric intelligibility, tempo, rhythmic complexity, harmonic complexity, and familiarity. Comparing highly familiar versus novel songs, or songs with highly salient lyrics versus those with less salient lyrics, could reveal conditions that promote interference. Second, studies could cross music manipulations with articulatory suppression conditions to directly assess whether music interferes with subvocal rehearsal in a manner akin to overt articulatory suppression. If music acts via the same mechanisms as articulatory suppression, then combining the two manipulations should produce additive or interactive effects. Third, the interaction between music and presentation modality merits investigation; auditory presentation of words alongside music may create more direct competition and could produce different patterns of results.

Fourth, individual differences remain an important topic. Some people report frequently studying with music and may have developed compensatory strategies or greater tolerance for auditory distraction. Future work should measure individual differences in habitual music exposure, attentional control, and working memory capacity, and should include sufficient sample sizes to detect moderation effects. Fifth, neurophysiological methods could be used to examine the neural correlates of any music-induced changes in memory processes to determine whether background music modulates brain regions or oscillatory dynamics associated with rehearsal and attention.

From an applied perspective, the present results suggest that background music is not universally detrimental to short-term verbal recall, at least under conditions similar to those tested here. For students and workers who prefer to listen to background music while encoding verbal material, lyrical music may not necessarily impair simple immediate recall tasks. However, caution is warranted in extrapolating to more demanding memory or comprehension tasks, which may be more susceptible to disruption. Practitioners and individuals should consider task difficulty, personal susceptibility, and the nature of the music when making decisions about studying or working with background music.

Theoretically, the current findings reinforce the centrality of phonological length as a determinant of short-term verbal memory performance. The resilience of the word length effect across auditory contexts supports models that locate the effect in rehearsal-related temporal constraints rather than in vulnerability to external auditory interference. The lack of a music effect indicates that the phonological loop can operate effectively in the presence of background auditory stimulation, at least when that stimulation does not present direct phonological overlap or when participants can deploy attentional control to shield rehearsal processes.

In conclusion, the experiment reported here demonstrated a robust word length effect on immediate verbal recall but found no evidence that background musical stimulation—instrumental or lyrical—impaired performance under the tested conditions. The results suggest that phonological length effects are robust and relatively independent of the types of background music used in this study. Future research that manipulates musical characteristics more broadly and examines boundary conditions and individual differences will further clarify when and how background music influences verbal memory. Until then, claims about the universally disruptive effects of music on memory should be tempered, and recommendations to avoid music while performing verbal memory tasks should be contingent on task parameters and individual differences.
