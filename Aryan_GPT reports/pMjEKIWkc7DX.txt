Introduction

Color is a pervasive feature of the visual environment and exerts a broad range of influences on human cognition. Research across perceptual, attentional, and mnemonic domains has documented that chromatic properties of stimuli can alter how information is processed and remembered. Color may affect basic perceptual encoding, influence attentional allocation, and modulate emotional and arousal states, all of which can in turn shape memory performance. The general idea that color is not merely decorative but functionally consequential for cognition has motivated substantial empirical work. One of the more robust themes in the literature is that certain colors appear repeatedly to be associated with facilitative or deleterious effects on memory. For instance, some studies report that warm colors enhance memory under certain conditions while other studies report little effect, highlighting complexity in the color-memory relationship. The interplay of hue, saturation, and luminance makes the study of color effects on cognitive processes inherently multifaceted. Beyond these low-level factors, color may carry semantic or affective associations that interact with task demands and participant characteristics to produce varied outcomes. Because of these multiple pathways, understanding when and how color affects memory requires careful specification of the color properties under study and the cognitive task employed.

Within the broader domain of color effects on memory, the color green has emerged as a color of particular interest. Several research programs have focused on green because it is commonly associated with natural environments and because green is thought to foster processes such as restoration, reduced stress, and improved concentration. The broader restorative effects linked to green landscapes and natural stimuli have been proposed to transfer to cognitive tasks, and memory has been a frequent outcome variable in investigations probing such links. In some experimental comparisons, materials presented on green backgrounds or associated with green contexts have been reported to be better remembered than materials presented on other colored backgrounds. Proposed mechanisms for green's mnemonic advantage include increased attentional focus, reduced cognitive load through stress reduction, and the compatibility hypothesis whereby green aligns with learning contexts that favor concentration and encoding. Other accounts emphasize more perceptual mechanisms, such as optimal contrast between green backgrounds and dark text or images, which might enhance perceptual clarity and thus support encoding.

Despite repeated attention to green in the literature, the findings are not monolithic and important qualifications remain. Some studies have failed to replicate the mnemonic advantages of green, and meta-analytic examinations suggest that effect sizes may be moderated by task type, stimulus complexity, study population, and presentation format. One key issue that has received relatively little systematic attention is the heterogeneity within the color category “green” itself. In everyday contexts and in experimental manipulations, green can vary substantially in both luminance and chromatic coordinates. Dark, saturated greens differ markedly in visual properties from pale, desaturated greens, and these variations could plausibly influence cognitive processing in distinct ways. For example, a dark green that reduces contrast with certain stimulus elements might impair encoding, whereas a bright, pale green that enhances contrast might facilitate it. Conversely, a very vivid green may increase arousal and thereby concentrate cognitive resources in ways that either enhance or interfere with associative encoding, depending on task demands. Thus, specifying whether the beneficial effects of green on memory hinge on particular shades or intensity levels is important for both theoretical clarity and practical recommendations.

Beyond theoretical considerations, practical implications motivate more fine-grained investigations of green shades. Educators and instructional designers often choose colored paper or backgrounds to present learning materials, sometimes guided by the belief that green or other colors can aid learning. If all greens are equally beneficial, such applications need only use any shade of green; if, however, specific shades confer advantages, then guidance should be more precise. Similarly, cognitive theory benefits from understanding whether color effects are hue-specific or gradient-dependent, because this distinction bears on proposed mechanisms. If mnemonic benefits are conferred across a wide range of greens, affective or semantic accounts might be more plausible; if benefits are restricted to particular luminance or chromatic bands, perceptual contrast or attention-driven mechanisms would warrant closer scrutiny.

Although multiple mechanisms could account for shade-dependent effects, three broad theoretical accounts are especially pertinent. The first account emphasizes perceptual contrast and legibility. From this perspective, lighter shades of green that provide greater luminance contrast with dark ink or dark pictorial elements improve encoding by rendering feature details more salient during study. The second account emphasizes arousal and affective modulation. Here, saturated and vivid shades may increase physiological or subjective arousal and thereby influence the depth of processing in complex ways. Moderate arousal could enhance encoding, whereas excessive arousal could narrow attentional scope and impair associative binding. The third account focuses on semantic associations and context congruity. For learners who associate certain shades of green with calm environments or ecological contexts, such shades may prime cognitive states that are conducive to memory consolidation. These accounts are not mutually exclusive, and it is plausible that shade effects emerge from an interaction of perceptual, affective, and semantic processes.

A further methodological consideration relates to task selection. Memory tasks vary in the extent to which they rely on item-specific encoding versus relational or associative encoding. Free recall tasks, which require participants to retrieve studied items without cues, typically rely on both item strength and inter-item associative structure and may be sensitive to encoding quality and distinctive processing. Recognition tasks, by contrast, provide retrieval cues and may be less sensitive to subtle differences in initial perceptual encoding. Consequently, shade-dependent color effects might be more likely to manifest in recall tasks than in recognition tasks, given the higher demands on encoding. Studies that have used free recall or cued recall have sometimes reported larger color-driven effects, suggesting that task choice could moderate outcomes. Therefore, employing a free recall paradigm offers a stringent test of shade influences on memory encoding.

Another methodological issue concerns the nature of the stimuli. Many studies in the color-and-memory domain use text-based materials, such as word lists or passages, while others use pictorial stimuli. Picture encoding is often more robust than word encoding and may exhibit different susceptibility to perceptual variations such as background color or paper shade. Using pictorial stimuli with clearly delineated features allows exploration of whether background shade influences memory for visually rich content. Moreover, cartoon pictures provide an ecologically valid and engaging set of stimuli that are less susceptible to ceiling effects than highly familiar photographic images. Thus, cartoons serve as an appropriate medium to test shade effects in a controlled way.

A final empirical point pertains to sample size and statistical power. Studies that have reported color effects on memory vary widely in sample sizes, and small samples increase the risk of both type I and type II errors. Because shade effects, if they exist, may be subtle, sufficient sample sizes are crucial to detect them reliably. A well-powered between-subjects design comparing multiple shades of green and a neutral control color provides a balanced approach that mitigates some of these concerns. Additionally, careful control of potential confounds—such as the ambient lighting, printing consistency, participant color vision, and random assignment—helps ensure that observed effects can be attributed to the manipulated shade.

Given the theoretical possibilities, methodological considerations, and practical implications, the present study aimed to examine whether shade of green moderates the known relationship between the color green and memory recall. Specifically, the study compared memory performance for cartoon pictures printed on dark green, light green, and white card. A between-subjects design was adopted to prevent carry-over effects and to simulate realistic educational choices where materials are printed on a single shade. Participants studied a set of cartoon pictures, completed a short filler task involving backward counting to prevent rehearsal, and then completed a free recall test in which they attempted to list as many pictures as they could remember. The free recall task was chosen to maximize sensitivity to differences in encoding quality across conditions. Based on the perceptual contrast account and prior suggestions that brighter backgrounds can improve legibility, it was predicted that light green would yield superior recall relative to dark green and white, with dark green expected to be intermediate or inferior due to potentially reduced contrast with pictorial content. Alternatively, if the beneficial effects of green are hue-general and driven by semantic or affective associations, then both shades of green would be expected to outperform the white control. Finally, if shade does not matter and previous effects of green reflect broader categorical factors, no differences between the green shades and white would be observed. The experiment therefore provides an opportunity to adjudicate among competing hypotheses about the nature of green-related mnemonic effects and to offer practical guidance for educational material design.

Methods

Participants were 90 adult volunteers recruited from an undergraduate participant pool and the local community. All participants were between 18 and 35 years of age. Participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological or psychiatric conditions that could influence cognitive performance. Prior to participation, all individuals completed a brief screening questionnaire that included a standard color vision self-report item to ensure no history of color blindness; anyone reporting color vision deficiency was excluded. The final sample consisted of 90 participants who met inclusion criteria and completed the experimental session. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three between-subjects conditions: dark green card, light green card, or white card. Random assignment was achieved using a random number generator and was balanced such that each condition contained 30 participants. The sample size of 30 per group was selected to provide reasonable sensitivity to medium-sized effects while remaining feasible within the available resources.

The stimuli consisted of 30 simple cartoon pictures depicting everyday objects and scenes. The cartoons were drawn in black line with minimal shading and were designed to be visually distinct from one another to facilitate discrete recall scoring. The cartoons included objects such as a bicycle, a lamp, a cat, a tree, and a clock, among others, ensuring a broad range of easily nameable items. Each cartoon was printed centrally on an A5 card using a high-quality laser printer. For the green conditions, identical printer settings were used to produce cards in two specified shades of green. The light green cards were produced using a pale mint hue with moderate saturation and high luminance. The dark green cards were produced using a deep forest hue with higher saturation but lower luminance. The white cards were standard bright white printer paper with no added hue. Care was taken to ensure that the black line art reproduced equivalently across the three background colors by using the same ink density and print settings. A spectrophotometer was used in pilot testing to measure the luminance values of the final printed cards to confirm the intended differences: the light green cards showed a higher luminance reading than the dark green cards, and both green conditions differed from the white cards in expected ways.

The experimental design was a simple one-way between-subjects design with color condition (dark green, light green, white) as the independent variable and the number of correctly recalled pictures as the dependent variable. Participants were tested individually in a quiet laboratory room under consistent overhead lighting. The experimenter used the same script for all participants to standardize instructions. Participants first provided written informed consent and demographic information. The experimenter then explained that they would see a series of pictures and should try to remember them for a later memory test. Participants were not informed about the importance of the card color to prevent demand characteristics.

During the study phase, each participant viewed the 30 cartoon pictures in a fixed randomized order on the card corresponding to their assigned condition. Each picture was presented for 4 seconds on a small stand on a table directly in front of the participant. A 500 ms blank interval separated successive pictures. Participants were instructed to pay close attention and try to remember each picture for a subsequent recall test. The total study phase therefore lasted approximately 2.5 minutes. Immediately following the study phase, participants completed a 60-second backward counting filler task. In this task, participants were asked to count backwards aloud from 500 by threes for 60 seconds. The filler task was designed to prevent verbal rehearsal and to impose a brief retention interval before recall.

After the filler task, participants were given 3 minutes to freely recall and write down as many of the pictures as they could remember. They performed this recall on a blank sheet of white paper, with no prompts or cues provided. The recall period was monitored by the experimenter, and participants were asked to list items in any order. The number of correctly recalled items served as the primary dependent measure. Two independent raters scored each recall sheet for accuracy. A recalled item was coded as correct if the description unambiguously matched one of the target cartoons. Minor semantic variants such as “bike” versus “bicycle” were accepted as correct. Inter-rater reliability was assessed across all recall sheets and achieved a high level of agreement; disagreements were resolved by discussion and consensus scoring.

Prior to the main analyses, data were inspected for outliers and assumption violations. Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were computed for each condition. The distributional properties of the dependent variable were examined using histograms, skewness and kurtosis statistics, and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Homogeneity of variances across the three groups was assessed using Levene’s test. The primary inferential analysis was a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) testing for differences in mean number of recalled pictures across the three color conditions. Following a non-significant ANOVA result, planned pairwise comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference method to guard against inflated type I error. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta-squared for the ANOVA and Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons. Given the potential value of evidence for equivalence in the context of null findings, a supplementary Bayesian analysis was conducted to estimate the relative likelihood of the null versus alternative models. Specifically, a Bayes factor was computed to quantify support for the null hypothesis of no color effect.

A post-hoc sensitivity analysis was also performed to assess the study’s power to detect effects of varying sizes. This analysis considered conventional benchmarks for small, medium, and large effects and estimated the minimal detectable effect size with the present sample size at conventional alpha and power levels. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. The alpha level for significance testing was set at .05. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, the experimental materials and coding procedures were documented, and anonymized data were retained in a secure repository for potential future sharing.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the recall scores are presented first to provide an overview of the data distribution across conditions. Participants in the dark green condition recalled an average of 12.2 pictures with a standard deviation of 3.1. Participants in the light green condition recalled an average of 12.8 pictures with a standard deviation of 3.0. Participants in the white condition recalled an average of 12.5 pictures with a standard deviation of 3.2. Inspection of histograms indicated that recall scores approximated a normal distribution for each group, with no extreme skewness or heavy tails. Skewness values for the three groups were within acceptable ranges, and kurtosis values did not indicate problematic peakedness. Shapiro-Wilk tests conducted for each group yielded p-values greater than .05, suggesting that the null hypothesis of normality could not be rejected. Levene’s test for equality of variances across the three groups returned a non-significant result, indicating that the assumption of homogeneity of variance was reasonably met.

Given the fulfillment of key assumptions, a one-way between-subjects ANOVA was performed to examine the effect of card color on free recall performance. The ANOVA compared mean recall scores across the dark green, light green, and white card conditions. The analysis produced an F statistic of 0.35 with degrees of freedom 2 and 87. This F value corresponded to a p-value of .705, indicating that there was no statistically significant difference in recall performance among the three color conditions. The partial eta-squared for the effect of color was calculated at 0.008, reflecting a very small proportion of variance in recall explained by color condition.

Because the overall ANOVA was non-significant, planned pairwise comparisons were nevertheless conducted using Tukey’s honestly significant difference approach to examine the magnitude and direction of any differences among conditions, although these comparisons were exploratory in light of the omnibus result. The comparison between light green and dark green produced a mean difference of 0.6 with a 95% confidence interval that crossed zero and a non-significant p-value. The comparison between light green and white produced a mean difference of 0.3 with a non-significant p-value, and the comparison between dark green and white produced a mean difference of -0.3 that was also non-significant. Cohen’s d effect sizes for these pairwise contrasts were all small, with values near 0.10 to 0.20, further indicating negligible practical differences between conditions.

Given the null results from the frequentist analyses, a Bayesian ANOVA was computed to provide an estimate of the relative evidence for the null hypothesis versus models including color effects. The Bayes factor comparing the null model to the model including color condition yielded a BF01 of approximately 4.0, indicating that the data were about four times more likely under the null model than under the model including color. This magnitude of Bayes factor is generally interpreted as moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis and provides convergent support for the ANOVA result that color condition did not meaningfully influence recall performance.

To ensure that the null finding was not due to ceiling or floor effects, the distribution of scores was examined relative to the maximum possible score of 30. The highest observed recall score was 21 and the lowest was 4, demonstrating ample room for both improvement and reduction, and indicating that ceiling effects did not mask potential condition differences. Additionally, variance within groups was of similar magnitude, suggesting comparable individual differences across conditions rather than inflated variability in any particular group.

Inter-rater reliability for scoring the recall sheets was high. The two independent raters agreed on 95% of the coded responses before consensus resolution. Cohen’s kappa for the categorical scoring of each potential item across all recall sheets was computed and indicated excellent agreement. Discrepancies that did arise were typically due to ambiguous item descriptions, and these were resolved through discussion in line with the pre-established scoring rules.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted post-hoc to estimate the minimal detectable effect size given the sample size and observed variance. For the present design with 30 participants per group and an overall alpha of .05, the minimal detectable effect size for one-way ANOVA was approximately f = 0.25 to achieve 80% power, which corresponds to a medium effect size. This analysis indicates that the study was well-powered to detect medium or larger effects but had limited sensitivity to detect small effect sizes. Given that the observed effect estimate was substantially smaller than this threshold and the Bayes factor provided moderate evidence for the null, the likelihood of an undetected medium-sized effect is low.

Exploratory analyses examined whether demographic factors such as age within the restricted range or gender were associated with recall performance irrespective of color condition. Correlations between age and recall were small and non-significant. A one-way ANOVA with gender as a factor showed no main effect of gender and no interaction with color condition. These exploratory checks suggest that the lack of color effect was not obscured by simple demographic moderators in the current sample.

Further exploratory analyses considered potential interactions between item characteristics and color condition. For example, recall rates for items with high visual distinctiveness versus low visual distinctiveness were compared across color conditions. These analyses did not reveal systematic interactions, and differences in item-level recall were consistent across color conditions, indicating that shade did not differentially benefit or hinder memory for particular types of cartoon pictures.

Given the absence of any statistically or practically significant differences across conditions, graphical representations of the means and confidence intervals were prepared. Error bars overlapped extensively across the three groups, visually reinforcing the statistical conclusion of no meaningful effect of color condition. The residuals from the ANOVA model were inspected and did not reveal departures from homoscedasticity or non-normality. Collectively, the assumption checks, frequentist analyses, Bayesian analyses, and exploratory checks converged on the same conclusion that in the present study, the shade of green—light or dark—did not influence the number of cartoon pictures participants could freely recall.

Methods-to-results cross-validation steps were also conducted to ensure data integrity. Data entry checks confirmed that responses were transcribed accurately and that random assignment was implemented correctly. No participant was excluded after data collection for reasons beyond the pre-established criteria. All statistical tests reported here were two-tailed. The overall pattern of evidence from multiple analytic angles supports the conclusion that no substantive differences in free recall performance were attributable to the shade of green on which the cartoons were printed.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether the shade of green on which pictorial study materials are printed affects subsequent free recall. Across 90 participants randomly assigned to study cartoon pictures printed on either dark green, light green, or white card, there was no significant effect of card color on the number of pictures recalled. Descriptive statistics indicated highly similar mean recall scores across the three conditions, and a one-way ANOVA yielded a non-significant result. Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence favoring the null hypothesis of no color effect. Inter-rater reliability for scoring was high, assumption checks for the inferential tests were satisfactory, and exploratory analyses did not uncover demographic or item-level moderators. Taken together, the convergent analytic outcomes suggest that within the parameters of this experiment, the specific shade of green did not confer mnemonic advantages or disadvantages for free recall of cartoon pictures.

These null findings must be interpreted in light of both theoretical expectations and methodological constraints. On the one hand, the results challenge accounts that posit straightforward shade-dependent enhancements to encoding via perceptual contrast or arousal modulation in the context of pictorial free recall. If lighter shades of green improved legibility or contrast substantially relative to darker shades, one might have expected better recall in the light green condition. That this was not observed suggests that either the perceptual differences between the chosen shades were insufficient to affect encoding, or that perceptual legibility differences do not translate directly into measurable differences in the types of mnemonic processes tapped by free recall of cartoons. Similarly, theories emphasizing arousal or affective priming associated with green shades would predict differential effects if darker versus lighter shades produce different affective responses. The present data do not support such an account for this task and participant population.

On the other hand, the null results are compatible with interpretations that emphasize hue-general rather than shade-specific effects. If the broader conceptual association with green—such as associations with nature or calmness—plays a role in memory, then the particular perceptual shade within the green category might matter less than the categorical presence of green. Since both green shades were used in the present study and neither produced advantages relative to white, the findings suggest that the mere presence of a green background may not be sufficient to produce robust memory gains in free recall of pictorial materials. In other words, any potential mnemonic benefits of green that have been reported in prior work might be context-dependent or contingent on stimulus type, task parameters, or participant characteristics rather than being broadly generalizable across all greens and tasks.

A consideration of prior literature reveals several plausible reasons for the discrepancy between studies that have reported green advantages and the null outcomes observed here. First, many earlier studies differ in the nature of the memory task, often employing words, educational text passages, or recognition-based measures rather than picture free recall. Text-based materials may interact with background color in ways that pictorial stimuli do not. For instance, background color could affect readability or text-background contrast more directly for words than for black line cartoons, thereby influencing encoding differently across stimulus modalities. Second, the magnitude of color effects may be modest and sensitive to subtle differences in experimental design, such as the duration of study presentations, the length of the retention interval, the complexity or familiarity of stimuli, and participant expectations. Third, individual differences such as baseline attentional capacity, experience with colored materials during study, or implicit color associations might moderate color effects in ways that are not captured by aggregate group analyses. It is therefore plausible that shade effects exist in some contexts but were not present or detectable within the specific parameters of the current experiment.

Methodological limitations of the present study warrant careful consideration as they bear on interpretation and generalizability. One limitation is that the study manipulated only two specific shades of green and one white control; the choice of shades, although informed by pilot luminance measurements, does not exhaust the continuum of possible greens. It remains possible that other hues—such as highly saturated neon greens, yellow-greens, or bluish greens—could have produced different outcomes. Likewise, the study used black line cartoons printed centrally on the cards. Materials with greater color complexity or chromatic interactions might exhibit stronger sensitivity to background shade. Another limitation concerns the between-subjects design, which was chosen to mirror real-world educational choices and to preclude carry-over effects, but which potentially introduces more between-person variance than a within-subjects design. A within-subjects approach that has participants study materials on multiple shades could be more sensitive to subtle effects but would require careful counterbalancing and control for order effects.

The sensitivity and power of the study also merit discussion. The sample size of 90 participants provided reasonable power to detect medium effect sizes but limited sensitivity to detect small effects. The post-hoc sensitivity analysis indicated that the design could reliably detect effects roughly corresponding to a medium-sized effect or larger. The observed effect estimates were much smaller than this threshold, and Bayesian analysis gave moderate evidence for the null, suggesting that any true effect of shade under the tested conditions is likely to be small if it exists. Nevertheless, small effects can be meaningful in applied educational contexts when accumulated across many learning episodes, and so the present findings do not conclusively rule out subtle shade-dependent effects that might have practical relevance in some situations.

The task-specific nature of the effect is also important to emphasize. Free recall of pictorial items relies on distinct processes compared with recognition tasks or learning of linked textual material. Because free recall places higher demands on associative retrieval and reconstruction of studied items, it is possible that color manipulations exert different influences depending on whether retrieval is cued or self-initiated. The null effect in free recall may therefore coexist with conditional effects in recognition or cued recall paradigms. Future work should examine multiple memory outcomes to map the boundary conditions of color and shade influences.

Ambient context and presentation conditions also play a role in visual perception of color and could moderate effects. The current study controlled room lighting and used consistent print settings, but subtle variations in monitor calibration (if stimuli were presented on screens), paper finish, or even individual differences in viewing distance can influence perceived color and contrast. Studies that aim to isolate shade effects may benefit from stringent spectral and luminance calibration and from measuring participant responses to the colors themselves, such as subjective ratings of brightness, saturation, or affective valence, to ensure that manipulations produce the intended perceptual and affective effects.

From a mechanistic standpoint, the null findings invite more nuanced theorizing. If green benefits for memory are truly driven by semantic or affective associations with natural environments, then experimental manipulations that directly prime nature-related concepts or incorporate ecological imagery might yield stronger and more robust effects than mere background color manipulations. Researchers could test this by contrasting green backgrounds with explicit nature imagery or by inducing restorative states via brief exposure to green landscapes before encoding. Alternatively, if perceptual contrast is the key mechanism, then manipulating the contrast between foreground and background systematically across a range of luminance and chromatic values should reveal a monotonic relationship with encoding quality. The present study did not find support for either of these mechanisms within its design, but it points to the need for more mechanistic manipulations to disentangle perceptual, affective, and semantic pathways.

Practical implications for educators and instructional designers are nuanced. The current findings suggest that when creating printed pictorial study materials similar to those used in this experiment, the choice between a light green, dark green, or white card may not substantially influence free recall performance. This indicates that flexibility in choosing card shade may be acceptable in such contexts, and that other design considerations—such as contrast between image and background, readability, and overall aesthetic preferences—may be more consequential. However, given the limited scope of the test materials and task, educators should be cautious about generalizing too broadly to different learner populations, subject matter, or learning goals. In particular, for text-heavy instructional materials or for learners with specific reading difficulties, color choices that maximize text-background contrast may still be recommended.

The present work also highlights fruitful avenues for future research. A valuable next step would be to conduct a high-powered within-subjects study that systematically varies hue, saturation, and luminance across a broad range of green shades and includes multiple memory tasks (free recall, cued recall, recognition) to profile the functional form of any color-memory relationship. Concurrent psychophysiological measurements, such as pupil dilation or electrodermal activity, could help adjudicate whether arousal differences accompany different shades and whether such differences mediate any mnemonic effects. Eye-tracking during study could reveal whether shade influences visual attention to stimulus features, and neuroimaging could explore whether color context modulates neural signatures of encoding. Incorporating subjective measures, including ratings of liking, perceived relaxation, and mood, would allow tests of semantic and affective mediation. Finally, examining diverse populations, such as children, older adults, and individuals with attentional disorders, may reveal differential susceptibility to color effects and thereby clarify boundary conditions.

In summary, the current experiment found no evidence that the shade of green—light versus dark—affects free recall of simple cartoon pictures relative to a white control. These results temper claims of robust shade-specific mnemonic benefits of green within the tested paradigm and emphasize the importance of considering task, stimulus, and methodological context when evaluating color effects on memory. While the null findings do not invalidate all prior reports of green-related advantages, they indicate that shade alone, at least as operationalized here, is not a reliable lever to enhance free recall of pictorial materials. Future research employing a broader array of shades, tasks, and mechanistic probes will be essential to fully chart the landscape of color influences on human memory and to translate such knowledge into effective educational practices.
