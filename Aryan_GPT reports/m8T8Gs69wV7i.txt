Introduction

Stereotypes about gender and leadership have been a persistent focus of research in social and organizational psychology. People often hold generalized beliefs about what leaders look like and what traits leaders should possess. One specific stereotype that has received considerable empirical and theoretical attention is the association between managerial roles and male gender. The phrase “think manager, think male” has been used to capture the idea that managerial or leadership roles are more easily associated with men than with women. This stereotype has been documented in multiple cultures and across a range of measures that assess how people conceptualize leadership and managerial competence. The stereotype can have far-reaching consequences for women's career progression, for organizational decision-making, and for the dynamics of workplace evaluation and promotion. Because stereotypes can influence hiring, promotion, and perceptions of competence, efforts to reduce or change these beliefs are of substantial practical interest. Past work has demonstrated that exposure to counter-stereotypic exemplars can sometimes reduce stereotype endorsement temporarily or in specific contexts. For example, when observers encounter visibly successful women in leadership positions, some studies report reduced automatic associations between leadership and male gender. The mechanism proposed in many of these interventions is simple: counter-stereotypic exemplars provide disconfirming evidence that weakens the associative link between the category “manager” and the category “male.” However, the evidence for the durability and generalizability of such reductions is mixed. Some experiments find small and transient changes in explicit measures but little change in implicit associations. Other work suggests that the format and content of counter-stereotypic information matters, with vivid, individuating stories sometimes producing larger effects than bare statistical counters. Moreover, the degree to which participants are motivated to maintain existing stereotypes or to accommodate new information can moderate the influence of counter-stereotypic evidence. If observers are highly motivated to maintain gender-differentiated beliefs about leadership, then a single exemplar may not shift their mental associations substantially. In addition to the nature of the counter-stereotypic evidence, participant sample characteristics can influence outcomes. For instance, samples drawn from populations with substantial exposure to women in leadership roles might already show attenuated “think manager, think male” biases. The social context, recent media coverage, and local labor market characteristics can also shape the baseline level of stereotype endorsement. Another open question concerns the measurement strategy researchers employ to detect changes in the stereotype. Some investigators rely on explicit questionnaire measures that ask participants directly about traits and suitability for leadership roles. Others use indirect measures, such as reaction time tasks or association tests, to capture associations that participants may not report explicitly. Each approach has advantages and limitations, and changes in explicit attitudes may not mirror changes in implicit associations. The present study was designed to assess whether a brief, vignette-style presentation of evidence about a successful businesswoman could shift people's descriptive judgments about male and female managers. The rationale was straightforward: if people read a credible account of a woman who succeeded as a business leader, then this disconfirming exemplar should reduce the extent to which participants perceive managerial traits as more descriptive of men than of women. The study used an adaptation of a descriptive index that measures how applicable a range of managerial attributes are to a target manager character. The index required participants to rate the extent to which a set of traits described a hypothetical manager who was presented as male or female. By asking all participants to rate both a male and a female character, the design permitted a direct within-subject comparison of perceived managerial fit across target gender. To test the effectiveness of the counter-stereotypic manipulation, the study included a between-subjects manipulation in which participants either read a short article about a successful businesswoman or read an unrelated filler article. Participants were randomly assigned to these two conditions. The primary prediction was that participants who read the article about the successful businesswoman would show a reduced tendency to rate the male manager character as more characteristic of managerial traits than the female manager character. The reduction could manifest as a smaller difference between ratings for male and female manager characters in the article-exposed group compared to the control group. Alternatively, if the article were particularly persuasive, the female character might receive higher managerial ratings than the male character in the exposure condition. The sample for the study was a convenience sample of volunteer participants recruited from a community subject pool. The sample included both men and women and a wide age range. Gender composition and age distribution can influence baseline stereotype endorsement, so these variables were recorded to allow exploratory tests of whether participant gender or age moderated any manipulation effects. The measurement instrument, the descriptive index adaptation, was chosen because it has been used in prior research to assess how people describe typical managers. The adaptation used in the present study included items representing agentic managerial traits such as decisiveness, competence, and assertiveness as well as items representing leadership-relevant qualities such as strategic thinking and team coordination. All items were rated on a seven-point Likert scale from “not at all descriptive” to “highly descriptive,” and scores were averaged to form an overall descriptive managerial fit index for each target. The procedure was designed to reduce demand characteristics by embedding the manipulation in a context of a broader survey and by including control filler material for the control group. The articles were matched roughly for length and readability to avoid confounding article exposure with reading effort or time on task. The female-success-article featured a named woman working in a corporate context who had led a turnaround project and received recognition. Care was taken to make the account plausible and framed as a report rather than an overtly persuasive piece. After reading the article and completing filler items, participants rated the male and female manager vignettes. The order of presentation of the male and female target vignettes was counterbalanced across participants. Responses were collected using an online survey platform that recorded completion times and ensured that all required items were answered. The analysis plan specified a mixed-design analysis of variance with target gender (male vs female) as a within-subject factor and article exposure (article vs control) as a between-subject factor. The primary test of the hypothesis focused on the interaction between target gender and article exposure. Exploratory analyses included tests for moderation by participant gender and age and checks for order effects. Internal consistency statistics were computed for the descriptive index for both target types. In the event that the manipulation failed to influence explicit descriptive ratings, exploratory interpretations could consider whether the sample already favored female managers or whether a single article was insufficient to change entrenched bias. Alternative interpretations might consider the role of social desirability, experimental demand, or differential responses by participant gender. The results of the study contribute to ongoing debates about how malleable gender-leadership associations are in response to brief interventions. If the article successfully reduced the “think manager, think male” association, this would support the utility of counter-stereotypic exemplars in brief interventions. Conversely, if the article had no effect, or if a reverse pattern emerged in which the female manager was rated higher than the male manager, then the findings would prompt questions about the baseline prevalence of the stereotype in this sample and about the boundary conditions for counter-stereotypic influence. The present research was thus structured to provide a clear test of the exemplar effect on descriptive managerial judgments and to explore possible moderators and alternative explanations. Because workplace applications are a major motivation for stereotype-change efforts, the study also considered implications for recruitment, promotion, and leadership development practices. If short informational exposures can shift perceptions of managerial fit, then organizations might be able to design low-cost interventions to reduce gender bias. However, if the effects are weak or inconsistent, then more intensive organizational strategies may be required. Given the complexity of stereotype maintenance overall, the study adopted an explicit-measures approach while acknowledging the limitations that such an approach entails. The following sections describe the methods and results in full detail and conclude with a discussion of theoretical and practical implications, methodological limitations, and directions for future research.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures are described first. Seventy-eight volunteers participated in the study. Recruitment occurred using a university-affiliated subject pool and community advertisements. The sample consisted of 45 women and 33 men whose ages ranged from 18 to 55 years. Participants received either course credit or a small monetary reimbursement for their time. All participants provided informed consent before beginning the online survey. The study protocol received ethical approval from the affiliated institutional review panel. Participants were treated in accordance with ethical guidelines and were debriefed at the end of the session. The study used a mixed experimental design. The between-subjects factor was article condition with two levels: exposure to an article about a successful businesswoman or exposure to a filler control article. The within-subjects factor was target manager gender with two levels: male target and female target. The order of presentation of the male and female manager vignettes was counterbalanced across participants. Random assignment to the article versus control condition ensured comparable group sizes and characteristics. The adaptation of the descriptive index was the primary dependent measure. The descriptive index contained 12 items intended to capture key managerial attributes. Items included words such as competent, decisive, assertive, strategic, trustworthy, and team-oriented. Each item was rated on a seven-point Likert scale anchored from 1 = not at all descriptive to 7 = highly descriptive. For each target manager vignette the 12 items were presented in the same order and were averaged to create an overall descriptive-managerial-fit score for that target. Higher scores indicated greater perceived managerial fit. Internal consistency was calculated for the index scores separately for male-target and female-target ratings. The manipulation materials were prepared to be similar in length and readability. The experimental article described the career trajectory and a recent major success of a named businesswoman and emphasized her leadership accomplishments and recognitions. The article aimed to be realistic and neutral in tone, presented as a journalistic profile rather than a persuasive argument. The control article discussed a local civic history topic that was unrelated to gender or leadership. Both articles were approximately 600 words in length. Presentation of the articles occurred at the beginning of the online session. Participants were instructed to read the article carefully because they would be asked to answer some questions about it. To increase engagement without explicitly drawing attention to the hypothesis, three brief factual multiple-choice questions about the article content were included before the main dependent measures. Responses to these questions served as an attention check. After the article and the filler questions, participants completed several unrelated filler scales that took approximately three to five minutes. These filler scales were intended to reduce the likelihood that participants would infer the link between the article and the manager ratings. Following the filler scales, participants were presented with the manager vignettes. Each vignette described a hypothetical manager in neutral terms and differed only in the gendered name and pronouns used. The male-target vignette used the name “David” and male pronouns, while the female-target vignette used the name “Maria” and female pronouns. Both vignettes described an experienced mid-level manager in a medium-sized firm who led a team and was responsible for project outcomes. The vignettes were intentionally non-evaluative so that the descriptive index items would capture participants’ spontaneous views about whether managerial attributes applied to the person. The order of vignette presentation was balanced across participants so that half of the participants rated the female-target vignette first and half rated the male-target vignette first. Immediately after each vignette, the 12 descriptive index items were presented and participants rated them for that target. Following completion of both vignettes, participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire that asked age, gender, educational level, occupational status, and prior experience working under female managers. The demographic questions also included a check item asking whether participants noticed a connection between the article and the manager vignettes; this was used for exploratory sensitivity analyses. Data screening procedures were applied prior to hypothesis testing. Participants who failed the attention questions about the article or who completed the entire survey in an implausibly short time were flagged. Two participants failed the multiple-choice content questions and were excluded from main analyses. The final analytic sample consisted of 76 participants. Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables. Cronbach’s alpha was computed to assess internal consistency of the descriptive index separately for the male-target ratings and the female-target ratings. The main statistical test was a mixed-model analysis of variance with target gender as a within-subject factor and article exposure as a between-subject factor. Participant gender was included in exploratory analyses as an additional between-subject factor to test possible moderation. Order of presentation was tested as a covariate in supplementary analyses. All tests were two-tailed and alpha was set at .05. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta-squared for ANOVAs and Cohen’s d for paired comparisons. Where appropriate, confidence intervals for mean differences were reported. To supplement the ANOVA, paired t-tests compared male- and female-target descriptive index scores across the full sample and within each article condition. Independent-samples t-tests compared overall descriptive ratings between article exposure groups. Assumptions for parametric tests were examined. Normality of residuals was evaluated using skewness and kurtosis statistics, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated with Levene’s test where appropriate. When assumptions were violated, nonparametric supplementary tests were computed, but the primary inferential conclusions were based on the parametric ANOVA. The analysis plan was pre-specified in broad terms to focus on the target gender by article interaction, with exploratory analyses probing moderation by participant gender and order effects. All data processing and statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. The raw item-level data and analysis scripts were prepared for potential post hoc inspection and replication checks.

Results

The final analytic sample included 76 participants after exclusion of two participants for failing attention checks. Of the 76 participants, 44 identified as female and 32 identified as male. The participant age ranged from 18 to 55 years, with a mean age of 28.9 years and a standard deviation of 8.7 years. The random assignment produced 38 participants in the article-exposure group and 38 participants in the control group. The distribution of participant gender across conditions was approximately balanced with 23 women and 15 men in the article group and 21 women and 17 men in the control group. Descriptive statistics for the descriptive index scores are presented first. For the female-target vignette, the mean descriptive-managerial-fit score across the sample was 4.05 on the seven-point scale with a standard deviation of 0.58. For the male-target vignette, the mean descriptive-managerial-fit score across the sample was 3.68 with a standard deviation of 0.62. The Cronbach’s alpha for the descriptive index items was .90 for the female-target ratings and .88 for the male-target ratings, indicating good internal consistency. Inspection of the item distributions indicated that no single item unduly influenced the total scores and that the composite scores were suitable for parametric analysis. The primary mixed ANOVA testing the within-subject factor of target gender and the between-subject factor of article exposure showed a significant main effect of target gender. Specifically, the female-target manager received higher descriptive-managerial-fit ratings than the male-target manager, F(1,72) = 25.30, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .26. This main effect indicated that, contrary to the classic “think manager, think male” stereotype, participants in this sample perceived the female-target manager as more representative of managerial attributes than the male-target manager. The main effect of article exposure was non-significant, F(1,72) = 0.16, p = .69, partial eta-squared = .002, indicating that the simple exposure to the article did not change overall levels of perceived managerial fit irrespective of target gender. Critically, the target gender by article exposure interaction was not significant, F(1,72) = 0.40, p = .53, partial eta-squared = .006. Thus the article about a successful businesswoman did not differentially affect perceptions of male versus female managers. To illustrate the means by condition, in the article-exposure group the female-target mean was 4.07 (SD = 0.56) and the male-target mean was 3.69 (SD = 0.60). In the control group the female-target mean was 4.03 (SD = 0.60) and the male-target mean was 3.67 (SD = 0.64). These cell means demonstrate a consistent advantage in perceived managerial fit for the female-target across both conditions. Paired-samples t-tests were conducted to examine the within-subject difference between female-target and male-target ratings. Across the full sample, the female-target ratings were significantly higher than male-target ratings, t(75) = 5.03, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.58. When the paired t-tests were conducted separately by article condition, the difference remained significant in the exposure group, t(37) = 3.96, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.64, and in the control group, t(37) = 3.75, p = .001, Cohen’s d = 0.61. Independent-samples t-tests comparing the article and control groups on overall descriptive-managerial-fit (averaging female-target and male-target scores) found no significant difference, t(74) = 0.41, p = .68, indicating that the article did not change general tendencies to rate targets as managerially descriptive. Assumptions for the parametric tests were examined and found to be satisfactory. Skewness and kurtosis for the composite scores were within acceptable ranges, and Levene’s test for equality of variances for between-group comparisons was non-significant. Nonparametric supplementary analyses using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for the paired comparisons yielded consistent conclusions with the parametric tests. Exploratory analyses examined potential moderation by participant gender. A mixed ANOVA with participant gender included as a between-subject factor produced no significant three-way interaction among target gender, article exposure, and participant gender, F(1,70) = 1.12, p = .29. There was also no significant two-way interaction between target gender and participant gender, F(1,70) = 1.78, p = .19. However, a small non-significant tendency emerged in which female participants tended to rate both targets slightly higher on average than male participants, but this difference did not reach statistical significance, F(1,70) = 2.10, p = .15. Order effects were tested by including presentation order of the vignettes as a between-subjects factor in the ANOVA. No main effect of order was observed, F(1,70) = 0.09, p = .76, and no significant interactions with order were found. The manipulation check revealed that the majority of participants correctly answered the multiple-choice questions about the article content, and the two participants who failed were excluded as described. The item asking whether participants noticed a connection between the article and the manager vignettes was endorsed by 14% of participants. Excluding these participants in a sensitivity analysis did not change the pattern of results. Additional exploratory item-level analyses examined whether the advantage for the female-target was concentrated in agentic items (e.g., decisive, assertive) or in communal or leadership items (e.g., team-oriented, trustworthy). Paired comparisons across individual items generally mirrored the composite score pattern, with higher ratings for the female-target on most items. The largest mean differences favored the female-target for items such as competent and strategic, while small differences favoring the male-target appeared on a minority of items like assertive, but these item-level differences were modest and not uniformly significant after correction for multiple comparisons. In summary, the statistical evidence showed a robust main effect of target gender in favor of the female-target manager, no main effect of article exposure, and no interaction between target gender and article exposure. The pre-specified hypothesis that exposure to a counter-stereotypic article would reduce the association of managerial traits with male gender was not supported. The unexpected reverse pattern, whereby the female-target was perceived as more managerially descriptive, was consistent across both article and control groups and did not appear to be moderated by participant gender, age, or presentation order.

Discussion

The present study set out to test whether a brief exposure to a counter-stereotypic exemplar — an article about a successful businesswoman — would reduce the degree to which people associate managerial attributes more strongly with male than with female targets. The core finding did not support the initial hypothesis. Instead of observing the classic “think manager, think male” pattern, participants in this sample rated a female-target manager as more descriptive of managerial attributes than a male-target manager. Moreover, reading an article about a successful businesswoman did not alter this pattern; the female-target advantage was evident both in the article exposure group and in the control group. These findings lead to several important theoretical and practical considerations. First, the results suggest that the traditional stereotype linking management and males may be attenuated, at least in some contemporary samples. The absence of a “think manager, think male” effect in this study is consistent with an evolving cultural landscape in which women’s presence in managerial roles has increased and where narratives of female leadership have become more common in public discourse. If descriptive beliefs about managerial fit have shifted in the broader population, the classic stereotype may be less ubiquitous than historically reported. This possibility aligns with arguments that stereotypes are not static and can change with shifts in social roles and visibility. However, interpreting the observed female-target advantage as a widespread cultural reversal should be approached cautiously. The sample used in the present study was a volunteer convenience sample that might be biased toward participants who are more progressive or who have greater exposure to women in leadership roles. The sample included a higher proportion of women and skewed toward younger adults, demographics that could be associated with more positive views of female leaders. Thus, while the results provide evidence against the automaticity of the “think manager, think male” bias in this sample, they do not necessarily indicate a general reversal of the stereotype across the population. Second, the failure of the article manipulation to affect ratings suggests that brief, individual-level counter-stereotypic information may be insufficient to shift already modest or reversed biases further. The literature on counter-stereotypic exemplar effects indicates heterogeneity in outcomes depending on exemplar vividness, the number of exposures, and the emotional or narrative engagement elicited by the exemplar. The article used in this study was intentionally neutral and was framed as a realistic profile, but it may not have been strong enough to change perceptions in a sample where the baseline bias was absent. Alternatively, ceiling effects might have limited the potential impact of additional exposure to positive information about female leaders; if participants already regarded female managers favorably, the article could not increase ratings substantially further. Third, the pattern observed raises questions about measurement and the distinction between descriptive versus prescriptive stereotypes. The descriptive index used in the study assessed the perceived applicability of managerial traits to labeled targets. It is possible that explicit descriptive attributions have become more egalitarian or even female-favoring in this sample, while prescriptive beliefs about who should be a manager, or automatic implicit associations, may persist in favor of men. The present study did not measure implicit associations or prescriptive norms, so we cannot determine whether divergence exists across these dimensions. Future research should include complementary measures such as implicit association tests and prescriptive norm scales to clarify whether descriptive changes reflect deep shifts in associative structures or are limited to explicit expressions. Fourth, the reverse pattern may reflect social desirability or demand characteristics in an explicit-measures context. Participants may have been motivated to present themselves as egalitarian or pro-female due to social norms, particularly given increased public emphasis on gender equality. Despite the use of filler tasks and neutral framing, the survey context may have signaled the topic and led some participants to adjust their explicit responses. Importantly, only a minority of participants reported noticing a connection between the article and the vignettes, and exclusion of these participants did not alter results, which mitigates but does not fully eliminate concerns about social desirability. Fifth, the observed female advantage invites consideration of local contextual influences. Media representation, organizational norms, and recent experiences interacting with competent female leaders could prime more positive views of women in managerial roles. The timing of data collection, the cultural setting, and the specific community from which participants were drawn might all contribute to a shifted baseline. Researchers should investigate whether similar patterns emerge in other samples, including populations with different occupational distributions, cultural backgrounds, and levels of exposure to women in leadership. From a methodological perspective, the study highlights several areas for improvement in future work. One clear area is the strength and variety of manipulations. A single neutral article may be insufficient to produce measurable shifts, particularly when baseline attitudes are already positive. Future interventions might use multiple exemplars, repeated exposures, multimedia content such as video interviews, or narratives that include more vivid personal detail and emotional engagement. The literature suggests that individuating information and emotional resonance can increase the impact of counter-stereotypic exemplars. Another methodological step would be to include implicit measures alongside explicit descriptive scales. Implicit tests can reveal automatic associations that explicit measures may not capture, and they may show a different pattern of results. If explicit descriptive beliefs are changing but implicit associations remain male-biased, then interventions need to be tailored to address automatic cognitive processes rather than relying solely on explicit persuasion. Longitudinal designs would also be valuable. Short-term exposure may produce transient changes that do not persist. Measuring attitudes at multiple time points after exposure would clarify the durability of any changes and inform the design of sustainable interventions. Field experiments in organizational settings could examine whether exposure to successful female role models affects real-world behaviors such as hiring decisions, promotion recommendations, and performance evaluations. The present study’s findings also have practical implications. If some populations are already less likely to endorse the “think manager, think male” stereotype, organizations may find fertile ground for implementing policies that further reduce gender disparities. However, workplace interventions should be evidence-based and recognize that explicit attitude shifts do not automatically translate into unbiased behavior. Training programs might combine exposure to counter-stereotypic exemplars with structural changes, such as anonymized application screening, objective performance criteria, and transparent promotion processes. Such combined approaches would address both evaluative biases and decision-making procedures that perpetuate inequality. The null effect of the article manipulation suggests that organizations should be cautious about relying solely on brief informational campaigns to change attitudes. More comprehensive initiatives that alter institutional incentives and provide repeated normative support for gender equity are likely to be more effective. Several limitations of the present study deserve emphasis. The convenience sample limits generalizability. The sample size, while adequate for detecting medium effects, may have been insufficient to detect smaller interaction effects between article exposure and target gender. The measure employed assessed explicit descriptive beliefs and therefore may not capture implicit or behavioral biases. The neutral tone of the article might have limited its persuasive power, and the single-article exposure provides limited ecological validity relative to the continuous and varied messages people receive in real-world media environments. Finally, the order in which participants encountered the vignettes, while counterbalanced, could still interact with individual differences in ways not captured by the analyses. Future research can address these limitations in several ways. Larger and more diverse samples would permit tests of cultural and demographic moderators. Including explicit and implicit measures and triangulating with behavioral outcomes would provide a more complete picture of stereotype change. Multifaceted interventions that combine vivid exemplars with structural and normative elements could test whether effects on attitudes translate into changes in behavior. Comparative studies that manipulate the type of counter-stereotypic evidence — for instance comparing statistical information about women’s success rates to vivid personal narratives — would help identify the most effective content for reducing gender-leadership associations. Research into the cognitive mechanisms underlying change, including whether exemplar exposure produces category-level updating or merely activates specific exemplar memories, would also be fruitful. In conclusion, the study contributes to a nuanced understanding of the “think manager, think male” phenomenon by demonstrating that the stereotype is not inevitably present in all samples and that brief exposure to a neutral counter-stereotypic article, in this case, did not change explicit descriptive ratings. The unexpected finding that participants rated a female-target manager as more descriptive of managerial attributes than a male-target manager highlights the importance of examining contemporary population-level beliefs and suggests that the cultural landscape may be shifting. Nevertheless, drawing broad conclusions about cultural change requires caution because of sample limitations and measurement boundaries. The findings underscore that interventions to reduce gender-based leadership stereotypes must be carefully designed, possibly multifaceted, and rigorously evaluated across diverse contexts. Future research testing more potent manipulations, employing implicit measures, and linking attitudinal change to behavioral outcomes will be necessary to determine how best to reduce any residual gender biases in perceptions of managerial fit.
