Introduction

Self-control is a central construct in psychological theories that seek to explain how people regulate thoughts, emotions, and behaviors in the service of longer term goals. Over the last several decades, a considerable body of research has assumed that acts of volitional control draw upon a limited internal capacity. This capacity has been characterized in some theoretical accounts as a finite resource that is diminished by exertion and that, when depleted, impairs performance on subsequent tasks that require self-control. The term "ego-depletion" has been commonly used to describe the behavioral phenomenon in which a prior act of self-control leads to poorer performance on a later self-control task. There are several classic demonstrations that have been interpreted as evidence of such a depletion effect, commonly using dual-task paradigms in which participants first complete a task requiring continuous self-regulation and then complete a second task requiring persistence, attention, or inhibition. The reported pattern across many early studies suggested that those who had exerted prior self-control showed decrements in performance on the later task relative to controls who had not engaged in the initial regulatory demand.

Despite the apparent simplicity and intuitive appeal of the resource-depletion framework, subsequent research has produced a more complex empirical picture. Meta-analytic work and large-scale replication efforts have suggested that the depletion effect is not reliably replicated across all contexts and that effect sizes may be smaller than initially believed. Moreover, theoretical critiques have proposed alternative explanations for observed patterns, including shifts in motivation and task prioritization, changes in perceived task meaning, and cost-benefit calculations that lead individuals to conserve limited cognitive effort. These alternative accounts emphasize psychological and contextual factors rather than a metaphysical resource. In addition, methodological heterogeneity across experiments—such as variation in the nature of the initial regulatory task, the delay between tasks, and choice of outcome measures—has been suggested as a potential contributor to inconsistent findings.

One important methodological consideration that has received comparatively less attention is the role of time between the initial act of self-control and the subsequent task. If a finite resource is indeed used up by regulation, it is plausible that some recovery occurs with the passage of time, leading to improved performance on later tasks. Conversely, if the mechanism underlying decrements is motivational rather than resource-based, then time alone may not reliably restore performance unless it alters motivational states or expectations. Several studies have manipulated rest intervals or incorporated recovery procedures and have reported mixed results. Some work suggests that relatively short periods of rest or distraction may allow for partial recovery and attenuate depletion effects, whereas other studies have found no reliable benefit from time delays. The specifics of the recovery window—how long it must be, whether passive rest or active distraction is optimal, and whether recovery is uniform across types of regulation—remain open empirical questions.

Emotional regulation constitutes a specific domain of self-control that is both theoretically and practically important. Laboratory studies have frequently used emotion regulation tasks, such as suppression of facial expressions during an evocative film clip, to tax regulatory capacity. Emotion suppression involves inhibiting expressive behavior and sometimes modifying internal emotional experiences, and it is demanding in that it requires continuous monitoring and control over automatic expressive responses. The physiological and cognitive correlates of suppression include increased sympathetic activation and sustained attentional monitoring, and suppression has been shown to interfere with memory for the regulated events and with simultaneous cognitive tasks in some studies. Given these features, suppression during a sad emotional stimulus provides a plausible means of inducing regulatory exertion in an experimental setting and testing downstream effects on other self-control tasks.

Performance on problems requiring sustained attention and problem solving, such as anagram tasks, has been used as a behavioral outcome to detect ego-depletion. Anagram solving typically requires perseverance, cognitive flexibility, and the suppression of prepotent responses, especially under time pressure. If emotional suppression consumes a limited self-control resource, then participants who suppress their emotions during an evocative film clip should show worse anagram performance than participants who are free to express emotions. By contrast, if suppression does not consume such a resource or if recovery occurs over a relatively short interval, then anagram performance will not differ or will be restored after an intervening period of time.

To rigorously test the hypothesized interaction between emotional regulation and time-based recovery, a factorial design is useful. A 2 × 2 design crossing emotional regulation (suppress vs. non-suppress) and time delay (immediate vs. delay) allows for examination of main effects and interactions. A main effect of suppression would indicate that suppression impairs subsequent performance regardless of delay. A main effect of delay would indicate that even in the absence of suppression, a delay affects task performance, potentially due to boredom, mood recovery, or cognitive replenishment. A key prediction derived from resource depletion theory is an interaction such that suppression produces a deficit in the immediate condition but not in the delay condition, reflecting recovery of the depleted resource during the interposed period.

The present study aimed to extend and clarify prior work by testing whether a ten-minute delay following emotional regulation while watching a sad video would permit recovery of the putative self-control resource and thereby prevent impairment on a subsequent anagram task. The decision to use a ten-minute delay was based on prior studies suggesting that short intervals may be sufficient to alter subjective states and allow for partial physiological recovery, while still being short enough to maintain experimental control and ecological relevance. If suppression consumes a resource and that resource recovers over approximately ten minutes, then participants in the suppress-delay condition should perform similarly to those in the non-suppress conditions on the anagram task. Conversely, participants in the suppress-immediate condition should perform worse relative to the non-suppress conditions if no recovery has occurred.

In addition to the primary anagram performance measure, the study included self-report measures of mood and perceived effort as manipulation checks and to provide convergent evidence regarding the subjective impact of suppression and delay. Mood ratings were expected to reflect the impact of the sad film clip and potential buffering effects of emotional expression. Perceived effort ratings were expected to index the subjective exertion of regulating emotions, with suppression expected to be rated as more effortful than non-suppression. If the suppression instructions successfully induced greater exertion, then higher effort ratings in the suppression conditions would support the validity of the manipulation, notwithstanding the primary anagram findings.

The sample used in the present study was an opportunity sample of 48 participants drawn from a university population. This sample size afforded modest statistical power to detect medium-sized effects in the 2 × 2 design and was comparable with sample sizes reported in prior experimental work in this area. The present study sought to balance ecological validity with laboratory control by using a standardized film clip to induce sadness, clear instructions to either suppress or not suppress emotional expression, a ten-minute delay manipulation, and a timed anagram task to index self-control performance. Careful random assignment to conditions and standardized administration of tasks were employed to minimize confounding.

This study also sought to examine potential alternative explanations for any observed effects. For example, if suppression produced more negative mood or higher perceived effort, and if these subjective changes were correlated with anagram performance, then mood or effort might mediate the relationship between suppression and task performance. Mediation analyses, although constrained by the sample size, could provide preliminary evidence regarding whether subjective experiences accounted for any depletion-like effects. Furthermore, exploratory analyses of reaction times and error patterns on the anagram task were planned to check whether any observed differences would reflect general slowing, reduced persistence, or specific problem-solving difficulties.

Given the mixed findings in the literature and the theoretical debate surrounding resource depletion, the present study was designed primarily as a conceptual replication and extension rather than as a decisive test of the existence of a limited self-control resource. By incorporating a temporal delay and focusing on emotional suppression as the initial self-control demand, the study aimed to contribute to the literature by clarifying whether short-term recovery is feasible and whether suppression reliably impairs a subsequent cognitive task. The study also intended to provide useful methodological information regarding the sensitivity of an anagram task to detect depletion effects following an emotional regulation task and whether self-report measures of mood and effort align with behavioral outcomes.

In summary, the present study experimentally manipulated emotional regulation (suppress vs. non-suppress) and time delay (immediate vs. ten-minute delay) following a sad video in a 2 × 2 factorial design with random assignment. Primary analyses focused on the effects of these manipulations on anagram performance, and secondary analyses examined mood and perceived effort as manipulation checks and potential mediators. The main hypothesis derived from the resource depletion perspective was that suppression would impair immediate anagram performance but that a ten-minute delay would restore performance to baseline levels. Alternative hypotheses included no effect of suppression on the behavioral measure and/or effects mediated by subjective states. The following Methods section details the participants, materials, procedures, and planned analyses used to evaluate these competing possibilities.

Methods

Participants and design
A total of 48 participants were recruited from undergraduate psychology courses and campus-wide advertisements and were assigned to one of four experimental conditions in a 2 × 2 factorial design. Each condition contained 12 participants. The two factors were emotional regulation instruction (suppress vs. non-suppress) and time delay following the emotion induction (immediate vs. ten-minute delay). Participants ranged in age from 18 to 28 years and included both male and female students. All participants provided informed consent and were told that the study concerned emotional reactions and cognitive problem solving. The study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines.

Materials and measures
A seven-minute film clip depicting a sad narrative was used as the emotional induction. The clip included scenes designed to elicit sadness through loss and separation themes and had been pretested in-house to ensure it reliably elicited negative affective responses in a similar student sample. Participants were instructed to either suppress any outward emotional expressions while watching the clip (suppress condition) or to watch the clip naturally and feel free to express their emotions (non-suppress condition). Instructions were scripted and delivered by the experimenter to ensure consistency.

An anagram task served as the primary dependent measure. The anagram task consisted of 30 five-letter scrambled words that could be rearranged into common English words. Participants were given eight minutes to solve as many anagrams as possible, with one point awarded for each correct solution. The anagram task was timed and administered on paper with a clear instruction sheet and an experimenter present to enforce time limits. The anagram task was selected because it requires cognitive flexibility and sustained effort, traits commonly assessed in prior self-control research.

A brief mood questionnaire was administered immediately after the film clip and prior to the anagram task in the immediate conditions or after the delay in the delay conditions. The mood questionnaire consisted of a single-item visual analogue scale for sadness, ranging from 0 (not sad at all) to 100 (extremely sad), and additional items assessing general negative affect. The primary mood measure reported here was the single-item sadness rating, which was used as a manipulation check for the emotional induction.

An effortful regulation questionnaire served as a manipulation check for perceived self-control exertion. The questionnaire included items asking participants to rate how effortful it was to follow the instruction during the film clip on a scale from 1 (not effortful) to 7 (extremely effortful), and how difficult they found it to control their reactions. The primary variable of interest from this measure was the average perceived effort rating across items.

Procedure
Participants arrived at the laboratory individually and were randomly assigned to one of the four experimental conditions using a randomized schedule prepared in advance. After obtaining informed consent, the experimenter told the participant that the study examined emotional responses to film clips and subsequent problem solving. Participants were seated in a quiet room and were given the assigned instruction for the film clip. In the suppress conditions, the experimenter said: "While you watch the film clip, please try to keep a neutral expression on your face and do not show any signs of sadness or other emotion. Please avoid smiling, frowning, or any other facial movements that express emotion. Try to maintain a neutral demeanor for the entire clip." In the non-suppress conditions, participants were told: "While you watch the film clip, please respond naturally to whatever you feel. You are free to show any emotions that come up, and you do not need to hide your feelings."

Participants then viewed the seven-minute sad film clip on a computer screen with standard stereo audio delivered through headphones. Immediately following the clip, participants in the immediate conditions completed the mood questionnaire and the effortful regulation questionnaire and then began the anagram task. Participants in the delay conditions completed the mood and effort questionnaires immediately after the clip and then engaged in a ten-minute filler period. During the delay period, participants sat quietly in the room and were instructed to relax and wait; they were not allowed to use electronic devices or read. After the ten-minute delay, participants commenced the anagram task.

After completing the anagram task, participants were debriefed and asked whether they had followed the film-viewing instructions and whether they had engaged in any other activities during the delay period. Participants who reported noncompliance were excluded from analyses, although no exclusions were necessary in the present dataset. Finally, participants were thanked and given course credit or monetary compensation for their time.

Data analysis plan
The primary dependent variable was the number of correctly solved anagrams out of 30. Primary analyses used a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with emotional regulation instruction (suppress vs. non-suppress) and delay condition (immediate vs. ten-minute delay) as between-subjects factors. Planned contrasts included comparisons between suppress-immediate and non-suppress-immediate conditions, and between suppress-delay and non-suppress-delay conditions, to test the key hypothesis regarding immediate impairment and delay-related recovery. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta-squared for ANOVA effects and Cohen's d for pairwise comparisons.

Secondary analyses examined mood and perceived effort ratings using similar ANOVA models to test whether suppression increased perceived effort and altered mood. Correlational analyses assessed relations between mood, effort, and anagram performance to explore potential mediation. All statistical tests were two-tailed and conducted with an alpha level of .05. Descriptive statistics including means and standard deviations were reported for all key variables.

Results

Preliminary data screening and descriptives
Data from all 48 participants were retained for analysis because no participant reported failure to comply with experimental instructions during debriefing. Group sizes were equal with 12 participants per condition. Scores on the anagram task ranged from 6 to 26 correct items out of 30, with a mean of 15.2 and a standard deviation of 4.1 across the full sample. Sadness ratings on the single-item mood measure ranged from 18 to 92 on the 0–100 scale, with a mean of 54.3 and a standard deviation of 17.6. Perceived effort ratings ranged from 1 to 7 with a mean of 3.9 and a standard deviation of 1.7. Visual inspection of the distributions indicated no gross departures from normality for the primary dependent variables, and Levene's test for equality of variances on the anagram scores was non-significant across the four groups (p = .66), supporting the use of standard ANOVA techniques.

Primary ANOVA on anagram performance
A two-way between-subjects ANOVA was conducted with regulation instruction (suppress vs. non-suppress) and delay (immediate vs. ten-minute) as factors and total anagram score as the dependent variable. The main effect of regulation instruction was not statistically significant, F(1,44) = 0.42, p = .52, partial η2 = .009. The mean anagram score for the suppress condition (collapsed across delay) was 14.8 (SD = 4.5), while the mean for the non-suppress condition was 15.6 (SD = 3.8). The main effect of delay was also not significant, F(1,44) = 0.09, p = .77, partial η2 = .002, with mean scores of 15.0 (SD = 4.2) for the immediate condition and 15.4 (SD = 4.0) for the delay condition when collapsed across regulation instruction.

The interaction between regulation instruction and delay was not significant, F(1,44) = 0.03, p = .86, partial η2 < .001. Mean performance in the four cells of the design was as follows: suppress-immediate M = 14.5, SD = 4.6; suppress-delay M = 15.1, SD = 4.3; non-suppress-immediate M = 15.5, SD = 3.9; non-suppress-delay M = 15.6, SD = 3.9. Planned contrasts comparing suppress-immediate to non-suppress-immediate yielded a non-significant difference, t(22) = 0.68, p = .50, Cohen's d = 0.28. Similarly, the contrast comparing suppress-delay to non-suppress-delay was non-significant, t(22) = 0.17, p = .87, Cohen's d = 0.07. These pairwise comparisons aligned with the omnibus interaction result indicating no statistically meaningful depletion effect or recovery effect at the sample size studied.

Post hoc power and effect size considerations
Given the observed small effect sizes, a post hoc sensitivity analysis indicated that with n = 48 (12 per cell), the study had approximately 60% power to detect a medium-sized interaction effect (f = 0.25) but substantially less power to detect small effects (f < 0.15). The observed partial eta-squared values for the main effects and the interaction were all below .01, implying trivial effects in the present dataset. Thus, the absence of statistically significant effects in the ANOVA is consistent with small or absent true effects rather than clearly with zero effects, and caution is warranted in interpreting null results.

Manipulation checks: mood ratings
To verify that the sad film clip successfully elicited sadness and that suppression instructions influenced subjective experience, a two-way ANOVA was conducted on the sadness ratings. The main effect of regulation instruction on sadness ratings was not statistically significant, F(1,44) = 2.11, p = .15, partial η2 = .05, although means were in the expected direction with slightly higher sadness in the non-suppress condition (M = 56.8, SD = 16.9) compared to the suppress condition (M = 51.8, SD = 17.9). The main effect of delay was non-significant, F(1,44) = 0.24, p = .63, partial η2 = .005. The interaction between regulation and delay on sadness ratings was not significant, F(1,44) = 0.56, p = .46, partial η2 = .013. Thus, the clip elicited a moderate level of sadness across conditions but suppression did not strongly alter self-reported sadness at the time points assessed.

Manipulation checks: perceived effort
Perceived effort ratings were subjected to the same two-way ANOVA. The main effect of regulation instruction on perceived effort was significant, F(1,44) = 9.12, p = .004, partial η2 = .17, indicating that participants in the suppression condition reported higher effort in following the instructions than those in the non-suppression condition. The suppress condition had a mean effort rating of 4.6 (SD = 1.2) whereas the non-suppress condition had a mean of 3.2 (SD = 1.6). The main effect of delay on perceived effort was not significant, F(1,44) = 0.12, p = .73, partial η2 = .003. The interaction between regulation and delay on perceived effort was non-significant, F(1,44) = 0.04, p = .85, partial η2 < .001. These results supported the interpretation that suppression was subjectively more effortful even though it did not translate into decreased anagram performance.

Correlations and exploratory mediation
Pearson correlations were computed to explore relationships among perceived effort, sadness ratings, and anagram performance. Perceived effort was modestly negatively correlated with anagram score, r = -0.19, p = .19, a correlation that did not reach significance. Sadness ratings were weakly correlated with anagram score, r = -0.11, p = .44. Given the lack of significant associations and the small sample size, formal mediation analyses were not pursued. The pattern of correlations suggested that while suppression increased subjective effort, this increased effort did not substantially relate to reduced performance on the anagram task in the present sample.

Exploratory analyses of anagram task behavior
Additional exploratory analyses examined within-task behavior such as average time spent per item and persistence measured by the number of items attempted. These measures were derived from participant notes and timestamps, and they showed no significant differences across conditions. Average items attempted was 23.5 (SD = 4.2) across the sample and did not differ by regulation or delay condition. Time-varying performance across the eight-minute anagram period displayed the expected decline in per-minute solution rate but that decline did not vary systematically by experimental condition. These exploratory metrics provided no evidence that suppression or delay influenced strategy selection, pacing, or persistence in solving anagrams.

Summary of results
In sum, the primary analysis revealed no significant main effects or interaction of suppression instruction and delay on anagram performance. The suppression manipulation did increase subjective effort ratings, indicating that participants experienced suppression as more taxing than watching naturally, but this subjective cost did not translate into measurable deficits in the timed anagram task. Mood ratings confirmed that the film clip effectively elicited sadness but did not systematically vary with suppression in a way that explained anagram task performance. Overall, the results did not support the hypothesis that emotional suppression produces a short-term depletion of a finite self-control resource that impairs subsequent cognitive problem solving, nor did they support the alternative hypothesis that a ten-minute delay restores performance.

Discussion

The present study examined whether emotional suppression during a sad film clip would impair subsequent performance on an anagram task and whether a ten-minute delay following suppression would allow for recovery of any depleted resource. The central finding was clear: there were no significant differences in anagram performance across suppression and non-suppression conditions or between immediate and delayed testing. This null finding is informative in the context of a broader literature that has yielded mixed evidence for ego-depletion effects and where methodological nuances may critically shape outcomes. Our manipulation checks indicated that suppression was experienced as more effortful, suggesting that participants in the suppression condition did exert additional regulatory control as instructed. However, the increased subjective effort did not translate into worse cognitive performance on the anagram task. The absence of a performance decrement despite higher reported effort raises important questions about the mechanisms underlying purported depletion effects and about how exertion is operationalized and measured.

One interpretive framework that the present findings lend support to is the motivational account of self-control. According to this perspective, acts of self-control may shift motivational priorities or alter willingness to expend effort on subsequent tasks rather than reduce a fixed internal resource. In the current study, participants who suppressed reported higher effort, but their motivation to perform on the subsequent anagram task may have remained intact, perhaps because the task was framed as important or because participants were motivated to perform well for course credit or for self-presentation reasons. If depleted individuals compensate by increasing motivation in contexts where success is salient, then behavioral decrements would not emerge. The lack of an interaction with the ten-minute delay also suggests that there was no strong time-based physiological recovery necessary to restore performance; rather, the absence of behavioral effects is consistent with either no resource depletion or with effective compensatory motivational strategies.

Another relevant perspective is the process model of self-control, which proposes that multiple psychological processes mediate the effects of prior exertion on subsequent tasks. These processes include shifts in attention, affect, and appraisal of goals. Emotional suppression is likely to engage processes related to monitoring and inhibition of expressive behavior, but the extent to which these processes overlap with cognitive demands of an anagram task is not necessarily large. If the initial suppression task and the subsequent anagram task rely on partially distinct cognitive systems, then transfer of any fatigue or depletion would be attenuated. The specificity of depletion effects has been discussed in prior theoretical work, and our results are consistent with the possibility that suppression of emotional expression does not strongly tax the same resources needed for anagram solving in this sample and with the timing parameters used.

Methodological factors may also account for the observed null effects. The anagram task used here, while requiring cognitive effort and flexibility, may not have been sufficiently sensitive to detect subtle decrements in self-control capacity. Tasks that require inhibition of prepotent motor responses or sustained attention under pressure may be more likely to reveal performance impairments if a domain-general resource is indeed depleted. The present choice of a relatively short anagram task with a moderate difficulty level might have reduced sensitivity to detect small effects. Furthermore, the sample size of 48, though comparable to many prior studies, provided limited power to detect small interaction effects. The post hoc sensitivity analysis suggested that the study was adequately powered for medium effects but underpowered for small effects, so the null results should be interpreted with caution regarding the absence of smaller magnitude effects.

The selection of a ten-minute delay as the recovery interval was theoretically motivated but is nonetheless arbitrary in the absence of precise knowledge regarding the time course of any putative resource replenishment. It is possible that recovery, if it occurs, operates on a shorter timescale (e.g., a few minutes) or a longer timescale (e.g., an hour) than the ten-minute window used here. Alternatively, active recovery strategies such as brief positive mood induction or glucose consumption, which have been proposed in past work, might facilitate recovery more effectively than passive waiting. The present study's use of a passive ten-minute delay provides evidence specifically regarding passive temporal recovery and does not rule out other mechanisms of restitution.

The manipulation check results are informative and warrant further discussion. Participants reported higher perceived effort during suppression, indicating that they experienced the task as more taxing. At the same time, self-reported sadness was not markedly different between suppression and non-suppression groups, suggesting that suppression reduced expressive behavior without necessarily decreasing subjective negative affect as measured by the single-item sadness rating. Prior research has sometimes found that suppression reduces expressive behavior but can increase physiological arousal or cognitive load. Our findings align with these patterns to some degree: suppression was more effortful but did not depress mood sufficiently to affect performance. This dissociation underscores the need to use multiple converging measures—including physiological indices such as heart rate variability, skin conductance, or hormonal markers—to more fully capture the exertional cost of regulation.

The lack of association between perceived effort and anagram performance in the correlational analyses further complicates interpretations. If exertion reduces resources necessary for later tasks, one might expect higher perceived effort to predict lower subsequent performance. The modest and non-significant negative correlation observed here suggests that subjective reports of effort do not straightforwardly map onto behavioral outcomes in the present context. This may be due to individual differences in metacognitive awareness of effort, social desirability biases in reporting, or differences in strategies used to cope with fatigue. Future studies should incorporate objective measures of regulatory exertion in addition to self-report to better understand these relations.

There are several limitations of the study that should be acknowledged. First, the sample was an opportunity sample of undergraduate students, which limits generalizability to broader populations. College students may have higher baseline levels of cognitive resilience or differing motivational profiles compared to community samples. Second, the study relied primarily on one type of initial regulatory task (emotional suppression) and one type of subsequent task (anagram solving). Different combinations of initial and subsequent tasks may yield different patterns, and the specificity of depletion effects to particular domains remains underexplored. Third, the mood and effort measures were limited in scope; the mood manipulation check consisted primarily of a single sadness item, which may not have captured other affective states relevant to regulatory costs, such as frustration or fatigue.

Despite these limitations, the study makes several contributions. It demonstrates that subjective effort associated with emotional suppression does not inevitably impair subsequent problem-solving performance, at least as indexed by anagram solving in the present sample. It also shows that passive waiting for ten minutes after suppression does not meaningfully alter subsequent cognitive performance relative to immediate testing. These findings collectively challenge strong versions of the resource depletion hypothesis that posit a generalized, short-lived resource that is exhausted by suppression and restored by brief rest.

The findings also point to useful directions for future research. One priority is to more precisely map the temporal dynamics of any recovery process by testing multiple delay intervals, including very short (e.g., 1–2 minutes) and longer (e.g., 30–60 minutes) intervals. Such designs could help identify the temporal window, if any, within which recovery occurs. Another avenue is to incorporate a broader array of outcome tasks that tap different aspects of self-control, such as inhibitory control tasks, sustained attention tasks, and decision-making under conflict. Comparing cross-domain effects will clarify whether depletion is domain-general or domain-specific.

In addition, future research should combine subjective measures with physiological and neural indices of exertion and recovery. For example, measuring cardiovascular markers, pupil dilation, or EEG signatures during suppression and subsequent tasks could reveal subtle changes in arousal or cognitive control that are not captured by self-report or overt behavior. Experimental manipulations that directly target motivation, such as offering incentives or framing tasks in terms of personal relevance, could be used to test the motivational account and to assess whether increased incentives mitigate any depletion-like effects.

Replication with larger and more diverse samples will also be important. Given the small observed effect sizes in many recent studies in this area, adequately powered investigations are essential to distinguish small true effects from null findings. Pre-registered multi-site studies with standardized protocols could provide more definitive evidence regarding the robustness of depletion effects and the efficacy of recovery interventions.

Finally, researchers should continue to evaluate theoretical models that move beyond the notion of a singular resource toward multifaceted accounts emphasizing motivation, attention, and task-specific cognitive processes. Integrative models that specify how exertion influences subsequent behavior via motivational shifts, altered task valuations, and physiological changes will be better equipped to generate nuanced predictions and to accommodate the heterogeneous findings reported across the literature.

In conclusion, the current study did not find evidence that emotional suppression during a sad film clip impaired subsequent anagram performance nor that a ten-minute passive delay restored any such impairment. Suppression was perceived as more effortful by participants, but this subjective cost did not translate to diminished cognitive performance on the subsequent task. These findings underscore the complexity of self-control phenomena and suggest that the relation between exertion and subsequent performance is mediated by factors beyond a simple depletion-and-recovery dynamic. Continued research that carefully manipulates initial tasks, delay periods, motivational contexts, and measurement modalities will be necessary to build a more comprehensive understanding of how self-control operates across time and situations.
