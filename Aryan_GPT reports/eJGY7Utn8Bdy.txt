Introduction

Understanding how personality relates to behavior in online environments is an enduring goal of social and personality psychology, and recent scholarship has focused increasingly on social media as a context in which individual differences are expressed and amplified. Personality traits are thought to organize preferences for social interaction, self-presentation, and information-seeking, and these tendencies plausibly map onto patterns of social media use. Extraversion in particular has been theorized to predict more visible, social, and approach-oriented behaviors, given its association with sociability, assertiveness, and a tendency to seek out stimulating social environments. Consequently, many investigators have hypothesized and tested whether extraverts differ from introverts in how they use networking platforms and in the kinds of social attention they attract. Empirical work has reported relationships between extraversion and several social media indicators; for example, some studies have found that extraverts tend to report larger friendship networks online and to cultivate more expansive social ties on platforms designed for broad connection. Other researchers have observed links between extraversion and the types of self-presentation choices people make, such as the greater likelihood of selecting attention-grabbing or experimental profile images. Taken together, these strands of research have fostered a relatively straightforward expectation that extraverts will be more active and socially connected on social platforms than introverts. Despite this expectation, a closer reading of prior work reveals important variations in outcomes across platforms, measures, and operational definitions of activity and connection. Social networking sites differ in norms, affordances, visual emphasis, and the kinds of social reciprocity they support, and therefore a relationship observed on one platform may not generalize to another. Moreover, measures of activity vary from simple counts of friends or followers to more nuanced behavioral indicators such as editing time, post frequency, content type, and engagement quality. Thus, any generalized claim that extraverts are uniformly more active across all social media contexts should be treated with caution. Instagram, as a visually oriented platform centered on images and brief captions, presents a distinct environment compared with text-heavy platforms or general-purpose networks. On Instagram, users curate visual content, often invest time in image editing and caption crafting, and participate in network dynamics that include following, being followed, and the visibility of posts as determined by both follower networks and algorithmic feeds. These features suggest multiple ways in which extraversion might manifest on Instagram: extraverts might post more pictures to seek social stimulation, spend more time editing images to optimize social reception, or accrue greater numbers of followers by virtue of broader social tendencies. Conversely, it is also possible that the relationship is more complex, and that other factors such as privacy concerns, platform-specific norms, or the social value of visual content moderate or supplant simple personality effects. Distinguishing between these possibilities requires direct empirical examination that operationalizes both personality and Instagram-specific behaviors with sensitivity to measurement and analytic considerations. A number of earlier studies serve as a useful background for such an investigation. For instance, research documenting that extraverts have more friends on certain social platforms suggests a link between extraversion and network size, while other work highlighting differences in profile-picture style indicates that extraversion can be related to the ways individuals craft their visual self-presentation. These findings jointly contribute to a plausible argument for expecting extraverts to show higher counts of social connections and greater emphasis on edited or attention-grabbing content. Nevertheless, the prior literature also reveals inconsistencies: some samples show no association between certain personality traits and online activity or show associations only for specific facets of extraversion such as sociability versus assertiveness. Theoretical accounts help explain why different patterns might be observed across contexts. From a person-environment fit perspective, extraverts are attracted to settings that provide high levels of social reinforcement, which could make public, interactive platforms attractive; from an impression-management perspective, extraversion might predict behaviors geared toward maximizing social approval; and from a social compensation perspective, quieter individuals might use online venues to expand their social presence in ways not predicted by offline personality alone. Each of these frameworks suggests distinct empirical signatures and potentially divergent relationships depending on the platform and the measures used. Beyond theoretical diversity, methodological factors can contribute substantially to variation in findings. Self-report measures of both personality and social media behavior are vulnerable to bias and error, objective behavioral logs are often difficult to obtain, and cross-sectional designs limit causal inference. Sampling differences in age, gender, culture, and social media experience further challenge generalization. The operationalization of Instagram activity itself is nontrivial: counting followers conflates popularity with strategic following and can be influenced by offline status or influencer activity, while measures like editing time are subjective and may not reflect careful curation in all users. In light of these complexities, it is important to evaluate the specific question of whether extraversion predicts Instagram activity using a focused empirical design that acknowledges and attempts to mitigate some of these methodological concerns. This study was designed to investigate the relationship between extraversion and a set of Instagram-relevant behaviors that are commonly theorized to be linked to sociability and self-presentation. We selected four outcome variables that capture different dimensions of Instagram engagement: number of followers (as an index of social reach or popularity), number of people followed (as a measure of network breadth), frequency of photo posting (as a direct behavioral indicator of visible activity), and amount of time spent editing photos (as an index of investment in curated self-presentation). These variables span both quantitative network indicators and effort-based self-presentation behaviors, providing a more comprehensive view of Instagram engagement than any single metric. Participants completed a validated extraversion measure and self-reported the Instagram variables in an online survey format, allowing for efficient data collection and consistent measurement across respondents. Given prior theoretical and empirical work suggesting that extraversion should be positively associated with socially oriented behaviors, we formulated directional hypotheses. Specifically, we hypothesized that higher extraversion scores would be associated with greater numbers of followers, following more people, posting photos more frequently, and spending more time editing photos before posting. We also recognized that age and gender might confound or moderate these relationships, since social media behaviors often vary systematically across demographic groups. Consequently, we planned secondary analyses to control for age and gender and to explore whether the hypothesized relationships persisted after accounting for these covariates. In addition to testing the main hypotheses, the study also sought to explore robustness across analytic decisions, including nonparametric tests when assumptions of normality were violated and sensitivity analyses for influential observations. These analytic choices were intended to provide a thorough examination of the hypothesized associations and to offer greater confidence in inferences drawn from the data. The study thus contributes to the literature in three ways: by focusing specifically on Instagram as a distinct social media context, by combining multiple indicators of platform engagement that tap both social reach and self-presentation investment, and by preregistering (conceptually) a plan for robust analytic checks that address common methodological issues. Ultimately, the research aimed to determine whether the reasonably intuitive assumption—that extraverts are more active and socially connected on Instagram—holds up under empirical scrutiny in a sample of typical social media users. The next sections detail the methods, present the results of the planned analyses as well as exploratory checks, and discuss the implications for theory and future work in this area.

Methods

Participants and recruitment were designed to capture a broad sample of active Instagram users. A convenience sample of adults was recruited via university participant pools, social media advertisements, and email lists to participate in an online survey about social media habits and personality. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old and to report having an active Instagram account that they had used within the past month. A total of 235 individuals began the survey; after excluding cases with incomplete responses on the key measures and implausible or inconsistent answers, the analytic sample comprised 218 participants. The sample included 130 women and 88 men, with a mean age of 27.4 years (SD = 8.6; range 18–56). Participants reported diverse educational backgrounds with the majority having at least some college experience. The sample was heterogeneous in self-reported Instagram use frequency and follower counts, which supported the planned correlation analyses across a range of behaviors. All participants provided informed consent electronically before proceeding to the survey, and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by an institutional ethics committee. Measures in the survey were selected to balance brevity with psychometric reliability and to capture Instagram-specific behaviors relevant to our hypotheses. Extraversion was measured using the extraversion subscale of a widely used five-factor personality inventory. The subscale consisted of eight items that assess dimensions such as sociability, activity level, assertiveness, and excitement-seeking. Items were presented on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ("Strongly disagree") to 5 ("Strongly agree"), and responses were averaged to produce an overall extraversion score for each participant. Prior work has shown that this subscale exhibits acceptable internal consistency in community and student samples, and we computed Cronbach's alpha to evaluate reliability in the present sample. Instagram behavior variables were collected via direct self-report questions. Participants were asked to report the total number of followers on their Instagram account (numerical entry), the total number of accounts they were following (numerical entry), and the typical frequency with which they posted photos to Instagram, operationalized as the average number of photos posted per week over the past month. To capture self-presentation investment, participants were asked to estimate how much time they typically spent editing a photo before posting, with response options in minutes (open numerical entry) and with an additional categorical question offering ranges (e.g., "less than 1 minute," "1–5 minutes," "6–15 minutes," "more than 15 minutes") to triangulate responses. We also collected control variables including age, gender, and self-rated Instagram usage intensity (a single-item measure on a 1–5 scale asking how actively they use Instagram relative to others). Before the main analyses, data cleaning and preparation steps were taken. Responses with clearly unrealistic follower counts (e.g., exceeding ten million without a verified account) were examined and verified where possible; none were excluded on that basis in the final analytic sample. Missing data were minimal (less than 3% across variables) and were handled by pairwise deletion in correlational analyses so that each analysis used the maximum available data for the variables involved. Distributional properties of the primary variables were assessed visually and via skewness and kurtosis statistics. Followers and following counts exhibited positive skew and kurtosis consistent with typical social network distributions; in response, a log10 transformation (with a constant added to avoid log(0)) was applied to follower and following counts for parametric analyses to better approximate normality. Editing-time responses were also right-skewed; both raw minutes and a log-transformed editing-time variable were computed and used as appropriate in sensitivity checks. The planned analytic strategy consisted primarily of bivariate correlations between extraversion and each Instagram behavior variable, supplemented by partial correlations controlling for age and gender. Pearson product-moment correlations were the primary test when distributional assumptions were adequately met after transformation; Spearman rank correlations were used in parallel where concerns about normality or outliers persisted. For each correlation, effect sizes and two-tailed p-values were reported, and 95% confidence intervals were estimated for the correlation coefficients. In addition, a multiple linear regression was conducted predicting each Instagram outcome from extraversion, age, gender, and Instagram usage intensity to evaluate the incremental predictive contribution of extraversion. Sensitivity analyses included repeating the correlations with nontransformed variables using Spearman's rho, checking for influential observations using Cook's distance in regression models, and conducting a median-split exploratory comparison between participants classified as "high extraversion" and "low extraversion" to facilitate interpretability for readers accustomed to mean comparisons. Finally, a post-hoc power analysis was performed to estimate the smallest effect size detectable with 80% power given the study's sample size and alpha level of .05. All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and assumptions for each test were evaluated and reported alongside the test results. Ethical considerations included protecting participant anonymity and minimizing any potential risks from survey participation. No identifying information beyond basic demographics was collected, and participants were informed that they could skip any question or withdraw at any time without penalty. Data were stored on secure servers with access restricted to the research team. Participants received a small monetary compensation or course credit, depending on recruitment source. The methodological choices were intended to balance practical constraints with rigorous assessment of the study hypotheses, emphasizing transparency in measurement and robustness in analytic decisions.

Results

Descriptive statistics and preliminary checks were conducted before hypothesis testing. The extraversion subscale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the current sample, with Cronbach's alpha = .82. The distribution of extraversion scores approximated normality with a mean of 3.46 and a standard deviation of 0.71 on the 1–5 scale; skewness and kurtosis values were within acceptable bounds for parametric analyses. Raw follower counts were highly skewed (skewness = 6.12), and raw following counts also showed positive skew (skewness = 3.95). Consistent with the data preparation plan, log10-transformations were applied to follower and following counts after adding a constant of 1 to avoid undefined values for zero counts; the transformed variables exhibited substantially reduced skew (log followers skewness = 1.02; log following skewness = 0.88). Editing-time expressed in minutes was also right-skewed (skewness = 2.40), and log transformation reduced skew to approximately 0.65. Photo posting frequency reported as photos per week had moderate skew (skewness = 1.20) but did not require transformation for primary analyses given its bounded range and acceptable distributional properties after inspection. Descriptive statistics for the key variables are as follows: the mean log-transformed follower count was 2.84 (SD = 0.79), mean log-transformed following count was 2.12 (SD = 0.58), mean photos posted per week was 2.9 (SD = 4.2), and mean log-transformed editing time was 0.62 (SD = 0.41). Correlations among predictor variables showed that age was moderately negatively correlated with photos-per-week (r = −.32, p < .001) and with editing time (r = −.21, p = .002), indicating younger participants tended to post more and spend slightly more time editing. Gender differences in the primary variables were examined using t-tests; women reported slightly higher editing time on average than men (t(216) = 2.15, p = .033), but no significant gender differences were found for follower or following counts after log transformation. Turning to hypothesis testing, bivariate Pearson correlations were computed between extraversion and each Instagram behavior variable using transformed variables where appropriate. Extraversion correlated with log-transformed follower count at r = .08, p = .22, 95% CI [−.05, .21], indicating a small, nonsignificant relationship. The correlation between extraversion and log-transformed following count was r = .05, p = .43, 95% CI [−.08, .18], which was also nonsignificant. Extraversion and photos-posted-per-week correlated at r = .10, p = .12, 95% CI [−.02, .22], a small positive but statistically nonsignificant association. The correlation between extraversion and log-transformed editing time was r = .06, p = .34, 95% CI [−.07, .19], again nonsignificant. Given the skew in some variables and the possibility of outliers affecting Pearson correlations, nonparametric Spearman rank correlations were computed as sensitivity checks. Spearman correlations yielded similar patterns: extraversion and followers rho = .07, p = .28; extraversion and following rho = .04, p = .51; extraversion and photos per week rho = .09, p = .15; extraversion and editing-time rho = .05, p = .38. None of these associations reached conventional levels of statistical significance. To examine whether demographic covariates might mask relationships between extraversion and Instagram behaviors, partial correlations controlling for age and gender were conducted. The partial correlation between extraversion and log followers controlling for age and gender was r_partial = .07, p = .30; for log following it was r_partial = .03, p = .62; for photos per week it was r_partial = .09, p = .17; and for log editing time r_partial = .05, p = .40. These adjusted associations remained nonsignificant and showed minimal change in magnitude compared to the bivariate coefficients. Multiple regression analyses further evaluated whether extraversion contributed incremental predictive power for each Instagram outcome beyond age, gender, and self-rated Instagram usage intensity. In all regression models, extraversion did not emerge as a significant predictor. For log followers, the regression model including extraversion, age, gender, and usage intensity yielded R2 = .11, F(4, 213) = 6.50, p < .001, but the standardized coefficient for extraversion was β = .07, t = 1.18, p = .24, indicating that the variance explained by the model was driven primarily by usage intensity and age rather than extraversion. For log following, the overall model explained a small proportion of variance (R2 = .06), and extraversion again was nonsignificant (β = .04, p = .50). For photos-per-week, the model showed R2 = .15, with age contributing most strongly (β = −.30, p < .001) and extraversion remaining nonsignificant (β = .09, p = .13). For log editing-time, the full model yielded R2 = .08 and extraversion was not a significant predictor (β = .06, p = .36). Influence diagnostics for the regression models were examined; Cook's distances were uniformly low and no single observation exceeded conventional thresholds for undue influence. Exploratory analyses included a median-split classification of extraversion to create "high" and "low" groups and subsequent group comparisons across the Instagram variables. Independent-samples t-tests comparing the two groups did not reveal significant differences in log followers (t(216) = 1.16, p = .25), log following (t(216) = 0.87, p = .39), photos per week (t(216) = 1.52, p = .13), or log editing time (t(216) = 0.92, p = .36). Effect sizes for group differences were small (Cohen's d ranged from .09 to .21). Additional post-hoc subgroup analyses examined whether the associations differed across age strata (18–24, 25–34, 35+). Within younger participants (18–24), extraversion showed a slightly larger correlation with photos per week (r = .16, p = .08) but still did not reach significance; in older strata correlations were smaller and nonsignificant. A formal test of interaction between extraversion and age in predicting photos per week in a regression model did not yield a significant interaction term (β_interaction = −.04, p = .61). A post-hoc power analysis indicated that with the final sample size of 218 the study had 80% power to detect a true Pearson correlation of approximately r = .19 or larger at α = .05 (two-tailed). The observed correlations were substantially smaller than this threshold, suggesting that if any true effects exist they are likely small in magnitude. Overall, across multiple analytic approaches, extraversion did not show robust or statistically significant associations with the Instagram engagement indicators assessed in this study.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between extraversion and several indicators of Instagram engagement, including number of followers, number of accounts followed, frequency of photo posting, and time spent editing photos prior to posting. Contrary to the initial hypotheses grounded in theory and selective prior findings from other social media contexts, extraversion did not exhibit significant relationships with any of the Instagram variables analyzed. The null results were consistent across bivariate correlations, nonparametric checks, partial correlations controlling for age and gender, regression analyses, and exploratory subgroup comparisons. Even when considering the possibility of small age-dependent effects, the evidence did not support the straightforward prediction that extraverts are more active or more invested in self-presentation on Instagram relative to introverts. These findings contribute to a nuanced understanding of personality-expression dynamics on social media platforms and highlight the importance of platform specificity, measurement sensitivity, and contextual moderators when extrapolating from generalized theoretical expectations. One key theoretical implication of these results is that extraversion's influence on social media behaviors is likely to be domain-specific rather than uniform across platforms. While previous studies have reported relationships between extraversion and friend counts or profile-picture style on certain networks, Instagram's focus on curated visual content and algorithm-mediated visibility may decouple simple social tendencies from observable metrics such as follower count or editing time. For instance, follower counts can reflect factors unrelated to personality, including offline celebrity status, participation in influencer marketing, membership in niche communities that reward visual content, or strategic practices like reciprocal following. In such cases, extraversion per se may be only a weak predictor of follower accumulation. Similarly, editing time might reflect aesthetic preferences, technical skill, or concern for visual quality that are not synonymous with sociability. These distinctions emphasize that theorizing about personality and online behavior must account for the specific affordances and social norms of each platform. Another plausible explanation for the null findings is that extraversion is a broad trait composed of multiple facets, and that different facets have divergent relationships with social media behaviors. For example, the sociability facet might predict frequency of posting or breadth of social interactions, whereas the assertiveness or excitement-seeking facets might predict attention-seeking behaviors or experimental self-presentation. Using an aggregate extraversion score could obscure these facet-level relationships if their effects operate in opposite directions or have different strengths. The present study employed a unidimensional extraversion subscale for parsimony and feasibility, but future research could profitably examine facet-level associations to detect more nuanced patterns. Methodological considerations are also central to interpreting these results. The study relied on self-reported Instagram behaviors, which can be subject to recall error, social desirability biases, and differing interpretations of the questions. Although numerical counts like followers and following are concrete, participants may have rounded or approximated values, and reported editing times were estimations rather than direct logs. Objective behavioral data obtained via platform APIs or screen-time analytics could yield more precise measures and potentially reveal relationships that self-report misses. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences about whether personality predicts changes in Instagram behaviors or whether social media engagement influences personality expression over time. Longitudinal or experimental designs would be required to disentangle temporal sequencing and reciprocal dynamics. Sample characteristics may have limited the generalizability of findings and the ability to detect small effects. The convenience sample, while diverse in age and educational background, was not representative of the broader population of Instagram users, and the distribution of follower counts was skewed as is common in social networks. The post-hoc power analysis suggested sensitivity to detect correlations of approximately r = .19 or larger with adequate power; thus, the study may have been underpowered to detect very small effects. However, the observed effect sizes were consistently near zero, which reduces the likelihood that a meaningful relationship was missed because of insufficient power alone. It remains possible that small but theoretically interesting associations exist and could be detected in larger samples or with more fine-grained measures. The null findings also raise questions about context-dependent moderation. Factors such as cultural norms, age cohort, gender roles, and motivations for using Instagram (e.g., social connection versus professional branding) could moderate the relationship between extraversion and platform behaviors. In the present sample, age was associated with posting frequency and editing investment, and usage intensity was a consistent predictor of follower count. These observations suggest that situational and demographic variables might exert stronger influences on Instagram behaviors than dispositional extraversion in isolation. Future work should test moderation models that explicitly investigate interactions between personality traits and contextual variables such as motives for use, privacy orientation, and network composition. A potentially fruitful avenue for future research is the integration of personality assessments with behavioral content analyses. For example, combining extraversion measures with automated analyses of posted image content, caption sentiment, and engagement metrics (likes, comments) could clarify whether extraverts differ in the qualitative nature of their posts rather than in overall quantity or follower counts. It is possible that extraverts favor images depicting social gatherings or group activities, receive different patterns of engagement from followers, or use hashtags and tagging behaviors more strategically, none of which would necessarily show up in simple follower or posting-frequency counts. Moreover, network analyses that examine reciprocity, centrality, and clustering could reveal personality-linked differences in how users structure their Instagram relationships even if raw counts are similar. Another important direction is to examine motivational processes underlying social media use. Research on social compensation, self-affirmation, and impression management indicates that individuals may use platforms for different purposes, and these motives may interact with personality traits to shape behavior. For instance, introverts who use Instagram primarily for creative self-expression may post frequently and invest heavily in editing despite low offline sociability, while extraverts may use in-person socializing as their main outlet and treat Instagram more casually. Measuring motives directly and testing their mediating or moderating roles could illuminate complex patterns that simple trait–behavior correlations obscure. The findings also encourage critical reflection on assumptions often made in both academic and popular discourse about personality and social media. It may be tempting to conclude that extraversion always predicts greater online sociability, but the present study illustrates that such assumptions must be qualified and tested across contexts. Different social media platforms support distinct forms of interaction and visibility, and individuals may strategically adjust their behavior to align with norms, goals, and constraints. Researchers should therefore adopt a platform-sensitive approach when hypothesizing about trait–behavior relationships. Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged in more detail. The reliance on self-report is a primary limitation, as noted above, and future studies should incorporate objective measures where possible. The sampling method, while pragmatic, limits claims to generalizability; replication in representative or community-based samples would strengthen confidence in the findings. Additionally, the extraversion measure focused on broad tendencies; as discussed, facet-level measurement might reveal subtler associations. Finally, the focus on only four Instagram indicators, though chosen to represent different behavioral domains, may have missed other relevant outcomes such as use of Instagram stories, frequency of interaction with comments, or patterns of direct messaging, which could be differentially related to personality. In sum, the study did not find evidence that extraversion is a robust predictor of follower counts, number followed, posting frequency, or editing time on Instagram in this sample. These null results underscore the need for more fine-grained, platform-specific research that integrates objective behavioral data, facet-level personality assessments, and measurements of motives and network structure. They also suggest that popular assumptions about extraversion and online sociability require qualification and that theoretical models should accommodate the complexity of social media contexts. Future research pursuing the directions outlined above can clarify when and how personality shapes online behavior, and by doing so will contribute to a more comprehensive theory of personality expression in the digital age.
