Introduction

Human memory for verbal material has been extensively studied using the framework of working memory, which posits distinct but interacting subsystems for temporary storage and manipulation. One widely discussed component within this framework is the phonological loop, which primarily supports the short-term retention of verbal and auditory information. The phonological loop is thought to consist of two subcomponents: a phonological store and an articulatory rehearsal process. The phonological store is believed to hold speech-based information in a rapidly decaying form, while articulatory rehearsal refreshes items in the store and supports longer retention. Research in this area has repeatedly shown that disrupting articulatory rehearsal impairs recall for auditory verbal stimuli, supporting the idea that subvocal rehearsal contributes to maintaining phonological representations. These experimental manipulations often use articulatory suppression tasks, such as repeating a neutral syllable aloud, to block subvocal rehearsal and thereby test the role of the articulatory component in immediate memory.

Closely related to the phonological loop model is a body of work on modality effects, which refers to differences in memory performance that arise when stimuli are presented in different sensory modalities. Classic findings indicate that auditory presentation often yields better immediate recall than visual presentation for certain types of verbal material, particularly at the end of lists, an effect attributed to more durable or accessible phonological encoding when input is auditory. Conversely, visual presentation may favor encoding into visuospatial or orthographic representations that rely less on phonological rehearsal. These modality effects suggest that the same memory task can recruit different working memory subsystems depending on how information is presented.

Beyond articulatory suppression, researchers have also investigated the effects of interfering tasks that target non-phonological stores, such as visual suppression tasks intended to disrupt visual or visuospatial maintenance. If maintenance of visually presented material relies on a visual cache or other visual-spatial workspace, then a demanding visual suppression task should reduce memory performance for visual stimuli. There is, however, less consensus about the robustness of visual suppression effects compared to articulatory suppression effects, and methodological choices for imposing visual interference vary considerably across studies.

The interplay between presentation modality and suppression type is therefore central to understanding the modularity and flexibility of working memory processes. If verbal recall primarily depends on phonological codes regardless of presentation modality, then articulatory suppression should impair recall following both visual and auditory presentation. By contrast, if modality-specific codes support recall—such that visual presentation engages visual cache mechanisms—then visual suppression should selectively impair recall for visually presented items while leaving auditory-based maintenance relatively intact. The pattern of cross-modal interference is therefore informative about whether a single phonological mechanism dominates verbal recall, or whether modality-specific stores contribute importantly to retention.

In applied contexts, the content of interest sometimes differs from simple word lists and instead includes longer, semantically coherent texts such as consent forms or informational notices. Memory for such materials has a clear practical significance: recalling the content of consent forms is important for informed decision making and for ensuring that participants or clients understand rights, risks, and responsibilities. Research that extends working memory paradigms to longer, more meaningful texts can therefore bridge basic and applied concerns. However, the added semantic structure in texts introduces additional influences on recall, including comprehension, inference, and integration processes, which can interact with working memory constraints.

Some prior studies have investigated memory for prose and for structured informational texts, finding that both phonological and visual processes can play roles in encoding and retrieval. For example, readers may convert visual text into phonological representations as part of a subvocal rehearsal strategy, especially when trying to remember exact wording. At the same time, visuospatial layouts and orthographic features can support memory for visual presentation. Therefore, the relative involvement of phonological and visual processes when recalling texts such as consent statements remains an open empirical question.

Experimentally, the suppression approach offers a way to tax specific maintenance mechanisms and to infer their contribution to recall. Articulatory suppression is typically effective at disrupting phonological rehearsal and has reliably reduced immediate recall for auditory lists. Visual suppression tasks have been more heterogeneous but often include continuous tracking, dynamic visual noise, or visual search components. The efficacy of such tasks in disrupting visual maintenance depends on the extent to which they engage the same low-level visual processes that are used to maintain representations of the target material. If the chosen visual suppression task fails to sufficiently engage those processes, null results may arise even if a visual cache contributes to memory.

Investigating how suppression tasks interact with presentation modality when recalling consent texts can thus provide useful tests of working memory theory and its application to document memory. If articulatory suppression selectively reduces recall for aural presentation, this would support the traditional view that auditory input is preferentially maintained in phonological form and benefits from rehearsal. If visual suppression selectively reduces recall for visual presentation, it would support the complementary view that visual codes contribute to memory for visually presented texts. Alternatively, failure to observe modality-specific suppression effects could indicate either that the suppression tasks were ineffective or that participants tended to convert all material into a common code (e.g., phonological) regardless of presentation modality.

Another consideration is the role of task demands and participant strategies. When asked to remember consent statements, participants may adopt different strategies based on perceived task difficulty or personal preference. For instance, visually presented texts might be read and then verbally rehearsed, bringing them into the phonological domain; conversely, auditory materials might be rehearsed using phonological processes by default. Such strategic conversion could reduce modality-specific suppression effects, because both presentation types would ultimately rely on the same maintenance mechanism. Only careful manipulation of suppression tasks that differentially target these mechanisms can disentangle these influences.

Compliance with suppression tasks is also crucial. Articulatory suppression is easy to monitor—observers can hear participants repeating syllables—whereas visual suppression tasks often require participants to perform a covert task or a task that is less easily monitored by the experimenter. Low compliance or variable engagement with visual suppression tasks may lead to inconsistent data and difficulty interpreting null effects. Therefore, experimental designs should incorporate manipulation checks or objective measures of engagement where possible.

In this study, we investigated recall of consent statements using an undergraduate sample and manipulated both presentation modality and the nature of a concurrent suppression task. Presentation modality had two levels: visual presentation, in which participants read consent statements on a computer screen, and aural presentation, in which participants listened to recorded readings of the same statements. Suppression had three levels: articulatory suppression, designed to disrupt phonological rehearsal; a visual suppression task, designed to disrupt visual maintenance; and a no-suppression control condition. The central question concerned whether suppression effects would be modality-specific and thus reveal distinct contributions of phonological and visual stores, or whether articulatory rehearsal would dominate recall across modalities.

We predicted that articulatory suppression would impair recall for aural presentation, based on the assumption that auditory inputs are primarily encoded phonologically and rely on rehearsal. We also predicted that visual suppression would impair recall for visually presented consents, but anticipated that the effect might be weaker given prior mixed evidence regarding the robustness of visual suppression. Furthermore, if participants habitually convert visual input into phonological codes, articulatory suppression might also impair recall for visual presentation, although perhaps to a lesser extent. Thus, we examined both main effects and interactions among modality and suppression type.

To maximize ecological validity while retaining experimental control, we used consent statements that were similar in length and complexity to typical research consent forms but standardized for the experimental task. Participants completed all three suppression conditions within their assigned modality, with condition order counterbalanced across participants to control for practice or fatigue effects. We included objective measures of compliance for articulatory suppression by recording audio and examined participant reports and performance on the visual suppression task as manipulation checks.

We analyzed recall accuracy using appropriate mixed-model statistical tests, focusing on whether suppression selectively reduced recall depending on modality. We also explored item-level patterns to determine whether certain types of information within consent statements were differentially affected by suppression. Finally, we assessed compliance and subjective reports to interpret the validity of the suppression manipulations.

Overall, this study aimed to provide a nuanced test of the modality-specific and domain-general contributions to memory for consent statements. The design allowed us to test core theoretical predictions from working memory models while addressing methodological challenges associated with suppression manipulations. The outcomes have potential implications for understanding how people remember important documentary information and for designing materials and procedures that support retention. In particular, clarifying whether oral presentations of consent rely on rehearsable phonological representations more than visual presentations could inform best practices for presenting critical information in settings where subsequent recall matters. Moreover, the study provides an opportunity to assess the efficacy of commonly used suppression tasks in realistic memory situations and to discuss methodological refinements for future research.

Methods

Participants were undergraduate students recruited from an introductory psychology participant pool at a large university. Participation was voluntary and course credit was offered as compensation. The final sample included 72 participants after excluding data from individuals who failed basic comprehension checks or who reported not following task instructions. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 23 years and most reported being native or near-native speakers of English. The gender distribution was balanced, though gender was not treated as a primary variable in the analysis. All participants gave informed consent prior to participation consistent with the protocol approved by the university research ethics board. The consent procedure for the experiment itself was administered separately from the consent statements used as stimuli, to avoid contamination of the memory task.

Design and overview were as follows. The study employed a mixed factorial design with presentation modality as a between-subjects factor (visual vs aural) and suppression as a within-subjects factor (articulatory, visual, none). Participants were randomly assigned to either the visual or the aural modality condition, with 36 participants in each modality group. Within each modality group, participants completed three suppression conditions in separate trial blocks. Block order was counterbalanced across participants using a Latin square to mitigate order and practice effects. Each participant therefore experienced all three suppression conditions, but only in a single presentation modality.

Materials consisted of six consent statements adapted to be of comparable length and complexity. Each consent statement contained four key information items that were scored for recall, producing 24 scoring units per block. Statements covered common consent topics such as purpose of participation, confidentiality, potential risks, and voluntariness of participation. The wording was standardized and pilot tested on a small sample to ensure comprehensibility and to avoid ambiguous phrasing. For the visual modality, statements were presented in a readable sans-serif font on a computer monitor with black text on a white background. For the aural modality, statements were recorded by a trained speaker and presented through headphones at a comfortable listening level; recording amplitude was normalized and playback was controlled by the experimenter to ensure equal exposure across participants.

Suppression tasks were operationalized as follows. The articulatory suppression task required participants to repeat the syllable “the” aloud at a steady pace of approximately two syllables per second for the duration of each block. Participants were trained on the cadence before starting and were monitored by the experimenter throughout the block; audio recordings were also made for later compliance checks. The visual suppression task was designed to occupy the visual maintenance system by presenting a dynamic dot-pattern display in which small dots moved on the screen and participants were required to continuously monitor for occasional color changes and press a designated key when a dot turned red. The dynamic visual display covered the central visual field and persisted for the duration of the block. In the no-suppression control condition, participants performed only the primary memory task without any concurrent secondary task.

Each block began with clear instructions about the suppression requirement for that block and with a brief practice interval for the suppression task. In the visual modality, participants read the six consent statements presented sequentially on the screen, each appearing for a fixed duration sufficient to read and encode (for example, 20 seconds each); in the aural modality, they listened to prerecorded readings of the same statements played at a natural pace with similar timing. After presentation of the six statements, there was a short distractor interval of 15 seconds during which participants performed a simple arithmetic filler task to reduce immediate rehearsal before recall. Following the distractor task, participants were given a recall period in which they typed as much of the content of the consent statements as they could remember into a text box on the computer. The recall period lasted for a fixed duration of five minutes per block. Participants were instructed to reproduce as many of the key information items as accurately as possible; exact verbatim recall was not required, but responses were scored based on the presence of the key informational units.

Scoring procedures were established prior to data collection. Two independent raters scored typed recall responses for the presence or absence of each of the 24 key information units per block. Raters followed a scoring rubric that allowed for synonymous phrasing; for example, “you may withdraw anytime” was scored as present if participants indicated voluntariness in equivalent wording. Inter-rater reliability was assessed on a subset of responses and exceeded 0.90 (Cohen’s kappa) before resolving discrepancies by discussion. The primary dependent variable for statistical analyses was proportion correct per block, computed as the number of key items recalled divided by 24.

Compliance and manipulation checks were included. For articulatory suppression, audio recordings were reviewed to confirm that participants engaged in continuous vocalization for at least 90% of the suppression interval. For visual suppression, performance on the color-change detection task (hit rate and false alarm rate) was recorded as an objective measure of engagement; participants who failed to maintain minimal attention, defined as a hit rate below 60% and/or no response on multiple target events, were flagged for exclusion or separate analysis. Additionally, participants completed a brief post-experiment questionnaire in which they rated the difficulty of each suppression task and reported on strategies used during encoding and recall. These subjective reports were used to interpret patterns in the data and to identify potential strategy shifts.

Statistical analyses were planned in advance and included mixed ANOVA to test for main effects of modality and suppression and their interaction. Given the within-subject nature of suppression, sphericity assumptions were examined and Huynh-Feldt corrections would be applied if necessary. Because the primary prediction concerned whether articulatory suppression would selectively impair aural recall, planned paired comparisons within modality groups were also specified; these paired t-tests contrasted the articulatory and visual suppression conditions with the no-suppression control. Effect sizes were computed for significant effects using Cohen’s d for pairwise contrasts and partial eta squared for ANOVA effects. Secondary exploratory analyses examined item-level patterns, correlations with suppression task performance, and potential order effects across blocks.

Participants were tested individually in a quiet laboratory room to minimize extraneous noise and distractions. The experimenter remained present during the session to ensure correct task performance but avoided providing additional cues that might influence encoding or recall. The entire session lasted approximately 50 to 60 minutes, including consent for the experiment, task instructions, practice trials, the three experimental blocks, post-task questionnaires, and debriefing. Debriefing included informing participants about the purpose of suppression tasks and the theoretical rationale for the study.

All data were anonymized and stored on secure university servers in accordance with institutional policies. Analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and data quality checks were performed prior to hypothesis testing. Data exclusions were limited and pre-specified, including failure to comply with suppression tasks or missing data on key variables. The final analytical sample of 72 participants included those who met compliance criteria for the articulatory suppression checks; five additional participants were excluded due to low engagement with the visual suppression task as determined by the objective performance threshold.

Results

Descriptive statistics for recall performance across conditions are presented first to provide a sense of the observed pattern. Mean proportion correct in the aural modality was highest in the no-suppression control condition, somewhat lower in the visual suppression condition, and lowest in the articulatory suppression condition. In the visual modality, mean proportion correct was similar across the three suppression conditions, with only small numerical differences. Standard deviations indicated moderate variability within conditions, particularly in the visual suppression blocks where engagement appeared more variable across participants.

A mixed-design ANOVA was conducted with suppression condition (none, articulatory, visual) as a within-subjects factor and presentation modality (visual, aural) as a between-subjects factor. The analysis first tested the main effect of suppression across modalities. This main effect did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance, indicating that averaging across both modalities, suppression condition did not produce a uniform reduction in recall. The main effect of modality was also examined and produced a small advantage for the aural group, but this difference did not reach significance in the omnibus test. These null main effects, however, masked a clear interaction pattern that was of primary theoretical interest.

The suppression by modality interaction was statistically significant, indicating that the impact of suppression differed depending on whether statements were presented visually or aurally. Follow-up tests were therefore conducted within each modality to clarify the interaction. Within the aural modality group, a repeated-measures ANOVA across the three suppression conditions yielded a significant effect of suppression. Planned paired comparisons showed that recall under articulatory suppression was significantly lower than recall under the no-suppression control condition. The difference between articulatory suppression and the visual suppression condition in the aural group was also significant, indicating that articulatory suppression specifically impaired memory for aural presentation relative to both other conditions. Effect size estimates for the articulatory vs. no-suppression contrast in the aural group suggested a medium to large effect, consistent with theoretical expectations regarding the role of articulatory rehearsal for auditory input.

Within the visual modality group, the repeated-measures ANOVA did not reveal a significant effect of suppression. Paired comparisons between articulatory suppression and no-suppression control showed a small, non-significant difference. Comparisons between visual suppression and no-suppression control likewise failed to show significant differences. The articulatory versus visual suppression contrast in the visual modality group was not significant either. These null results suggest that, for visually presented consent statements, neither articulatory nor visual suppression produced consistent decrements in recall in our sample.

To quantify the interaction further, we computed difference scores contrasting each suppression condition with the no-suppression baseline within each modality and then compared those difference scores across modalities. The difference score for articulatory suppression (versus none) was significantly larger in the aural group than in the visual group, reaffirming that articulatory suppression produced modality-specific interference for aural presentation. For the visual suppression difference score, there was no significant difference between modalities, providing no support for a modality-specific visual suppression effect.

We also examined the relationship between compliance with suppression tasks and recall performance to evaluate whether engagement moderated the effects. Compliance for articulatory suppression was high; audio checks indicated that over 95% of participants in the articulatory condition produced continuous vocalizations for the required duration. Within the aural group, articulatory suppression compliance correlated modestly with impairment magnitude such that participants with higher continuous vocalization tended to show larger decrements in recall. In contrast, compliance metrics for the visual suppression task were more variable. The objective detection task associated with visual suppression showed a wide range of hit rates across participants, and lower hit rates were associated with smaller or absent suppression effects. A subgroup analysis that restricted attention to participants who met a more stringent visual suppression engagement criterion (hit rate above 80%) still did not reveal a reliable visual suppression effect, although the small sample size in this subgroup limited statistical power.

We explored whether there were any systematic item-level effects, such as certain informational items being more vulnerable to suppression. Analyses of recall probability for individual key items revealed that items requiring verbatim-like phrasing (e.g., exact wording of a refund policy) were more affected by articulatory suppression in the aural modality than more gist-like items (e.g., the general purpose of the study). This pattern is consistent with the idea that articulatory rehearsal supports retention of surface-level phonological or lexical details which are more relevant for verbatim recall than for gist memory. In the visual modality, item-level analyses revealed no consistent pattern of susceptibility to suppression across items.

Potential order effects across blocks were examined by including block order as a covariate in the analysis. Block order did not significantly influence the main findings; the suppression by modality interaction remained significant when controlling for order, and there were no reliable practice or fatigue trends that could account for the observed interaction. Participant reports from post-experiment questionnaires provided additional context. Most participants reported that the articulatory suppression task was effortful but straightforward to perform. In contrast, reported difficulty and engagement in the visual suppression task varied widely, with some participants indicating that they found the dot-detection task monotonous or easy and some reporting occasional lapses of attention.

To ensure that the significant articulatory suppression effect for aural presentation was not an artifact of baseline differences between the modality groups, we compared no-suppression control performance across the two modalities. These baseline scores did not differ significantly, indicating that initial encoding and recall ability were comparable across groups in the absence of suppression. Thus, the interaction effect is unlikely to be due to pre-existing differences in memory performance between the visual and aural groups.

Given concerns about potential violations of sphericity in the within-subject factor, we applied Huynh-Feldt corrections to the ANOVA tests where appropriate; these corrections did not alter the primary conclusions. Effect size estimates for the suppression by modality interaction indicated a small to medium effect, suggesting a meaningful moderation of suppression effects by modality even if the overall variance explained was not large.

Finally, exploratory correlational analyses examined whether individual differences in self-reported rehearsal strategy moderated the pattern of results. Participants who reported frequently using subvocal rehearsal strategies tended to show larger articulatory suppression effects in the aural modality, but this correlation did not reach statistical significance after correction for multiple comparisons. In sum, the results provide clear evidence that articulatory suppression impairs recall for aural presentation of consent statements, while visual suppression did not produce reliable effects for visual presentation under the present experimental conditions.

Discussion

The present study investigated how presentation modality and suppression tasks interact to influence recall of consent statements in an undergraduate sample. The central finding was that articulatory suppression produced a significant and meaningful reduction in recall accuracy when consent statements were presented aurally. This result aligns with theoretical models that posit a central role for phonological rehearsal in maintaining auditory verbal information, supporting the notion that the articulatory component of working memory is crucial for retaining spoken materials. In contrast to our expectations, however, visual suppression failed to produce a reliable impairment in recall for visually presented consent statements. Additionally, articulatory suppression did not significantly impair recall for visually presented materials in our study. These outcomes bear on multiple theoretical and methodological issues in working memory research and applied considerations for presenting critical information.

The observed detrimental effect of articulatory suppression on aural recall replicates and extends classic findings from the working memory literature. When participants were required to produce concurrent vocalizations, their ability to recall key informational units from spoken consent statements declined substantially compared to baseline. This selective impairment is consistent with the view that auditory inputs are preferentially encoded into phonological representations and that articulation-based rehearsal is instrumental in sustaining those representations until retrieval. The effect was specific to the aural modality rather than appearing as a general suppression effect across modalities, which suggests that the articulatory mechanism was particularly engaged by the auditory presentation format.

The lack of a significant visual suppression effect on visual presentation raises several possible interpretations. One possibility is that the visual suppression task did not sufficiently interfere with the specific visual-maintenance processes that support memory for the presented consent statements. Visual suppression tasks can vary widely in sensory demand and in the degree to which they compete for the same perceptual or cognitive resources used to maintain target information. Our dynamic dot-detection task was designed to be continuous and attentionally engaging; however, despite objective monitoring, participants’ engagement with the task may have been inconsistent or insufficient to disrupt the visual cache. The variability in hit rates and subjective reports support this interpretation, as some participants indicated that the task was monotonous or easy, leading to lapses in sustained attention. Therefore, methodological limitations in implementing a robust visual suppression manipulation likely contributed to the null effect.

Another plausible reason for the absence of a visual suppression effect is that participants may have spontaneously converted visually presented text into phonological form, thereby relying more on the phonological loop than on visual storage. Such strategic encoding would render the memory representation vulnerable to articulatory suppression but immune to visual suppression, consistent with the pattern we observed. Indeed, even when stimuli are presented visually, readers often engage in subvocalization or phonological recoding as part of comprehension or memorization strategies. Our post-task reports indicated that a number of participants habitually used verbal rehearsal, which could explain why neither suppression condition in the visual modality produced clear effects: participants may not have been using the visual cache to a substantial degree.

The absence of a significant articulatory suppression effect in the visual modality, despite the possibility of phonological recoding, suggests that the degree of reliance on rehearsal strategies may vary across individuals or contexts. For instance, reading and encoding strategies might prioritize gist over verbatim retention when material is visually presented, especially when participants anticipate a single recall test rather than repeated retrieval. Thus, even if some phonological recoding occurred, it may not have been extensive enough to make articulatory suppression disruptive. Alternatively, the nature of the consent statements—semantically coherent and meaningful—may have allowed participants to form more durable semantic or gist-based representations that were resilient to both types of suppression.

The item-level analyses offered additional nuance, indicating that articulatory suppression particularly impaired recall of items requiring more verbatim-like retention in the aural modality. This pattern is theoretically coherent: phonological rehearsal supports retention of surface-level linguistic form, so items that depend on exact phrasing or lexical details are more vulnerable when that rehearsal is disrupted. In contrast, gist-based items that can be reconstructed from semantic understanding are less reliant on phonological maintenance and therefore show less impact from articulatory suppression. Practical implications follow from this distinction: when exact wording is important—for instance, in legal or clinical consent contexts—oral presentation may leave such surface-level details more susceptible to loss if listeners cannot engage in rehearsal.

Methodological challenges associated with the visual suppression task deserve careful attention. First, our visual suppression task required continuous monitoring for color changes in a dynamic display, which was intended to tax sustained attention and low-level visual processing. However, the task’s detectability and monotony may have allowed participants to adopt strategies that minimized interference with encoding, such as allocating attention preferentially to the consent statements during critical moments or slowing responses to the dot-detection task. Future research could employ more intrusive visual interference methods—for example, alternating visual noise presentations that overlap spatially with the text area, or a rapid serial visual presentation task that demands continuous visual updating—to create more direct competition with visual maintenance. Moreover, providing incentives for task performance or adaptive difficulty that calibrates challenge to individual ability may enhance engagement and produce stronger interference.

Another limitation concerns the measurement of suppression compliance. While articulatory suppression is easily verified through audio recordings, visual suppression compliance is less straightforward. Our objective measure—the hit rate for color-change detection—offered some insight but may not have fully captured the degree to which the task interfered with the visual cache. Some participants may have prioritized the memory task over the suppression task despite performing acceptably on the detection measure, thus reducing interference. Implementing dual-task designs that more tightly couple the suppression and primary tasks could help ensure that the suppression task occupies the same representational space as the target material.

The mixed design used in this study—between-subjects for modality and within-subjects for suppression—was chosen to reduce carryover effects across modalities while maintaining efficiency. This approach, however, introduces potential between-group variability that could mask or accentuate effects. We addressed this concern by comparing baseline no-suppression performance across modalities and found no significant differences, which reduces the likelihood that pre-existing group differences account for the interaction. Nevertheless, a fully within-subjects design in which participants experience both modalities with adequate washout periods might provide more sensitive tests of modality-specific suppression effects, though such a design would require careful counterbalancing to prevent cross-modal strategy transfer.

The pattern of results also speaks to broader theoretical debates about the modularity of working memory systems. The modality-specific impairment observed for articulatory suppression with auditory presentation supports the notion of some functional specialization within working memory, with articulatory rehearsal playing a pivotal role for maintaining spoken information. However, the absence of a complementary visual suppression effect cautions against a simple one-to-one mapping between presentation modality and storage subsystem. The findings suggest that strategic encoding and the semantic structure of materials can shift reliance among subsystems, thereby underscoring the flexibility of working memory processes. The data therefore lend support to models that emphasize both domain-specific buffers and dynamic strategy-dependent allocation of cognitive resources.

Future research should aim to resolve the remaining ambiguities by employing more robust visual suppression manipulations, by probing strategy use more directly, and by varying the semantic nature of the materials. For instance, comparing memory for verbatim wording versus gist across modalities and suppression conditions could illuminate the representational substrates most affected by different types of interference. Additionally, incorporating neurophysiological measures such as eye-tracking during visual presentation or electrophysiological indices during suppression tasks could provide converging evidence about which perceptual and cognitive systems are taxed under each manipulation.

Applied implications of the present work are noteworthy. Our results suggest that oral presentation of consent information may render verbatim details particularly vulnerable if listeners cannot engage in rehearsal—an issue that could arise in noisy environments or in contexts where individuals are prevented from rehearsing (e.g., during simultaneous conversation). Therefore, practitioners who need to ensure retention of specific wording might favor providing written copies that participants can re-read, or combine oral presentation with visual aids that promote encoding into durable semantic representations. However, given the lack of clear visual suppression effects in this study, further research is needed before definitive recommendations can be issued.

In conclusion, this study provides clear evidence that articulatory suppression impairs recall for aurally presented consent statements, reinforcing the role of phonological rehearsal in maintaining spoken information. At the same time, the anticipated visual suppression effects for visually presented materials were not observed, likely due to methodological limitations of the visual suppression task and the strategic conversion of visual input into phonological codes by participants. These findings underscore the importance of careful task design when probing modality-specific processes and highlight the need for future studies that more effectively disrupt visual maintenance. By refining suppression methodologies and exploring the interplay of strategy, material type, and modality, future research can further elucidate how people retain important documentary information in real-world settings.
