Introduction

Sleep is a foundational biological process that supports a wide range of cognitive, emotional, and physiological functions. It is broadly recognized that variations in sleep patterns, sleep duration, and subjective sleep quality can have substantial consequences for daytime functioning. Across populations, poor sleep has been associated with impaired attention, reduced academic and occupational performance, and increased vulnerability to mood disturbances. Mental health researchers have long observed associations between sleep disturbances and a variety of psychiatric conditions. In particular, depressive disorders have been repeatedly linked to alterations in sleep architecture, insomnia symptoms, and subjective sleep problems. These findings have led to a growing interest in how sleep and psychopathology are interconnected, as well as to the possibility that improving sleep could ameliorate mental health symptoms. While much of the literature has emphasized depression, anxiety disorders represent another class of common mental health conditions for which sleep may be a relevant factor. Anxiety is characterized by excessive worry, physiological arousal, and hypervigilance, all of which have plausible connections to sleep onset and continuity. Mechanistically, hyperarousal associated with anxiety may interfere with sleep initiation and maintenance, while chronic sleep loss may in turn exacerbate cognitive and affective processes that sustain anxiety. Thus, a bi-directional relationship between sleep and anxiety is plausible from both psychological and physiological perspectives.

University students represent a population in which both poor sleep and elevated anxiety levels are frequently observed. The transition to university life often involves disrupted routines, irregular sleep schedules, academic pressures, and social stressors, all of which can contribute to variable sleep quality. At the same time, the prevalence of anxiety symptoms and clinically significant anxiety disorders is elevated in younger adult samples, and these problems can impair academic progress and general well-being. Given the co-occurrence of sleep disturbances and anxious symptomatology in student populations, there is a pressing need to better characterize the association between sleep quality and anxiety specifically in university samples. Furthermore, many prior studies have used small samples, clinical populations, or have focused narrowly on sleep duration without considering broader indices of sleep quality and disturbances. Sleep quality is a multifaceted construct that encompasses subjective sleep satisfaction, latency, maintenance, sleep efficiency, and daytime dysfunction, and studying these dimensions offers a more nuanced understanding of how sleep relates to anxiety.

Existing empirical work has provided consistent evidence of an association between poor sleep and increased anxiety symptoms across varied demographic groups. Studies that focus on general adult samples often report moderate correlations between global measures of sleep quality and scales assessing generalized anxiety symptoms. Research with clinical populations also indicates that individuals diagnosed with anxiety disorders frequently report sleep disturbances in both subjective measures and objective assessments. Nonetheless, the literature is heterogeneous in terms of the strength of reported associations, the operationalization of sleep variables, and the extent to which potential confounders such as age, gender, and lifestyle factors are accounted for. Additionally, a number of investigations have been cross-sectional in nature, which constrains causal interpretation and leaves open questions about the directionality of effects.

A further gap concerns the degree to which specific aspects of sleep quality—such as sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep fragmentation, and daytime dysfunction—differentially associate with anxiety symptoms. While global indices of sleep quality are informative, parsing these subcomponents may reveal particular features of sleep that are most strongly linked to anxiety. For example, prolonged sleep latency and nocturnal awakenings may be especially sensitive to cognitive and physiological hyperarousal, whereas reduced total sleep duration may influence mood regulation and threat sensitivity via cumulative sleep debt. Understanding these patterns in a student sample can inform targeted interventions aimed at the most relevant aspects of sleep that relate to anxiety.

Methodological considerations also shape the interpretability of prior findings. Many studies rely on self-report measures, which capture subjective experiences of sleep and anxiety but are vulnerable to shared method variance and reporting biases. Conversely, objective measures such as actigraphy and polysomnography provide precise indices of sleep continuity and architecture but are resource intensive and less commonly used in large, nonclinical samples. Given these trade-offs, research that combines reliable self-report instruments with careful data screening and appropriate statistical controls remains valuable for elucidating associations in accessible samples such as university students.

Practical implications of clarifying the sleep–anxiety relationship are substantial. If robust associations exist between poor sleep quality and elevated anxiety symptoms in student populations, this could motivate the development and dissemination of sleep-focused interventions as a component of student mental health services. Interventions ranging from sleep hygiene education to cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia have shown promise in reducing sleep problems and, in some studies, in ameliorating concomitant mood symptoms. However, effective translation to university settings requires evidence that sleep problems are indeed correlated with anxiety among students and that the magnitude of this relationship merits targeted effort.

The present investigation was designed to address these gaps by examining the association between sleep quality and levels of anxiety in a university sample using a correlational design. The study sought to assess global sleep quality and specific sleep disturbance components alongside self-reported anxiety symptoms in a sufficiently large sample to provide stable estimates of effect size. Additionally, the study planned to control for demographic variables that could confound the association and to explore whether sleep quality predicts clinically relevant levels of anxiety. Given prior literature indicating links between sleep disturbances and anxiety, the primary hypothesis was that poorer sleep quality would be associated with higher levels of anxiety symptoms. Secondary hypotheses included the expectation that particular sleep dimensions related to sleep initiation and continuity would show especially strong associations with anxiety, and that sleep quality would account for unique variance in anxiety levels after controlling for age and gender.

This study is framed as an exploratory but hypothesis-driven investigation conducted within a cross-sectional design, with the recognition that causal claims cannot be made from correlational data. The present work emphasizes the careful description of measurement, rigorous data handling, and analytical transparency to maximize the value of its findings for informing future longitudinal and interventional research. By focusing on a university sample, the study aims to contribute evidence that is directly relevant to settings where mental health and sleep interventions could be deployed at scale to support student well-being. The following sections detail the methods used, the results obtained, and the implications of the findings for theory, clinical practice, and further empirical inquiry.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a mid-sized university and consisted of undergraduate and postgraduate students who volunteered to participate in an online survey study about sleep and well-being. Recruitment occurred through campus email lists, online student groups, and flyers in common areas, and participants were offered a small course credit or entry into a prize draw as compensation for their time. Initial eligibility requirements were minimal, restricted to being enrolled at the university and being at least 18 years of age. The final sample included 254 participants after applying exclusion criteria and data screening procedures. The sample comprised 164 female participants, 88 male participants, and 2 participants who selected a nonbinary response; the mean age of the sample was 21.7 years with a standard deviation of 3.6 years. Participants represented a range of academic years and programs, and a small number reported current treatment for mental health concerns.

All procedures were approved by the university’s human research ethics committee, and participants provided informed consent electronically prior to beginning the survey. The study was presented as research on sleep and mental health, and participants were provided with debriefing information and contact details for mental health resources at the conclusion of the survey. Respondents were informed that they could discontinue participation at any time without penalty.

The survey was administered online via a secure survey platform and consisted of background demographic questions followed by standardized self-report questionnaires assessing sleep quality and anxiety symptoms. Sleep quality was measured using a widely used global instrument that captures subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. This measure yields a global score with higher values indicating poorer sleep quality, and the subcomponents provide additional granularity for exploratory analyses. Anxiety symptoms were assessed using a validated brief measure of generalized anxiety symptomatology that assesses frequency and severity of common anxiety symptoms over the preceding two weeks. This anxiety instrument yields a total score with established cutoffs for identifying elevated anxiety symptom levels.

Participants completed demographic items that inquired about age, gender, year of study, living arrangement (e.g., on-campus accommodation, living with family), caffeine and alcohol consumption, and current use of sleep medication. Given the potential influence of sleep duration and lifestyle factors on both sleep quality and anxiety, these variables were included as covariates in planned analyses. Participants also reported whether they had ever received a diagnosis of an anxiety disorder or depression, but diagnostic status was not required for participation.

To ensure data quality, attention-check questions were embedded in the survey and respondents who failed these checks were excluded from analyses. Cases with substantial missing data on primary measures (defined as more than 20 percent of items missing on either the sleep or anxiety instruments) were removed. For participants with minimal item-level missingness, mean imputation of the participant’s available items on that scale was used to compute scale scores. The final analytic sample of 254 participants represented those who completed the primary measures and passed data quality checks.

Scale scoring followed standard procedures for the chosen instruments. The global sleep quality score was computed by aggregating component scores into the global index with a possible range that reflected no to severe sleep problems. The anxiety measure’s total score was computed by summing item responses, with higher scores indicating more severe anxiety symptoms. Internal consistency for both measures was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, and both instruments demonstrated acceptable reliability in this sample.

Statistical analyses were conducted using a commonly used statistical software package. Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize sample characteristics and the distribution of key variables. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used as the primary test of association between global sleep quality and anxiety symptom total score. Given the expectation that the sleep and anxiety measures would be approximately continuous and normally distributed in a sample of this size, Pearson correlation was judged to be appropriate as a first-line analysis. To assess the robustness of the association, partial correlations controlling for age and gender were computed. Additional analyses included multiple linear regression in which anxiety score was the dependent variable and global sleep quality, age, gender, and self-reported caffeine consumption were entered as predictors. The regression model allowed estimation of the unique contribution of sleep quality to anxiety levels while accounting for potential demographic confounders.

Exploratory analyses examined associations between individual sleep components (e.g., sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep disturbances) and anxiety scores to identify particular aspects of sleep quality that were most strongly related to anxiety. These analyses employed Pearson correlations and were interpreted with caution given multiple comparisons. To aid interpretation of practical significance, Cohen’s conventions were used as benchmarks for effect size magnitudes, and 95 percent confidence intervals were reported for key statistics.

An additional analytic approach dichotomized the anxiety score based on an established cutoff to create a binary indicator of elevated anxiety symptoms. A logistic regression examined whether global sleep quality predicted the odds of elevated anxiety symptoms, adjusting for age and gender. This approach provided a clinically interpretable estimate of how variation in sleep quality related to the likelihood of clinically relevant anxiety symptom levels in this sample.

Assumptions underlying parametric analyses were evaluated. Normality of residuals in regression models was examined graphically and via skewness and kurtosis statistics, and homoscedasticity was assessed with plots of residuals against fitted values. In cases where assumptions were mildly violated, robust standard errors were computed to verify the stability of inference. Statistical significance thresholds were set at alpha = .05 for primary analyses, and corrections for multiple comparisons were applied for exploratory tests to reduce Type I error risk. The analytic plan was determined prior to data inspection except for routine data cleaning and exclusion decisions.

Results

Initial descriptive analyses characterized the distribution of sleep and anxiety measures in the sample. The mean global sleep quality score in the final sample was 7.8 with a standard deviation of 3.4, indicating a range of sleep experiences that included many participants reporting poor sleep. The mean anxiety score was 8.4 with a standard deviation of 5.6, reflecting a distribution with a subset of participants scoring above common clinical cutoffs for elevated anxiety symptoms. Frequencies of demographic variables indicated that the sample was predominantly female and composed mostly of undergraduates, and living arrangements varied across participants.

Reliability estimates confirmed acceptable internal consistency for the primary instruments. The sleep quality global index demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of .71 in this sample, which is within the commonly accepted range for research instruments. The anxiety symptom measure yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of .88, indicating strong internal consistency. These reliability values supported the use of the scales in correlational and regression analyses.

The primary analysis examined the association between global sleep quality and anxiety score using Pearson correlation. The correlation coefficient was r = .62, indicating a strong positive association between poorer sleep quality and higher anxiety symptoms. This correlation was statistically significant at p < .001, and the 95 percent confidence interval around the correlation excluded zero, reinforcing the robustness of the association. Inspection of a scatterplot showed a positive linear trend with some dispersion, and no individual cases exerted undue influence on the correlation as assessed by visual inspection and influence diagnostics.

Because demographic variables could plausibly impact both sleep and anxiety, partial correlations controlling for age and gender were computed. The partial correlation between sleep quality and anxiety, after adjusting for age and gender, remained substantial at r_partial = .59 and was statistically significant at p < .001. This suggests that the association between sleep quality and anxiety was not explained by simple demographic differences in the sample.

A multiple linear regression further examined the unique contribution of sleep quality to anxiety score after adjusting for covariates. In this regression model, global sleep quality was entered alongside age, gender (coded with female as the reference for interpretive purposes), and average daily caffeine consumption. The overall model explained 38 percent of the variance in anxiety scores (adjusted R^2 = .38). Global sleep quality was a significant predictor of anxiety in the model, with a standardized beta coefficient of .60 (t = 12.3, p < .001), indicating a large effect. Age and gender were not significant predictors in the presence of sleep quality, and caffeine consumption showed a small, non-significant association. The model residuals were approximately normally distributed and showed no evidence of severe heteroscedasticity, and robust standard errors produced similar p-values, indicating that the result was not driven by assumption violations.

Exploratory analyses examined associations between anxiety and individual sleep components. Sleep latency (difficulty falling asleep) had a correlation of r = .54 with anxiety symptoms, sleep disturbances (nocturnal awakenings and disruptions) correlated at r = .49, and daytime dysfunction correlated at r = .57. Sleep duration showed a weaker correlation of r = .28 with anxiety, suggesting that subjective duration alone was less strongly linked to anxiety than aspects related to initiation and daytime impairment. When the sleep components were entered simultaneously in a regression predicting anxiety, sleep latency and daytime dysfunction emerged as the most consistent unique predictors, whereas sleep duration did not contribute significant unique variance.

To examine associations in clinically interpretable terms, the anxiety score was dichotomized using the instrument’s recommended cutoff to indicate elevated anxiety symptoms. In this sample, 87 participants (34 percent) met or exceeded the cutoff for elevated anxiety symptoms. A logistic regression predicting elevated anxiety status from global sleep quality, controlling for age and gender, showed that each one-point increase in the sleep quality score (indicating worse sleep) was associated with an increased odds ratio of 1.45 for elevated anxiety status (95 percent CI [1.30, 1.62], p < .001). This result indicates that poorer sleep quality was meaningfully associated with higher likelihood of clinically relevant anxiety symptoms.

Sensitivity analyses tested whether excluding participants who reported current treatment for mental health concerns altered the pattern of results. After removing these participants, the Pearson correlation between sleep quality and anxiety remained strong (r = .58, p < .001), and the regression results were consistent with the primary analysis, suggesting the findings were robust to the exclusion of participants in treatment. Additional analyses examined whether living arrangements or year of study moderated the sleep–anxiety association by testing interaction terms in regression models; these interaction terms were not significant, indicating that the relationship between sleep quality and anxiety did not differ appreciably across these subgroups in this sample.

Assessment of potential confounds related to substance use indicated that self-reported alcohol consumption was modestly correlated with sleep quality (r = .18) and anxiety (r = .12). Including alcohol consumption in the regression model did not change the substantive association between sleep quality and anxiety, and the coefficient for sleep quality remained statistically significant and large in magnitude. These supplementary analyses provide evidence that the core association was not explained by simple covariation with common lifestyle variables measured in the study.

Overall, the data showed a robust, moderate-to-large positive association between poorer subjective sleep quality and higher levels of anxiety symptoms in this university sample. The pattern of associations across individual sleep components pointed to sleep initiation problems and daytime dysfunction as particularly relevant to anxiety. While causality cannot be inferred from these cross-sectional data, the observed effect sizes indicate that sleep quality is strongly related to anxiety symptom severity and to the likelihood of elevated anxiety symptomatology in this population.

Methods and results integrity checks confirmed that statistical assumptions were met or that analyses were robust to deviations from assumptions. All p-values reported are two-tailed. The principal findings were stable across sensitivity analyses and remained significant after controlling for a range of potential demographic and lifestyle confounders.

Discussion

The present study investigated the relationship between subjective sleep quality and levels of anxiety in a sample of university students using a correlational design. The findings indicated a strong positive association between poorer sleep quality and higher levels of anxiety symptoms. This association was robust to adjustment for age, gender, and common lifestyle variables, and it persisted in sensitivity analyses that excluded participants currently receiving mental health treatment. These results are consistent with a body of literature that has documented links between sleep disturbances and anxiety-related symptomatology in diverse samples.

One notable aspect of the findings is the magnitude of the association: a correlation of approximately .62 suggests a substantial relationship between the constructs in this sample. This magnitude is larger than many small-to-moderate effect sizes reported in some prior community studies, which might reflect the particular vulnerability of university students to both sleep disruption and anxiety. The university environment often features irregular schedules, academic deadlines, and social demands that can compromise sleep routines and heighten stress, thereby potentially amplifying associations between sleep problems and anxious symptoms. Additionally, the reliance on self-report measures may have contributed to the observed effect size via shared method variance, although the measures used demonstrated good internal consistency and the main association remained strong after adjusting for multiple covariates.

The pattern of results across sleep subcomponents sheds light on potentially important mechanisms. Problems with sleep initiation (i.e., prolonged sleep latency) and measures of daytime dysfunction showed particularly strong associations with anxiety symptoms, whereas total sleep duration exhibited a more modest correlation. This suggests that the subjective experience of difficulty falling asleep and consequent daytime impairments may be more salient correlates of anxiety than sleep duration per se. From a mechanistic perspective, prolonged sleep latency can reflect cognitive hyperarousal, worry, and physiological activation that are core features of anxiety. Daytime dysfunction may both result from poor nocturnal sleep and feed back into anxiety processes by reducing coping resources and increasing stress sensitivity. These dynamics are compatible with models that posit bidirectional links between sleep and emotional regulation.

The logistic regression analysis further translated the findings into clinically relevant terms by showing that worse sleep quality was associated with elevated odds of meeting a screening threshold for anxiety symptoms. From a public health standpoint, this implies that identifying and addressing sleep problems within university populations could have meaningful impacts on the prevalence of anxiety symptoms at a population level. Screening programs that incorporate brief sleep assessments alongside mental health screening might detect students who are at heightened risk and who could benefit from targeted interventions.

It is important to emphasize the limits of causal inference in this study. The cross-sectional design precludes definitive statements about whether poor sleep leads to increased anxiety, whether anxiety leads to poorer sleep, or whether shared upstream factors contribute to both. Longitudinal data and experimental manipulations are necessary to disentangle directionality. Nevertheless, the current findings provide a strong rationale for such longitudinal and intervention research by demonstrating a robust contemporaneous association in an at-risk population.

Several plausible causal pathways could underlie the observed association. One pathway posits that anxiety-driven cognitive hyperarousal and physiological activation interfere with the initiation and maintenance of sleep, which then exacerbates daytime anxiety through reduced restorative sleep and impaired emotional regulation. Another pathway suggests that chronic sleep disruption produces alterations in neural circuitry implicated in threat detection and emotion regulation, thereby increasing susceptibility to anxiety. A third possibility is that common factors, such as chronic stress, substance use, or circadian misalignment, predispose individuals to both poor sleep and heightened anxiety. The relative contribution of these pathways likely varies across individuals, and future research should aim to quantify these mechanisms, potentially through multimodal assessments that include objective sleep measures, biomarkers of stress and arousal, and ecological momentary assessment of daily stress and sleep processes.

The findings have potential implications for interventions within university settings. Sleep-focused interventions, including sleep hygiene education, cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), and brief online programs targeting sleep routines and sleep-related cognitions, have demonstrated efficacy for improving sleep and, in some trials, for reducing co-occurring mood symptoms. Given the observed association between sleep quality and anxiety, implementing scalable sleep interventions on campuses could plausibly reduce anxiety symptom burden among students. Such interventions may be particularly useful as adjuncts to traditional anxiety treatments or as preventive strategies for students not currently engaged in mental health care. The relative ease of delivering digital sleep interventions and their appeal to younger adults make them promising candidates for university mental health initiatives.

Nevertheless, several limitations qualify the interpretation and generalizability of the findings. Most prominently, the correlational cross-sectional design limits causal inference and may overstate the stability of the observed associations over time. The use of self-report measures, while pragmatic for large-scale surveys, introduces the possibility of reporting biases, social desirability effects, and common method variance. Although internal consistency for the scales was acceptable, self-report cannot capture objective aspects of sleep architecture that might reveal different patterns of association. The sample was drawn from a single university and was predominantly young and female, which may limit generalizability to other student populations or to nonstudent groups. Additionally, the study did not comprehensively assess all potential confounding variables, such as chronic medical conditions, medication use beyond sleep medication, or detailed measures of substance use and stress exposure, which could influence both sleep and anxiety.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs that can address temporal sequencing and potential causal relationships between sleep and anxiety. Prospective cohort studies that include multiple assessment waves across an academic year would help clarify whether changes in sleep precede changes in anxiety or vice versa. Experimental trials that target sleep (for example, randomized controlled trials of CBT-I or digital sleep interventions) and measure subsequent changes in anxiety symptoms would provide stronger evidence for causal effects. Incorporating objective sleep measures such as actigraphy or polysomnography in a subsample could illuminate which objective sleep parameters most closely map onto anxiety symptomatology and could validate self-report measures.

Further work should also examine heterogeneity within student populations. It will be valuable to determine whether the sleep–anxiety association differs by factors such as gender, socioeconomic status, international student status, and the presence of existing mental health diagnoses. Studies enrolling students with diagnosed anxiety disorders would clarify whether the same sleep patterns hold in clinical subgroups and whether sleep interventions are differentially effective among those with clinical versus subclinical anxiety.

Mechanistic research could benefit from integrating physiological measures of stress and arousal, such as heart rate variability and cortisol profiles, to test whether hyperarousal mediates the connection between sleep initiation problems and anxiety. Likewise, neuroimaging and cognitive assessments could examine whether sleep disturbances alter neural systems implicated in threat processing and executive control, thereby increasing anxiety vulnerability.

From a translational perspective, universities should consider incorporating sleep assessment into routine student health checks and offering accessible sleep resources. Low-intensity interventions delivered via mobile platforms have the potential to reach a large number of students and could be combined with screening and triage processes to identify those requiring higher-intensity care. Evaluations of such programmatic approaches should include cost-effectiveness analyses and assessments of downstream academic and well-being outcomes, in addition to mental health symptom change.

In conclusion, the present study provides robust evidence of a substantial association between poorer subjective sleep quality and higher levels of anxiety symptoms in a university student sample. The findings highlight the relevance of sleep initiation and daytime dysfunction in relation to anxiety and suggest that sleep-focused assessment and intervention may be a fruitful avenue for supporting student mental health. While causal inferences are limited by the cross-sectional design, the consistency and magnitude of the associations warrant further longitudinal and experimental investigation to determine causality and to inform intervention development. Addressing sleep problems in university populations holds promise as part of a comprehensive strategy for improving student well-being and reducing the burden of anxiety on academic and personal functioning.
