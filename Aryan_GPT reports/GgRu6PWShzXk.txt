Introduction

Social influence is an enduring and well-documented force in human behavior that shapes choices and attitudes across many domains of life. Research over decades has shown that individuals often align their decisions with perceived norms, with the behaviors of peers, and with cues that signal what others approve of or have already done. Both classic laboratory work and contemporary field studies have demonstrated that people are sensitive to social information when making judgments about trivial and consequential matters alike. The mechanisms that drive social influence have been characterized broadly as normative influence, whereby people conform to gain social acceptance, and informational influence, whereby people conform because others are perceived as a source of accurate information. These dual-process perspectives help explain a range of phenomena from conformity in perceptual tasks to persuasion in political and consumer contexts. Social proof, a related concept popularized in social psychology and behavioral economics, captures the idea that the observed actions of others provide cues about what is appropriate or effective in a given situation. Social proof has been used to predict and alter behaviors such as donating to charity, choosing products, and complying with requests. The present study situates itself within this extensive body of work by examining how social influence affects a decision situated in a legal and civic context: whether people will sign a petition calling for a harsh or lenient outcome for a convicted individual.

Decisions about punishment, clemency, and legal outcomes are often thought to rest on legal principles, evidence, and moral reasoning, but public reactions to crime and punishment are also shaped by social context. Public opinion about criminal justice policies, sentencing reform, and individual cases can be influenced by media coverage, organizational advocacy, and visible grassroots mobilization. From a theoretical standpoint, legal decision-making in public arenas can be conceptualized as subject to the same social informational and normative pressures that affect other types of decisions. For instance, when an advocacy group displays a high number of signatures on a petition or when prominent figures endorse a stance, observers may infer widespread support and be more likely to adopt the majority position. Conversely, the absence of visible support may leave individuals more likely to rely on their independent judgments or to abstain from public actions such as signing.

Studies examining online petitions, civic engagement, and collective action have found that cues about others’ participation can substantially affect rates of engagement. Experimental work has demonstrated that social information—like a displayed count of prior signers or visible endorsements—can boost participation in petitions, increase donations, and alter voting behavior. These findings are consistent with the idea that individuals derive not only informational signals from others but also reputational cues: participating in an action that many others have already taken can signal that the action is acceptable, valued, and low risk. On the other hand, when petitions or calls to action run counter to prevailing norms or when the cause is morally contentious, the effect of social information may be attenuated or may operate differently depending on whether informational or normative considerations dominate.

The legal content of a petition, such as a call to free or to prosecute an individual, introduces valence and moral judgment into the decision. People may differ in their baseline inclination to sign petitions that are sympathetic to an offender compared to petitions that advocate for punishment. Factors such as political ideology, personal experiences with crime, and fundamental moral values can shape these baseline tendencies. Some research on public attitudes toward crime suggests that punitive impulses are common, especially when crimes are framed as threatening or salient. However, other work suggests that when mitigating circumstances, rehabilitation narratives, or fairness issues are highlighted, public support for leniency can increase. Given these potentially competing forces, the interaction between social influence and petition valence is theoretically interesting: social cues could amplify baseline tendencies toward punitiveness or leniency, or they could override those tendencies by signaling broad support for either outcome.

Despite the intuitive relevance of social influence to legal and civic behavior, fewer experimental studies have directly compared the effect of social information across opposite petition valences—namely, petitions advocating for prosecution versus petitions advocating for release or clemency. Understanding whether social cues have symmetric effects across these valence domains speaks to the generality of social influence mechanisms and to practical concerns about how advocacy campaigns operate. If social influence increases support equally for calls to punish and calls to free, this would suggest that the cue acts as a generic mobilizer, encouraging conformity to perceived majority action irrespective of moral content. Alternatively, if social influence is more effective for one valence than the other, this would suggest boundary conditions for social influence in moralized contexts and might point to interactions between moral convictions and social cues.

The present experiment was designed to probe these issues directly by presenting participants with a fictitious case involving an individual, referred to as Paul Bennet, who had been sentenced to a 30-year prison term. Participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions in a 2 (social influence: social vs. non-social) by 2 (petition content: free vs. prosecute) between-subjects design. The social influence manipulation sought to create a clear cue indicating that many others had already acted by displaying a count of prior signers and a short simulated list of anonymous endorsements. The non-social condition presented the same petition without any indication of prior signers. Petition content was systematically varied so that in one variant the petition requested that authorities free Paul Bennet from his sentence and in the other variant the petition requested that authorities prosecute Paul Bennet further or uphold his sentence. This factorial design allows for the assessment of a main effect of social influence on participation as well as any main effect of petition valence and potential interaction effects.

We predicted that the social influence cue would increase the likelihood that participants would sign the petition relative to the non-social cue, consistent with prior research showing that social information increases participation in petitions and other civic behaviors. Regarding petition valence, we did not have a strong directional hypothesis because competing effects were plausible: petitions advocating for punitive outcomes might attract more signatures due to baseline punitiveness in public opinion, whereas petitions advocating for release might attract more signatures if participants responded to perceived injustice or rehabilitation narratives. We did, however, anticipate the possibility of an interaction such that social influence might amplify whichever baseline tendency was present in a given sample. In particular, if participants were more likely overall to favor prosecution, social cues might further boost signatures for prosecution petitions; conversely, if there was more sympathy for clemency in the sample, social cues might preferentially boost signatures for release petitions.

Beyond testing these primary hypotheses, the experiment also allowed for exploratory analyses of demographic moderators and individual differences. Political ideology, for instance, is likely to correlate with attitudes toward criminal justice and could plausibly moderate responses to petition valence or to social information. Age, gender, and prior exposure to legal issues might also play roles. We included brief measures of these variables to examine whether the hypothesized social influence effect was robust across subgroups or whether it was driven by a particular demographic. Finally, the study aimed to contribute to applied conversations about how social influence functions in civic contexts and to identify boundary conditions on the persuasive power of social proof in legal and moral domains. The following sections describe the methods used in detail, report the statistical analyses and outcomes, and discuss the implications of the findings for theory and practice.

Method

Participants were recruited from an online research platform that provides access to a diverse U.S. adult sample. Recruitment targeted adults aged 18 and older, and participation was restricted to individuals who were fluent in English. A total of 420 individuals began the study. Of these, 12 were excluded for failing embedded attention checks or providing implausible open-ended responses, and 8 were excluded because they did not finish the survey. The final sample therefore consisted of 400 participants. The sample size was chosen with the goal of achieving adequate power to detect small-to-medium effects in a 2x2 between-subjects design. Prior to data collection we conducted a sensitivity analysis which suggested that with N = 400 we would have approximately 80% power to detect an effect size equivalent to a small-to-medium chi-square (w ≈ 0.14) for the main effect of social influence at an alpha of .05.

Participants ranged in age from 18 to 78 years old, with a mean age of 36.8 years and a standard deviation of 11.2 years. The sample was 52% female, 47% male, and 1% identifying as non-binary or another gender identity. Participants reported a variety of educational backgrounds, with approximately 28% holding a bachelor’s degree, 35% having some college experience, 22% with a high school diploma, and the remaining participants reporting graduate-level education or vocational training. Political ideology was measured on a seven-point scale ranging from “very liberal” to “very conservative,” with a mean of 4.1 and a standard deviation of 1.6, indicating a roughly centrist-leaning sample with heterogeneity in political orientation.

The study used a 2 (social influence: social vs. non-social) by 2 (petition content: free vs. prosecute) between-subjects design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four experimental conditions using the survey platform’s randomization function, which ensured roughly equal cell sizes. The four cells each contained 100 participants. The experimental materials consisted of a short vignette introducing a fictitious defendant and a petition interface that simulated an online petition environment. The vignette described Paul Bennet as an individual who had been convicted of a serious crime and had received a 30-year prison sentence. Care was taken to present the case neutrally, avoiding graphic details or emotive language that could differentially bias participants beyond the valence manipulation. The petition text was short and straightforward; the “free” version requested that relevant authorities release Paul Bennet or reconsider his sentence, whereas the “prosecute” version requested that authorities pursue further prosecution or ensure that the sentence would be upheld. Both versions were matched for length and reading complexity.

The social influence manipulation was operationalized by including or excluding visible cues about prior signers. In the social influence condition, participants viewing the petition saw a prominent line stating “Over 1,200 people have already signed this petition,” accompanied by a simulated, scrollable list of 20 anonymized signers (first names and initials only) and a small graphic indicating recent signatures. The numbers and the list were conspicuous but not presented in a way that would overwhelm the page. In the non-social condition, the petition page lacked any mention of prior signers and presented only the petition text and the call-to-action button. Both versions included an identical call-to-action button labeled “Sign the petition.” Participants were explicitly told that signing would register their support, and the action was completed within the survey interface. Signing was a behavioral measure coded dichotomously as signed versus did not sign.

Before exposing participants to the vignette, the survey collected informed consent and included a brief attention check to ensure participants were reading instructions carefully. Following the petition phase, participants completed measures assessing their perceptions of the petition and its legitimacy. These included Likert-type items measuring perceived social normativity (e.g., “It seemed like many people support this petition”), perceived moral rightness of the petition, and perceived credibility of the petition source. These items were included to test whether the manipulation successfully altered participants’ perceptions of social support and to examine whether perceptions mediated the relationship between the manipulation and signing behavior. Participants also reported their level of familiarity with legal proceedings and their previous experience signing petitions, to examine whether prior behavior predicted response patterns.

Demographic information including age, gender, education level, and political ideology was collected at the end of the survey. The study protocol and materials were reviewed and approved by the investigators’ institutional review board prior to data collection. Participants received modest monetary compensation consistent with the platform’s norms and with ethical guidelines for fair payment. Participation was anonymous; no identifying information beyond the platform’s pseudonymous participant ID was retained by the researchers.

Data preparation involved coding the primary dependent variable, checking randomization balance across cells, and screening for outliers or suspicious response patterns. Randomization checks indicated that demographic variables were evenly distributed across the four conditions, with no cell differing from the overall sample on age, gender, education, or political ideology by more than one percentage point on categorical variables or by more than 0.2 standard deviations on continuous variables. The attention-check failures and incomplete responses were removed as pre-registered, leaving a final analytic sample of 400 participants distributed evenly across the four cells.

The primary analyses tested the main effect of social influence on petition signing using chi-square tests and logistic regression, the main effect of petition content, and the interaction between social influence and petition content. Secondary analyses examined whether perceived social normativity mediated the effect of the manipulation on signing and whether demographic variables moderated the main effects. Mediation analyses used bootstrapped confidence intervals to assess indirect effects, and moderators were tested via interaction terms in logistic regression models. Effect sizes and confidence intervals were reported to provide a clear sense of the magnitude and precision of the observed effects.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary dependent variable indicated that overall, 60% of participants (240 out of 400) signed the petition. Rates of signing varied by experimental condition. In the social influence conditions, 70% of participants signed the petition (140 out of 200), whereas in the non-social conditions 50% of participants signed (100 out of 200). When broken down by petition content, the “free” petition received 61% signatures overall (122 out of 200), and the “prosecute” petition received 59% signatures (118 out of 200). The observed pattern therefore suggested a notable main effect of the social influence manipulation and little difference across petition content.

A chi-square test was conducted to examine the association between social influence condition and signing. The test revealed a statistically significant relationship, with social influence associated with higher signing rates, chi-square(1, N = 400) = 16.67, p < .001. The phi coefficient for this association was .20, indicating a small-to-medium effect size. This result supports the primary prediction that social information would increase the likelihood of petition signing.

Next, a chi-square test examined the effect of petition content (free vs. prosecute) on signing. The difference in signing rates between the two petition types was small and not statistically significant, chi-square(1, N = 400) = 0.17, p = .68. The phi coefficient for this comparison was .02, indicating a negligible effect size. These analyses suggest that petition valence alone did not substantially alter participants’ decisions to sign in this sample.

To test whether there was an interaction between social influence and petition content, a two-by-two contingency table analysis was performed and a logistic regression including an interaction term was estimated. The contingency table showed that in the social-free condition 71% of participants signed (71/100), in the social-prosecute condition 69% signed (69/100), in the non-social-free condition 51% signed (51/100), and in the non-social-prosecute condition 49% signed (49/100). Although the raw percentages show slight variation, the interaction did not reach statistical significance. The logistic regression including predictors for social influence (coded 1 = social, 0 = non-social), petition content (coded 1 = free, 0 = prosecute), and their interaction yielded a significant coefficient for social influence (b = 0.91, SE = 0.23, Wald z = 3.96, p < .001; odds ratio = 2.48, 95% CI [1.62, 3.78]), a non-significant coefficient for petition content (b = 0.07, SE = 0.21, Wald z = 0.33, p = .74; odds ratio = 1.07, 95% CI [0.70, 1.63]), and a non-significant interaction term (b = -0.05, SE = 0.30, Wald z = -0.17, p = .87; odds ratio = 0.95, 95% CI [0.52, 1.73]). These findings indicate a robust main effect of social influence but no evidence of a main effect of petition valence or of an interaction between the two factors.

Additional logistic regression models tested whether key demographic variables altered the observed pattern. In models that included political ideology, age, gender, and education as covariates, the main effect of social influence remained significant and of similar magnitude (adjusted odds ratio = 2.40, 95% CI [1.57, 3.66], p < .001). None of the demographic covariates exhibited a significant main effect that changed the core result, though political ideology showed a small negative association with signing such that more conservative respondents were slightly less likely to sign overall (b = -0.18 per unit increase on the 7-point ideology scale, SE = 0.09, p = .045; odds ratio = 0.84, 95% CI [0.71, 0.99]). Importantly, adding interaction terms between social influence and each demographic variable did not reveal significant moderators; the social influence effect was stable across age, gender, education, and political ideology groups in this sample.

Perception measures confirmed that the manipulation influenced participants’ sense of perceived social normativity. Participants in the social influence condition rated the petition as more socially normative (mean = 5.1 on a 7-point scale) than participants in the non-social condition (mean = 3.2), t(398) = 16.8, p < .001. Perceived credibility of the petition source did not differ substantially between conditions, and moral rightness ratings did not differ significantly by condition either. A mediation analysis using bootstrapped confidence intervals examined whether perceived social normativity mediated the effect of the social influence manipulation on signing behavior. The indirect effect was statistically significant (indirect effect estimate = 0.42, 95% bootstrap CI [0.28, 0.58]), suggesting that part of the effect of the manipulation on signing operated through increases in perceived social normativity. The direct effect of the manipulation on signing remained significant after accounting for perceived normativity (direct effect in logistic model b = 0.54, SE = 0.21, p = .01), indicating partial mediation.

Exploratory analyses examined whether prior petition-signing behavior predicted current signing. Participants who reported having signed petitions previously were more likely to sign in the present study (67% vs. 53% for those without prior petition experience), chi-square(1, N = 400) = 8.16, p = .004, phi = .14. However, including prior petition behavior as a covariate did not eliminate the social influence effect; social influence remained a significant predictor of signing in models that controlled for prior behavior.

Given the absence of significant differences between the free and prosecute petition types, we conducted a sensitivity check to ensure that participants were reading and distinguishing the content of the two petitions. An item that asked participants to summarize the petition’s requested outcome showed that 94% of participants correctly identified whether the petition called for freeing or prosecuting Paul Bennet, indicating that participants understood the valence manipulation. Therefore, the null effect for petition content is unlikely to be attributable to participants’ failure to attend to the petition’s message.

Finally, we examined response latencies to see if social influence affected decision speed, which can be informative about whether the manipulation promoted more heuristic or deliberative responding. Median response times for the decision to sign were slightly faster in the social condition (median = 9.4 seconds) than in the non-social condition (median = 11.2 seconds), and this difference reached statistical significance in a nonparametric test (Mann-Whitney U = 17,450, p = .03). This result suggests that social cues may have facilitated quicker decisions to sign, consistent with a heuristic social-proof mechanism. Overall, the results provide robust evidence that visible social support increases petition signing behavior in this legal-context vignette, while the valence of the petition—whether it called to free or to prosecute—did not significantly influence the decision to sign in this sample.

Discussion

The present experiment examined whether social influence, operationalized as visible evidence of prior signers, would affect the likelihood that people sign a petition calling either for the release or the prosecution of a fictitious individual who had received a long prison sentence. The principal finding was clear and consistent: participants exposed to social information indicating substantial prior support were significantly more likely to sign the petition than participants who were not presented with such information. This effect was robust across analyses, yielded a small-to-medium effect size, and remained significant after controlling for demographic covariates. In contrast, the valence of the petition—whether it advocated freeing Paul Bennet or prosecuting him further—had no appreciable influence on signing behavior in this study. Moreover, there was no evidence of an interaction such that social influence differentially affected one petition valence relative to the other. These results have implications for our understanding of social influence in civic and legal contexts, for theories of social proof and normative influence, and for practical concerns about advocacy strategies.

From a theoretical perspective, the finding that social information increased petition signing supports the broad generality of social influence mechanisms. The social cue used in this study signaled descriptive normativity—that many others had already acted—and this information functioned as a persuasive cue that increased participation in a public action. The mediation analysis indicated that participants’ perceptions of the petition’s social normativity partially explained the effect, which aligns with models that emphasize the role of perceived majority behavior in shaping individual choices. The faster response times in the social condition further suggest that social cues can elicit relatively quick, heuristic decisions consistent with social-proof processing rather than solely slow deliberation. This pattern dovetails with prior work showing that visible counts of prior participants can lower thresholds for action by reducing perceived risk, increasing perceived legitimacy, or signaling social endorsement.

The null effect of petition valence is an interesting and important outcome. It suggests that in this sample and with the way the petition was framed, people’s baseline propensity to sign was not strongly determined by whether the petition advocated for punitive or lenient outcomes. Several interpretations of this result are possible. One interpretation is that the vignette and petition framing were relatively neutral and did not evoke a strong moral valence that would differentially motivate signatures for punishment versus clemency. We took care to present the case without graphic or emotionally loaded content, which may have reduced potency of valence-driven differences. A second interpretation is that the action of signing a petition is itself influenced more by process cues (e.g., perceived social support) than by fine-grained content when the content does not trigger intense moral convictions. The petition context may put particular emphasis on social information as a cue to action; in other words, signing may be more of a social behavior than a pure expression of moral judgment in some circumstances. A third interpretation is that the sample’s heterogeneity—politically and demographically—resulted in opposing tendencies across participants that averaged out at the aggregate level. For instance, differences between liberals and conservatives in attitudes toward criminal justice could have produced canceling effects. However, exploratory moderator analyses did not find clear evidence that political ideology moderated the absence of a valence effect, making this explanation less likely in these data.

The lack of interaction between social influence and petition valence indicates that social-proof effects may operate similarly across morally divergent messages, at least under the experimental conditions tested here. This symmetry has practical implications: social cues that signal broad participation may be equally effective in promoting support for punitive actions and for leniency campaigns. For practitioners and advocates, this suggests that mobilization strategies that highlight existing engagement can boost participation regardless of the moral direction of the cause. At the same time, this symmetry raises questions about the ethical use of social cues, since such cues can potentially be used to amplify support for both socially beneficial and harmful causes. The findings underscore the power and neutrality of social proof as a mobilizing force.

The present work also contributes to discussions about the boundaries of social influence. While much prior research has documented robust social effects, there has been less experimental work that directly juxtaposes opposing moral appeals in a legal context. By showing that social cues increase signing behavior even in a context involving a severe sentence, the study provides evidence that social influence is active in civic domains characterized by moral and legal content. It also highlights that social influence is not necessarily contingent on alignment with moral intuitions; instead, it can sway behavior by altering perceived norms and cues to action.

Despite these contributions, there are important limitations to consider. First, the study used a vignette and a simulated petition interface within an online survey environment, which may not capture all aspects of real-world petitioning dynamics. Field contexts involve more diverse cues, social interactions, and potential consequences, and responses observed in an online experiment may differ in magnitude in natural settings. Second, the fictitious nature of the defendant and the neutral framing may have tempered the emotional engagement of participants. Future research could examine whether stronger narratives, vivid personal stories, or the presence of mitigating or aggravating details would interact with social cues in different ways. Third, the social influence manipulation relied on a particular operationalization—an explicit count of prior signers and a short list of anonymous names—and other forms of social proof (e.g., endorsements from known figures, demographic composition of signers, or visible comments) might produce different effects or interact with petition valence differently.

Another limitation concerns sample generalizability. Although the online sample was reasonably diverse, it is not representative of any particular population beyond the platform’s user base. Cultural, legal, and political contexts vary, and social influence effects might be larger or smaller in different populations, particularly where civic engagement norms vary or trust in institutions diverges. Additionally, while the study included demographic covariates and exploratory moderators, it did not measure or model every possible individual difference that could matter—such as personality traits, need for cognition, or moral foundations—which could moderate responsiveness to social cues.

Future research should build on these findings in several directions. One promising avenue is to vary the credibility and source of social cues. For example, cues that indicate endorsement by local community members, experts, or celebrities might have differential effects on signing behavior and might interact with petition content in important ways. Manipulating the magnitude of reported prior signers (e.g., tens versus thousands) could map out dose-response curves of social influence. Alternatively, making the signers' demographic characteristics salient might reveal whether similarity to self moderates the influence of social proof. Another direction is to examine long-term effects: does being exposed to social cues about a petition affect subsequent political behavior, attitudes, or willingness to advocate on related issues? Longitudinal designs could assess whether social-proof interventions have persistent effects beyond immediate participation.

Researchers should also investigate boundary conditions related to moral intensity and content. It would be informative to test whether social influence effects are attenuated or reversed when petitions involve extremely polarizing issues, clear violations of social norms, or highly salient victims. Similarly, exploring the interaction of social cues with message framing—such as emphasizing injustice, rehabilitation, deterrence, or community safety—could clarify when social proof enhances or undermines movement goals. Experimental manipulations that incorporate multimedia elements, real-time social feedback, or opportunities for public visibility of one’s support could further approximate real advocacy environments and yield richer insights.

From a policy and practical standpoint, the findings suggest that organizers and communicators should be mindful of the power of displaying social support. On one hand, highlighting widespread participation can be an effective and relatively low-cost strategy for increasing engagement. On the other hand, regulators and platforms that host petitions may need to consider how such cues shape public discourse and whether interventions are warranted to prevent manipulation or misinformation. Transparency about the origins of signature counts and safeguards against fabricated social cues could be important to maintain the integrity of civic engagement processes.

In sum, this experiment demonstrates that visible social support increases the likelihood that individuals will sign petitions in a legal-context scenario, while the petition’s moral valence—advocating freedom or prosecution—did not significantly alter signing rates in this sample. The results highlight social influence as a powerful, broadly applicable mechanism that can mobilize action across different issue domains. They also encourage further inquiry into the nuances of how social information interacts with content, source credibility, and individual differences. Understanding these dynamics is essential not only for theories of social influence but also for practical efforts to foster responsible civic engagement and to ensure that public opinion formation processes are transparent and fair.
