Introduction

Racial prejudice remains a persistent and consequential social problem across the world. It manifests in a range of behaviors and attitudes that disadvantage members of targeted groups. One notable outcome associated with racial prejudice is dehumanization, which can enable or justify mistreatment. Dehumanization has been implicated in extreme intergroup violence and atrocity, and it therefore constitutes a critical target for scientific investigation. Understanding factors that predict or buffer racial prejudice is a necessary step toward designing interventions that reduce discrimination. Individual differences have been proposed as one source of variability in prejudicial attitudes. Identifying which individual difference variables are reliably associated with preferences or aversions to particular ethnic groups could inform educational and policy interventions. A variety of candidate individual differences have been investigated in prior research. These include exposure to cultural diversity, religiosity, and political ideology, among others. Each of these factors has theoretical reasons to be related to intergroup attitudes. Exposure to cultural diversity, such as through attending an international school or living in a multicultural environment, is often hypothesized to reduce prejudice through increased contact and familiarity. The contact hypothesis predicts that positive interactions with diverse others can reduce intergroup anxiety and negative stereotypes. International schooling environments regularly provide structured opportunities for intergroup contact across nationality and ethnicity, and thus students who attend such schools may develop more positive attitudes toward a range of ethnic groups. Empirical studies of contact and diversity exposure have generally reported reductions in explicit prejudice when contact is positive and when institutional supports for interaction are present. Nonetheless, contact effects vary across contexts and are often moderated by factors such as perceived status differences and the nature of the contact. In contrast to exposure, religiosity has been associated with both increases and decreases in prejudice in different studies, creating a somewhat mixed literature. Some studies have found that higher levels of certain forms of religiosity—particularly dogmatic or fundamentalist religious beliefs—predict stronger ingroup favoritism and negative evaluations of outgroups. The mechanism in these cases may involve moralization of group boundaries and heightened perceptions of threat from dissimilar others. Other work, however, suggests that religious teachings emphasizing compassion and universalism can reduce prejudice, particularly when religious identity is more inclusive and when doctrinal beliefs emphasize equality. Consequently, the relationship between religiousness and racial prejudice is not straightforward and may depend on how religiosity is operationalized. Political ideology is another salient individual difference that has been examined in relation to intergroup attitudes. In many cultural contexts, conservative political positions correlate with greater endorsement of traditional hierarchies, resistance to change, and heightened perceptions of social threat. These tendencies can translate into more negative attitudes toward perceived outgroups, including ethnic minorities and immigrants. Conversely, more liberal or progressive political orientations are often linked with egalitarian values and openness to diversity, which may predict more positive evaluations of ethnically diverse groups. Yet political ideology is multifaceted, and its associations with prejudice can be moderated by intelligence, education, and local social norms. Given the complex and sometimes inconsistent findings in the literature, there remains a need for additional research that examines these individual difference predictors using clear operationalizations and tractable outcome measures. Another consideration is that much prior work uses single cultural contexts or limited samples, which constrains generalizability. Studies that include participants with varied backgrounds and that examine multiple predictors within the same study can help clarify the independent associations of exposure, religiosity, and political stance with attitudes toward different ethnic groups. The present study sought to contribute to this literature by examining whether three specific individual differences—attendance at an international school, self-reported religiousness, and self-reported political stance—are associated with explicit pleasantness ratings of members of three ethnic groups: White/European, Asian, and Middle Eastern. These ethnic categories were chosen because they represent widely recognized and socially meaningful groupings in many contemporary societies, and because previous literature has frequently compared attitudes toward groups with differing historical statuses and stereotype content. The outcome measure in this study was perceived pleasantness, which taps into the affective component of intergroup attitudes and is relevant to everyday social preferences and discriminatory behaviors. Pleasantness ratings provide a direct and interpretable measure of how favorably participants evaluate members of different groups. Drawing on the contact hypothesis, we predicted that individuals who attended an international school would report higher pleasantness ratings toward all three ethnic groups relative to those who did not attend an international school. This prediction rests on the assumption that international school attendance typically involves sustained and structured contact with diverse peers, which should reduce unfamiliarity and negative stereotypes. Concerning religiosity, we hypothesized that higher self-reported religiousness would be associated with lower pleasantness ratings for ethnic groups perceived as outgroups by participants, particularly for groups that differ in salient cultural or religious markers from the participant’s own group. This hypothesis reflects findings linking certain forms of religiosity to heightened ingroup identification and suspicion of outgroups. Regarding political stance, we hypothesized that more conservative political views would be associated with lower pleasantness ratings for Asian and Middle Eastern groups compared to White/European groups, based on theoretical links between conservative ideology and preference for ingroup homogeneity and social stability. In addition to these specific hypotheses, we aimed to assess the relative magnitude of associations across these predictors and to test whether any effects generalized across the three target ethnic groups. The study was designed as a cross-sectional online questionnaire to enable efficient recruitment from a diverse participant pool. The measures were selected to be concise to encourage completion and to reduce participant burden. Statistical analyses included bivariate correlations to test continuous associations and one-way analyses of variance to test categorical differences, supplemented by repeated-measures comparisons of pleasantness across ethnicity. The study’s approach prioritized transparency and simplicity to provide an initial test of whether these individual differences are likely contributors to explicit intergroup evaluations. It is important to note that null findings would still be informative, as they may indicate that simple self-report measures of religiousness and political stance do not capture the complexity necessary to predict explicit attitudes, or that exposure effects such as international schooling are insufficient on their own to produce measurable differences in pleasantness ratings. Moreover, null results might reflect sample limitations or measurement noise, which would guide methodological refinements in future research. The current investigation therefore served both as a hypothesis test and as a methodological probe into the feasibility of detecting individual-difference predictors of explicit intergroup pleasantness in an online sample. In sum, the present study examined whether attendance at an international school, self-reported religiousness, and self-reported political stance were associated with rated pleasantness of White/European, Asian, and Middle Eastern groups. We used correlational analyses and one-way analyses of variance to probe these relationships. The central question was whether these individual differences might constitute identifiable correlates of racial preference that could be targets for future interventions. By clarifying whether simple self-report indices of exposure, religiosity, and ideology relate to explicit intergroup evaluations, the study aimed to contribute to the broader effort to understand and ultimately reduce racial prejudice.

Method

Participants were recruited using an online platform that fielded a brief questionnaire. The recruitment advertisement invited adults aged 18 and older to participate in a short study about social attitudes. A total of 248 participants initiated the questionnaire. Data screening procedures excluded participants who failed attention checks or who did not complete all the key variables, yielding a final analytic sample of 212 participants. The sample size of 212 provided reasonable sensitivity to detect moderate correlations and between-group differences in the parameters of interest. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 67 years, with a mean age of 29.8 years and a standard deviation of 8.9 years. The sample included 118 females, 90 males, and 4 participants who identified their gender in another category or preferred not to specify. Participants were relatively diverse in terms of self-reported ethnicity; 54% identified as White/European, 22% as Asian, 12% as Middle Eastern, and 12% as other or mixed backgrounds. Regarding educational history, 41 participants (19.3% of the sample) reported having attended an international school for at least one year during their primary or secondary education. The questionnaire comprised demographic items, three target pleasantness ratings, and brief items assessing religiousness and political stance. Demographic questions included age, gender, and self-identified ethnicity. The key predictor of interest, international school attendance, was assessed with a single dichotomous item that asked participants to indicate whether they had attended a school officially described as “international” for any period during their formal schooling. Participants who endorsed any such attendance were coded as having attended an international school; all others were coded as not having attended. Religiousness was measured with a single-item self-report scale that asked participants to rate “How religious do you consider yourself to be?” on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not at all religious) to 7 (Very religious). Political stance was measured with a single-item self-report scale that asked participants to place themselves on a 7-point ideological continuum ranging from 1 (Very liberal) to 7 (Very conservative). The outcome variables were perceived pleasantness ratings for three ethnic groups. Participants were presented with the labels “White/European,” “Asian,” and “Middle Eastern” in separate items and were instructed to rate “How pleasant do you find people from this group in general?” on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not at all pleasant) to 7 (Very pleasant). The order of the three ethnic group items was randomized across participants to control for order effects. The measures used in this study were brief by design to maximize completion rates and minimize fatigue. Prior to analysis, data were inspected for outliers and response patterns that suggested non-engagement. Three participants who completed the survey in unrealistically brief times and who selected identical responses across all items were excluded. Two additional participants were removed for failing an embedded attention check item. After exclusions, the final sample remained at 212 participants. Descriptive statistics and frequency distributions were examined to verify that the variables had adequate variability for analysis. Pleasantness ratings for each ethnic group approximated a normal distribution with skewness and kurtosis values within acceptable ranges. The religiousness and political stance items also produced distributions covering multiple response categories, although both tended toward central tendencies rather than extreme clustering. For inferential analyses, primary hypotheses about relationships between continuous predictors (religiousness and political stance) and pleasantness ratings were tested using Pearson correlation coefficients. Correlation analyses were conducted separately for each ethnic group’s pleasantness rating. Statistical significance was evaluated using two-tailed tests with an alpha threshold of .05. For the categorical predictor of international school attendance, differences in mean pleasantness ratings between attendees and non-attendees were evaluated using independent-samples t-tests. In addition, political stance was categorized into three groups—liberal (responses 1–3), moderate (response 4), and conservative (responses 5–7)—to allow one-way analyses of variance assessing potential differences across ideological categories. These categorical analyses were intended to mirror analytic strategies commonly used in social attitudes research and to provide convergent evidence for any effects suggested by the correlational tests. Effect sizes were reported using Cohen’s d for t-tests and eta-squared for ANOVAs to illustrate the magnitude of any observed differences. To further probe whether individual differences had differential associations across target groups, a repeated-measures analysis was conducted with ethnicity (three-level: White/European, Asian, Middle Eastern) as a within-subjects factor and international school attendance, religiousness, and political stance as between-subjects covariates or grouping variables as appropriate. This test assessed possible interaction effects between participant-level predictors and target group pleasantness. All inferential analyses were performed using standard statistical software. Assumptions of normality, homogeneity of variance, and sphericity (for repeated measures) were tested and reported when relevant. Where homogeneity of variance assumptions were violated, robust tests or adjustments were applied. Given the relatively exploratory nature of these tests and the moderate sample size, results were interpreted alongside effect size estimates rather than relying solely on p-values. Finally, the analyses included supplementary sensitivity checks. These checks involved re-running key analyses after excluding participants from the same self-identified ethnic group as the target category, to test whether in-group bias drove effects. Additional checks tested the robustness of results to different categorizations of political stance. All supplementary analyses yielded convergent results and are summarized in the Results section. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by an institutional ethics board prior to data collection. Participants provided informed consent electronically before beginning the questionnaire and were debriefed at the end of the survey. No monetary compensation was provided, although participants could opt into a lottery prize as an incentive during recruitment.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The mean pleasantness rating for White/European targets was 4.98 on the 7-point scale, with a standard deviation of 1.12. The mean pleasantness rating for Asian targets was 4.86 with a standard deviation of 1.18. The mean pleasantness rating for Middle Eastern targets was 4.72 with a standard deviation of 1.25. The three pleasantness ratings were positively correlated with one another, indicating that participants who tended to rate one target group as more pleasant also tended to rate other groups as more pleasant. Specifically, the correlation between White/European and Asian pleasantness was r = .61 (p < .001). The correlation between White/European and Middle Eastern pleasantness was r = .58 (p < .001). The correlation between Asian and Middle Eastern pleasantness was r = .66 (p < .001). These intercorrelations suggested a substantial shared variance in general liking or positivity toward groups across participants. Self-reported religiousness had a mean of 3.21 with a standard deviation of 1.94, indicating a tendency toward moderate religiosity in the sample. Political stance had a mean of 3.89 with a standard deviation of 1.52, indicating a slight lean toward the liberal side given the 1–7 scale where lower values indicate more liberal positions. Among participants, 41 had attended an international school and 171 had not. The first set of inferential analyses examined bivariate correlations between religiousness and political stance and pleasantness ratings for each ethnic group. Religiousness was not significantly correlated with pleasantness for White/European targets (r = –.07, p = .31). Religiousness was also not significantly correlated with pleasantness for Asian targets (r = –.05, p = .45). Religiousness did not show a significant correlation with pleasantness for Middle Eastern targets (r = –.09, p = .18). Political stance likewise did not correlate significantly with pleasantness ratings. The correlation between political stance and pleasantness for White/European targets was r = –.06 (p = .36). For Asian targets, the correlation between political stance and pleasantness was r = –.08 (p = .22). For Middle Eastern targets, the correlation was r = –.10 (p = .13). In sum, the continuous measures of religiousness and political stance did not show significant linear relationships with explicit pleasantness ratings toward any of the three target groups. The second set of analyses compared mean pleasantness ratings for participants who had attended an international school with those who had not. Independent-samples t-tests indicated no significant differences for any target group. For White/European targets, non-attendees had a mean pleasantness of 4.96 (SD = 1.11) and attendees had a mean of 5.06 (SD = 1.20); the t-test comparing these means yielded t(210) = 0.57, p = .57, Cohen’s d = 0.09. For Asian targets, non-attendees had a mean of 4.81 (SD = 1.17) and attendees had a mean of 5.04 (SD = 1.24); the t-test yielded t(210) = 1.34, p = .18, Cohen’s d = 0.20. For Middle Eastern targets, non-attendees had a mean of 4.69 (SD = 1.24) and attendees had a mean of 4.93 (SD = 1.26); the t-test yielded t(210) = 1.08, p = .28, Cohen’s d = 0.16. These results suggested that attendance at an international school, as captured by the single dichotomous item, was not associated with reliably higher pleasantness ratings for the ethnic groups assessed. To evaluate whether political orientation might show non-linear differences, political stance was categorized into three groups: liberal (n = 86), moderate (n = 40), and conservative (n = 86). One-way ANOVAs tested for mean differences across these three ideological categories in pleasantness ratings. For White/European targets, the one-way ANOVA produced F(2,209) = 0.52, p = .60, eta-squared = .005, indicating no meaningful differences across political groups. For Asian targets, the ANOVA yielded F(2,209) = 1.13, p = .33, eta-squared = .011. For Middle Eastern targets, the ANOVA produced F(2,209) = 1.76, p = .17, eta-squared = .017. Effect sizes for these comparisons were small and did not approach conventional thresholds for practical significance. Given the moderate intercorrelations among the three pleasantness ratings, a repeated-measures analysis was conducted to assess whether individual differences interacted with target ethnicity in predicting pleasantness. A repeated-measures ANOVA with ethnicity (three levels) as the within-subjects factor and international school attendance as a between-subjects factor was conducted. The main effect of ethnicity on pleasantness was not significant, F(2,420) = 1.83, p = .16, indicating that mean pleasantness did not differ significantly across the three target ethnic groups at the group level. The interaction between ethnicity and international school attendance was not significant, F(2,420) = 1.02, p = .36. Parallel repeated-measures models that included religiousness and political stance as continuous covariates also failed to detect significant interactions with ethnicity. Specifically, ethnicity by religiousness interaction was non-significant, F(2,418) = 1.10, p = .33, and ethnicity by political stance interaction was non-significant, F(2,418) = 1.44, p = .24. To further probe the robustness of the null findings, sensitivity analyses were performed. These included excluding participants who self-identified as members of the target group for a given comparison (e.g., removing participants who identified as Middle Eastern when analyzing Middle Eastern pleasantness). The pattern of results did not change appreciably in these restricted samples. Additionally, analyses that excluded participants with extreme response styles or very high item non-response produced the same conclusion of non-significant associations. Observed effect sizes across the main tests were uniformly small, and confidence intervals around key correlations included zero. For example, the 95% confidence interval around the correlation between political stance and Middle Eastern pleasantness was –.23 to .03. An informal post hoc power calculation indicated that the sample provided approximately 80% power to detect a correlation of r = .19 at alpha = .05. Thus, the study had adequate power to detect small-to-moderate relationships, but might have been underpowered to detect very small effects. Given that the observed correlations were in the range of |r| < .10, the data suggested that any true associations between the individual differences measured here and explicit pleasantness ratings of the three ethnic groups would be of very small magnitude. Finally, exploratory analyses examined whether combinations of predictors might jointly relate to pleasantness ratings by entering religiousness, political stance, and international school attendance as simultaneous predictors in linear regression models for each pleasantness outcome. None of the coefficients reached statistical significance in these multivariate models. The full models accounted for between 2% and 4% of variance in pleasantness ratings, indicating that the predictors together explained only a small portion of the variability in explicit group evaluations. Taken together, the analyses converged on a common conclusion: attendance at an international school, self-reported religiousness, and self-reported political stance did not show reliable associations with explicit pleasantness ratings of White/European, Asian, or Middle Eastern groups in this sample.

Discussion

This study tested whether three individual differences—attendance at an international school, self-reported religiousness, and self-reported political stance—were associated with explicit pleasantness ratings for White/European, Asian, and Middle Eastern target groups. Across multiple analytic approaches, the study produced null results with respect to the hypothesized relationships. Correlations between religiousness and political stance and pleasantness ratings were small and non-significant. Comparisons between participants who had and had not attended international schools showed no reliable differences. Repeated-measures analyses assessing interactions between ethnicity and the individual difference variables likewise failed to reveal significant effects. The absence of detectable associations in this dataset warrants careful interpretation. First, these null results should not be taken to imply that individual differences never relate to intergroup attitudes. Rather, within the operationalizations and sample employed here, the specific measures of religiosity and political stance and the single-item assessment of international school attendance did not predict explicit pleasantness of the three target groups. The findings therefore speak directly to the utility of these brief self-report indicators for predicting explicit affective evaluations in an online sample. One plausible explanation for the null results is that the measures used were too coarse to capture the theoretically relevant constructs. Religiousness was assessed with a single item asking participants how religious they considered themselves. Political stance was assessed with a single left–right item. International school attendance was coded as a binary variable. Single-item measures are efficient but can lack nuance. Religiosity, for instance, is a multifaceted construct encompassing identity, practice, beliefs, and doctrinal content. Prior research linking religiosity to prejudice has often found that particular dimensions—such as religious fundamentalism, intrinsic versus extrinsic orientation, or doctrinal emphasis on universalism—show different associations with outgroup attitudes. A single-item religiosity scale would not distinguish these dimensions. Similarly, political ideology is multidimensional; social conservatism and economic conservatism can have different relations with prejudice. The single-item political stance used here conflates potential sub-dimensions and may miss variance relevant to intergroup affect. Therefore, the null findings may reflect measurement limitations rather than genuine absence of association. Another potential explanation concerns the outcome measure. Explicit pleasantness ratings are susceptible to social desirability and normative influences. Participants in contemporary contexts may be reluctant to endorse strongly negative evaluations of ethnic groups on an explicit measure. If social desirability suppressed variance in explicit ratings, it could attenuate correlations with individual difference predictors. Implicit measures of prejudice or behavioral indicators (e.g., resource allocation tasks, simulated hiring decisions) may reveal associations that explicit self-report does not. Future research employing a broader set of outcome measures, including implicit and behavioral indices, could address this possibility. A related point is that the three pleasantness ratings were moderately to highly intercorrelated, suggesting substantial shared variance in participants’ general positivity or negativity toward social groups. This pattern indicates that participants may have been expressing a general tendency toward positive or negative affect rather than specific attitudes toward particular groups. When outcome variance is dominated by a general evaluative disposition, specific predictors tied to differential responses to target groups may be harder to detect. Designing tasks that elicit differential responses—such as comparing attitudes toward groups that differ on dimensions of perceived threat or status—could enhance sensitivity to predictors that operate in a context-specific manner. Sample composition and statistical power also merit consideration. The sample comprised 212 participants with moderate demographic diversity. A post hoc power calculation suggested adequate power to detect small-to-moderate correlations but not very small ones. The observed effect sizes were generally small (|r| < .10), and thus it is possible that very small true effects exist but were below the detection threshold of this study. Whether such very small effects would be meaningful for theory or intervention is an open question. Moreover, the sample included participants with varying cultural backgrounds, which enhances generalizability but also introduces heterogeneity that could obscure relationships. For example, the meaning of attending an international school or the content of religious belief may differ substantially across national contexts. This heterogeneity could reduce the likelihood of detecting consistent associations when aggregated across diverse participants. The null results regarding international school attendance are notable in light of theoretical expectations from the contact hypothesis. The hypothesis predicts that positive intergroup contact—frequent, cooperative, and supported by institutional norms—should reduce prejudice. International schools are environments that might foster such contact. However, simply attending an international school does not guarantee that contact was positive, that it was intergroup in the relevant ways, or that it was sustained enough to alter attitudes. Some international school environments may reflect stratification or separation among students based on nationality or socioeconomic status. In such contexts, mere attendance would not necessarily produce the benefits predicted by contact theory. More detailed measures of the quality and frequency of intergroup contact, as well as measures of institutional climate, would be required to test the contact mechanism adequately. The null association between political stance and pleasantness toward Asian and Middle Eastern groups contrasts with some prior literature linking conservatism to greater prejudice toward particular outgroups. Several factors could account for this. First, the current measure of political stance may have lacked sensitivity to the specific ideological dimensions associated with threat perceptions that drive intergroup negativity. Second, the sociopolitical context of the sample may shape how political labels map onto attitudes toward specific ethnic groups. In some settings, political conservatism does not uniformly translate into hostility toward all outgroups. Third, social desirability and shifting social norms may reduce the explicit expression of politically aligned prejudice on self-report measures. The mixed findings in prior literature mirror these complexities, and the present study adds to the evidence that the relationships between political ideology and prejudice are context-dependent and measurement-sensitive. The study has several methodological limitations that future research should address. As noted, reliance on single-item measures constrains construct validity. Future studies would benefit from multi-item, validated scales for religiosity (including dimensions such as fundamentalism, extrinsic/intrinsic orientation, and doctrinal content) and political ideology (including social and economic dimensions). For the exposure variable, more granular measures of international experience should be collected, such as years spent in international schools, the diversity of the peer cohort, and the quality of intergroup interactions. Outcome measures should include implicit tasks, behavioral choices, and situational judgments that may be less susceptible to social desirability bias. Longitudinal designs could test causal directions, for example whether exposure predicts changes in affect over time. Experimental manipulations of intergroup contact or exposure to normative messages about inclusivity could also shed light on mechanisms. Another important avenue for future work is to examine moderators and mediators of any relationships between individual differences and intergroup attitudes. For instance, the relation between religiosity and prejudice may be moderated by the content of religious teachings or by individuals’ cognitive styles. Political conservatism may predict prejudice more strongly when coupled with high perceived threat or low intergroup contact. Mediators such as perceived threat, empathy, and perceived common humanity could elucidate the processes through which individual differences translate into attitudes. The present study’s findings also have implications for intervention and policy. The lack of robust associations in this sample cautions against simplistic targeting of individuals based on single-item measures of religiosity or political stance when designing anti-prejudice programs. Interventions may be more effective when they focus on specific behaviors and contexts that give rise to discrimination, rather than assuming that certain demographic or attitudinal profiles are primary drivers of prejudice. Educational programs that promote high-quality intergroup contact, emphasize shared values, and cultivate perspective-taking may be more promising than approaches that rely on changing broad identity labels. It is worth emphasizing that null results are not inherently uninteresting or uninformative. The present findings suggest that readily obtained self-reports of religiousness and political stance, and a simple binary indicator of international schooling, may not be strong predictors of explicit, self-reported pleasantness toward broad ethnic categories. This conclusion helps refine theoretical expectations and points to the kinds of methodological improvements needed. In closing, the current study attempted a focused test of whether international school attendance, religiousness, and political stance predict explicit pleasantness toward White/European, Asian, and Middle Eastern groups. The analyses consistently indicated that these particular operationalizations of individual differences were not associated with substantial differences in explicit pleasantness ratings. Future research should use more nuanced, multi-dimensional measures, incorporate implicit and behavioral outcomes, and consider longitudinal and experimental designs to more fully understand the complex antecedents of racial prejudice. By refining measurement and expanding methodological approaches, future work can better identify the stable correlates and causal drivers of intergroup attitudes and more effectively inform interventions to reduce discrimination.
