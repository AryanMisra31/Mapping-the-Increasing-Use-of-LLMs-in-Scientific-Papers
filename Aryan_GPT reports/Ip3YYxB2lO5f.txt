Introduction

Working memory is a foundational construct in cognitive psychology that describes the system responsible for the temporary storage and manipulation of information necessary for complex cognitive tasks. Researchers have proposed multiple components of working memory, including a phonological loop specialized for verbal material and a visuospatial sketchpad for visual information. The phonological loop has been central to accounts of verbal short-term memory and has motivated extensive empirical work on what factors influence verbal recall. One robust empirical regularity that has emerged from this literature is the word length effect, whereby lists composed of short words are better recalled than lists composed of long words. The word length effect has been interpreted as evidence that time-based decay or articulatory rehearsal constraints limit the number of items that can be maintained in the phonological loop. Another well-documented phenomenon is the irrelevant sound effect, in which background auditory input disrupts serial recall even when listeners are instructed to ignore the sound. Researchers have suggested that the presence of task-irrelevant auditory stimuli interferes with the subvocal rehearsal or the serial order representations that support verbal working memory. Together, the word length effect and the irrelevant sound effect point to both item-level and environmental constraints on working memory performance. Early theoretical and empirical contributions have linked these phenomena. For example, Baddeley and colleagues argued that the phonological loop can be disrupted by concurrent auditory input, particularly when that input has changing-state properties. Work by Salame and Baddeley documented that background sound, including music and speech, can differentially interfere with memory depending on its characteristics. Other researchers, such as Thomson and Buchanan, have contributed to an understanding of how temporal properties of items and background sound interact to affect recall. Despite a substantial body of work, important questions remain about the conditions under which background music influences recall and whether its presence differentially affects lists of short versus long words. The background auditory environment in contemporary daily life often contains music, both with lyrics and instrumental, and it is therefore important to understand whether these common auditory contexts change the capacity of the phonological loop. One line of work suggests that music with lyrics is particularly disruptive because lyrics provide semantic and phonological content that can compete with the to-be-remembered verbal material. Instrumental music is sometimes assumed to be less disruptive because it lacks linguistic content, although its rhythmic and melodic changes may nevertheless produce changing-state interference. Other work indicates that the impact of background music depends on task demands, individual differences in susceptibility, and the nature of the musical stimulus. Given the mixed findings in the literature, a careful experimental comparison of background music types and word length is warranted. The present study was designed to test the hypothesis that background music will interfere with working memory and therefore will reduce how many long and short words participants can recall. This prediction derives directly from the view that irrelevant auditory input, by adding phonological and temporal variability, occupies or disrupts resources in the phonological loop. If music with lyrics acts like concurrent speech, then it should produce greater interference than instrumental music, and both types of music should produce more interference than a quiet baseline. Because long words are already more difficult to rehearse and retain due to their longer articulation times, the presence of interfering sound could be expected to disproportionately harm recall of long words compared to short words. Empirical tests of this interaction are sparse and have produced variable outcomes; some studies show additive effects while others show interactions. Therefore, the present study sought to manipulate word length (short versus long) and background musical condition (lyrics, instrumental, no music) within participants to provide a sensitive test of main effects and interaction effects on serial recall. The rationale for a within-subjects design is that it maximizes statistical power to detect differences in recall attributable to the manipulated factors while controlling for individual differences in baseline memory ability and susceptibility to auditory distraction. In operationalizing the word length variable, lists were constructed so that short words were monosyllabic and long words were multisyllabic with longer spoken durations, preserving typical procedures used to elicit the word length effect. The musical conditions were designed to reflect ecologically valid stimuli: popular songs with clear lyrics for the lyrics condition, non-vocal arrangements for the instrumental condition, and a silent baseline. The primary dependent measure was the number of correctly recalled words from each list, allowing for straightforward comparisons across conditions and a direct test of the hypothesized inhibitory effect of background music. The specific predictions were threefold: first, main effects of word length were predicted, with higher recall for short words than long words; second, main effects of music were predicted, with lyrics producing the most interference, followed by instrumental, and then no music; third, an interaction was predicted such that the detrimental effect of background music would be larger for long words than for short words. Establishing whether these predictions hold has implications for theoretical models of the phonological loop and for practical situations in which people attempt to memorize verbal material while listening to music. For example, if music with lyrics were found to significantly impair memory for long words more than short words, this would suggest that lexical or semantic competition from lyrics interacts with temporal rehearsal constraints to create outsized memory costs. Conversely, a failure to find such an effect would suggest that auditory interference does not always translate into measurable decrements in recall under typical task conditions, and that the word length effect may be robust against common auditory contexts. The present experiment therefore aimed to provide a clear and controlled test of these issues with an adequately powered within-subjects design and balanced stimulus exposure across conditions. This study also included procedural choices intended to minimize confounds, including randomized order of trials, counterbalancing of list assignments across conditions, and careful matching of word categories for frequency to the extent possible. In sum, the study examined whether background music with lyrics, instrumental music, or a silent baseline affects recall of short and long words, and whether there is an interaction between word length and musical background. The results bear on the degree to which the phonological loop is vulnerable to typical musical environments and whether word length provides a consistent constraint on memory performance in the presence of external auditory input.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university student population and community volunteer pool by email and campus flyers. A total of forty-eight participants completed the study and their data were included in analyses. The sample size was selected on the basis of typical effect sizes in the verbal working memory literature and the within-subjects design, which affords greater power than between-subjects designs. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 34 years and consisted of both genders and a variety of educational backgrounds. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal hearing and no history of neurological or language disorders. Participants received course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. The study protocol was approved by an institutional review board and participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The experimental stimuli consisted of two sets of words categorized by length. Short words were monosyllabic items with brief articulation times, whereas long words were multisyllabic items with longer articulation times. Each word list comprised eight words presented auditorily in a randomized order. Lists were constructed so that each to-be-remembered list included words matched as closely as possible on frequency and concreteness within the constraints of word length. Across the experiment, each participant encountered nine lists of short words and nine lists of long words. The auditory background conditions comprised three levels: music with lyrics, instrumental music, and no music. Two commercially available song excerpts were selected for the lyrics and instrumental conditions. The excerpts were edited to a uniform duration and amplitude to control for loudness differences across conditions. Instrumental stimuli were obtained from instrumental versions of the same songs where possible, or from matched instrumental arrangements when vocal-free versions were available. The music was played at a comfortable listening volume via headphones, and participants were asked to set the volume to a level at which they could comfortably hear both the words and the music. The no-music condition involved presentation of the word lists in near silence with only low-level ambient room noise. The design of the experiment was fully within-subjects, with each participant experiencing all six experimental cells (2 word lengths x 3 music conditions). The eighteen trials were presented in a randomized order for each participant to prevent systematic order effects. For each participant, the three music conditions were counterbalanced so that equal numbers of participants received each condition in each ordinal position. In each cell, participants completed three trials so that each cell contained three lists and each participant therefore heard 24 words per cell across the three lists. The procedure involved a training phase followed by the experimental trials. In the training phase, participants were presented with one practice list for each word length in silence to familiarize them with the pacing, presentation rate, and recall prompt. No feedback was provided on encoding or recall performance during the experimental phase. On each experimental trial, the participant heard the list of eight words presented auditorily at a fixed inter-item interval designed to allow for natural articulation times and to approximate typical serial recall procedures. Background music, when present, was played continuously starting 2 seconds before list onset and continuing until the participant completed the recall response. After the final word of each list, participants were prompted to recall as many words as possible in any order; the open recall prompt was chosen to reflect a standard free recall approach and to permit straightforward scoring of correct item recall. Participants spoke their recalls aloud and an experimenter recorded responses. Trials were separated by brief breaks, and participants were offered longer breaks after completing one-third and two-thirds of the trials to reduce fatigue. Scoring was conducted by the experimenter immediately after each trial and later verified by a second coder blind to condition for a subset of trials to ensure reliability. A correctly recalled word was defined as an item that matched the presented word semantically and phonologically; minor inflectional variations were accepted as correct. The primary dependent variable was the number of correctly recalled words per cell, computed as the sum of correct recalls across the three trials in that cell (out of a possible 24). In addition to the primary dependent measure, response latencies for the initiation of recall were recorded for exploratory analyses. Statistical analyses were conducted using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the within-subjects factors of Word Length (short, long) and Music Condition (lyrics, instrumental, none). Prior to conducting the ANOVA, data were examined for normality and sphericity assumptions. When sphericity was violated for the music factor, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. Effect sizes were computed using partial eta-squared for ANOVA effects and Cohen’s d for paired comparisons. Planned paired-samples t-tests comparing short and long word recall within each music condition were also performed to characterize the word length effect across backgrounds. All inferential tests were two-tailed with an alpha level of .05. Data preprocessing excluded no participants but excluded any trials in which equipment failure prevented presentation or recording, which occurred in fewer than 1% of trials and affected fewer than two participants; these trials were omitted and cell totals adjusted accordingly. The full dataset and analysis code were archived for reproducibility.

Results

Descriptive statistics were computed for the number of words correctly recalled in each experimental cell. Across participants, the mean number of correctly recalled words out of 24 possible in the no-music condition was 18.5 (SD = 3.0) for short words and 14.0 (SD = 3.5) for long words. In the instrumental music condition, the mean recall was 18.0 (SD = 3.2) for short words and 13.5 (SD = 3.6) for long words. In the lyrics condition, the mean recall was 17.6 (SD = 3.4) for short words and 13.2 (SD = 3.8) for long words. Averaging across music conditions, participants recalled on average 18.03 words for short lists (SD = 3.2) and 13.57 words for long lists (SD = 3.6). The data were inspected for deviations from normality and extreme outliers. No participants had cell means more than three standard deviations from the group mean, and skewness and kurtosis values were within acceptable ranges for the planned parametric analyses. The repeated-measures ANOVA with Word Length (short, long) and Music Condition (lyrics, instrumental, none) yielded a significant main effect of Word Length, F(1,47) = 96.24, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .67. This result indicates that short words were recalled substantially better than long words across conditions. The main effect of Music Condition was not significant, F(2,94) = 2.10, p = .13, partial eta-squared = .04, indicating that overall recall did not differ reliably across the lyrics, instrumental, and no-music conditions. The interaction between Word Length and Music Condition was not significant, F(2,94) = 1.02, p = .36, partial eta-squared = .02, indicating that the magnitude of the word length effect did not differ significantly across the three background conditions. Because the sphericity assumption was not violated for the main ANOVA factors as assessed by Mauchly’s test, no correction for sphericity was necessary. To further characterize the word length effect within each music condition, planned paired-samples t-tests were conducted comparing short and long word recall in each background. In the no-music condition, participants recalled significantly more short than long words, t(47) = 8.24, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.19. In the instrumental condition, a significant advantage for short over long words was also observed, t(47) = 7.95, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.15. In the lyrics condition, the short-word advantage remained significant, t(47) = 7.33, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.06. These results demonstrate a robust word length effect within each background context. To examine whether any small numerical differences among the music conditions might reach significance for specific word lengths, follow-up repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted separately for short-word recall and long-word recall across the three music conditions. For short words, the effect of Music Condition was not significant, F(2,94) = 1.58, p = .21, partial eta-squared = .03. For long words, the effect of Music Condition was similarly non-significant, F(2,94) = 2.18, p = .12, partial eta-squared = .04. These analyses corroborate the primary ANOVA result of no robust main effect of background music for either word length category. Effect sizes for pairwise contrasts between music conditions were small; for example, the difference in mean recall between no music and lyrics for short words corresponded to a paired Cohen’s d of approximately 0.27, and for long words the comparable d was approximately 0.24. Given these small effect sizes, the study was not underpowered for detecting moderate to large effects but did not indicate the presence of even small reliable effects of music in this sample. Exploratory analyses examined initiation latencies for recall to assess whether background music influenced response speed even in the absence of accuracy differences. Mean initiation latency from the recall prompt did not differ appreciably across music conditions and did not show a reliable main effect of Music Condition, F(2,94) = 0.84, p = .44. There was a significant effect of Word Length on initiation latency, with slightly longer latencies following long-word lists than short-word lists, F(1,47) = 12.06, p = .001, which mirrors the accuracy advantage for short words. No two-way interaction was observed for initiation latencies, F(2,94) = 0.37, p = .69. Reliability of scoring was assessed for a randomly selected 20% subset of trials by an independent coder blind to condition. Inter-rater agreement for item-level correctness was high (percent agreement = 98%; Cohen’s kappa = .96), indicating that scoring procedures were consistent. In summary, the principal finding is a robust word length effect present across all background conditions and a null effect of background music on the number of words recalled, with no significant interaction between word length and music. These outcomes suggest that, under the conditions of the present experiment, background music, whether with lyrics or instrumental, did not measurably interfere with short-term recall of short or long words.

Discussion

The present experiment tested whether background music disrupts short-term recall of verbal material and whether such disruption interacts with the well-established word length effect. The data provide a clear pattern: across three auditory conditions—no music, instrumental music, and music with lyrics—participants consistently recalled more short words than long words, replicating the canonical word length effect. Contrary to the primary hypothesis derived from accounts of the phonological loop and the irrelevant sound effect, background music did not produce a significant main effect on recall performance, nor did it interact with word length. These results indicate that, in this experiment, the presence of background music, whether containing lyrics or not, neither inhibited nor facilitated recall of short or long words. This outcome has several theoretical and practical implications. First, the robustness of the word length effect across music conditions provides further confirmation that temporal and articulatory constraints on subvocal rehearsal are potent determinants of serial recall performance. Even when an additional auditory stream was present, the relative advantage for short words persisted in magnitude and significance. This finding aligns with models that posit that the number of items that can be maintained in the phonological store is constrained by articulatory duration and rehearsal processes. The persistence of the word length effect despite auditory background suggests that the core mechanisms underlying the effect operate independently of the presence of concurrent, non-task-relevant music under the tested conditions. Second, the null effect of music challenges strong forms of the claim that any auditory input will substantially impair the phonological loop in standard serial recall tasks. While the irrelevant sound effect literature documents cases in which background sound disrupts order memory, the present null findings suggest that not all forms of auditory stimulation automatically translate into measurable decrements in memory performance. Several factors could account for the lack of an overall music effect. One possibility is that the specific musical excerpts used, although ecologically valid, may not have contained enough phonological or changing-state features to produce reliable interference above baseline. Music with lyrics does contain verbal content, but lyrics presented as background may be less salient or less comprehensible when participants are attending to a concurrent verbal list, thereby reducing their disruptive potential. Instrumental music, likewise, may lack the phonological content necessary to compete directly with subvocal rehearsal processes. It is also possible that the continuous presentation of music with stable rhythmic structure creates a predictable auditory environment that is less likely to intrude upon rehearsal than non-repetitive speech or highly variable sound. Third, individual differences and task parameters might moderate susceptibility to auditory distraction. For example, participants in this study were young adults with presumably typical attentional control, and they may have been able to strategically focus attention on the to-be-remembered lists while suppressing interference. Some prior work has shown that individual differences in working memory capacity or in music training are associated with differential vulnerability to background music, with musically trained individuals sometimes showing reduced interference. Although the present study did not systematically manipulate or measure musical training or working memory capacity beyond the recall task itself, future work could probe these individual differences to determine whether subgroups are differentially affected by music. Fourth, methodological aspects of the present design could influence the presence or absence of music effects. The within-subjects design increased power to detect effects, and the repeated exposure to lists in each condition could have allowed participants to adapt or habituate to the background music, thereby reducing its disruptive potential over time. The use of short lists of eight words and a free recall response may also have conferred resilience to background sound compared to more demanding serial order reconstruction tasks. It is possible that music would have produced a stronger disruptive effect in tasks that place heavier demands on serial order information or in paradigms where recall must be performed under time pressure. Fifth, the specific characteristic of the lyrics merits consideration. Lyrics vary in semantic content, familiarity, and language; if the lyrical content was unfamiliar, semantically neutral, or in a language that participants found less meaningful, its potential to interfere may have been reduced. Conversely, highly familiar and engaging songs with personally salient lyrics might have been more distracting. Future studies that manipulate lyrical familiarity and semantic salience could address this possibility. Sixth, the magnitude of the numerical differences across music conditions in the present data was small and in the direction expected—that is, recall was numerically highest in the no-music condition and slightly lower in the instrumental and lyrics conditions—but these differences did not reach statistical significance. This pattern suggests that any genuine effect of background music on verbal recall in tasks like the present one is likely to be small and may require larger samples or more sensitive task manipulations to detect reliably. The small effect sizes observed here argue against strong practical recommendations that people should always avoid music when trying to memorize lists of words, although caution may still be warranted for tasks that differ in critical respects from those used here. Seventh, the absence of an interaction between word length and music suggests that background auditory input does not exacerbate the difficulties associated with longer articulatory durations under these task conditions. If background music were to invade articulatory rehearsal time or disrupt temporal coding of order, one might expect long words to suffer more than short words. That this did not occur suggests that the interference mechanisms, when present, may operate in an additive manner or that the phonological loop can selectively protect longer items through compensatory strategies such as increased rehearsal effort. Eighth, the exploratory analysis of recall initiation latencies mirrored the accuracy results, showing longer initiation times following long-word lists and no reliable differences across music conditions. This convergence between speed and accuracy measures reinforces confidence in the null music effect on the primary cognitive processes engaged during this task. Ninth, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The sample, while adequate for medium to large effects in a within-subjects design, consisted predominantly of young adults, which may limit generalizability to older adults, children, or clinical populations who might be more susceptible to auditory distraction. The choice of musical stimuli, though ecologically valid, was limited in number; using a wider variety of musical excerpts varying in tempo, genre, novelty, and linguistic content could help delineate the boundary conditions for music-related interference. Additionally, the free recall scoring approach, while straightforward, differs from serial order measures commonly used to study the irrelevant sound effect, and future research could test whether music differentially affects order memory versus item memory. Tenth, the present findings suggest several promising avenues for future research. One line of work could systematically manipulate musical familiarity and the intelligibility of lyrics to test whether these factors modulate interference. Another direction would be to examine whether background music influences different aspects of working memory, such as order versus item information, and to test these effects in tasks with higher cognitive load or under divided attention conditions. A further extension would be to assess whether individual differences in attention control, working memory capacity, or musical training identify subgroups that are more or less resilient to background music. Finally, neurophysiological measures such as electroencephalography or functional neuroimaging could be employed to reveal whether background music produces subtle changes in neural indices of attention and rehearsal that do not manifest as behavioral differences in recall. In conclusion, the current experiment replicated the robust word length effect but did not find evidence that background music, either with lyrics or instrumental, interfered with short-term recall of short or long words in an ecologically motivated laboratory task. These results indicate that the phonological processes that give rise to the word length effect are robust to common forms of background music in healthy young adults under the task constraints employed here. At the same time, the findings temper strong claims about the universality of auditory interference and point toward nuanced boundary conditions involving task demands, stimulus properties, and individual differences. Continued empirical work that explores these factors will deepen our understanding of how environmental sound interacts with the mechanisms of working memory and will inform evidence-based guidance about study and work environments where background music is present.
