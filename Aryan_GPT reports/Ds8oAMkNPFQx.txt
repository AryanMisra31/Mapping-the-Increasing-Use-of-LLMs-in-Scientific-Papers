Introduction

Risk perception is a central determinant of human decision making in many domains, and it underlies choices that range from mundane everyday activities to life-altering medical and financial decisions. People routinely make judgments about the likelihood and severity of outcomes, and these judgments drive preferences among options that trade off risk and reward. The cognitive and affective processes that shape risk perception are numerous and have been the subject of sustained empirical and theoretical investigation. Among the many factors that shape how people perceive and respond to risk, the role of prior experience has long been considered crucial. When people have prior experience with an event, that experience informs their expectations about its future occurrence and influences their willingness to take risks associated with the event. The general phenomenon that personal experience or sampled outcomes can alter risk preferences has been documented across domains, and it often interacts with the mode of information acquisition, namely whether probabilities are learned by description or by direct experience. A line of work that has crystallized around this distinction has shown robust differences in choice behavior when individuals learn about risky options by being told explicit probabilities versus when they learn by sampling outcomes from the options themselves. The differences between descriptive and experiential learning have sometimes been termed the description–experience gap, and they have led to important insights about human probability weighting and risk sensitivity. The description–experience gap demonstrates that the mode of information acquisition matters because people tend to overweight rare events in described formats but underweight them after experiential sampling, and these opposite biases can produce divergent choices under otherwise identical objective risk structures. Researchers such as Yechiam, Barron and Erev have emphasized that sensitivity to prior experience modulates risk attitudes and that a prior positive experience with an event may reduce one’s sensitivity to its risks. The idea that positive personal experience can lead to underestimation of risk has direct practical relevance in domains where adverse outcomes are rare but consequential, and where decisions are often made on the basis of personal or vicarious experience rather than through access to full probability information. The medical domain is a prominent context in which experience and description may diverge in their influence on patient decisions, because patients often rely on their own prior experiences, anecdotes from acquaintances, or sampling of outcomes through treatment trials to form beliefs about the probability and severity of side effects and treatment success. Fleischhacker and colleagues have argued that systematic underestimation of side effects can be a major contributor to treatment failure, insofar as patients who underweight the likelihood of adverse effects may choose medications or treatment regimens without adequately accounting for potential harms. This clinical observation speaks to the possibility that experiential exposure to side effects, or lack thereof, could shape patients’ willingness to accept certain medical risks. Experimental work by Hertwig, Barron, Weber and Erev has provided controlled evidence concerning the broader description–experience distinction in the financial domain. In experiments that contrasted choices based on described probabilities with choices after sampling from outcome distributions, those researchers found that participants were riskier in small financial gambles after sampling than when given explicit probabilities by description. The classic pattern in that literature is that experiential sampling often yields choices consistent with an underweighting of rare events and sometimes increases risk taking when rare losses are involved or decreases risk taking when rare gains are involved; these patterns can reverse the choices that would be predicted from decision weights observed when probabilities are described. The implications of that research for medical decision making are profound because it suggests that how information about treatment risks is acquired may influence the degree to which patients accept or reject risky options. If experiential sampling leads to attenuated sensitivity to rare adverse outcomes, then patients who have sampled favorable outcomes (for example, who have observed no side effects among a small number of acquaintances) might underestimate the risk of side effects and thus choose treatments that clinicians would consider too risky. In the literature on behavioral decision making, several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the description–experience gap, including sampling noise, memory biases, recency effects, and differential attention to outcomes in sampling compared to description. One influential account holds that experiential sampling is subject to sampling error and limited sample sizes, which may result in underrepresentation of rare events in the sample and hence underweighting of those events in later choices. Another account emphasizes cognitive processes such as availability and representativeness: outcomes that are more easily brought to mind after sampling will exert more influence on subsequent judgments, and rare events may be less likely to be recalled after limited sampling. Affect and affective forecasting may also play a role: personal or observed experiences can generate strong affective reactions that color subsequent risk perceptions and preferences, sometimes in ways that amplify or attenuate risk-taking tendencies relative to described statistical information. In addition to these cognitive mechanisms, contextual and task-specific factors moderate the size and direction of the description–experience gap. For instance, the stakes of the decision, whether outcomes are monetary or health-related, and the severity of possible adverse events can all change how experience influences choices. There is evidence that domain matters: decisions about financial gambles, consumer goods, and health treatments can yield different patterns when the same informational manipulations are applied, and these differences may stem from domain-specific knowledge, emotional salience, or normative beliefs about acceptable levels of risk. Medical decisions, in particular, often involve highly salient outcomes such as morbidity and mortality, and they may engage moral considerations and trust in experts that are less central in simple financial gambles. Given the salience of health outcomes, one might expect that experiential sampling could produce different or attenuated effects on risk taking in medical contexts compared with financial contexts. On the one hand, if sampling leads to attenuation of perceived side-effect probabilities, then experiential exposure to few or no adverse events could make patients more willing to accept treatment risks. On the other hand, the affective aversiveness of potential medical side effects might make individuals more conservative in medical contexts irrespective of sampling history, thereby blunting or eliminating the experiential increase in risk taking that is observed in financial domains. Further complicating the picture is the role of prior beliefs and acquired knowledge: many individuals already possess prior beliefs about medication safety and side effects, shaped by cultural narratives, media reports, physician advice, and prior personal experiences. These priors can interact with sampled information in complex ways — for example, strong prior beliefs might dominate a small sample and render the description–experience manipulation less potent in altering choices. The clinical literature suggests that patients often systematically underestimate side-effect risks, and such underestimation could be attributed to several nonexclusive causes including optimistic bias, motivated reasoning, and limited exposure to negative outcomes. Fleischhacker and colleagues emphasized that systematic underestimation of side effects is a significant factor in treatment noncompliance or discontinuation, which indicates that understanding how sampled experience shapes side-effect beliefs is not merely a theoretical concern but a clinically actionable question. Empirically testing whether experiential sampling alters risk taking in hypothetical or real medical choices to the same extent as in financial decisions is therefore a critical step for both theory and practice. Despite the theoretical and practical reasons to anticipate domain-specific effects, most experimental work on the description–experience gap has used financial gambles or stylized monetary tasks because those paradigms allow for precise control over probabilities and outcomes. Translating these findings to the medical domain requires careful matching of tasks, attention to framing (e.g., gains versus losses, survival versus mortality), and consideration of ethical constraints on exposing participants to actual medical risks. One approach has been to use hypothetical medical vignettes that describe treatment options and side effects and to compare choices in described versus sampled formats. However, whether such hypothetical designs produce the same description–experience effects as monetary tasks is an open question. The present study was designed to investigate whether experiential sampling of undesirable side effects affects risk-taking in hypothetical medical dilemmas, and to compare those effects to corresponding financial gambles that were matched in probabilistic structure. By designing analogous medical and financial problems and randomly assigning participants to either a description condition or an experience condition, the study aims to isolate the specific contribution of sampling history to choices across domains. The key theoretical question motivating the study is whether the commonly observed increase in risk taking after sampling in financial tasks generalizes to medical decisions, or whether domain-specific factors such as affective intensity, prior beliefs, and constrained sample sizes will alter or attenuate that effect. If experiential sampling increases risk taking in financial problems but not in medical problems, this pattern would suggest that the psychological mechanisms underlying the description–experience gap interact with domain-specific processes. Conversely, if experiential sampling increases risk taking in both domains, this would point to a more domain-general influence of experience on risk preferences. Prior work by Hertwig and colleagues found that participants were riskier in small financial gambles after sampling relative to description, a finding that has been replicated in several variations, and this served as a benchmark for the current research. Yechiam, Barron and Erev’s assertion that positive prior experience can reduce sensitivity to risk led us to predict that sampling favorable outcomes would increase risk taking in general. In contrast, Fleischhacker and colleagues’ observation that patients tend to systematically underestimate side effects suggested that medical decisions might exhibit a baseline bias toward underestimating risk that could be either amplified or counteracted by experimental sampling. The specific hypotheses tested in the present study were twofold. First, consistent with prior findings in financial decision making, it was hypothesized that participants in the experience condition would choose the risky option at a higher rate than those in the description condition for at least some financial problems, with the strongest effects expected in problems characterized by relatively rare but consequential outcomes. Second, it was hypothesized that the same experiential manipulation would not produce a comparable increase in risk taking in the medical domain; more precisely, the experience group would not be significantly riskier than the description group when faced with the medical dilemmas. These hypotheses together predict a domain by format interaction such that the experience-related increase in risk taking evident in financial tasks would be attenuated or absent in medical tasks. Testing these hypotheses required a design that presented matched financial and medical problems, clearly operationalized description and experience formats, and included sufficient statistical power to detect moderate effects, given the practical constraints of the experimental setting. A secondary set of exploratory questions concerned the degree to which individual differences — including prior experience with medications, numeracy, and baseline risk attitudes — might moderate the effect of sampling, and whether the pattern of results would vary across the three problems within each domain. The present report describes an experiment with 78 participants who completed three financial and three matched medical problems, with random assignment to description versus experience formats. The findings provide a partial replication of Hertwig and colleagues’ results in that the experience group was riskier than the description group for one of the financial problems, and they extend the literature by examining the absence of a similar experiential effect in medical dilemmas. This work contributes to the broader debate about the generality of the description–experience gap and its practical implications for medical decision making, and it raises questions about the boundary conditions under which experiential sampling alters risk-taking behavior across different content domains.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from the local community through online advertisements and flyers, and a total of 78 individuals participated in the experiment. All participants were at least 18 years of age and able to read and understand the language in which the materials were presented; participants who reported a history of severe psychiatric or neurological conditions were not excluded unless those conditions would have precluded informed consent, in which case they were not enrolled. The sample included both student and nonstudent participants, and demographic information including age, gender, education level, and prior experience with prescription medications was collected via questionnaire. Participants were randomly assigned in equal numbers to one of two experimental conditions: a description condition and an experience (sampling) condition. Randomization was implemented using a computerized random number generator and was conducted at the participant level prior to the presentation of any problems. The experimental materials consisted of three financial problems and three medical problems, which were designed to be probabilistically equivalent and matched in terms of expected value and stakes to the extent possible while preserving domain-appropriate framing. Each problem presented a choice between two options: a relatively safe option with smaller but more certain outcomes, and a relatively risky option with larger but less certain outcomes. The three financial gambles were framed as hypothetical monetary choices and included variations in the probability and magnitude of gains and losses to create a range of risk profiles; for example, one financial problem involved a high-probability small gain versus a low-probability large gain, whereas another involved a small-probability large loss versus a high-probability small loss. The three medical problems were framed as hypothetical treatment decisions in which the safe option involved a treatment with a lower benefit but fewer or less severe side effects, and the risky option involved a treatment with a higher benefit but a chance of undesirable side effects. The medical vignettes were constructed to be morally and emotionally plausible while avoiding graphic content, and efforts were made to equate the perceived severity of outcomes across domains insofar as the medical scenarios allowed. For participants in the description condition, the relevant probabilities and outcome magnitudes for each option were presented explicitly in written form immediately prior to each decision, and participants were given as much time as needed to read the information and indicate their choice. The description format emphasized that the probabilities were objectively known and provided numerical expressions (e.g., "20% chance of side effect") and frequency equivalents (e.g., "2 out of 10 people") to facilitate comprehension. For participants in the experience condition, the description of the options was minimal and the focal source of information was experiential sampling. Specifically, for each option the participant could draw a sample of outcomes by clicking a button that generated a single outcome observation drawn from the option's underlying distribution. Participants were encouraged to sample as much as they wished before making their choice, and a maximum number of samples per option was imposed to keep the session duration manageable; participants could take between zero and 50 samples per option, and they were free to sample both options in any order. For the experience condition, each sampling draw presented feedback indicating the realized outcome for that draw in domain-appropriate terms (for example, a monetary gain or loss amount for financial gambles and whether a side effect occurred for medical options, with clarifying consequence descriptions). The sampling algorithm was implemented so that draws were independent and identically distributed according to the true probability structure of each option. After sampling, participants were asked to make the same binary choice between the two options that the description group faced, and they were also asked to provide a judgment of the probability of the adverse outcome for the risky option on a continuous scale as a manipulation check. All decisions were hypothetical in nature; participants were told that their choices would not have direct financial consequences or lead to actual medical treatment, though the problems were framed to encourage thoughtful reflection as though they were real decisions. Following the six decision trials, participants completed a short set of questionnaires assessing prior experience with medications (e.g., "Have you taken prescription medicine in the past year?"), baseline risk attitudes (a brief four-item self-report scale), numeracy (a brief subjective numeracy scale), and demographic characteristics. Participants were also asked to indicate whether they recognized or had prior experience with scenarios similar to those presented, to provide a measure of prior familiarity. The procedure for the entire session lasted approximately 30 to 45 minutes, depending on the amount of sampling taken by participants in the experience condition, and participants were thanked for their time and debriefed at the end of the session. The dependent variable of primary interest was a binary indicator for each problem denoting whether the participant chose the risky option (coded 1) or the safe option (coded 0). Secondary dependent measures included the judged probability of adverse outcomes on the continuous scale and the number of samples taken by participants in the experience condition for each option. Data were examined for completeness, and participants who failed to provide responses to more than one-third of the decision trials or who took zero seconds and gave implausible response patterns were flagged for potential exclusion; however, no participants met pre-specified exclusion criteria and the final sample included all 78 participants. Statistical analyses were planned to evaluate the primary hypotheses using a combination of between-subjects tests for each problem and mixed-effects logistic regression models that leveraged the repeated-measures structure of the design. For the primary analyses, simple chi-square tests or Fisher's exact tests were run for each problem to compare the proportion of risky choices across the description and experience groups. In addition, generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with a binomial link function were estimated to test for main effects of format (description vs experience), domain (financial vs medical), and their interaction while controlling for random participant effects and for the within-subject correlation across the six trials. In these mixed models, problem-level fixed effects were included to capture differences across the three problems in each domain, and random intercepts for participants accounted for between-subject variability in baseline risk propensity. Effect sizes for chi-square tests were reported using phi coefficients, and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported for logistic regression coefficients; p-values were used for hypothesis testing with a conventional alpha of 0.05. Exploratory analyses were pre-registered in the analysis plan and included examining whether the number of sampled adverse outcomes experienced by participants in the experience condition predicted choice behavior and whether individual differences such as prior medication experience and numeracy moderated the effect of format. Manipulation checks included comparing judged probabilities of adverse outcomes between the two format groups and comparing these judgments with the objective probabilities used in the materials. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the university institutional review board, and all participants provided informed consent. Data were collected anonymously, and no personally identifiable medical information was solicited.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the sample indicated that the 78 participants had a mean age of 26.8 years (SD = 8.3), with 49% identifying as female and the remainder identifying as male or nonbinary; approximately two-thirds were current university students and one-third were community members. Prior prescription medication use in the past year was reported by 58% of participants, and the sample displayed a range of self-reported baseline risk attitudes and numeracy scores. Random assignment yielded two groups that were similar on age, gender, prior medication experience, and baseline risk attitude, with no statistically significant differences between the description and experience groups on these demographic or baseline measures. Overall, participants completed the six decision trials with high compliance, and no participants met the pre-specified exclusion criteria for incomplete or implausible responding. The proportion of participants choosing the risky option varied across the six problems, reflecting the designed differences in probability structure and expected utility; for ease of presentation, the three financial problems are referred to as F1, F2, and F3, and the three medical problems are referred to as M1, M2, and M3. In the description group, the proportions choosing the risky option were as follows: F1 = 0.38, F2 = 0.21, F3 = 0.46, M1 = 0.28, M2 = 0.31, M3 = 0.33. In the experience group, the corresponding proportions were: F1 = 0.44, F2 = 0.54, F3 = 0.41, M1 = 0.33, M2 = 0.29, M3 = 0.36. These descriptive rates suggest that the largest difference between the experience and description groups occurred on financial problem F2, where the risky choice proportion increased from 21% to 54% in the experience group. A chi-square test comparing risky choice frequency on F2 across formats showed a statistically significant difference (chi-square(1) = 10.12, p = .0015), with an effect size phi = 0.36, indicating a moderate effect. Odds ratio analysis for F2 indicated that participants in the experience condition were approximately 4.39 times more likely to choose the risky option than those in the description condition (95% CI [1.74, 11.07], p = .0017). These findings for F2 are consistent with the hypothesis that experiential sampling can increase risk taking in certain financial problems, replicating the qualitative pattern reported by Hertwig and colleagues. For the other financial problems, no significant differences were observed. For F1, the difference between description (38%) and experience (44%) was small and not statistically significant (chi-square(1) = 0.30, p = .58). For F3, the difference between description (46%) and experience (41%) was likewise small and not significant (chi-square(1) = 0.13, p = .72). Thus, the experiential effect on financial risk taking appeared to be problem-specific rather than uniform across all financial problems in this study. Turning to the medical problems, none of the three medical dilemmas showed a significant difference between the description and experience groups. For M1, the proportions were 28% in the description group and 33% in the experience group (chi-square(1) = 0.26, p = .61). For M2, the proportions were 31% in the description group and 29% in the experience group (chi-square(1) = 0.03, p = .86). For M3, the proportions were 33% in the description group and 36% in the experience group (chi-square(1) = 0.06, p = .80). These null findings for the medical problems indicate that experiential sampling did not lead to increased risk taking in the medical domain, consistent with the second hypothesis. Manipulation checks showed that judged probabilities of adverse outcomes differed between the description and experience groups in predictable ways. For the financial domain, participants in the description group provided probability estimates that were close to the objective probabilities provided in the written materials, whereas participants in the experience group showed estimates that were more variable and sometimes deviated from the true probabilities in the direction implied by their samples. In particular, for F2 the average judged probability of the rare beneficial outcome in the experience group was higher than in the description group, reflecting the sampling histories that participants had experienced; this divergence corresponded to the increased selection of the risky option in the experience group for F2. For the medical problems, judged probabilities of side effects were not significantly different between the description and experience groups on average, suggesting that sampling did not strongly shift subjective probability judgments for the medical vignettes. Mixed-effects logistic regression models that combined all six trials into a single analysis with random intercepts for participants provided a more nuanced test of domain and format effects. In a model predicting risky choice from format (experience = 1, description = 0), domain (medical = 1, financial = 0), and the format × domain interaction, with fixed effects for problem nested within domain and with participant random intercepts, the main effect of format was positive but not statistically significant (β = 0.28, SE = 0.20, z = 1.40, p = .16). The main effect of domain (medical vs financial) was negative (β = −0.22, SE = 0.15, z = −1.47, p = .14), reflecting a nonsignificant tendency for participants to be slightly less likely to choose risky options in medical trials overall. Crucially, the format × domain interaction term was negative and approached significance (β = −0.45, SE = 0.25, z = −1.80, p = .07), suggesting that the effect of experience on risk taking was more pronounced in the financial domain than in the medical domain; although this interaction did not reach conventional levels of significance in the combined model, it was in the direction predicted by the hypotheses and echoed the pattern observed in the single-problem analyses. To examine whether problem-level variation drove the mixed-model results, additional models included problem-specific format effects. These analyses revealed that the format effect was significant and large for F2 (β = 1.48, SE = 0.49, z = 3.02, p = .0025), replicating the single-problem chi-square finding within the mixed-model framework. No other problem showed a significant format effect in the mixed model. Exploratory analyses examined the relationship between the number of adverse events observed during sampling and subsequent choice among participants in the experience condition. For financial problems, the number of sampled beneficial outcomes for the risky option predicted choosing that option on F2 (Spearman's rho = .42, p = .006), indicating that participants who happened to sample more favorable outcomes were more likely to select the risky gamble. For medical problems, the number of sampled side effects experienced during sampling did not predict choosing the safe versus risky treatment in a consistent manner; correlations for M1–M3 were small and nonsignificant (all ps > .20). Moderation analyses evaluated whether individual differences such as prior medication use and numeracy influenced the format effect. Prior medication experience did not significantly moderate the format effect in the medical domain (interaction p = .44), and numeracy showed a weak moderation effect in the financial domain such that higher numeracy participants were slightly less influenced by sampling on F2 (interaction β = −0.32, SE = 0.18, z = −1.78, p = .075). These moderation findings were exploratory and should be treated with caution. Overall, the key pattern in the results was a replication-like increase in risk taking for the experience group relative to the description group for one of the financial problems (F2), while no reliable experience-related increase in risk taking was observed across the medical problems. The mixed-effects models suggested a trend toward a domain-dependent format effect, and exploratory analyses indicated that variation in sampled outcomes partly accounted for choice behavior in the financial domain but not in the medical domain. Manipulation checks and individual difference analyses provided supplementary evidence consistent with the central conclusions and underscored the role of sampling variability in shaping choice in some but not all problems.

Discussion

The present study examined whether experiential sampling of undesirable side effects influences risk-taking in hypothetical medical dilemmas and whether experiential effects observed in financial gambles generalize to the medical domain. The results showed that experiential sampling increased risk taking for one of the three financial problems, replicating the general pattern reported by Hertwig and colleagues for small financial gambles in which sampling can lead to increased selection of risky options. At the same time, experiential sampling did not produce a comparable increase in risk taking for any of the three medical problems, supporting the hypothesis that the description–experience effect may be attenuated or absent in hypothetical medical decision contexts. These findings contribute to the literature on the description–experience gap by highlighting domain-specific boundary conditions and by suggesting that the psychological processes that cause sampling to influence choice may operate differently when the subject matter is health-related rather than monetary. The significance of the experience-related increase in risk taking observed for financial problem F2 and the absence of such an effect for medical scenarios deserve careful interpretation. The effect for F2 was robust and statistically significant in both single-problem tests and mixed-effects models, and it was accompanied by judged probability estimates that were shifted in the direction of the sampling histories. This pattern is consistent with a straightforward sampling error account: when participants sample a small number of outcomes, the representation of the underlying probability distribution can be biased, leading to under- or over-representation of rare events and thereby altering subsequent choices. In our case, the sampling histories for F2 apparently yielded boosted subjective estimates of the favorable low-probability outcome, which increased selection of the risky option. The fact that F2 exhibited this effect while F1 and F3 did not suggests that sampling effects are contingent on the specific probabilistic structure of the gamble and on the likelihood that a limited sample will misrepresent rare outcomes. In prior work, such heterogeneity across problems has been noted and is thought to reflect the stochastic nature of sampling combined with certain shapes of probability distributions that make some problems more susceptible to sampling-induced biases. The failure to observe an experience-related increase in risk taking across medical problems raises several possible explanations that bear on both theory and practice. One possibility is that medical decisions inherently evoke stronger affective responses or moral considerations that stabilize preferences against transient sampling influences. Health outcomes are often more salient and emotionally charged than monetary rewards of the magnitudes used in behavioral experiments, and this affective salience may anchor choices in a manner less susceptible to the idiosyncrasies of small samples. In other words, the prospect of experiencing a side effect, even when observed as rare or absent in a sample, may evoke aversion that persists independent of sampled outcomes. Alternatively, participants may have entered the medical decision trials with stronger and more structured prior beliefs about medication safety that dominated the information gained through sampling. Broadly held cultural narratives, personal or vicarious past experiences with medical treatments, and general attitudes toward risk in health contexts could have constituted priors that a small sample could not meaningfully update. If participants already believed certain side effects to be possible or likely, limited experiential sampling that failed to produce clear evidence to the contrary might not have been sufficient to shift their choices. This interpretation aligns with Bayesian accounts of belief updating in which prior beliefs can overwhelm weak or noisy evidence. Another potential explanation concerns the perceived realism and consequentiality of the hypothetical medical scenarios. Even though the medical vignettes were designed to be plausible, participants may have treated medical problems as more consequential and more deserving of caution than hypothetical monetary gambles; this difference in perceived stakes may have resulted in more conservative risk attitudes across formats and thereby limited observable variance attributable to sampling. The experimental design used hypothetical decisions for both domains, but participants may have spontaneously attributed greater real-world consequences to medical vignettes, effectively increasing risk aversion and reducing the operational influence of sampling. Additionally, the nature of the sampling procedure itself may have interacted with domain in a way that reduced its impact on medical choices. In the experience condition, participants sampled outcomes and received feedback indicating whether side effects occurred for given draws, but the affective intensity of a simulated side effect described in a short vignette might have been insufficient to produce the same type of vivid experiential learning that a real or personally salient side effect would engender. In contrast, sampling monetary gains and losses may be more easily conceptualized and directly linked to choices in the experimental setting, yielding a more seamless mapping between experiences and subsequent monetary choices. Methodological aspects of the present study also merit consideration as possible contributors to the observed pattern. The sample size of 78 participants provided reasonable power to detect moderate effects in single-problem tests but may have been underpowered for detecting smaller format × domain interactions in the mixed-effects framework. The pattern of results could therefore reflect a true but modest domain-dependent experiential effect that the current study lacked the sensitivity to detect across all problems. Moreover, the decision to allow participants to sample up to 50 draws per option introduced variability in sampling behavior that may have affected the degree of belief updating; participants who sampled extensively may have converged on the true probabilities and thereby behaved more like the description group, while those who sampled sparingly may have been more susceptible to sampling noise. This heterogeneity in sampling intensity could have attenuated average effects, particularly in the medical domain where participants may have chosen to sample less frequently because of discomfort with the content or because they felt confident in their priors. The exploratory finding that, within the experience group, the number of sampled beneficial outcomes predicted selection of the risky option for the salient financial problem supports the sampling error interpretation for financial choices. That relationship did not hold in the medical domain, suggesting that sampling had a weaker or more ambiguous influence on subjective beliefs about side effects. The present results also engage with theoretical accounts emphasizing memory biases and recency effects as contributors to the description–experience gap. If memory accessibility of specific sampled outcomes drives choice, then sampling that yields vivid or frequently remembered favorable monetary outcomes could bias subsequent decisions in the financial domain. For medical outcomes, however, the relative lack of vividness or personal relevance in the simulated side effects could lead to weaker memory encoding and less influence on later choices. Future research should measure memory and affective responses explicitly to adjudicate between these mechanisms. The clinical implications of the findings are nuanced. On the one hand, the absence of a robust experiential effect in hypothetical medical choices might be taken as reassuring, suggesting that patients do not readily collapse into greater risk taking merely because their limited personal experience includes few side effects. On the other hand, clinical observations reported by Fleischhacker and colleagues regarding systematic underestimation of side effects remain salient, and the present results do not negate the possibility that in real clinical contexts — where personal stakes are high and experiences are more emotionally charged — sampling could meaningfully bias patients’ perceptions and choices. Indeed, the controlled laboratory sampling in this experiment may only approximate the complex experiential histories that patients accumulate over time, which include vivid firsthand side effects, anecdotes from friends and family, and information gleaned from media and clinicians. Because such real-world experiential inputs can be emotionally salient and reinforced by social consequences, they may have a stronger and more persistent influence on medical choices than the brief sampling episodes used here. Several limitations of the present study constrain the scope of inferences that can be drawn and suggest directions for future research. First, the use of hypothetical choices, while ethically necessary for medical scenarios, may have reduced the ecological validity of the medical trials and differentially affected engagement across domains. Future studies should attempt to enhance realism through richer vignettes, immersive simulations, or the use of incentivized monetary trade-offs that better approximate real stakes. Second, the sample size and homogeneity of a university-centered recruitment strategy limited generalizability; replication with larger and more diverse samples, including patient populations with relevant lived experiences, is needed to assess whether the observed domain differences hold in broader populations. Third, the experiment did not systematically manipulate sample size, sampling costs, or sampling modality, and these factors are likely to moderate the extent to which sampling influences choice. A systematic variation of sampling parameters would help clarify the boundary conditions for the description–experience gap in different content domains. Fourth, individual differences such as trait anxiety, trust in medical professionals, and numeracy may affect how people integrate experiential and descriptive information, and future work should include more extensive measures of these moderators to explain heterogeneity in effects. Fifth, the medical vignettes in this study were intentionally constrained to be ethically acceptable and relatively nonthreatening; more emotionally intense medical scenarios might engage affective processing differently and potentially yield stronger experiential effects. In light of these limitations, several concrete steps for future research emerge. Experiments that manipulate prior beliefs explicitly, for example through pre-exposure to narratives that instantiate strong priors, could test whether sampling can overcome entrenched beliefs in medical contexts. Longitudinal designs that follow patients through actual treatment experiences would offer ecologically valid insights into how real-world sampling of side effects shapes adherence and risk perception over time. Neuroimaging and psychophysiological measures could also illuminate whether affective engagement differs systematically across domains during sampling and whether such differences predict the impact of experience on choice. From an applied perspective, the pattern of results highlights the importance of carefully considered risk communication strategies in medical contexts. If patients are particularly resistant to being swayed by small samples in clinical settings, then descriptive risk information (e.g., explicit probabilities, frequency formats) and clinician guidance remain essential tools for ensuring informed choices. Conversely, if experiential information in real-world contexts can bias perceptions, then clinicians and public health communicators should be vigilant about the narratives and sampled experiences that patients bring to decision-making encounters. Decision aids that combine clear descriptive statistics with vivid but calibrated experiential representations (e.g., simulated patient stories that accurately reflect base rates) may help bridge the descriptive and experiential modes in a way that promotes accurate risk comprehension. The results also prompt caution about relying on anecdotes or limited personal experience as a basis for medical decisions. Educational interventions that emphasize the unreliability of small samples and that train patients in probabilistic reasoning could reduce the potential for sampling-induced biases to shape choices in harmful ways. In sum, the present study provides partial replication of prior work demonstrating experiential increases in risk taking for financial gambles and extends the literature by showing that similar effects may not emerge in hypothetical medical dilemmas under the conditions tested. These findings underscore the domain specificity of the description–experience gap and call for more nuanced theoretical accounts that incorporate affective salience, prior beliefs, and sampling properties when predicting the influence of experience on risk preferences. Future research that systematically manipulates sample size, affective intensity, and prior belief strength, and that employs ecologically valid medical scenarios, will be essential to delineate the conditions under which experiential sampling alters clinical decision making. Until then, clinicians and policymakers should recognize that patients may integrate experiential and descriptive information in complex ways, and that robust risk communication strategies will require attention to both modes of learning.
