Introduction

Human development unfolds within a network of social relationships, and family bonds are among the most influential of those relationships. Siblings in particular provide a distinctive social context because they share household resources, peers, and often school experiences while also engaging in direct competition for parental attention and material advantages. A growing body of literature has documented robust associations between sibling structures and individual differences in personality, cognitive development, and social skills. Researchers have proposed multiple mechanisms through which siblings might shape individual outcomes, including modeling of behavior, direct competition, social learning, and allocation of parental resources. The developmental significance of sibling dynamics extends into domains such as emotional regulation, prosocial behavior, and academic development. Given the centrality of achievement in many societies, an important question is whether sibling dynamics influence not only academic outcomes but also the orientation toward competition in academic contexts. Competition for grades, recognition, and academic status is a feature of many schooling environments, and an individual's propensity to engage in academic competition has implications for motivation, anxiety, and social relationships. Despite the relevance of this trait-like tendency, relatively little work has examined sibling influences on patterns of academic competitiveness outside the family. Several lines of theoretical work suggest that siblings could influence competitive orientation in broader social contexts. Social learning theory suggests that behaviors and attitudes are learned through observation and imitation, and siblings serve as salient models for achievement-related strategies. Social comparison theory proposes that individuals evaluate their own abilities and statuses by comparing themselves to relevant others, and siblings often serve as primary comparison targets during development. Resource competition theories argue that competition within families for finite resources may cultivate competitive strategies that generalize to other domains. Birth-order theories have similarly posited that positions within sibling hierarchies carry specific roles and tendencies that might shape outward-facing social behaviors, although that literature is contested. Empirical research has found links between sibling composition and various outcomes; for example, some studies indicate that children with more siblings receive less parental attention per child and exhibit different educational trajectories than only children, while other studies suggest that sibling interaction fosters cooperation and emotional intelligence. The mixed nature of prior findings, combined with the theoretical plausibility of multiple mechanisms, indicates that empirical work focused specifically on academic competitiveness across contexts is needed. Prior studies have often considered academic achievement or attainment as outcomes, rather than competitiveness as a psychological disposition or situational orientation. Competitiveness is a multifaceted construct that can be expressed in domain-specific ways; some individuals may be highly competitive in sport but not in academic pursuits, and vice versa. If sibling interactions cultivate a general competitive orientation, then one would expect sibling-related competitive behavior to be associated with competitiveness toward classmates. Conversely, if sibling-related competition is highly context-specific, then one might observe little correspondence between sibling-based competition and competition among peers in school settings. Moreover, the number of siblings in a family could plausibly influence competitive orientation via exposure to intrafamilial competitive dynamics; having multiple siblings could increase opportunities for rivalry, yet having only one or no siblings might limit direct competition but encourage other forms of social comparison outside the family. Empirical work on sibling number and competitive dispositions has been sparse and inconsistent, leaving open the question of whether the number of siblings predicts competitiveness with classmates. Another factor that may influence the relationship between sibling dynamics and academic competitiveness is participation in competitive extracurricular domains, such as organized sport. Participation in sport is highly associated with competitive orientation and may serve as an alternative outlet for competitive drives. Individuals who participate in sport may diffuse or amplify competitive tendencies in ways that interact with sibling influences. For instance, a person who engages in organized sport may learn competitive skills and norms that transfer to academic contexts, or sport involvement may satisfy the need for competition, reducing competitive behavior in school. Consequently, it is important to account for sport participation when examining sibling effects on academic competitiveness with classmates. Taken together, theory and prior empirical findings motivate an investigation of three related questions. First, does the number of siblings an individual has relate to their reported academic competitiveness with classmates? Second, is competitiveness between siblings associated with competitiveness between classmates, suggesting cross-contextual transfer of competitive orientation? Third, does participation in sports account for, moderate, or otherwise affect these associations? The present study was designed to examine these questions in a sample of young adults recruited from a university population. The study used self-report questionnaire measures to assess number of siblings, self-reported academic competitiveness with siblings, and self-reported academic competitiveness with classmates. In addition, the questionnaire included a measure of participation in organized sport to permit statistical control for this potentially confounding domain of competition. There were several reasons for focusing on a university sample. Young adults in higher education are in a relevant developmental period for academic competition, and they can reliably report on both current and retrospective sibling dynamics. University environments also provide a clear context for comparisons with classmates. Although a community sample might broaden generalizability, a university sample allows for tighter control over contextual heterogeneity in schooling experiences. Based on the theoretical perspectives outlined above, competing predictions were possible for the role of sibling number. If the frequency of intrafamilial competition is a function of sibling number, then more siblings might be associated with greater propensity to engage in competition with peers at school. Alternatively, if competitive orientation derives more from qualitative features of sibling relationships (for example, rivalry intensity or modeling) than from quantity, then the number of siblings may not predict competitiveness with classmates. Regarding cross-contextual transfer, if competitive strategies learned within the family generalize to peer contexts, then sibling-directed academic competitiveness should be positively associated with classmate-directed academic competitiveness. Finally, if competitive behavior is domain-general and sport is a significant locus of competition, then participation in sport might explain part or all of any observed associations between sibling and classmate competitiveness. The present study therefore tested three primary hypotheses. Hypothesis 1 predicted a positive bivariate association between number of siblings and academic competitiveness with classmates. Hypothesis 2 predicted a positive association between academic competitiveness with siblings and academic competitiveness with classmates. Hypothesis 3 predicted that any association between number of siblings and classmate competitiveness would be attenuated or eliminated after controlling for participation in sport. Because the literature did not provide unambiguous guidance about effect sizes, the present study was primarily exploratory in the sense of testing whether the expected relationships would be present in a modestly sized sample. The analyses were planned to include descriptive statistics, tests of internal consistency for multi-item scales, Pearson correlations, partial correlations controlling for sport participation, and multiple regression models to evaluate the simultaneous effects of predictors. The study also planned to check assumptions of normality and linearity to ensure that parametric analyses were appropriate. Given the potential for gender differences in competitive orientation, gender distributions were recorded and basic comparisons were examined as exploratory analyses. By addressing the triad of sibling quantity, sibling-directed competitiveness, and sport involvement, the study aimed to clarify the extent to which family-based competition shapes competitive behavior among classmates. The findings would contribute to theoretical debates about whether competitive tendencies are shaped primarily by family context, extracurricular activities, or general dispositional factors. Through explicit measurement of both sibling and classmate competitive behavior, the study would also speak to the issue of cross-contextual generalization of competitive orientations. The results were expected to have implications for educators and parents interested in understanding the transmission of competitive norms and for researchers designing interventions to foster adaptive forms of achievement motivation. If sibling competitiveness strongly predicts classmate competitiveness, then interventions targeting sibling interactions may have downstream effects on classroom dynamics. If the number of siblings does not predict classmate competitiveness, then concerns about family size as a driver of competitive behavior may be misplaced. Finally, if sport participation accounts for a significant portion of competitive tendencies, then extracurricular programming might be a fruitful focus for shaping competitive orientation. This study therefore sought to provide empirical evidence to clarify these conceptual issues.

Methods

Participants were 100 undergraduate students recruited from a mid-sized public university through a participant recruitment system and through flyers posted in common areas. The sample included 62 female participants and 38 male participants, and participants ranged in age from 18 to 24 years with a mean age of 19.6 years and a standard deviation of 1.4 years. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and completed the study in a single online session that took approximately 12 minutes. The study protocol was approved by the university's human subjects review committee. Demographic information collected included age, gender, year in school, and parental education level as a crude proxy for socio-economic background. The key variables of interest were number of siblings, academic competitiveness with siblings, academic competitiveness with classmates, and participation in organized sports. Number of siblings was measured via a single-item question that asked participants to report the number of biological or adoptive siblings they had, with instructions to exclude half-siblings and step-siblings unless they were raised in the same household. Responses were recorded as a discrete integer variable and ranged from 0 to 5 in the present sample. Academic competitiveness with classmates was measured using a five-item self-report scale developed for the present study with items that asked participants to indicate their agreement with statements such as "I try to outperform my classmates academically" and "I am motivated to get better grades than my peers." Items were rated on a seven-point Likert scale anchored by 1 = strongly disagree and 7 = strongly agree. The items were summed and averaged so that higher scores reflected greater self-reported academic competitiveness with classmates. Academic competitiveness with siblings was measured with a parallel five-item scale that asked participants to rate their agreement with statements such as "I compete with my siblings to get higher grades" and "I compare my academic achievements to my siblings' achievements." Items used the same seven-point Likert response format and were summed and averaged to produce a scale score. The two scales were designed to be parallel to facilitate comparison across social contexts. Participation in organized sports was assessed with a single dichotomous item that asked participants whether they currently participated in organized sport or had done so regularly in the past 12 months; responses were coded 1 = yes and 0 = no. This variable was included because organized sport provides a salient competitive environment that might be associated with general competitive orientation. The psychometric properties of the new scales were evaluated in the sample. Internal consistency reliability for the academic competitiveness with classmates scale was estimated with Cronbach's alpha and found to be 0.87. The sibling competitiveness scale demonstrated adequate internal consistency with Cronbach's alpha of 0.84. These reliability estimates indicated that the multi-item scales provided reasonably coherent measurements of the constructs of interest. Data cleaning procedures included inspection for missing values and outliers. Missing data were minimal, with fewer than 2% of item responses missing, and available-case analysis was used so that each analysis included all available observations. Univariate distributions of the continuous variables were examined for skewness and kurtosis. Number of siblings showed a modest positive skew, which is to be expected in a sample where smaller families are common. The scale scores for competitiveness approximated normal distributions with skewness and kurtosis within acceptable bounds for parametric tests. Scatterplots of pairs of variables suggested linear associations where present. To evaluate the primary hypotheses, a sequence of analyses was conducted. First, descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables, including means, standard deviations, and frequency distributions for categorical variables. Second, bivariate Pearson product-moment correlations were computed among number of siblings, sibling competitiveness, classmate competitiveness, and sport participation coded as 0/1. Third, a partial correlation analysis was performed to estimate the relationship between number of siblings and classmate competitiveness while statistically controlling for participation in sport. Fourth, multiple linear regression models were estimated with classmate competitiveness as the criterion variable and number of siblings, sibling competitiveness, and sport participation as simultaneous predictors. The regression analyses provided estimates of unique associations while adjusting for the other predictors. Diagnostic checks for regression assumptions included tests for multicollinearity, inspection of residuals for homoscedasticity, and evaluation of influential points via standardized residuals and Cook's distance. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors. Additional exploratory analyses included independent-samples t-tests to examine gender differences in classmate competitiveness and sibling competitiveness. Effect sizes were reported using standardized beta coefficients in regression models and Cohen's d for group comparisons where appropriate. Statistical significance was assessed using two-tailed tests with an alpha level of 0.05. All analyses were conducted using a standard statistical software package. The sample size of 100 provided adequate power to detect medium-sized effects in correlational analyses and regression models, although the power to detect small effects was limited. The study emphasized effect size estimation as well as hypothesis testing because sample size constraints make precise estimation of small effects difficult. The analytic approach prioritized clarity and parsimony by focusing on simple bivariate and multivariate associations aligned with the primary research questions. Ethical considerations included debriefing participants at the end of the session and providing contact information for counseling services in case discussion of family dynamics raised distress for any participant. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated beyond course credit where applicable. Data were stored on a secure server with identifying information removed for analysis to protect participant confidentiality. The study sought to balance the need for concise measurement with the goal of capturing meaningful variation in competitive orientation across social contexts.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary study variables are presented first. The mean number of siblings in the sample was 1.82 with a standard deviation of 1.15, and the distribution ranged from 0 to 5 siblings. The mean score on the academic competitiveness with classmates scale was 3.42 on the seven-point scale with a standard deviation of 1.06. The mean score on the academic competitiveness with siblings scale was 3.11 with a standard deviation of 1.18. Forty-seven percent of the sample reported current participation in organized sports whereas 53% reported no participation in the past 12 months. Gender distributions indicated that 62% of participants were female and 38% were male. Initial inspection of variable distributions showed that competitiveness scores approximated normality sufficiently for parametric analyses, with skewness and kurtosis values within conventional bounds. The reliability analyses confirmed that the competitiveness scales were internally consistent, with Cronbach's alpha equal to 0.87 for the classmate competitiveness scale and 0.84 for the sibling competitiveness scale. These reliability coefficients suggested that the scale scores provided stable measurement of the constructs. Bivariate Pearson correlations among the primary variables are summarized next. The correlation between number of siblings and academic competitiveness with classmates was small and not statistically significant, r = 0.08, p = 0.42. This finding indicated that there was no evidence of a linear relationship between how many siblings an individual had and how competitively they reported interacting with classmates in academic domains. The correlation between number of siblings and academic competitiveness with siblings was modest, r = 0.12, but this association was also not statistically significant at conventional levels, p = 0.24. In contrast, academic competitiveness with siblings was moderately and positively correlated with academic competitiveness with classmates, r = 0.45, p < 0.001. This relationship suggested a substantial degree of correspondence between competitive orientation toward siblings and toward peers in the academic domain. The point-biserial correlation between participation in organized sports (coded 0/1) and academic competitiveness with classmates was r = 0.10, which did not reach statistical significance, p = 0.31. The correlation between sport participation and sibling competitiveness was r = 0.09, p = 0.35. Given the non-significant bivariate association between number of siblings and classmate competitiveness, a partial correlation was computed to examine whether controlling for sport participation would reveal an association that was otherwise masked. The partial correlation between number of siblings and classmate competitiveness controlling for sport participation was small and non-significant, partial r = 0.06, p = 0.54. Thus, sport participation did not account for or reveal any hidden relationship between sibling number and competitiveness with classmates. To examine unique contributions of predictors simultaneously, a multiple linear regression model was estimated with academic competitiveness with classmates as the dependent variable and number of siblings, sibling competitiveness, and sport participation as predictors. The overall model was statistically significant, F(3,96) = 8.99, p < 0.001, and the model accounted for approximately 22% of the variance in classmate competitiveness (R^2 = 0.22). Within the model, sibling competitiveness emerged as a strong and significant predictor of classmate competitiveness, standardized beta = 0.43, t = 4.73, p < 0.001. The positive beta indicated that higher sibling-directed competitiveness was associated with higher classmate-directed competitiveness, controlling for number of siblings and sport participation. Number of siblings was not a significant predictor in the model, standardized beta = 0.07, t = 0.70, p = 0.48. Sport participation was also not a significant predictor when included alongside the other variables, standardized beta = 0.09, t = 0.91, p = 0.36. Diagnostic checks of the regression model did not indicate problematic multicollinearity; variance inflation factors for the predictors were all below 1.20. Inspection of residuals indicated homoscedasticity and approximate normality of residuals. No single observation had a Cook's distance suggesting undue influence on the model estimates. These diagnostics supported the appropriateness of the linear regression approach. Exploratory analyses examined gender differences in the key competitiveness measures. An independent-samples t-test comparing females and males on academic competitiveness with classmates showed no significant difference, t(98) = 1.21, p = 0.23, Cohen's d = 0.24. Similarly, there was no significant gender difference for sibling competitiveness, t(98) = 0.89, p = 0.38, Cohen's d = 0.18. In a follow-up regression that included an interaction term for gender by sibling competitiveness, the interaction was not significant, indicating that the relationship between sibling and classmate competitiveness did not differ as a function of gender in this sample. Given the moderate correlation between sibling and classmate competitiveness, an additional analysis probed whether the association held for participants with no siblings compared to those with one or more siblings. Among participants reporting zero siblings (n = 18), the mean classmate competitiveness score was 3.10 (SD = 1.12), while participants with one or more siblings had a mean of 3.48 (SD = 1.04). The difference was not statistically significant, t(98) = 1.36, p = 0.18. The correlation between sibling competitiveness and classmate competitiveness necessarily could not be computed for the no-sibling group for sibling-directed items, but analyses restricted to participants with at least one sibling (n = 82) showed a similar correlation between sibling and classmate competitiveness, r = 0.46, p < 0.001. This finding indicated that the cross-contextual association was present among individuals with siblings and accounted for a meaningful proportion of variance. To gauge practical significance, the squared correlation between sibling and classmate competitiveness indicated that approximately 20% of the variance in classmate competitiveness was shared with sibling-directed competitiveness in bivariate terms. Adjusting for other predictors in the regression attenuated but did not eliminate this association. Overall, the pattern of results was consistent across analytic approaches: number of siblings was unrelated to academic competitiveness with classmates, sport participation did not explain associations, and sibling-directed competitiveness was a robust predictor of classmate-directed competitiveness. These findings were summarized and interpreted further in the following discussion.

Discussion

The present study investigated the relationships among number of siblings, academic competitiveness with siblings, academic competitiveness with classmates, and participation in organized sport in a sample of university students. The principal finding was that self-reported competitiveness directed toward siblings was a substantial and significant predictor of self-reported competitiveness directed toward classmates. In contrast, the number of siblings bore no detectable association with competitiveness toward classmates, and participation in organized sport did not account for the observed relationships. These results speak to the theoretical issue of whether competitive orientations in academic contexts are shaped primarily by quantitative structural features of the family or by qualitative patterns of interaction within the family that generalize to other contexts. The finding that sibling-directed competitiveness predicted classmate-directed competitiveness supports the proposition that competitive tendencies cultivated within the family context can transfer to peer contexts. This cross-contextual correspondence aligns with social learning perspectives whereby patterns of behavior and normative orientations modeled and reinforced within close relationships become generalized cognitive schemas and behavioral strategies. If an individual frequently competes with siblings for grades or recognition, that individual may come to regard competitive striving as an appropriate or effective way to achieve academic goals in broader social settings. The observed moderate correlation between sibling and classmate competitiveness suggests that sibling dynamics are a meaningful contributor to an individual's general competitive orientation, explaining roughly one-fifth of the variance in classmate-directed competitiveness in our sample. That proportion of shared variance is substantial in psychological research on personality and social attitudes and indicates that sibling interactions merit attention as a developmental influence on competitive dispositions. In contrast, the lack of association between number of siblings and classmate competitiveness suggests that sheer sibling quantity is not a driver of how competitive individuals are with their classmates. This null finding is informative because it counters a simple resource-competition model in which having more siblings necessarily increases intrafamilial rivalry and thereby produces a more competitive orientation in other domains. Instead, the results imply that the presence and quality of competitive interactions, rather than their frequency or the mere existence of many siblings, are the important variables. In other words, rivalry intensity, parental comparisons, and interpersonal dynamics among siblings may be the operative mechanisms rather than family size per se. The lack of association between sport participation and classmate competitiveness indicates that involvement in organized competitive extracurricular activity did not explain the relationship between sibling and classmate competitiveness. This result suggests that the transfer of competitive orientation from siblings to classmates is not simply an artifact of a generalized competitive personality that is cultivated primarily through sport. Rather, family interactions appear to have an independent contribution. It remains possible, however, that more fine-grained measures of sport involvement, such as level of competition, years of participation, or individual versus team sport orientation, could reveal different patterns; the simple dichotomous assessment used in the present study may not have captured relevant variation. The regression analyses provided further support for the primacy of sibling-directed competitiveness in predicting classmate-directed competitiveness. When sibling competitiveness, number of siblings, and sport participation were entered simultaneously, sibling competitiveness was the only significant predictor. This pattern indicates that sibling-directed competitiveness has a unique association with classmate-directed competitiveness beyond any shared variance with sport involvement or family size. The model accounted for about 22% of the variance in classmate competitiveness, which suggests a meaningful but not exhaustive role for sibling dynamics. Other factors such as personality traits, parenting styles, school climate, and peer group norms likely also contribute to competitive orientation and were not measured in the current study. The results also intersect with theoretical debates concerning birth order and family size effects. While some perspectives have argued that birth order confers stable personality differences that could include competitive tendencies, the present study's focus on number of siblings did not support the broader family size argument. It is possible that birth order effects are more nuanced and that factors like age spacing, gender constellation of siblings, and parental behavior interact with birth order in ways not captured by a simple count of siblings. Future research could examine these more nuanced family structural variables to refine understanding of how within-family position shapes competitive behavior. The strengths of the study include explicit measurement of competition in two social contexts using parallel scales, adequate internal consistency of the measures, and a clear analytic strategy that tested both bivariate and multivariate associations. Nonetheless, several limitations constrain the interpretation of the findings. First, the study relied entirely on self-report measures, which may be influenced by social desirability, retrospective bias, or lack of introspective accuracy. Participants may have varying standards for what constitutes competitive behavior, and their reports may reflect subjective interpretations rather than objective behavior. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. While sibling-directed competitiveness was associated with classmate-directed competitiveness, the direction of influence cannot be established. It remains possible that individuals with a general competitive disposition seek out rivalry with both siblings and classmates, or that peer contexts shape sibling interactions through feedback processes. Third, the sample was limited to university students at a single institution, which may restrict generalizability to other age groups, socio-economic backgrounds, or cultural contexts. Fourth, the measure of sport participation was dichotomous and thus may have lacked sensitivity to capture the nuanced ways in which involvement in competitive extracurricular activities affects general competitive orientation. Fifth, because the sibling-directed competitiveness scale could only be completed by those with siblings, analyses examining the full sample necessarily excluded participants without siblings for certain comparisons, reducing effective sample size for some analyses. To extend this work, several avenues of future research are recommended. Longitudinal studies that follow children from early schooling into adolescence and young adulthood would permit stronger inferences about causal pathways and the developmental unfolding of competitive orientations across contexts. Such studies could assess sibling interactions, parental reinforcement, and school-based experiences repeatedly to model trajectories and potential mediators. Experimental interventions that modify sibling interaction patterns or encourage cooperative rather than competitive sibling activities could test whether changes within the family lead to changes in competitive orientation toward peers. Including observational data of sibling and classroom interactions would complement self-reports and provide behavioral validation of competitive tendencies. More detailed measurement of extracurricular involvement, including sport level, role in team versus individual sports, and duration of participation, would help clarify how external competitive domains interact with family experiences. Research that incorporates genome-environment interplay could also be valuable because genetic predispositions toward competitiveness may interact with sibling dynamics in shaping outcomes. Cross-cultural investigations would be informative as well, given that family structures, socialization practices, and the value placed on competition vary widely across cultures; the generalizability of the present findings to non-Western or collectivist contexts is an open question. Practical implications of the present findings include the suggestion that parents, educators, and counselors who are concerned about unhealthy competitive dynamics in classrooms might consider addressing sibling interactions as part of a broader strategy. Promoting healthy rivalry that emphasizes personal improvement and mastery rather than zero-sum comparison could help mitigate transfer of maladaptive competitive behaviors. Schools might also attend to students' family backgrounds as part of understanding classroom social dynamics, recognizing that competitive styles can be shaped at home. In conclusion, the current study provides evidence that the nature of competitive interactions within the family, as captured by self-reported sibling-directed academic competitiveness, is associated with competitive orientation toward classmates. The mere number of siblings, however, does not appear to influence how competitively one engages with peers in academic domains. These results highlight the centrality of interaction quality over family size in the transmission of competitive orientations and underscore the value of examining behavior across multiple social contexts to understand the development and expression of competition-related traits.
