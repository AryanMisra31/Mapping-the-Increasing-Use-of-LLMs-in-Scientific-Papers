Introduction

Empathy is a foundational capacity in human social life that facilitates understanding and responding to the emotional states of others. Empathic responding supports prosocial behavior, social bonding, conflict resolution, and moral reasoning. Empathy has been conceptualized as a multifaceted construct that includes both affective resonance with another person's feelings and cognitive perspective-taking that allows one to imaginatively adopt another's viewpoint. Research in social psychology and cognitive neuroscience has emphasized that empathy can be measured in multiple ways, including self-report questionnaires, behavioral tasks, and physiological indices. The format in which information about another person's experience is conveyed can influence the degree to which empathy is elicited. Communication channels bring with them different affordances for conveying details, evoking imagery, and prompting cognitive elaboration. Video-based stimuli provide rich audiovisual information that can make experiences appear more vivid and immediate, and they can capture facial expressions, tone of voice, bodily cues, and environmental context. Text-based stimuli, by contrast, rely on linguistic description and invite readers to reconstruct scenes and emotions using their own mental imagery and prior knowledge. Theoretical perspectives differ in their predictions about which modality will produce greater immediate empathic response. Some frameworks emphasize the power of vivid perceptual cues, predicting that richer sensory input will produce more pronounced affective resonance. Other perspectives highlight the role of active cognitive engagement and imaginative elaboration, proposing that modalities that require the reader or viewer to supply missing elements will foster deeper perspective-taking. Empirical work has documented that video can be more effective than text for certain outcomes such as procedural learning, memory for dynamic events, and immediate emotional arousal. These findings have informed practice in educational contexts and in media-based interventions where video is commonly used to maximize engagement. At the same time, a broader literature on narrative processing shows that stories presented in text can produce strong feelings of transportation and identification with characters, particularly when they engage readers' imagination and promote perspective-taking. Cognitive elaboration and narrative immersion can be powerful pathways to empathy that do not depend on vivid sensory detail provided externally. There has also been research showing that self-generated imagery and mental simulation can amplify emotional responses and foster personal relevance. In addition to differences in sensory richness and cognitive demand, modality effects may interact with individual differences in reading habits, media preferences, prior exposure to the depicted content, and cultural norms regarding emotional expression. For example, people who habitually consume narrative fiction through reading may be particularly skilled at conjuring mental images that support empathy when presented with text. Conversely, individuals who rely more on audiovisual media may find video more engaging and may be more likely to experience immediate affective resonance in response to filmed content. The measurement of empathy also matters for observed modality effects. Immediate self-report instruments designed to capture empathic concern following brief exposure to a stimulus may be more sensitive to short-term arousal, whereas measures that assess enduring perspective-taking tendencies may be less influenced by format. Prior experimental studies that directly compare text and video have sometimes produced mixed results, with some showing stronger responses to video and others showing equivalent or stronger responses to text, depending on the outcome and the specifics of the stimuli. These equivocal findings suggest that simple predictions about the superiority of one medium may be insufficient and that careful experimental work is needed to adjudicate the competing mechanisms. Understanding modality effects on immediate empathic response has practical importance because many interventions aimed at reducing prejudice and increasing compassion rely on media-based exposure to others' narratives. For instance, programs designed to reduce discrimination toward refugees and minority groups often use videos to portray personal stories of hardship and resilience. These programs operate under the assumption that video will maximize emotional engagement and therefore promote attitude change. If text-based materials were found to be at least as effective or even superior in eliciting immediate empathic reactions under some conditions, this would have implications for the design and scalability of interventions, particularly in contexts where resource constraints make video production impractical. Moreover, text-based approaches have advantages in terms of cost, ease of distribution, and the potential for repeated, self-paced engagement. The comparison between text and video is also timely in light of trends in digital communication, where social media platforms frequently host both short video clips and text-based testimonials. Understanding how format influences the immediate empathic response can inform strategies for public messaging and community interventions aimed at reducing prejudice. In the current study, we examined differences in immediate empathic response to matched text-based stimuli and video-based stimuli using a well-validated empathy questionnaire administered immediately after exposure. We prepared stimulus pairs that were equivalent in content and did not differ in the core informational elements they conveyed, and we tested whether the format itself would produce differential empathic responding. Based on prior research demonstrating stronger responses to video in domains such as learning and emotional arousal, we initially predicted that the video condition would elicit a greater immediate empathy score than the text condition. However, theoretical reasons based on cognitive elaboration and narrative processing suggested an alternative possibility that text could engender stronger perspective-taking by prompting readers to generate personal imagery. We also considered that modality effects might be mediated by subjective experiences such as perceived vividness, personal relevance, and level of cognitive engagement. Finally, given the applied significance of format choice for anti-discrimination training and public outreach, we included measures to assess the potential practical implications of any observed differences. The study therefore aimed not only to test the simple hypothesis about modality superiority but also to explore mechanisms and boundary conditions that could inform the use of media in interventions targeting empathy toward out-groups such as refugees and minority communities. The following sections describe the detailed methodological approach, the analytic strategy, the observed results, and the implications of the findings for theory and practice.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a metropolitan university community and surrounding city area using online advertisements, flyers, and a participant database. A total of 220 individuals initially expressed interest, and 200 participants completed the study and provided usable data. The final sample included adults aged 18 to 65, with a mean age of 27.9 years and a standard deviation of 9.6 years. Participants were 58 percent female, 40 percent male, and 2 percent nonbinary or other, and they represented a range of ethnic backgrounds. All participants were fluent in the language of the stimuli and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing. Participants provided informed consent electronically prior to participation, and the study procedures were approved by the institutional review board of the sponsoring institution. The study employed a between-subjects design in which participants were randomly assigned to either a text condition or a video condition. Random assignment was implemented using an online randomization tool integrated into the survey platform, and assignment occurred after baseline measures were collected. Baseline measures included demographic information, prior contact with the target out-group described in the stimuli, general media consumption habits (including hours per week spent reading long-form text and hours per week spent watching video content), and a brief baseline empathy scale to capture trait empathic tendencies prior to stimulus exposure. The stimuli depicted a short first-person narrative about an individual's experience of displacement and hardship, crafted to be representative of experiences often associated with refugees and other marginalized populations. The content was developed through consultation with subject-matter experts to ensure sensitivity and accuracy, and the same narrative script was used to create both the text and video versions. The text stimulus was presented as a written first-person account approximately 500 words in length, formatted for readability with short paragraphs and a clear narrative arc. The video stimulus was produced by filming an actor reading the same script in a neutral setting with minimal background elements, accompanied by close-up shots of the speaker's face and subtle visual cues to context, and the final video clip lasted approximately 90 seconds to match the average reading time for the text stimulus. Efforts were made to equate the two conditions on information, voice, and duration while acknowledging intrinsic differences in modality. After exposure to their assigned stimulus, participants completed the primary outcome measure, which was a well-validated self-report measure of immediate empathic response designed for use following brief narrative exposure. The empathy questionnaire included items assessing empathic concern, emotional resonance, perspective-taking, and compassion, with items rated on a Likert scale from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much). The questionnaire was administered immediately after stimulus exposure to capture the short-term empathic reaction. In addition to the primary empathy measure, participants completed a set of manipulation check items assessing perceived vividness, perceived realism, emotional intensity, personal relevance, and degree of cognitive engagement elicited by the stimulus. These items were also rated on 7-point Likert scales. We also included attention-check questions to ensure participants attended to the stimulus and instructions. The procedure took place online with instructions that participants should complete the study in a quiet environment without interruptions. Participants first completed baseline measures, were then randomly assigned and exposed to either the text or video stimulus, and completed the post-exposure questionnaires. On average, the session took 20 minutes to complete. The primary analytic approach involved comparing immediate empathy scores between the text and video conditions using independent-samples t-tests. Prior to hypothesis testing, we screened the data for quality, removed participants who failed attention checks or who had incomplete data on the primary outcome, and assessed the distribution of the empathy scores for normality. We also calculated Cronbach's alpha to assess the internal consistency of the empathy questionnaire in this sample. Because baseline empathy and several demographic variables could theoretically influence post-exposure empathy, we conducted supplemental analyses using analysis of covariance with baseline empathy as a covariate. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen's d for mean differences and partial eta-squared for ANCOVA results. To explore potential mechanisms, we conducted mediation analyses to examine whether subjective vividness, perceived realism, or cognitive engagement mediated the relationship between stimulus modality and immediate empathic response. These mediation models used bootstrapped confidence intervals with 5,000 resamples. We also ran moderation analyses to assess whether individual differences such as prior contact with the depicted out-group, habitual reading or video consumption, and trait empathy moderated the effect of modality on immediate empathic response. Statistical significance was evaluated at the conventional alpha level of .05, and where multiple comparisons were conducted, we applied false discovery rate correction to control for Type I error. We also estimated the statistical power prior to data collection using a conservative effect size of d = 0.40, with power of 0.80 requiring approximately 200 participants, which guided our recruitment target. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software and were pre-registered prior to data collection to enhance transparency. Participants received either a small monetary compensation or course credit for their involvement. Data and study materials were stored on secure institutional servers and anonymized for analysis.

Results

Data screening indicated that 200 participants completed the study and passed attention checks, and these participants comprised the final analysis sample. Inspection of the primary empathy measure showed that the scale demonstrated good internal consistency in the current sample, with Cronbach's alpha of .89. Descriptive statistics indicated that mean baseline trait empathy scores did not differ significantly between the two experimental groups prior to stimulus exposure, supporting the success of random assignment. Specifically, the baseline empathy means were highly similar across conditions, and an independent-samples t-test revealed no significant pretest difference. Participant characteristics such as age, gender distribution, and prior contact with the target out-group were also comparable across the text and video conditions, with no group differences reaching statistical significance. Manipulation check responses showed that participants in the video condition rated the stimulus as more vividly presented and more realistic compared with the text condition, and these differences were statistically significant. The perceived vividness mean in the video group exceeded that in the text group by a notable margin, and a t-test confirmed this effect. Likewise, perceived realism and immediate emotional intensity ratings were higher in the video group relative to the text group. In contrast, participants in the text condition reported higher cognitive engagement and reported that they had engaged in more personal mental imagery to fill in descriptive gaps, and these differences were also statistically significant. The direction of these manipulation check results is consistent with the notion that video provides greater sensory richness while text elicits greater active imagination. The primary analysis compared immediate empathy scores across modalities. Contrary to our initial prediction, participants in the text condition displayed a significantly higher mean immediate empathy score than participants in the video condition. The mean empathy score in the text group was 4.98 (on the 1-to-7 scale) with a standard deviation of 0.95, whereas the mean empathy score in the video group was 4.62 with a standard deviation of 1.01. An independent-samples t-test indicated that this difference was statistically significant, t(198) = 2.83, p = .005, with a Cohen's d of 0.40, representing a small-to-medium effect size. The 95 percent confidence interval for the mean difference did not include zero, supporting the reliability of the effect. To account for potential baseline differences in trait empathy, we conducted an analysis of covariance in which baseline empathy was entered as a covariate and post-exposure empathy was the dependent variable. The modality effect remained significant after controlling for baseline empathy, F(1,197) = 7.60, p = .006, partial eta-squared = .037. This pattern suggests that the modality effect is not explained by pre-existing differences in empathic disposition. Because subjective experience variables differed between groups, we conducted mediation analyses to test whether cognitive engagement or perceived vividness mediated the modality effect on immediate empathy. Results from bootstrapped mediation models indicated that cognitive engagement served as a significant mediator of the modality effect. The indirect effect of modality on immediate empathy via cognitive engagement was significant, and the bootstrapped 95 percent confidence interval for the indirect effect did not include zero. In contrast, perceived vividness did not significantly mediate the relationship between modality and immediate empathy; the indirect effect for vividness was not statistically significant. These results suggest that increased cognitive engagement in the text condition helped account for the higher empathic response observed. We conducted exploratory moderation analyses to examine whether prior contact with the target out-group or habitual media consumption patterns influenced the modality effect. There was evidence that habitual readers experienced a somewhat larger modality effect favoring text, but this moderation effect reached only marginal significance after correction for multiple comparisons. Prior contact with members of the depicted out-group did not significantly moderate the effect. Political orientation, measured on a single-item liberal-conservative scale, did not moderate the modality effect either. We also assessed whether gender moderated the effect and found no reliable interaction between gender and condition in predicting immediate empathy. To provide a robustness check, we ran nonparametric comparisons using the Mann-Whitney U-test, and these analyses confirmed the direction and significance of the modality effect. We also conducted a Bayesian t-test which yielded moderate evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis that the mean empathy score in the text condition exceeded that in the video condition. Additional analyses examined item-level patterns on the empathy questionnaire to determine whether the modality effect was driven more by affective concern versus perspective-taking items. Results showed that both affective concern and perspective-taking subcomponents were elevated in the text condition relative to the video condition, though the effect was numerically larger for perspective-taking items. This pattern aligns with the mediation finding implicating cognitive engagement. We also examined response time for the post-exposure questionnaire as an indirect index of processing and found no significant differences in completion time between conditions, indicating that the observed empathy differences are not likely due to differential haste in responding. Finally, we checked whether the difference in empathy translated into downstream intentions such as willingness to help, donate, or engage in further learning about the depicted individuals. Intentions were measured with three items, and analyses revealed a modest but significant difference favoring the text condition on willingness to engage in further learning, though differences on self-reported willingness to donate or help were not significant. Taken together, the results indicate that text-based presentation of an emotionally relevant narrative elicited stronger immediate empathic responses than a matched video presentation, and that this effect was at least partially mediated by cognitive engagement elicited by text.

Discussion

The present study examined whether immediate empathic response differed when participants encountered a first-person narrative in text form compared with a matched video presentation. Our data revealed an effect contrary to the initial prediction rooted in previous work emphasizing the power of audiovisual media: participants who read the text version reported higher immediate empathy than those who watched the video. This finding emerged despite the fact that participants in the video condition rated the stimulus as more vivid and more realistic, which aligns with expectations about sensory richness in video. The paradoxical pattern of higher vividness but lower empathic response in the video condition suggests that vividness alone is not sufficient to maximize immediate empathy. Instead, subjective cognitive engagement and the degree to which participants actively constructed the narrative may play a pivotal role in eliciting empathic concern and perspective-taking. The mediation analyses supported this account by showing that cognitive engagement significantly mediated the modality effect. In other words, when text prompted greater mental simulation and engagement, those processes were associated with higher empathy. The study contributes to a nuanced understanding of how communication format shapes empathic outcomes and underscores the importance of considering active cognitive processes rather than relying on the assumption that sensory realism will automatically translate into greater compassion. These results should be interpreted in the context of competing theoretical accounts. The vividness hypothesis holds that richer sensory cues provided by video amplify affective resonance through direct perception of emotional cues. Conversely, the constructive simulation hypothesis posits that when individuals generate mental imagery and fill in narrative gaps themselves, they may undergo deeper perspective-taking and personalized emotion that can be more conducive to empathy. Our findings provide empirical support for the constructive simulation perspective in the context of immediate empathic responses to this type of narrative. It appears that the cognitive work of imagining someone else's experience may yield an empathic payoff that surpasses the benefit of simply observing sensory detail. The observed pattern has implications for both theory and practice. Theoretically, it invites refinements to models of empathy that emphasize automatic affective contagion in response to perceptual cues by placing greater emphasis on the role of top-down cognitive processes. It suggests that interventions or communications designed to elicit empathy should consider how they prompt recipients to actively imagine and personalize the experiences of others rather than merely presenting vivid depictions. Practically, the finding that text can produce stronger immediate empathic responses than video has potential implications for the design of anti-discrimination programs, public health campaigns, classroom pedagogy, and community outreach. Many current programs seeking to reduce prejudice toward refugees and minority groups lean heavily on video content under the assumption that the media's vividness will maximize empathic engagement. Our results suggest that carefully crafted textual narratives, which encourage readers to construct mental images and reflect personally on others' circumstances, might be equally or more effective at eliciting immediate empathy. This could be particularly important in contexts where video production resources are limited or where textual dissemination is more feasible, such as community newsletters, policy briefs, or text-based social media. The data also indicate that the route to enhanced empathy may be to design textual materials that maximize cognitive engagement, for example by using first-person perspective, evocative but leave-space-for-imagination language, and prompts that encourage readers to imagine details not explicitly provided. Despite the strengths of the study, several limitations warrant attention. First, the study relied on self-reported immediate empathic reactions, and these subjective reports may not translate directly into behavioral outcomes such as actual helping behavior or long-term attitude change. Although we observed a modest effect on willingness to engage in further learning, measures of concrete behavior were not included and should be the focus of future work. Second, participants were recruited mainly from a university-affiliated population and the surrounding metropolitan area, which may limit generalizability to broader populations with different media habits, literacy levels, or cultural norms. Third, the stimuli focused on a particular type of narrative about displacement and hardship representative of refugee experiences, and results may differ for other content domains such as health narratives, chronic illness, or systemic injustice where visuals might play a different role. Fourth, although the video and text stimuli were matched on content and approximate duration, intrinsic differences between modalities (e.g., timing, audio prosody, and the presence of a real person's image) remain and could interact with individual differences to influence outcomes. Fifth, the study assessed immediate empathic response only, and longer-term effects of repeated exposure, reinforcement, or reflection might produce different modality dynamics. Sixth, while the mediation analysis implicates cognitive engagement, causal inference regarding the mediation mechanism is limited by the cross-sectional measurement of mediators and outcomes immediately following exposure. Future research should manipulate cognitive engagement directly to test its causal role. Several avenues for future research could extend and refine these findings. First, longitudinal studies could examine whether the initial advantage for text in eliciting immediate empathy persists over time or whether video has advantages for retention of emotional response or for translating empathy into sustained behavior. Second, experimental manipulations that independently vary vividness and cognitive engagement within each modality could disentangle their specific contributions. For example, a video could be edited to include more ambiguous scenes that invite inference, and a text could be made highly vivid to reduce the need for mental imagery; such manipulations would test the boundary conditions of the cognitive engagement mechanism. Third, incorporating behavioral outcome measures such as volunteering, monetary donations, or actual contact with out-group members would clarify whether empathic responses measured by self-report predict consequential actions and whether modality influences the empathy-behavior link. Fourth, including physiological indices of emotional arousal, such as heart rate variability, skin conductance, or facial electromyography, could reveal whether video provokes stronger autonomic responses even when self-reported empathy is lower, and how these physiological responses relate to subsequent behavioral choices. Fifth, examining population subgroups with varying literacy levels, media preferences, and cultural storytelling traditions would inform the tailoring of interventions to different communities and contexts. Sixth, mixed-modality interventions that combine text prompts with video content or that intersperse reflective questions between video segments could harness the advantages of both formats and might produce synergistic effects. Seventh, research in real-world applied settings such as schools, refugee resettlement agencies, or community organizations would be valuable for testing the scalability and ecological validity of modality-based approaches for empathy training. Eighth, in light of our findings, it would be productive to explore how interactive media, such as narrative games or virtual reality, compare with static video and text in eliciting empathy because interactivity may enhance cognitive engagement while preserving sensory richness. The current study also raises theoretical questions about the nature of perspective-taking and immersion. It suggests that immersion is not a monolithic construct and that different forms of immersion—sensory immersion afforded by video and imaginative immersion elicited by text—may have distinct consequences for empathy. Future theoretical work could aim to map the psychological processes underlying these different immersion types and to identify situational moderators that determine which is more effective. In conclusion, the present findings challenge the assumption that video is necessarily the superior medium for eliciting immediate empathic responses. Instead, the results highlight the importance of cognitive engagement and active imagination as pathways to empathy and suggest that text-based narratives can be powerful tools for fostering empathic concern. These insights have implications for research on media effects, for the design of empathy training and anti-discrimination interventions, and for broader efforts to promote social understanding and prosocial action. Continued research that triangulates self-report, behavioral, and physiological measures, examines diverse populations and contexts, and experiments with hybrid and interactive formats will deepen our understanding of how best to harness media to cultivate empathy in a pluralistic society.
