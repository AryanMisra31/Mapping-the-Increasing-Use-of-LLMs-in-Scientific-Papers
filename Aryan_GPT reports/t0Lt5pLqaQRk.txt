Introduction

Binaural beats are a perceptual phenomenon that arises when two pure tones of similar but non-identical frequency are presented dichotically, one to each ear, and the auditory system integrates them to produce the percept of a third tone corresponding to the frequency difference between the two inputs. The phenomenon has been known and described in psychoacoustics for many decades and has attracted attention both from basic researchers interested in sensory integration and from applied researchers interested in potential cognitive and affective effects. When the difference between the two tones lies within the frequency range commonly associated with neural oscillatory activity in particular frequency bands, it has been proposed that the binaural beat may interact with or entrain those neural rhythms. For example, when the frequency difference is in the alpha band, usually defined as roughly 8–12 Hz, the resulting percept is called an alpha binaural beat (ABB), and it has been hypothesized to influence endogenous alpha oscillations. Alpha oscillations are among the most extensively studied neural rhythms in human electrophysiology. They have been linked to attention, inhibition of task-irrelevant processing, and certain aspects of memory performance. The functional role of alpha activity in cognition is complex, with different patterns of alpha power and phase being associated with facilitation and suppression of neural processing depending on task demands and anatomical locus. Given this complexity, the possibility that external auditory stimulation that mimics alpha-frequency activity could modulate behavior has been an intriguing topic for experimental investigation.

A number of studies have investigated whether binaural beats, and ABB in particular, can influence cognitive functions. Findings in the literature have been mixed, with some reports suggesting modest improvements in working memory, attention, or mood following exposure to binaural beats, whereas other studies have failed to replicate such benefits or have observed effects only under specific circumstances. One line of work has focused on working memory, which is a form of short-term memory involving the active maintenance and manipulation of information. Working memory is typically assessed with tasks that involve brief retention intervals and controlled processing. It has been suggested that augmentation of alpha activity via ABB could facilitate the suppression of distracting inputs and thereby improve working memory performance. In support of this idea, prior research by Kraus and Porubanova reported enhancing effects of ABB on working memory measures, suggesting that ABB exposure during task performance may lead to modest but reliable improvements in tasks requiring short-term maintenance and manipulation of information. Such findings have encouraged further exploration of ABB as a noninvasive, low-cost intervention to modulate cognition.

Despite reported effects on working memory, it is less clear whether ABB can influence long-term memory processes, which depend on successful encoding, consolidation, and retrieval mechanisms that operate over longer time scales. Long-term memory for verbal materials such as word lists typically engages medial temporal lobe structures and benefits from deep encoding strategies and rehearsal, as well as from sleep-dependent consolidation processes. The effects of transient modulation of cortical oscillations at encoding on later long-term retention are theoretically plausible because oscillatory states during encoding can influence the patterns of neural activity destined for consolidation. The notion of state-dependent learning further raises the possibility that matching the brain state at encoding and retrieval could improve retrieval probability. In light of such considerations, testing whether ABB presented during encoding enhances long-term memory performance is a logical extension of work on ABB and working memory.

There are several mechanisms by which ABB might plausibly influence long-term memory. First, ABB could enhance attentional focus during encoding by modulating alpha-band activity in ways that reduce interference from distractors, thereby leading to deeper or more elaborative encoding. Second, ABB might alter the neural dynamics in cortical regions involved in memory encoding, yielding neural patterns that are more readily consolidated during offline periods. Third, ABB might induce a subjective state of relaxation or heightened alertness that indirectly benefits encoding by optimizing arousal level. Fourth, ABB exposure could interact with participants’ expectancy or placebo effects, which might affect their engagement with the encoding task. Each of these mechanisms has different implications for experimental design and for interpretation of null or positive effects.

Empirically, however, evidence for ABB effects on long-term memory is sparse and inconsistent. Some experiments in the broader literature on rhythmic sensory stimulation have found that entrainment at particular frequencies can influence perception and memory under tightly controlled laboratory conditions, but those effects have not always generalized to more naturalistic learning settings. Moreover, many previous studies have methodological limitations that complicate interpretation, including small sample sizes, lack of appropriate control conditions, and failure to monitor neural entrainment directly. In the specific case of ABB, several studies have used short exposure times or have combined ABB with other auditory stimuli, making it difficult to isolate the contribution of the binaural beat itself. Additionally, control conditions have varied widely across studies, from silence to white noise to monaural beats, and these differences can have substantial consequences because background sound can influence arousal and cognitive performance.

Another consideration in evaluating the potential for ABB to affect long-term memory is the distinction between encoding and retrieval processes. If ABB primarily affects the sensory and attentional state during encoding, benefits might be expected when encoding occurs under ABB but retrieval occurs under the same state or at least a similar neural state. If ABB effects are transient and state-dependent, then presenting ABB at encoding but not at retrieval could reduce the likelihood of observable benefits on delayed recall tests. Conversely, if ABB produces enduring changes in neural plasticity or consolidation processes, then benefits might be observed even when retrieval occurs without ABB. Resolving these possibilities requires careful experimental manipulation of whether ABB is present at encoding, retrieval, or both.

Naturalistic settings introduce additional complexities. Real-world learning often involves environmental variability and concurrent cognitive demands beyond those in tightly controlled laboratory tasks. If ABB effects are subtle, they may be washed out in naturalistic environments. However, demonstrating efficacy in naturalistic contexts is essential if ABB is to be considered a practical cognitive enhancement tool for everyday learning. Therefore, experiments that test ABB in ecologically valid conditions are valuable, provided they control adequately for confounds and employ robust sample sizes.

Given the mixed prior evidence and the theoretical rationale linking alpha oscillations to attentional and mnemonic processes, we sought to test whether ABB presented during encoding would enhance long-term memory performance in a naturalistic learning paradigm. The primary hypothesis was that participants who encode a word list in the presence of ABB would demonstrate superior free recall after a 24-hour delay compared to participants who encode the same material in silence. The experimental design was intended to be straightforward and to mirror common study conditions: participants studied a list of words while wearing headphones and then returned 24 hours later for a surprise free recall test in silence. We chose free recall as the primary outcome because it is sensitive to encoding depth and is commonly used in applied learning research. A delayed interval of 24 hours was selected to target long-term memory rather than immediate retrieval or short-term maintenance.

We were also mindful of methodological issues that have complicated prior studies. To address concerns about sample size and power, we recruited a moderate-sized sample to detect small-to-moderate effects. To reduce expectancy and demand characteristics, the study was single-blinded insofar as participants were not informed of the specific hypothesis that ABB would enhance memory. The ABB stimulus was carefully constructed to produce a clear binaural beat in the alpha range while keeping carrier frequencies within comfortable hearing range. The control condition involved wearing the same headphones in silence to balance the physical experience across groups. Additionally, we collected several covariates to characterize the sample and to enable exploratory analyses of potential moderators, including self-reported sleep quality during the retention interval and baseline verbal short-term memory capacity measured via a digit span task.

We also considered alternative explanations for possible null findings. If ABB fails to produce measurable changes in neural alpha power in the majority of participants, then behavioral effects would be unlikely. To address this, we included a simple manipulation check asking participants to report whether they heard a beat and to rate its perceived strength and pleasantness. Although subjective reports are not a substitute for EEG verification, they provide some indication that the stimulus was perceptible. We also deliberately designed the encoding task to be moderately challenging: the word list length was long enough to produce forgetting but not so long as to create floor effects. Our retention interval and test format were intended to tap long-term retention processes that are ecologically relevant to educational contexts.

We were further attentive to the statistical approach. The primary analysis would be a straightforward comparison of delayed free recall between ABB and silence groups using an independent-samples t-test, complemented by an analysis of covariance incorporating baseline digit span and sleep quality as covariates. Given contemporary emphasis on reporting effect sizes and confidence intervals, we planned to present these metrics along with p-values. Recognizing that null results can be informative, we also planned to conduct Bayesian analyses to estimate the evidence in favor of the null hypothesis relative to the alternative.

Finally, we considered the broader implications of potential outcomes. Demonstrating a reliable ABB effect on long-term memory would extend prior reports of ABB effects on working memory and suggest practical utility for study aids and learning interventions. Conversely, failing to find such an effect would suggest that ABB either does not influence long-term memory under naturalistic conditions or that its effects are contingent on more specific parameters such as concurrent presentation at retrieval or stronger neural entrainment. Either outcome would help refine theoretical models of how rhythmic auditory stimulation interacts with memory systems and would guide future experimental work to clarify boundary conditions for any cognitive benefits of ABB.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university community via email and posted advertisements. A total of 120 participants were enrolled in the study and completed both sessions. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 32 years. All participants reported normal hearing and were fluent English speakers. Exclusion criteria included self-reported neurological or psychiatric conditions, current use of medications that could affect cognition, and prior experience with binaural beat research. Participants received course credit or a small monetary compensation for participation. The study protocol was approved by the institutional human subjects review board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions: the ABB encoding condition (n = 60) or the silence encoding condition (n = 60). Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random number sequence by a researcher not involved with data collection. The experimenter who administered the sessions did not reveal the study hypothesis to participants. Participants were told that the study was examining study strategies and that they would hear different auditory conditions during the study phase; the true aim concerning ABB and memory enhancement was withheld to minimize expectancy effects.

The ABB stimulus was designed to produce a 10 Hz binaural beat, which lies in the middle of the canonical alpha band. Two pure tones were used as carriers: a 400 Hz tone presented to one ear and a 410 Hz tone presented to the other ear, resulting in a 10 Hz binaural beat percept. The tones were generated digitally at 44.1 kHz sampling rate and saved as stereo audio files. The amplitude of each tone was adjusted so that overall presentation was approximately 60 dB SPL as measured with a standardized headphone calibration procedure. The tones were presented continuously during the encoding phase. The control condition involved participants wearing the same headphones with no sound played; ambient room noise was minimized and equivalent room conditions were maintained for both groups. Headphones provided passive attenuation of external noise to create similar experiential conditions across groups.

The word list used for encoding consisted of 40 common English nouns drawn from standardized word pools used in memory research. Words were chosen to be concrete, one- to two-syllable nouns, and to have moderate frequency in English; care was taken to avoid emotionally charged or highly distinctive items that could introduce variability. The list was presented visually on a computer screen one word at a time. Each word appeared for 3 seconds with an interstimulus interval of 1 second during which a fixation cross was shown. Prior to the study phase, participants were instructed to try to remember as many words as possible for a later memory test but were not informed that the test would be delayed by 24 hours. Participants were not given specific encoding aids or rehearsal strategies; the instruction was to study naturally as they normally would.

During the encoding phase, participants in the ABB group began hearing the binaural beat stimulus for one minute before the first word appeared and continued to hear it for one minute after the last word to ensure a steady exposure encompassing the study interval. Participants in the silence group wore the headphones for the same period but heard no stimulus. After the study phase, participants completed a brief questionnaire that included the manipulation check items: whether they noted any sound, whether they perceived a beat, and ratings of perceived intensity and pleasantness of any sounds on Likert scales. They also completed a digit span task to assess baseline verbal short-term memory and completed a brief sleep quality questionnaire for the upcoming 24-hours retention interval.

Participants returned approximately 24 hours later for the delayed test session. The retrieval session was conducted in silence for all participants; the headphones were not worn at retrieval. Participants were asked to perform a free recall test in which they had five minutes to write down as many words as they could remember from the study list. No cues were provided, and responses were collected on paper. The number of correct words recalled was the primary dependent variable. Intrusions and repetitions were noted but were not included in the correct recall score.

Data preprocessing involved checking for missing or implausible responses. Two participants were excluded from the final analyses because they did not return for the second session, yielding a final sample of 118 participants (ABB n = 59; silence n = 59). Descriptive statistics were computed for demographic variables, digit span scores, reported sleep quality, and manipulation check responses to evaluate baseline equivalence between groups.

The primary statistical analysis was an independent-samples t-test comparing mean delayed free recall between the ABB group and the silence group. Assumptions for the t-test were checked by inspecting distributions and variances; Levene’s test was used to assess equality of variances. To account for potential baseline differences, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed with delayed recall as the dependent variable, condition as the fixed factor, and digit span and sleep quality as covariates. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s d for the t-test and partial eta-squared for the ANCOVA. Two-tailed tests were used throughout and an alpha threshold of .05 was adopted for statistical significance.

Recognizing the interpretive value of evidence for the null hypothesis in this domain, we also conducted Bayesian t-tests to quantify the relative support for the null versus the alternative hypothesis. Bayes factors (BF01) indicating support for the null hypothesis were computed using default prior settings appropriate for two-sample t-tests. Exploratory analyses examined correlations between manipulation check ratings (perceived beat intensity) and recall performance within the ABB group to assess whether subjective perception of the beat related to memory outcomes. Additional exploratory analyses tested for interactions between condition and self-reported sleep quality.

All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Data and analysis scripts were archived in a secure repository and are available upon reasonable request. Throughout the experimental procedure, participants were monitored for comfort and were allowed to withdraw at any time without penalty. The experimenters recorded any adverse events or participant complaints; none of note occurred that would affect data interpretation.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The ABB group (n = 59) had a mean delayed free recall of 12.3 words (SD = 3.8). The silence group (n = 59) had a mean delayed free recall of 12.9 words (SD = 3.4). Digit span scores were similar across groups, with the ABB group showing a mean digit span of 6.2 (SD = 1.1) and the silence group showing a mean of 6.3 (SD = 1.0). Self-reported sleep quality during the retention interval averaged 6.8 on a 10-point scale for the ABB group (SD = 1.5) and 6.9 for the silence group (SD = 1.4). Manipulation check responses indicated that 46 of 59 participants in the ABB group reported perceiving some sound, and 38 of 59 specifically reported perceiving a rhythmic beat. Subjective ratings of perceived beat intensity in the ABB group had a mean of 5.1 on a 10-point scale (SD = 2.3), and perceived pleasantness averaged 5.8 (SD = 2.0).

An independent-samples t-test comparing delayed recall between the ABB and silence groups showed no significant difference. The t-test yielded t(116) = -0.78, p = .437, with a mean difference of -0.6 words (95% CI = -2.4 to 1.2). The effect size for the group difference was small, with Cohen’s d = 0.14 (95% CI = -0.20 to 0.48), a value conventionally interpreted as a negligible to small effect. Levene’s test for equality of variances was not significant (F = 0.62, p = .432), indicating that the assumption of homogeneity of variance was reasonable.

An ANCOVA with delayed recall as the dependent variable, condition as the between-subjects factor, and digit span and sleep quality as covariates produced consistent results. In this model, the main effect of condition was not significant, F(1,113) = 0.61, p = .437, partial eta-squared = .005. Digit span was a significant covariate, F(1,113) = 7.11, p = .009, partial eta-squared = .059, indicating that higher digit span scores were modestly associated with better delayed recall across conditions. Sleep quality did not significantly predict delayed recall in the ANCOVA, F(1,113) = 1.02, p = .314, partial eta-squared = .009.

Given the absence of a significant group effect in traditional inferential testing, we turned to Bayesian analyses to quantify support for the null hypothesis relative to the alternative. The Bayesian independent-samples t-test yielded a Bayes factor BF01 = 3.2, indicating that the data were approximately three times more likely under the null model (no difference between groups) than under the alternative model (a difference). This value is commonly interpreted as moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis.

Exploratory correlational analyses within the ABB group examined whether subjective perception of the beat was associated with recall performance. The correlation between perceived beat intensity and delayed recall was r = .07, p = .587, indicating no reliable relationship. Similarly, perceived pleasantness of the sound was not correlated with recall, r = -.03, p = .813. Participants in the ABB group who explicitly reported perceiving a beat did not differ in recall from those who did not report a beat, t(57) = 0.42, p = .676.

We also examined the distribution of recall scores for potential floor or ceiling effects. The range of recall scores in the ABB group was 4 to 22 words, and in the silence group it was 5 to 23 words, indicating variability across participants and no evidence of clustering at extremes that would preclude detection of differences. Inspection of skewness and kurtosis suggested approximately normal distributions for both groups. Nonparametric analysis using the Mann-Whitney U test yielded a similar, non-significant pattern, U = 1645.5, p = .412.

To probe whether ABB effects might interact with individual differences in short-term memory, an interaction term between condition and digit span was added to an exploratory linear regression predicting delayed recall. The interaction term was not significant, β = .03, p = .732, indicating no evidence that ABB benefited participants differently as a function of digit span. Similarly, no interaction was found between condition and self-reported sleep quality, β = -.08, p = .341.

Additional analyses considered whether exclusion of participants who failed to perceive the beat would alter the outcome. Restricting the ABB group to the subset who reported perceiving a beat (n = 38) and comparing them to the full silence group produced a mean recall of 12.5 (SD = 3.6) in the perceived-beat subset and 12.9 (SD = 3.4) in the silence group. The t-test comparing these groups was not significant, t(95) = -0.52, p = .604. This result suggests that subjective perception of the ABB did not mediate the (null) effect on delayed recall.

We also examined potential order effects or experimenter-related variability by checking whether the day of testing or the specific experimenter who ran the session was associated with recall performance. No systematic effects of these procedural variables were observed. Finally, the overall pattern of results did not differ when analyses were run excluding participants with extreme sleep reports (very low or very high sleep quality), indicating robustness of the null finding across reasonable subsets of the data.

Taken together, the analyses consistently indicate that ABB exposure during encoding did not produce a measurable enhancement in delayed free recall after 24 hours under the conditions tested. The combination of null results in frequentist tests, moderate Bayes factor evidence favoring the null, lack of correlation between perceived beat intensity and recall, and absence of interactions with individual difference measures collectively support the conclusion that ABB, as implemented here, did not improve long-term memory performance relative to silence.

Discussion

The present experiment tested whether exposure to alpha binaural beats during encoding would enhance delayed free recall of a word list after a 24-hour retention interval. Despite theoretical reasons to expect that modulation of alpha-frequency activity during encoding could influence mnemonic processing, our results did not support this hypothesis. Participants who studied words while listening to a 10 Hz binaural beat did not recall more words after 24 hours than participants who studied the same list in silence. This absence of a behavioral effect was supported across multiple analytic approaches, including independent-samples t-tests, ANCOVA controlling for baseline digit span and sleep quality, and Bayesian analysis which provided moderate evidence in favor of the null. Additionally, subjective perception of the ABB was not associated with better recall, and restricting analyses to those who reported perceiving the beat did not reveal a hidden benefit. These convergent findings suggest that, under the specific conditions and population sampled in this study, ABB exposure during encoding does not reliably enhance long-term verbal memory.

Several explanations can account for the null result observed here, and these merit careful consideration. One important possibility is that ABB did not produce substantive neural entrainment in the majority of participants; subjective reports of perceiving a beat are an imperfect proxy for neural entrainment, and without concurrent EEG recording we cannot confirm that alpha-band power or phase dynamics were altered by the stimulation. Prior studies that have demonstrated cognitive effects of rhythmic stimulation often include direct electrophysiological confirmation that the target frequency band was modulated. If ABB in our implementation failed to influence endogenous alpha oscillations, then null behavioral outcomes would be unsurprising. Future studies should include EEG or other neural measures to verify entrainment and to relate neural changes to behavioral outcomes.

Another consideration is the distinction between short-term working memory and long-term memory. Prior reports of ABB benefits have focused on working memory tasks that require active maintenance and manipulation over seconds. The mechanisms underlying working memory improvements may differ from those that support encoding into long-term stores and subsequent consolidation. ABB might be more effective at modulating attentional gating or momentary suppression of distractors—processes that directly impact working memory performance—than at inducing neural changes that facilitate durable consolidation. Thus, ABB could have observable effects on tasks with immediate recall demands but not on delayed recall after sleep-dependent consolidation processes. This possibility aligns with theoretical accounts that posit distinct neural substrates for short-term maintenance and for the encoding-consolidation cascade.

A related explanation concerns state-dependent effects. If ABB produces a transient state that facilitates encoding but retrieval is more efficient when the brain is in a similar state, then presenting ABB only during encoding and not at retrieval could lead to diminished observable benefits. Our design presented ABB only during encoding and tested recall in silence 24 hours later. If state-dependent retrieval is relevant, then matching ABB at retrieval might be necessary to observe benefits. An experiment that crosses ABB presence at encoding and at retrieval (ABB/ABB, ABB/Silence, Silence/ABB, Silence/Silence) could disentangle encoding-specific versus state-dependent retrieval effects. Our conclusion in the abstract that another experimental condition where individuals memorize with ABB and recall with ABB should be introduced is consistent with this interpretation and suggests a clear empirical next step.

The naturalistic aspects of our design may also have attenuated possible effects. Real-world learning is subject to variability in engagement, environmental distractions, and endogenous fluctuations in arousal. If ABB effects are subtle and require controlled conditions to emerge, then a naturalistic setting with varied participant behaviors may reduce the signal-to-noise ratio and render effects undetectable. At the same time, demonstrating effects under ecologically valid conditions would be most informative for applied use; hence, null results in such contexts are meaningful in highlighting the potential limitations of ABB as a practical tool for enhancing everyday learning.

Methodological features of the present study could also have limited the likelihood of detecting an effect. The ABB was delivered continuously during the encoding period using carrier tones of 400 Hz and 410 Hz. While this configuration is standard in binaural beat research, individual differences in auditory sensitivity, skull conduction properties, and attention to the auditory input could influence the efficacy of the stimulation. Some participants may have found the stimulus distracting or unpleasant, counteracting any potential benefits; though our manipulation check did not reveal systematic negative appraisals on average, subtle variability remains possible. Moreover, the intensity and duration of ABB exposure were constrained by practical considerations. It is possible that longer exposure durations or repeated ABB sessions could be necessary to produce durable changes in memory systems.

The choice of a silence control condition presents both strengths and limitations. Silence is a straightforward baseline and minimizes additional auditory influences on cognitive state. However, silence differs qualitatively from presenting a neutral auditory stimulus such as white noise or monaural beats. If the presence of any sound, regardless of binaural properties, influences arousal or distractibility, then silence might not be the optimal comparator. Comparative studies that include both silence and alternative auditory controls could help isolate the specific contribution of binaural-beat-induced stereophonic phase differences versus general auditory stimulation.

Sample size and statistical power are practical concerns in this line of research. We recruited 118 participants who completed both sessions, which provided reasonable power to detect small-to-moderate effects. The observed effect size was small and confidence intervals around the difference included values near zero, and Bayesian analysis favored the null. While it remains possible that extremely small effects exist that our study was not powered to detect, such effects would likely have limited practical significance. Researchers aiming to detect minute behavioral changes would need to design much larger studies and weigh the cost-benefit tradeoff of such efforts.

Individual differences likely moderate responsiveness to ABB. Factors such as baseline alpha activity, susceptibility to auditory entrainment, musical training, age, attentional control capacities, and trait suggestibility may all influence whether ABB produces neural and behavioral effects. Our study included digit span and sleep quality covariates but did not systematically assess many of these other moderators. Future work could pre-screen participants for high versus low trait alpha power or directly assess entrainment responsiveness with EEG prior to testing to enrich samples for potential responders and thereby increase sensitivity to effects.

Theoretical implications of the null finding are noteworthy. Our results suggest that modulating ongoing alpha-band activity via binaural beats during a single encoding session may not be sufficient to alter the processes that lead to durable long-term memory retention in a general university-aged sample. This outcome refines theories that posit a straightforward mapping from enhancement of alpha oscillations to improved memory consolidation. It encourages a more nuanced model in which the cognitive impact of externally induced rhythmic stimulation depends on timing relative to memory stages, on matching between encoding and retrieval states, on participant-specific susceptibility to entrainment, and on the nature of the mnemonic task.

There remain several promising directions for future research. First, integrating multimodal measures—most importantly EEG—would allow direct assessment of whether ABB exposure reliably entrains alpha activity in individual participants and whether the degree of entrainment predicts subsequent memory outcomes. Demonstrating a brain-behavior link would markedly strengthen causal inferences. Second, experimental designs that manipulate ABB at encoding and retrieval independently can test for state-dependent memory effects. Such crossing would clarify whether ABB benefits are encoding-specific, retrieval-specific, or contingent on encoding-retrieval state congruence. Third, comparing ABB to other auditory controls such as white noise, monaural beats, or isochronous amplitude modulations could isolate the unique contribution of binaural processing. Fourth, longitudinal interventions that apply ABB over multiple sessions and track cumulative effects on learning and retention would test whether repeated exposure is necessary to produce lasting cognitive changes. Fifth, studies could explore different frequency bands and beat frequencies to determine frequency specificity and whether particular bands (e.g., theta for episodic encoding) are more efficacious for certain memory types.

From an applied perspective, the present results counsel caution regarding the promotion of ABB-based devices or apps as generic enhancers of long-term learning. Consumers and educators should be aware that evidence for immediate working memory benefits does not necessarily translate into improved retention over longer intervals, and that boundary conditions such as the timing of exposure and individual differences may be critical. Nonetheless, the avenue remains promising enough to warrant further well-controlled studies, particularly those that combine behavioral testing with neural monitoring and that test for state-dependency and dose-response relationships.

In conclusion, the current experiment found no evidence that exposure to alpha binaural beats during encoding enhances delayed free recall of a word list after 24 hours. The null results were robust across traditional and Bayesian statistical approaches and were not explained by perceptual failure to detect the beat or by simple baseline differences in short-term memory or sleep quality. These findings suggest that, under naturalistic and single-session encoding conditions, ABB may not meaningfully influence long-term verbal memory. Future work should incorporate neural measurements of entrainment, manipulate ABB presence at retrieval, explore individual differences in susceptibility, and test alternative control conditions to clarify under what circumstances, if any, binaural beats can serve as a reliable aid to long-term learning.
