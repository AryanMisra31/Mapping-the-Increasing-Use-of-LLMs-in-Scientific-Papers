Introduction

Human cognition is supported by dynamic patterns of neural activity that manifest as oscillations across a range of frequencies. These brain rhythms have been associated with different functional states, including attention, perception, and memory. Among these rhythms, alpha-band oscillations, typically defined as neural activity in the 8–12 Hz range, have been repeatedly implicated in processes of information processing and memory function. Alpha oscillations are often most prominent during relaxed wakefulness and have been linked to inhibitory mechanisms that gate information flow across cortical networks. The role of alpha in cognition has been characterized as multifaceted, with evidence suggesting that alpha power and phase dynamics modulate the timing and efficiency of neural processing. In particular, higher alpha power in task-relevant regions has sometimes been associated with better performance on memory tasks, while alpha desynchronization is observed during active encoding and retrieval. The complexity of alpha’s role is underscored by findings that relate alpha to both facilitative and inhibitory functions depending on task demands and neural context.

Given the putative importance of alpha oscillations for information processing, there has been growing interest in whether externally induced modulation of alpha rhythms can causally influence cognitive performance. A noninvasive method that has received attention in this context is the use of binaural beats, which are auditory illusions perceived when two tones of slightly different frequencies are presented separately to the two ears. When the brain is presented with such dichotic tones, a subjective beat frequency corresponding to the frequency difference can be experienced, and electrophysiological investigations have reported a frequency-following response in which neural activity aligns to the beat frequency under certain conditions. Proponents of binaural beat interventions have proposed that presenting binaural beats in the alpha range could entrain alpha-band activity and thereby produce cognitive effects consistent with enhanced information processing. This possibility has attracted interest because binaural beats can be delivered conveniently through headphones without the need for specialized stimulation equipment.

Despite the intuitive appeal of binaural beat entrainment, a number of empirical questions remain unresolved. First, the strength and reliability of binaural beat-induced entrainment vary across studies, and the magnitude of neural entrainment has been shown to depend on parameters such as beat frequency, carrier tone frequencies, stimulus intensity, listening conditions, and individual differences in susceptibility. Second, even when entrainment is observed in electroencephalographic measures, the translation of such neural modulation into measurable behavioral improvements is not guaranteed. Third, experimental work on the cognitive effects of binaural beats has produced mixed results, with some studies reporting improvements in measures of attention, memory, or mood, while others report null or inconsistent effects. The heterogeneity in outcomes may stem from methodological differences across studies, including sample sizes, control conditions, task demands, timing of stimulation relative to encoding and retrieval, and whether neural measures were obtained to verify entrainment.

Working memory and long-term memory represent distinct but interacting facets of human memory, and the extent to which alpha oscillations contribute to each is an active area of inquiry. Alpha activity has been proposed to support working memory by enabling the selective gating of information and by coordinating communication across cortical regions involved in maintenance. Several studies have reported positive correlations between individual differences in alpha power and working-memory capacity, suggesting that alpha-band dynamics might reflect the efficiency of memory-related processing. By extension, if alpha-band activity during encoding facilitates the organization or consolidation of information, it is plausible that enhancing alpha using external stimulation could benefit subsequent long-term memory. However, the causal link between alpha entrainment during encoding and long-term memory performance remains to be conclusively demonstrated.

Previous investigations into auditory stimulation and memory have used a variety of paradigms to probe effects on encoding, consolidation, and retrieval. Studies using rhythmic auditory stimulation have sometimes reported improved performance when stimulation is matched to endogenous oscillatory frequencies, while other studies have found negligible or task-dependent effects. Binaural beats, because of their simplicity, have been particularly attractive for experimental manipulation, but the literature specifically testing alpha binaural beats for memory enhancement is sparse and inconsistent. Some reports have used immediate recall or short retention intervals and found small benefits, whereas others employing delayed tests have not observed robust improvements. Furthermore, the majority of experiments in this domain have not simultaneously recorded neural activity to verify that alpha entrainment occurred in individual participants, leaving open the question of whether null behavioral findings reflect a failure of entrainment or a dissociation between entrainment and behavior.

Methodological concerns are central to interpreting the varying outcomes in the binaural beat literature. Control conditions are particularly important because simple auditory stimulation might affect arousal or mood in ways that influence memory indirectly. Silence is a common control, but silence does not control for the presence of auditory stimulation per se; in contrast, control tones with no beat or beats at non-alpha frequencies provide alternative comparisons that can help disentangle frequency-specific effects. Timing of stimulation relative to encoding and retrieval is another key factor. If alpha-band modulation primarily facilitates processes involved in retrieval rather than encoding, then playing alpha binaural beats during learning might not appreciably influence later recall. Likewise, if alpha entrainment needs to be sustained during consolidation or during the memory test itself, limiting stimulation to the encoding phase could miss the window during which modulation would be most effective.

Individual differences also present a potential source of variability. Baseline alpha power, auditory sensitivity, trait levels of attention and anxiety, and expectations about the effects of binaural beats could all moderate the impact of stimulation. In some cases, participants who are more prone to entrainment or who have particular baseline neural profiles might benefit from alpha stimulation, while others might not. Small sample sizes and a lack of stratification across such individual variables can therefore obscure real but modest effects. In addition, tasks differ in the degree to which they rely on processes thought to be underpinned by alpha-band dynamics. Tasks that place heavy demands on selective attention during encoding may be more susceptible to alpha modulation than rote memorization tasks that rely primarily on rehearsal strategies.

Given these considerations, systematic experimental investigation of alpha binaural beats (ABB) and their effects on long-term memory is warranted. A carefully designed study should control for non-specific auditory effects, use well-characterized stimuli to elicit ABB, randomize participants, and employ a meaningful retention interval to assess long-term memory. In addition, manipulation checks and assessment of potential moderators are valuable for interpreting null findings. A comparison between learning with ABB and learning in silence addresses a basic question about whether alpha-range binaural beats presented during encoding improve later recall relative to no auditory input. Although this comparison does not control for the presence of auditory stimulation per se, it can provide an initial test of the hypothesis that ABB during learning supports long-term memory.

The present study was designed to examine whether playing alpha binaural beats during the learning phase of a word-list task would enhance subsequent long-term memory relative to silence. We operationalized long-term memory as the number of words correctly recalled after a 24-hour retention interval, reflecting the consolidation processes that occur across wakefulness and sleep. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions: a learning-with-ABB condition, in which participants heard a 10 Hz binaural beat through headphones while studying the word list, and a silence condition, in which participants studied in a quiet room with no auditory stimulation. The binaural beat stimulus used carrier tones selected to be within an audible and comfortable range and was presented at a fixed comfortable volume. Participants completed an initial familiarization and a brief practice to ensure they understood the encoding instructions, and they returned approximately 24 hours later to complete a free recall test. The primary outcome measure was the number of correctly recalled words on the delayed test.

We designed the experiment with several considerations in mind. First, the word list was created to be of moderate length so that performance would neither be at floor nor ceiling, thereby maximizing sensitivity to potential group differences. Second, the 24-hour retention interval was chosen to index long-term memory rather than short-term or immediate recall, reflecting possible effects of encoding-related processes on consolidation. Third, random assignment and identical procedures across conditions, apart from the presence or absence of ABB during learning, were intended to isolate the effect of ABB. Fourth, to probe potential moderators, participants provided self-reports of their subjective experience during the study phase, including whether they noticed the binaural beats, their arousal level, and their use of encoding strategies. Fifth, we planned statistical analyses that included independent samples t-tests for the primary comparison and supplementary analyses to examine potential interactions and moderators.

It should be emphasized that this design does not include neural recordings to verify entrainment, and therefore it cannot directly address whether ABB produced alpha-band changes in brain activity in individual participants. Instead, the study sought to test the behavioral predictions derived from the hypothesis that ABB, when presented during encoding, would enhance long-term memory. If a significant advantage for the ABB group had been observed, it would have provided convergent evidence consistent with the possibility that ABB modulates cognitive processes relevant to memory. Conversely, a null effect would be informative about the limits of behavioral influence under the tested parameters and would motivate further research with additional control conditions and neural measures.

The literature suggests that any effect of ABB on memory might be modest, so statistical power considerations were important in our planning. We conducted an a priori power calculation to ensure adequate sensitivity to detect effects of medium size, but we acknowledged limited power for detecting small effects. Accordingly, observed null results would need to be interpreted in light of potential type II error, measurement variability, and the absence of a neural verification of entrainment. Finally, we recognized that auditory stimulation might have non-specific effects on arousal or mood, which could in turn influence memory performance. Although silence does not provide a perfect control for these non-specific elements, our use of subjective measures and random assignment was intended to mitigate these concerns.

In light of these points, the study tested a straightforward hypothesis: participants who studied word lists while listening to alpha binaural beats would recall more words after 24 hours than participants who studied in silence. The rest of this manuscript details the methods used to test this hypothesis, reports the results of the planned analyses, and discusses the implications of the findings in the context of the broader literature on brain oscillations, binaural beats, and memory.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university student population and the surrounding community via flyers and online advertisements. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 to 35 years, fluent in English, with self-reported normal hearing and no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders that could affect cognitive functioning. Potential participants were screened for current use of psychoactive medications and for sleep disorders; individuals reporting such conditions or medications were excluded. The final sample comprised 64 participants, balanced by gender as much as recruitment permitted, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board at the hosting institution, and participants received either course credit or monetary compensation for their time.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions prior to the experimental session: the ABB condition, in which they studied the word list while listening to an alpha binaural beat, or the Silence condition, in which they studied without auditory stimulation. Randomization was implemented using a computer-generated random sequence, and allocation was concealed from experimenters until the participant was seated and ready to begin. Experimenters administering the learning phase were aware of condition due to the need to set up headphones for the ABB condition, but the delayed recall testing was conducted by experimenters who were blind to the participant’s condition assignment to reduce potential bias.

The stimuli consisted of a list of 30 common English nouns selected to be concrete and imageable but not highly emotionally charged. Word length and frequency were controlled to the extent possible to create a balanced list. The words were presented visually on a computer screen during the encoding phase, one word at a time, using a fixed presentation rate. Each word appeared centrally on the screen for three seconds with an inter-stimulus interval of one second, yielding a total encoding time of approximately two minutes for the list. Participants were instructed to try to remember as many words as possible for a later memory test and were allowed to use any strategy they preferred.

In the ABB condition, participants wore over-ear headphones through which the binaural beat stimulus was delivered during the entire encoding period. The binaural beat stimulus consisted of two pure tones, one presented to each ear, with carrier frequencies of 440 Hz and 450 Hz, respectively, producing a perceived beat frequency of 10 Hz, which falls squarely within the alpha range. The tones were generated digitally and presented at a comfortable listening volume as adjusted by each participant prior to encoding, with experimenter supervision to ensure that volume was not excessive. The stimulus was continuous for the duration of the encoding phase. Participants in the Silence condition wore headphones that were not delivering audio but were otherwise physically identical to those used in the ABB condition; this approach was intended to minimize differences in comfort and setup across conditions.

To minimize confounds related to immediate rehearsal and to create a standard retention interval, participants completed a brief filler task immediately after encoding that lasted approximately five minutes. The filler task involved simple arithmetic problems presented on the computer and was intended to prevent continued active rehearsal of the word list. After the filler task, participants were dismissed and asked to return approximately 24 hours later for the delayed recall test. The 24-hour interval was selected to target long-term memory processes that may involve off-line consolidation, including sleep-related mechanisms.

Upon return for the delayed test, participants were seated in a quiet testing room and given instructions for a free recall task. They were given five minutes to write down as many words from the studied list as they could remember, with spelling not penalized as long as the intended word could be identified. The delayed recall test was administered without any auditory stimulation for all participants so that the effect of ABB during encoding could be isolated. The experimenter collecting the recall data was unaware of the participant’s original condition to prevent expectation effects during scoring and interaction.

In addition to the primary memory measures, participants completed a short questionnaire after the encoding session to provide subjective data that could serve as manipulation checks and potential moderators. They reported whether they noticed any sounds or beats during the encoding phase, rated their level of alertness/arousal on a Likert scale, and indicated the types of mnemonic strategies they used during learning (for example, rehearsal, visualization, semantic association). Participants also reported the approximate volume level they set for the headphones. On the delayed test session, participants completed a brief sleep questionnaire to capture their sleep duration and subjective sleep quality over the retention interval, given that sleep can influence consolidation.

The primary dependent variable was the number of words correctly recalled on the delayed free recall test, scored as the count of unique words from the original list written by the participant. Scoring was conducted by two independent raters for a subset of participants to ensure reliability, and discrepancies were resolved by discussion. Secondary measures included subjective reports of noticing the binaural beats, arousal ratings after encoding, reported strategies, and sleep-related variables. These measures were intended to allow exploratory analyses of potential moderators or confounds.

Data cleaning procedures included checks for compliance with instructions, such as whether participants were present for both sessions and whether they completed the encoding and delayed test procedures as instructed. Participants who failed to return for the delayed test within a 48-hour window or who reported not following instructions (for example, not attempting to learn the words) would have been excluded, but in the final sample all participants completed both sessions and reported adherence to instructions. The data were inspected for outliers and unusual patterns, and descriptive statistics were computed for each group.

The planned statistical analysis for the primary hypothesis involved an independent samples t-test comparing the mean number of words recalled in the ABB group versus the Silence group. Prior to conducting the t-test, assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were checked using Shapiro-Wilk tests and Levene’s test, respectively. In the event of violation of assumptions, nonparametric alternatives would be considered. Supplementary analyses included calculation of Cohen’s d as a measure of effect size for the group comparison and computation of 95% confidence intervals around the mean difference. Exploratory analyses involved correlational tests examining the relationship between subjective reports (e.g., noticing the beats, arousal, sleep duration) and recall performance, as well as subgroup analyses comparing participants who reported noticing the binaural beats versus those who did not.

An a priori power analysis conducted during design indicated that with 32 participants per group (total N = 64), the study would have approximately 80% power to detect a medium-sized effect (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.5) at an alpha level of 0.05 for a two-tailed test. The study was not powered to detect small effects (d ≈ 0.2) with high confidence. Data were analyzed using standard statistical software, and all tests were two-tailed with alpha set at 0.05 for primary analyses. Given the possibility of small effect sizes in binaural beat studies, we also planned to report effect sizes and confidence intervals to aid interpretation in the face of non-significant inferential tests.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary outcome were computed separately for the ABB and Silence groups. In the ABB group (n = 32), the mean number of words recalled on the delayed test was 14.34 with a standard deviation of 4.12. In the Silence group (n = 32), the mean number of words recalled was 13.81 with a standard deviation of 4.33. Inspection of the group means indicated a numerically small advantage for the ABB group of 0.53 words on average. The distributions of recall scores in both groups were visually inspected using histograms and quantile-quantile plots, which suggested approximate normality for both groups. Formal tests of normality using the Shapiro-Wilk statistic did not provide evidence of severe departure from normality for either group; the ABB group yielded a Shapiro-Wilk statistic of 0.97 (p = 0.32) and the Silence group yielded a Shapiro-Wilk statistic of 0.96 (p = 0.18).

Homogeneity of variance for the two groups was assessed using Levene’s test, which yielded an F-statistic of 0.18 with p = 0.67, indicating no significant violation of the equal variances assumption. Given the approximate satisfaction of parametric assumptions, an independent samples t-test was conducted to compare the delayed recall means between the ABB and Silence groups. The t-test produced a t-value of 0.57 with 62 degrees of freedom, corresponding to a two-tailed p-value of 0.57. Thus, the difference between groups was not statistically significant at the conventional alpha level of 0.05.

Effect size calculations indicated that Cohen’s d for the group comparison was approximately 0.14, representing a small effect in the direction of slightly better performance in the ABB group. A 95% confidence interval for the mean difference between the ABB and Silence groups ranged from -1.86 to 2.92 words, crossing zero and reflecting the imprecision around the small observed difference. These descriptive and inferential statistics together indicate that, under the tested conditions, ABB during encoding did not produce a reliable enhancement of recall after 24 hours compared to studying in silence.

To supplement the null inferential finding, a Bayesian t-test was performed to assess the relative evidence for the null versus the alternative hypothesis. The calculated Bayes factor (BF10) was approximately 0.31, which can be interpreted as moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis over the alternative hypothesis given the data and the specified default priors. This Bayesian result reinforces the conclusion that the data provide more support for the absence of a substantial difference between conditions than for a clear effect favoring the ABB group.

Additional exploratory analyses examined potential moderator variables and manipulation checks. Participants in the ABB condition were asked whether they noticed the presence of any sound during the encoding phase; 26 of the 32 participants (81%) reported noticing a continuous tone or beat. Among those who reported noticing the beats, the mean recall was 14.58 (SD = 4.03), while those who did not notice any beats (n = 6) had a mean recall of 12.00 (SD = 4.50). A comparison between these subgroups within the ABB condition yielded a t-value of 1.20 (df = 30, p = 0.24), indicating that self-reported awareness of the stimulus did not significantly predict recall performance in this sample. As an additional check, we compared participants in the ABB group who reported using visualization or semantic association strategies against those who reported primarily rote rehearsal; no significant differences in recall emerged between these strategy subgroups.

We also examined the relationship between subjective arousal ratings taken after encoding and delayed recall performance. Correlational analysis across the full sample revealed a small, non-significant correlation between arousal and recall (r = 0.09, p = 0.47). Sleep duration reported for the intervening night was modestly correlated with recall (r = 0.18, p = 0.15), though this correlation did not reach statistical significance. Including sleep duration as a covariate in an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) comparing ABB and Silence groups did not materially alter the outcome; the main effect of condition remained non-significant (F(1,61) = 0.32, p = 0.57), and the effect size estimate did not appreciably change.

Given the relatively small observed effect and the sample size, we examined whether the study had sufficient power to detect plausible effects. Post hoc power analysis indicated that with the observed effect size (d = 0.14) the achieved power was quite low (< 20%), suggesting that the study was underpowered to detect effects of this small magnitude. However, the Bayesian analysis suggested that the data were more compatible with the null than with a reliable effect, lending additional interpretive weight to the non-significant t-test. Confidence intervals further illustrated that any true effect, if present, was likely small and within a range that would be of limited practical significance for memory tasks of this nature.

To explore whether the lack of observed effect might be attributable to heterogeneity in responses, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. First, outlier analysis identified two individuals with recall scores more than three standard deviations below their group mean; excluding these cases did not meaningfully alter the pattern of results, with the group means remaining similar and the t-test still non-significant (t = 0.61, p = 0.54). Second, performance was examined across the serial positions of the word list to see whether ABB may have selectively affected primacy or recency effects; analysis of recall probability by serial position revealed the typical primacy trend but no interaction with condition.

Finally, we conducted exploratory regression analyses predicting delayed recall from condition (coded 0 = Silence, 1 = ABB), baseline variables such as age and gender, subjective arousal, sleep duration, and reported strategy usage. In these models, condition was not a significant predictor, and the overall models explained a modest proportion of variance in recall (R^2 values in the range of 0.10 to 0.18 depending on the model specification). None of the exploratory predictors or their interactions with condition consistently indicated that ABB during encoding improved long-term memory performance under the tested parameters.

In summary, the primary analyses did not support the hypothesis that alpha binaural beats presented during encoding enhance delayed recall after 24 hours compared to studying in silence. Descriptive statistics indicated only a small numerical advantage for ABB that did not reach statistical significance, effect size estimates were small, and Bayesian analysis favored the null hypothesis. Exploratory analyses of subjective awareness, arousal, sleep, and strategy usage did not reveal consistent moderators that could account for a concealed positive effect of ABB in this sample.

Discussion

The present study examined whether presenting alpha-range binaural beats during the encoding of a word list would enhance long-term memory as measured by delayed free recall after a 24-hour retention interval. The data provided no evidence for a statistically reliable advantage of the ABB condition over silence in terms of the number of words recalled, and the observed effect size was small. Bayesian analysis suggested that the data offered more support for the null hypothesis than for a meaningful effect of ABB under the tested conditions. Exploratory analyses of potential moderators, including subjective awareness of the beats, arousal, sleep duration, and strategy use, did not yield clear patterns indicating that ABB benefited particular subgroups of participants. Taken together, the findings indicate that, in this implementation, alpha binaural beats presented only during encoding do not produce a detectable improvement in long-term memory compared to a silence control.

These null results contribute to the broader literature on binaural beats and cognitive enhancement, which has been characterized by mixed outcomes and methodological heterogeneity. On the one hand, some studies have reported cognitive benefits associated with binaural beats, including improvements in attention, mood, or short-term memory under specific parameters. On the other hand, numerous studies have failed to find reliable behavioral effects, particularly when more stringent controls and adequately powered samples are used. The current findings align with the subset of investigations that do not observe robust behavioral gains from binaural beats, especially when effects are probed for processes that reflect long-term consolidation rather than short-term modulation of arousal or attention.

Several interpretations of the null effect are plausible and deserve consideration. One possibility is that the specific ABB stimulus used in this study did not produce sufficient neural entrainment in the alpha band to drive behavioral changes. We did not record electroencephalographic data, so we cannot directly assess whether participants’ neural activity synchronized with the 10 Hz beat. Prior research suggests that the strength of binaural beat entrainment is variable across individuals and can depend on carrier frequencies, beat intensity, and listening conditions. Without direct neural measurement, it remains an open question whether the absence of a behavioral effect reflects a failure of entrainment or a lack of behavioral consequence even when entrainment occurs.

A related possibility is that alpha entrainment during encoding is not the critical factor for enhancing long-term retention. If alpha-band dynamics are more important during consolidation or retrieval, then altering alpha during the learning phase alone may not be sufficient to affect performance on a delayed recall test. Indeed, the cognitive functions supported by alpha oscillations include attentional gating and temporal coordination of activity, which may be more relevant when selective attention or retrieval operations are engaged. Consequently, alpha modulation during the encoding window might not strongly influence the downstream consolidation processes that determine later recall.

Another consideration concerns the control condition. Silence is a basic control and was chosen here for its simplicity, but it does not account for non-specific effects of auditory stimulation such as auditory masking, changes in mood, or arousal induced by sound. It is possible that ABB and other auditory stimulation would produce similar effects on memory via non-frequency-specific mechanisms, thereby obscuring frequency-specific benefits. Future studies employing active control conditions that provide auditory stimulation without binaural beat properties, or with beats at different frequencies, would help disambiguate frequency-specific effects from general auditory influences.

Individual differences likely played a role as well. The degree to which a person can be entrained by binaural beats varies, and baseline alpha power or neurophysiological traits may moderate responsiveness. In our sample, although most participants reported awareness of hearing a continuous tone during the ABB condition, self-reported awareness did not translate into superior recall. However, subjective awareness is a crude proxy for entrainment; some individuals may experience robust neural modulation without explicit awareness of the beat, and vice versa. Future research that pairs binaural-beat presentation with electrophysiological monitoring could identify responders and non-responders and test whether behavioral effects are confined to those who exhibit verified entrainment.

Power and effect size considerations also merit discussion. The present study was powered to detect medium-sized effects but had limited sensitivity to detect small effects. The observed effect size was small, and post hoc calculations indicated that substantially larger sample sizes would be needed to reliably detect such small effects if they are genuine. While the Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence favoring the null for the present sample and design, it remains possible that ABB produces small effects that are of limited practical significance but theoretically informative. Larger-scale replication studies or meta-analytic syntheses could clarify whether consistent but small effects exist across different implementations.

Task specifics are another relevant factor. We used a 30-word list and free recall after 24 hours to index long-term memory, which is a reasonable and ecologically relevant measure. Nonetheless, different memory tasks might be more sensitive to ABB-related modulation. For example, associative memory tasks, recognition paradigms, or tasks that explicitly tax attentional selection during encoding might interact differently with alpha-band modulation. It is also plausible that ABB could influence memory consolidation during sleep if delivered during sleep or during awake rest periods following encoding, a possibility not tested in the current design.

The timing and duration of stimulation are additional parameters that warrant systematic exploration. In the present study, ABB was delivered only during the encoding window for approximately two minutes. Some prior studies that have reported cognitive effects used longer stimulation periods or repeated sessions, raising the possibility that cumulative exposure or stimulation during off-line periods (including sleep) may be necessary to produce measurable behavioral change. Dose-response investigations varying stimulation duration and timing relative to encoding, consolidation, and retrieval would be informative.

Methodological refinements could strengthen future research. Including an active control condition with monaural beats or non-beat auditory stimulation would help isolate frequency-specific effects. Recording EEG or magnetoencephalography during stimulation and subsequent memory probes would enable direct tests of the hypothesized mechanism linking alpha entrainment to memory outcomes. Pre-registering hypotheses and analysis plans and ensuring adequate sample sizes to detect small-to-medium effects would enhance interpretability and reproducibility. Moreover, combining binaural beat stimulation with cognitive training or targeted mnemonic strategies could reveal interactive effects whereby ABB augments the efficacy of strategy use in supporting long-term memory.

Theoretical implications of the present null findings relate to broader questions about the causal role of externally modulated neural oscillations in cognition. While endogenous oscillatory dynamics clearly correlate with cognitive states, the degree to which externally imposed rhythms can steer complex cognitive processing in meaningful ways remains contested. Our results suggest caution in assuming that simple auditory entrainment at alpha frequencies will straightforwardly enhance long-term memory consolidation. This is not to deny the potential utility of oscillatory stimulation altogether, but rather to emphasize that the translation from neural modulation to behavioral improvement is contingent on multiple factors including the strength and specificity of entrainment, individual susceptibility, task demands, timing of stimulation, and measurement sensitivity.

Practical implications for those considering binaural beats as a tool for memory enhancement are also pertinent. Our findings do not support the use of brief ABB exposure during study as an effective method for improving delayed recall after 24 hours, at least under the specific conditions tested. Consumers and practitioners should be skeptical of claims that simple ABB applications reliably boost long-term memory without further evidence delineating effective parameters and demonstrating robust, replicable effects. That said, it remains possible that alternative protocols, such as concurrent ABB during retrieval or prolonged interventions across multiple sessions, could yield different outcomes.

Several concrete directions for future research emerge from the present work. First, incorporating neural recordings during stimulation is a high priority to establish whether ABB produces reliable alpha entrainment at the scalp and whether such entrainment correlates with behavioral measures. Second, testing ABB during different phases of the memory process—encoding, consolidation, and retrieval—within a factorial design would clarify the temporal window during which alpha modulation might be most influential. Third, employing active controls and varying beat frequency systematically would help determine frequency specificity. Fourth, recruiting larger samples and characterizing individual difference variables such as baseline alpha power, auditory processing traits, and trait attentional control could reveal moderators of responsiveness. Fifth, exploring dose and timing parameters, including stimulation during sleep, could illuminate whether ABB interacts with consolidation mechanisms.

In conclusion, the current experiment did not find evidence that alpha binaural beats presented during learning enhance long-term free recall after 24 hours relative to silence. The results contribute to a nuanced picture of binaural beat effects on cognition, suggesting that under brief, single-session encoding-phase stimulation, behavioral gains in delayed memory are not evident. The findings motivate further investigation using neural measures, active control conditions, and varied timing of stimulation to more precisely map the conditions under which binaural beats might influence memory and other cognitive processes.
