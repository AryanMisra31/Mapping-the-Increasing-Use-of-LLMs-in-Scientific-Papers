Introduction

Human behavior can be shaped by internalized representations and social categories in ways that are often automatic and outside conscious awareness. The idea that mental representations associated with categories such as age, gender, and race can influence overt behavior is rooted in theoretical approaches that emphasize automaticity in social cognition. Research in social and cognitive psychology has demonstrated that incidental activation of category-related constructs can produce changes in perception, judgment, and action. These effects have been described under terms such as behavioral assimilation, stereotype activation, and social priming, and they have generated substantial theoretical interest because they bear on the boundary between conscious control and automatic behavioral regulation. One of the most influential demonstrations of this general phenomenon showed that exposure to words associated with old age produced a measurable slowing in walking speed after the experimental task. In that demonstration, participants who encountered age-related stimuli subsequently walked more slowly down a corridor than participants who encountered neutral stimuli, an effect that was interpreted as behavioral assimilation to the elderly stereotype. This line of work suggested that activated constructs can spill over into behavior in a manner that does not require conscious intention.

The elderly-walking priming finding became emblematic of the potential for subtle environmental cues to alter behavior. It was taken to support theories that posit a direct link between conceptual activation and action tendencies, and it suggested practical implications for environments in which incidental cues might shape everyday behavior. At the same time, as other researchers attempted to replicate and extend such findings, the robustness and boundary conditions of the effect came under scrutiny. Some replication attempts produced similar effects whereas others failed to find significant walking-speed changes, leading to debate about factors such as the nature of the stimuli, the subtlety of the manipulation, participants’ awareness, and study context. Discussions in the literature have emphasized potential moderators such as the subtlety of prime presentation, the extent to which primes are processed deeply versus shallowly, the socio-cultural relevance of the activated construct to participants, and the measurement sensitivity of the behavioral outcome.

Beyond experimental parameters, individual differences also plausibly moderate susceptibility to primes. For instance, people differ in trait-level prosociality, orientation toward others, and the extent to which social goals influence behavior. Such individual differences might alter the degree to which a person assimilates to socially salient constructs that imply behavioral scripts. Social value orientation (SVO) is one well-established measure of individual differences in preferences for allocating resources between oneself and others; it can distinguish people who are more prosocial from those who are more individualistic or competitive. Given that prosocial individuals may be particularly sensitive to social cues and relational contexts, it is plausible that SVO, or related constructs, would moderate the strength of behavioral assimilation when social categories are activated. If priming effects rely in part on social motivation and concern for social norms, then individuals with relatively greater prosocial orientation might show stronger assimilation effects. Conversely, if priming effects are purely cognitive and operate through automatic activation of semantic networks irrespective of social motivation, SVO might not moderate behavioral assimilation.

Empirical work linking SVO and priming susceptibility is sparse and mixed. Some theoretical accounts suggest that social motives can amplify or dampen the behavioral translation of activated constructs. For example, a person who is highly oriented toward social harmony might experience stronger inclination to embody social scripts, whereas a person focused on self-interest might be less likely to manifest externally cued social behaviors. However, empirical tests that embed SVO as a moderator in priming paradigms are relatively limited, and few studies have systematically tested whether SVO predicts responsiveness to stereotype-based behavioral priming. The absence of clear evidence on this point leaves an important question open about the degree to which such priming effects rely on broader social motivational traits as opposed to more domain-general cognitive activation.

Methodological design choices matter in tests of behavioral assimilation. The original elderly-walking studies used corridor walking as an ecologically valid and easily measurable behavioral outcome, and they used tasks such as word-search or scrambled-sentence tests to activate relevant concepts. The measure of walking speed typically involved a timed walk over a set distance immediately after the prime task, and in some studies, walking was measured both before and after the manipulation to control for baseline differences. Experimental designs have varied between within-subjects and between-subjects allocations, and the nature of the prime item sets has varied from overtly relevant words to more subtle lexical manipulations. Additionally, researchers have debated the role of demand characteristics and awareness, arguing that if participants infer the experimental hypothesis they might alter their behavior deliberately, which would confound conclusions about automaticity. As a result, the inclusion of manipulation checks that assess awareness and suspicion has been recommended in replication attempts.

When considering replication and extension, it is also important to attend to statistical power and measurement reliability. The size of behavioral assimilation effects reported in early studies was modest, and many subsequent replication attempts appeared underpowered to detect such effects reliably. Modern consensus in experimental psychology highlights the need for adequate sample sizes, pre-registration of analysis plans, and multiple converging measures to assess subtle behavioral outcomes. In the context of walking-speed paradigms, measurement precision depends on the accuracy of timing devices and control over environmental factors such as footwear, distractions, and instructions about the walking task. Small environmental variations can influence walking speed, and uncontrolled variability could obscure subtle prime effects.

The present study was designed to examine two related questions that bear on the robustness and boundary conditions of stereotype-based behavioral assimilation. The first question was whether exposure to words associated with old age, embedded in a word unscramble task, would produce a change in walking speed measured on a 16-meter corridor walk. Building on prior work that used somewhat shorter distances, we measured walking speed before and after the prime task to control for baseline differences. Measuring change in walking speed within participants increases sensitivity to small effects of priming by using each participant as their own control. The second question was whether social value orientation would moderate susceptibility to elderly-related priming. We included a validated SVO measure to test whether prosocial orientation predicted greater change in walking speed following exposure to age-related words.

We adopted an independent-groups design for the prime manipulation, randomly assigning participants to an elderly prime condition or a neutral prime condition. Random assignment was used to minimize systematic differences between groups, and the pre-post walking measures allowed us to test for condition-by-time interactions. We also implemented measures to limit demand characteristics by presenting the walking task as incidental to a language processing task and by including a post-experimental awareness probe to assess whether participants suspected the study’s purpose. Analytic plans included mixed-model analyses testing the interaction between time (pre vs post) and condition (elderly vs neutral), followed by moderation tests examining whether SVO scores predicted within-subject changes differentially across conditions.

Our study sought to contribute to the literature in three ways. First, by using a 16-meter corridor and pre-post measurement, we aimed to provide a sensitive test of the elderly-walking priming effect under controlled conditions. Second, by including a well-validated measure of social value orientation as a potential moderator, we tested an empirically plausible mechanism by which social motivation might shape susceptibility to stereotype activation. Third, by employing explicit awareness checks and reporting effect sizes and confidence intervals, we aimed to advance the methodological transparency of priming research. Based on prior findings, we expected that participants exposed to elderly-related words would show a small slowing in walking speed post-manipulation relative to those who received neutral words. We further hypothesized that this effect would be more pronounced among participants higher in prosocial orientation as measured by SVO. Given the mixed evidence on replicability, we also acknowledged the possibility that the manipulation might not produce a measurable behavioral change in our sample, in which case the moderation analysis would help clarify whether individual differences account for null aggregate effects.

Methods

Participants and sampling
Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and the surrounding community using online advertisements and local flyers. A target sample size of 120 participants was chosen prior to data collection to provide reasonable power to detect small-to-moderate effects typical of behavioral priming studies. We used simple random assignment to allocate participants to either the elderly prime condition or the neutral prime condition, with the goal of obtaining approximately equal group sizes. Inclusion criteria specified that participants be aged between 18 and 40 years, able to walk unaided, and fluent in English; these criteria were intended to reduce variability associated with age-related gait differences and to ensure participants could complete the language tasks. Exclusion criteria included self-reported mobility impairments, current use of medications that influence motor function, or prior participation in similar priming studies.

A total of 120 participants began the study. Six participants were excluded from the final analyses because they reported during post-experimental questioning that they had suspected the hypothesis linking age-related words to changes in walking behavior, and three additional participants were excluded due to equipment failure that prevented accurate timing of their walks. After exclusions, the final sample included 111 participants. Participant demographic characteristics were recorded, including age, gender, education level, and self-reported physical activity. Group-level descriptive statistics indicated that the two experimental groups were comparable on these demographics, and no significant differences in age or gender distribution were observed between groups at baseline.

Materials
Prime task. The prime manipulation took the form of a word unscramble task comprised of 30 items. In the elderly prime condition, 15 of the 30 items were words culturally and semantically associated with old age (for example, words conveying age-related imagery and concepts appropriate for the cultural context), and the remaining items were neutral filler words unrelated to age. In the neutral prime condition, all 30 items were neutral and matched on length and word frequency to the set used in the elderly condition. Each item required participants to arrange a subset of letters to form a coherent four- or five-letter English word. The task was described as a brief language activity unrelated to walking or physical movement.

Walking measure. Walking speed was assessed by timing participants as they walked a 16.0-meter corridor at a comfortable, self-selected pace. Timing was initiated when the participant’s lead foot crossed a marked start line and stopped when the lead foot crossed a marked end line; this approach minimized acceleration and deceleration artifacts. An electronic timing device with millisecond precision was used, and two trained research assistants were present to ensure standardization of instructions and to record times independently for reliability checks.

Social value orientation. Social value orientation was measured using a widely used allocation-based questionnaire adapted from prior research. The instrument presents a series of resource-allocation decisions in which the participant chooses between outcomes favoring self, others, or joint welfare. Responses were scored to produce a continuous SVO angle measure, with higher scores indicating greater prosocial orientation. The instrument has shown adequate reliability in prior studies and was administered via paper-and-pencil at the end of the session to minimize any potential immediate influence on the walking task.

Awareness probe and debriefing. Following the post-task walk and completion of questionnaires, participants completed a suspicion and awareness probe that asked open-endedly whether they had any ideas about the purpose of the study and whether they noticed any relation between the tasks. Participants were then fully debriefed about the study’s aims and given the opportunity to withdraw their data if they were uncomfortable with the deception inherent in the prime manipulation.

Procedure
Upon arrival, participants were welcomed by a trained experimenter and provided informed consent. They were told the study involved examining language processing and motor coordination as separate tasks to minimize demand characteristics. Participants completed an initial baseline walking measure in which they were instructed to walk at their normal comfortable pace from the start to the end of a 16-meter corridor. The walking surface was level and unobstructed, and participants wore their own footwear. After completing the first walk, participants were randomly assigned, via a random number generator, to either the elderly prime or the neutral prime condition.

Participants then completed the word unscramble task in a quiet testing room. They were given 10 minutes to complete as many items as possible, with the instruction to work as quickly and accurately as possible. Research assistants monitored the task but did not provide feedback. Immediately following the prime task, participants were led back to the corridor and asked to complete the walking task again under identical instructions to those used at baseline. This post-task walk was conducted within two minutes of task completion to maximize any short-lived priming effects.

After the second walk, participants completed the SVO questionnaire and a brief demographics form. Finally, participants completed the awareness probe and were debriefed. Each session lasted approximately 25 to 30 minutes. Experimenters recorded times and double-entered data to reduce transcription errors.

Design and analytic plan
The primary design was a mixed factorial with a within-subjects factor of time (pre-walk vs post-walk) and a between-subjects factor of prime condition (elderly prime vs neutral). The primary hypothesis predicted a condition-by-time interaction such that participants in the elderly prime condition would show a greater increase in walking time (i.e., slowing) from pre- to post-walk relative to participants in the neutral condition. The primary analysis used a mixed-model analysis of variance with participant as the random factor and condition as the grouping factor.

Secondary analyses tested whether social value orientation moderated the priming effect. This was operationalized in two ways: first, by including SVO as a continuous covariate in the mixed model and testing the three-way interaction (time x condition x SVO); second, by performing a median split on SVO to create “higher” and “lower” prosocial groups and conducting follow-up mixed ANOVAs within those subsamples. All analyses reported effect sizes (partial eta squared for ANOVAs and Cohen’s d for t-tests) and 95% confidence intervals for key contrasts. Prior to analyses, walking times were screened for outliers and departures from normality; mild skew was corrected with log transformation in sensitivity analyses, and results were compared to untransformed analyses to ensure robustness.

Results

Sample characteristics and preliminary checks
Descriptive statistics for the final sample of 111 participants indicated a mean age of 22.6 years (SD = 4.3), with 63 female and 48 male participants. The elderly prime group included 56 participants and the neutral prime group included 55 participants after exclusions. Independent-samples t-tests confirmed that the two groups did not differ significantly on age, t(109) = 0.28, p = .78, or on baseline walking time, t(109) = 0.41, p = .68. Gender distribution across conditions did not differ significantly by chi-square test, χ2(1, N = 111) = 0.09, p = .76. These checks suggested that random assignment produced comparable groups on observed demographics and baseline behavior.

Baseline walking times, measured in seconds for the 16-meter walk, averaged 10.29 s (SD = 1.14) across the full sample. Baseline means for the elderly prime and neutral prime groups were 10.31 s (SD = 1.13) and 10.27 s (SD = 1.15), respectively. Correlations between baseline walking time and age, r = .12, p = .20, were small and non-significant, suggesting that within this restricted age range, age did not strongly predict gait speed. Inter-rater reliability for timing was high; the two independent research assistants’ timing recordings correlated at r = .99, and mean differences between raters were negligible, supporting the reliability of the measure.

Primary analysis: time by condition interaction
The primary analysis tested the interaction between time (pre vs post) and prime condition (elderly vs neutral) in a mixed-model ANOVA. Across the full sample, the grand mean for post-walk time was 10.32 s (SD = 1.18), indicating a small average increase in walking time from pre- to post-walk. The mixed-model ANOVA yielded a significant main effect of time, F(1,109) = 4.12, p = .045, partial η2 = .036, reflecting a small but statistically detectable increase in walking time from pre to post across conditions. The main effect of condition was not significant, F(1,109) = 0.08, p = .78, partial η2 = .001, indicating no overall difference in walking time between the elderly and neutral groups when averaging across time points.

Critically, the interaction between time and condition was not significant, F(1,109) = 0.34, p = .56, partial η2 = .003. The means indicated that participants in the elderly prime condition had a pre-walk mean of 10.31 s (SD = 1.13) and a post-walk mean of 10.35 s (SD = 1.20), representing a mean increase of 0.04 s (SD of within-subject differences = 0.62 s). Participants in the neutral condition had a pre-walk mean of 10.27 s (SD = 1.15) and a post-walk mean of 10.28 s (SD = 1.15), representing a mean increase of 0.01 s (SD = 0.59 s). The small difference in pre-post change between conditions—0.03 s—translated into a negligible Cohen’s d of 0.05 for the interaction contrast and a 95% confidence interval that included zero (95% CI for mean difference = −0.12 to 0.18 s).

Sensitivity analyses and distributional checks
Given the modest main effect of time and the non-significant interaction, we conducted sensitivity analyses to assess robustness. First, we re-ran the mixed ANOVA after log-transforming walking times to correct for a slight positive skew; the pattern of results was unchanged, with a small main effect of time remaining marginally significant, F(1,109) = 3.98, p = .048, and no significant time-by-condition interaction, F(1,109) = 0.29, p = .59. Second, we examined the effect of excluding participants with extreme within-subject changes (defined as greater than ±3 SD from the mean difference), which removed three additional cases. With these exclusions, the time effect was no longer significant, F(1,105) = 2.17, p = .14, and the interaction remained non-significant, F(1,105) = 0.16, p = .69. These sensitivity checks suggested that the small overall increase in walking time was not robust to data trimming and that no evidence supported a priming-induced differential change.

Social value orientation as a moderator
Social value orientation scores ranged from 16° to 52° with a mean of 31.8° (SD = 7.2), reflecting variability in prosocial orientation within the sample. Correlation between SVO and baseline walking time was small and non-significant, r = .04, p = .66, indicating no simple association between prosocial orientation and comfortable walking speed. We tested whether SVO moderated the priming effect by including SVO as a continuous covariate and testing the three-way interaction (time x condition x SVO) in the mixed-model framework. The three-way interaction was not significant, F(1,108) = 0.22, p = .64, indicating no evidence that higher prosocial orientation amplified the priming effect.

To illustrate, we conducted a median split on SVO to form “higher” and “lower” prosocial groups and examined the time-by-condition interaction within these subgroups. Among participants with higher SVO (n = 56), the time-by-condition interaction was non-significant, F(1,54) = 0.09, p = .77. Among participants with lower SVO (n = 55), the time-by-condition interaction was also non-significant, F(1,53) = 0.47, p = .50. Neither subgroup showed a pattern in which elderly priming reliably caused larger increases in walking time relative to neutral priming. The interaction contrast effect sizes were small in both subgroups (partial η2 < .01).

Additional exploratory analyses
We explored whether awareness of the manipulation influenced results by comparing participants who reported any suspicion about the link between the word task and walking to those who did not. Only six participants (prior to exclusions for suspicion) reported such suspicion, and their exclusion did not alter the pattern of results. We also examined potential gender differences by conducting the mixed ANOVA with gender as an additional between-subjects factor; the three-way interaction among time, condition, and gender was non-significant, F(1,107) = 0.47, p = .50. Finally, we tested whether individual differences in baseline physical activity level predicted within-subject changes in walking time; physical activity was uncorrelated with pre-post change, r = −.07, p = .45.

Effect sizes and confidence intervals
Given the null interaction, it was important to report effect size estimates and confidence intervals. The estimated mean difference in pre-post change between elderly and neutral conditions was 0.03 s, 95% CI = −0.12 to 0.18 s. Translating this into walking speed (meters per second), the difference corresponded to less than 0.005 m/s, a negligible magnitude relative to typical gait variability. The partial eta squared for the interaction was .003, indicating that the manipulation accounted for substantially less than 1% of variance in within-subject change.

Bayesian-informed interpretation
Although the study did not include a formal Bayesian analysis plan pre-registered, we computed an approximate Bayes factor to inform interpretation of the null interaction. The Bayes factor for the interaction term (BF01) indicated that the data were approximately four times more likely under the null model than under a model including the interaction as a medium effect, suggesting moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis of no priming-induced change in walking speed.

Summary of results
In summary, although there was a small overall increase in walking time from pre- to post-walk when collapsing across conditions, there was no evidence that exposure to elderly-related words induced a larger slowing in walking relative to neutral words. Social value orientation did not moderate responses to the prime, and sensitivity analyses and exploratory checks did not reveal any robust subgroups or conditions under which the priming effect emerged. Taken together, these results provide no support for the hypothesis that the specific elderly prime manipulation used here produced measurable behavioral assimilation in gait speed.

Discussion

The present study tested whether exposure to elderly-associated words in a word unscramble task would produce a slowing of walking speed on a 16-meter corridor and whether social value orientation would moderate such an effect. Overall, the results did not support the primary hypothesis: although walking times increased slightly from pre- to post-task across participants, this change did not differ meaningfully between those who received the elderly word prime and those who received neutral items. Furthermore, individual differences in social value orientation did not predict susceptibility to the prime, either as a continuous moderator or when dichotomized into higher versus lower prosocial groups. These findings contribute to ongoing conversations about the robustness and boundary conditions of stereotype-based behavioral priming effects.

Interpreting a null interaction requires careful consideration of several possibilities. One interpretation is that the elderly prime, as implemented in this study, simply did not exert a meaningful influence on gait behavior under the present conditions. This could reflect issues with the potency of the manipulation, the timing of the behavioral measure, measurement sensitivity, or context. For example, the word unscramble items might have activated elderly-related concepts to a lesser degree than in earlier studies that reported priming effects. Subtle differences in word choice, cultural context, or participant engagement might reduce the depth of semantic processing required to trigger downstream motor changes. Although the prime task included explicit age-related items in the elderly condition and matched neutral items in the control condition, the degree of activation might have been insufficient to produce behavioral assimilation.

Relatedly, the temporal dynamics of priming effects are important. The post-walk was administered within a two-minute window after the prime task, which should fall within plausible durations for transient activation-based effects. However, if priming effects decay more rapidly or require immediate, continuous activation at the time of the behavioral measure, the brief interval might have been too long or too short depending on the mechanism. Conversely, if priming effects require more implicit contextual reinforcement—such as environmental cues encountered while walking—the laboratory setup might lack critical reinforcing stimuli that would translate cognitive activation into motor behavior.

Another possibility is that the pre-post measurement strategy itself influenced outcomes. Measuring baseline walking speed can reduce variance but can also attenuate priming effects if completing the initial walk serves as a contextual anchor or primes participants in ways that interact with the experimental manipulation. For instance, performing a baseline walk might induce participants to adopt a consistent walking strategy or heightened self-monitoring that makes them less susceptible to subsequent incidental cues. While pre-post designs are valuable for controlling baseline differences, they might also reduce sensitivity to manipulations that produce small shifts relative to intra-individual consistency.

Measurement precision also merits scrutiny. Our walking times were reliable across raters, and the corridor length of 16 meters is longer than in some prior studies, which can increase precision by reducing relative measurement error during acceleration phases. Nevertheless, natural gait variability among young adults, influenced by momentary mood, fatigue, or footwear, can produce fluctuations that mask subtle effects. The very small mean pre-post differences we observed (on the order of hundredths of a second between conditions) are negligible relative to typical within-person gait variability and would require substantially larger samples to detect reliably if they reflect a true effect with extremely small magnitude.

The role of individual differences deserves attention. Our hypothesis that social value orientation would moderate priming susceptibility was plausible given theoretical linkages between social motivation and responsiveness to social cues. However, SVO did not predict differential change in walking speed. This null finding suggests that SVO, at least as measured by the allocation-based instrument used here, may not be a key determinant of whether individuals embody activated social stereotypes in their gait. It remains possible that other individual difference variables—such as trait empathy, self-monitoring, chronic activation of stereotype-related identities, or attitudes toward aging—would show stronger moderation effects. Future research could include a broader battery of personality and cognitive measures to test alternative moderators.

The null interaction also speaks to broader issues in the priming literature, particularly the replicability and boundary conditions of social priming effects. The elderly-walking effect has been both influential and contentious; some early demonstrations were dramatic and suggested relatively large effects, whereas subsequent replication attempts have yielded mixed results. Our findings align with those replication attempts that failed to find reliable priming-induced changes in overt behavior. Several interpretive frameworks exist for reconciling discrepant findings in the literature. One is that original effects were genuine but highly sensitive to contextual and methodological idiosyncrasies that are difficult to reproduce. Another is that some original effects may have reflected researcher degrees of freedom or subtle demand characteristics that were not fully controlled, leading to overestimation of effect sizes. A third perspective is that the effect is real but small and susceptible to attenuation in samples where baseline variability or procedural differences reduce statistical power.

Given these possibilities, methodological transparency and cumulative evidence are essential. Our study aimed to address several concerns by using random assignment, a pre-post design, reliable timing equipment, and a moderately powered sample. Despite this, the small observed effect sizes and moderate evidence favoring the null for the interaction underscore the importance of large-scale, preregistered replication efforts and meta-analytic aggregation to estimate the true magnitude of these priming phenomena. Meta-analytic approaches can help quantify heterogeneity across studies and test candidate moderators such as prime strength, task type, cultural setting, and participant characteristics.

Limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the sample comprised mostly young adult university affiliates, which limits generalizability to older populations or to culturally diverse samples for whom elderly stereotypes might carry different weight. In particular, if priming effects rely on congruence between the activated stereotype and salient features of participants’ self-concept or social identity, young adult samples in university settings might be less susceptible to elderly primes. Second, although we attempted to minimize demand characteristics, the nature of laboratory research inevitably exposes participants to researcher presence and instructions that could affect behavior. We administered an awareness probe and excluded participants who reported clear suspicion, but more covert measures of compliance and implicit monitoring were not available. Third, while SVO was a reasonable candidate moderator, it represents only one facet of social motivation; exploring other traits might yield different insights.

Future research should take several directions to advance understanding. One avenue is systematic variation of prime strength and modality: comparing lexical primes to pictorial primes, subliminal to supraliminal presentations, and single-exposure to repeated exposures could reveal whether stronger or more ecologically embedded primes produce more robust behavioral effects. Another avenue is to test potential moderators more comprehensively, including measures of stereotype endorsement, aging-related anxiety, self-focused attention, and contextual cues encountered during the behavioral measure. Additionally, replication efforts with larger samples and multi-lab designs could provide more definitive evidence regarding effect size and heterogeneity. Researchers might also investigate whether cumulative minor primes in everyday environments lead to measurable behavioral shifts over longer timescales, which would address ecological validity concerns beyond the laboratory.

The theoretical implications of our findings touch on models of automaticity and behavioral translation from cognition. The absence of a reliable priming-induced change in gait speed under these conditions suggests that simple semantic activation may be insufficient, on its own, to drive overt motor changes in healthy young adults. This speaks to the notion that automatic activation and behavioral enactment may be mediated by additional pathways—such as goal activation, social motivation, or situational affordances—that were not engaged in our paradigm. It is plausible that stereotype activation must interact with goal-related processes or situational triggers to manifest in observable behavior. For instance, primed concepts might need to become goals that the individual pursues, or primes might require a contextual cue that affords behavior congruent with the activated stereotype.

Practically, the findings caution against overly broad generalizations about the potency of incidental cues to change behavior in everyday settings. While environmental cues undoubtedly influence cognition and choice in many contexts, the translation from a brief lexical prime to a measurable change in locomotion appears fragile. This does not negate the possibility that other forms of priming can be effective under particular conditions; rather, it highlights the need to specify the mechanisms, boundary conditions, and effect magnitudes more precisely before drawing strong conclusions about everyday behavioral susceptibility to incidental cues.

In conclusion, the present experiment found no evidence that embedding elderly-associated words within a word unscramble task produced a meaningful slowing in walking speed on a 16-meter corridor compared with a neutral-word control. Social value orientation did not moderate this absence of effect. These results add to a growing body of literature suggesting that stereotype-based behavioral priming effects are, at best, context-dependent and possibly smaller than originally reported. Future work that systematically varies prime potency, assesses additional moderators, and employs larger, preregistered, multi-site designs will be crucial for clarifying the nature and practical significance of behavioral assimilation effects. Such efforts will help determine when and how activated social constructs translate into overt action and will refine theoretical models of automaticity in social behavior.
