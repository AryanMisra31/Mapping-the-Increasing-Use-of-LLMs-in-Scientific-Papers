Introduction

Individual differences in personality and self-perception have long been recognized as important predictors of behavior and performance across a range of domains. Two constructs that have received particularly sustained attention in psychological research are locus of control and self-esteem. Locus of control refers to the degree to which individuals perceive events in their lives as contingent upon their own actions versus external forces. Self-esteem refers to a person’s overall sense of self-worth or self-regard. Both constructs have been linked to educational outcomes, workplace performance, mental health, and coping with stress. Because both locus of control and self-esteem reflect enduring ways of perceiving the self and the environment, understanding their interrelationship is important for theory and practice. A clear understanding of how these constructs relate to one another can inform interventions aimed at improving academic outcomes and psychological well-being. Despite a substantial literature examining each construct separately, the relationship between locus of control and self-esteem is not yet settled in the context of high-performing academic populations. Many studies have examined these constructs in broad community samples, clinical samples, or general student samples, but fewer studies have examined them specifically among students attending highly selective universities. Examining locus of control and self-esteem in this context is valuable because elite academic environments may differentially shape or select for particular profiles of self-perception and attributional style. The present investigation therefore aimed to examine the relationship between locus of control and self-esteem in a sample drawn from Russell Group universities. Doing so offers an opportunity to consider whether students in these environments differ in meaningful ways from broader population norms on either construct. There are theoretical reasons to expect a relationship between locus of control and self-esteem. An internal locus of control, characterized by a belief that outcomes are contingent upon one’s own efforts, may support feelings of personal efficacy and competence that underlie healthy self-esteem. Conversely, an external locus of control, wherein outcomes are attributed to luck, fate, or powerful others, may erode feelings of agency and contribute to lower self-regard. Furthermore, attributional style and the sense of control that it engenders are thought to interact with feedback about performance to shape self-evaluations over time. For example, individuals who credit external factors for success may be less likely to internalize achievement and thus may maintain lower global self-esteem. Conversely, individuals with higher self-esteem may be more likely to endorse internal control beliefs because they view their actions as effective and worthy of positive outcomes. There is also a plausible reciprocal influence; repeated experiences of success or failure that are interpreted through a particular locus of control may shape global self-esteem across development. Empirically, some prior investigations have reported moderate associations between internal control beliefs and higher self-esteem. Other studies have found weaker or context-dependent relationships that depend on age, culture, or specific outcome domains. The heterogeneity of findings may in part reflect variation in measurement instruments, sampling frames, or analytic approaches. Additionally, much of the existing research has not systematically tested whether levels of these constructs in elite student samples differ from established norms. If students in selective universities display unique patterns of locus of control or self-esteem, such differences could have implications for how they manage stress, respond to failures, and prepare for transition out of university. In particular, if students display elevated external control beliefs relative to norms, they might be at greater risk for performance anxiety or reduced persistence in the face of setbacks. If they show lower self-esteem relative to norms, it could raise concerns about mental health and well-being even among high-achieving populations. Conversely, higher than average internality or self-esteem would suggest that selection into elite environments or the socialization within them reinforces adaptive self-perceptions. The literature also raises methodological considerations that are relevant for the current study. Measurement of locus of control commonly uses forced-choice inventories that assess attributional tendencies across multiple scenarios. Measurement of self-esteem commonly employs brief global self-report scales. The psychometric properties of these instruments are generally adequate in young adult samples, but reliability and distributional properties should be examined empirically in any new sample. Prior studies that did not find strong associations between the constructs sometimes reported low reliability or restricted variance as potential explanations. Therefore, the present study assessed internal consistency and examined distributional characteristics prior to conducting formal tests of association. Another methodological issue concerns the appropriate statistical models to assess the relationship between locus of control and self-esteem. Simple correlation analyses offer a straightforward test of linear association, but they do not account for potential confounding variables such as age or gender. Regression models that include relevant covariates can help clarify whether observed associations are robust across demographic groups. Moreover, examining whether sample means differ from population norms requires reference points derived from established scale manuals or large normative studies. The current study included one-sample tests to evaluate whether the Russell Group student sample differed from those published norms. Theoretical perspectives also suggest potential moderators of the relationship between locus of control and self-esteem. For example, social support, cultural values related to independence, and situational demands may strengthen or weaken the link between control beliefs and global self-worth. While the present study was not designed to test such moderators extensively, demographic data were collected to allow for exploratory analyses. Taken together, these considerations motivate a targeted empirical examination of the association between locus of control and self-esteem in an elite university student sample. The primary objective was to test whether there is a direct linear association between individual scores on a locus of control instrument and scores on a self-esteem scale. Based on theoretical expectations and prior empirical trends, a negative association was anticipated if locus of control is scored such that higher values indicate greater externality. Specifically, it was hypothesized that higher external locus of control scores would be associated with lower self-esteem scores. The secondary objective was to determine whether sample means for each scale differed significantly from population norms reported in standardized manuals or large-scale studies. It was hypothesized that students in Russell Group universities might differ from population norms on one or both constructs, but the direction of such differences was treated as an empirical question because selective environments could plausibly foster greater internality and higher self-esteem or, alternatively, higher externality and lower self-esteem through elevated stress and performance pressure. To address these objectives, a cross-sectional study design was implemented, recruiting students from several Russell Group universities. Standardized, widely used self-report measures were administered online in a single session. Data quality checks and psychometric evaluations were performed prior to hypothesis testing. Correlational analyses and comparisons to published normative means were conducted using conventional statistical tests. The following sections describe the specific methods used, report the results of the analyses, and discuss the theoretical and practical implications of the findings for understanding self-perception and control beliefs in elite academic settings.

Methods

Participants were undergraduate students enrolled at Russell Group universities in the United Kingdom. Recruitment was conducted through university mailing lists, student societies, and online forums that students commonly use to access research participation opportunities. Participation was open to any undergraduate student aged 18 or older who was currently enrolled at one of the participating institutions. A total of 132 students provided complete data and met eligibility criteria for the analyses reported here. The sample size of 132 was determined a priori to provide adequate power to detect a moderate correlation between locus of control and self-esteem. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 24 years with a mean age of 20.8 years. The sample included 77 women, 54 men, and one participant who preferred not to specify gender. Participants represented a range of academic disciplines, including humanities, social sciences, natural sciences, and engineering. The sample was diverse with respect to year of study, though the majority were in their first or second undergraduate years. All participants provided informed consent prior to completing the survey and were informed that participation was voluntary and confidential. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from an institutional review body at one of the participating universities. Measures included a widely used global self-esteem scale and a standard locus of control inventory. Self-esteem was assessed with a ten-item global self-esteem scale that asks respondents to indicate agreement with statements about their self-worth on a four-point Likert scale. Items include positively and negatively worded statements and are scored such that higher totals indicate greater self-esteem. The possible score range of the self-esteem scale is 0 to 30, with higher values representing higher global self-esteem. Locus of control was assessed using a commonly used forced-choice internal-external scale comprising 23 items. Each item presents two statements and respondents indicate which statement they agree with most. Items were scored to yield a total locus of control score with a range of 0 to 23, where higher scores represent greater external locus of control. The locus of control inventory used in this study is intended to assess generalized expectancies about control rather than attributions for single events. The survey also collected demographic information, including age, gender, year of study, and academic discipline. The order of the two primary measures was randomized across participants to mitigate order effects. Data were collected online using a secure survey platform. The survey took approximately 10 to 15 minutes to complete. Participants could leave the study at any point without penalty. At the end of the survey, participants were provided with debriefing information and signposted to student support services and counseling resources in case responding to items raised any personal concerns. Data preparation included inspection for completeness, response patterns, and time taken to complete the survey. Cases with substantial missing data on the primary measures were excluded from analyses. Instances of implausibly rapid completion suggesting inattentive responding were reviewed and excluded when necessary. After these quality checks, 132 complete cases remained for analysis. Internal consistency of the self-esteem measure was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. Internal consistency of the locus of control measure was also evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, recognizing that forced-choice items sometimes yield lower alpha values than Likert-type scales due to reduced item variance. Descriptive statistics were computed for each measure, including mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis. Normality of distributions was assessed using visual inspection of histograms and quantile-quantile plots, supplemented by formal tests such as the Shapiro-Wilk statistic. Correlational analyses were conducted to test the primary hypothesis regarding the linear association between locus of control and self-esteem. Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients were computed when both variables met assumptions of interval scaling and normal distribution. If distributional assumptions were not met, Spearman rank-order correlations were computed as a nonparametric alternative. In addition to bivariate correlations, exploratory multiple regression analyses were conducted to evaluate whether the association between locus of control and self-esteem persisted after controlling for age and gender. The regression models included locus of control as the predictor and self-esteem as the dependent variable, with demographic covariates entered in a second step to examine incremental variance explained. To address the secondary objective, one-sample t-tests compared the sample mean for each scale against published normative means reported in scale manuals or large-scale studies. For the self-esteem scale, the normative mean adopted for comparison was 20.0 on the 0–30 metric, which is commonly cited in normative summaries for college-aged samples. For the locus of control scale, the normative mean adopted was 12.0 on the 0–23 metric, which reflects an often-reported midpoint in general adult samples. These normative values served as reference points for assessing whether the Russell Group student sample differed meaningfully from broader population expectations. Effect sizes for mean comparisons were calculated using Cohen’s d computed as the difference between the sample mean and the normative value divided by the sample standard deviation. For the correlation analysis, confidence intervals around the correlation coefficient were computed to quantify precision. Statistical significance was evaluated at an alpha level of .05 with two-tailed tests. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Where appropriate, nonparametric robustness checks were performed. Finally, exploratory analyses examined group differences in locus of control and self-esteem by gender using independent-samples t-tests, and the association between categorical self-esteem levels and locus of control using analysis of variance. These exploratory analyses were interpreted cautiously given multiple comparisons and limited power for subgroup tests.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary measures are presented first. The self-esteem scale had a mean of 18.5, with a standard deviation of 4.9. The distribution of self-esteem scores was approximately normal by visual inspection of histogram and Q-Q plot, though there was a slight leftward skew indicating a modest proportion of lower self-esteem scores. The Shapiro-Wilk test for self-esteem did not indicate a significant departure from normality at conventional levels. The locus of control scale had a mean of 14.8 and a standard deviation of 3.7. The distribution of locus of control scores showed a modest rightward skew, indicating a greater number of participants endorsing items consistent with external control tendencies. The Shapiro-Wilk test indicated that the distribution of locus of control scores did not deviate markedly from normality after accounting for sample size. Reliability analyses indicated acceptable internal consistency for the self-esteem measure. Cronbach’s alpha for the ten-item self-esteem scale was .88, which is consistent with prior reports for young adult samples. The locus of control inventory yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of .72 in this sample, which is acceptable given the forced-choice format of the items and the heterogeneous content typically included in such scales. Item-level analyses did not suggest any single item unduly reducing the scale’s internal consistency. Prior to conducting correlational analyses, scatterplots were inspected to evaluate linearity and to identify potential outliers that might exert undue influence. The scatterplot of locus of control scores against self-esteem scores showed an overall negative linear trend, with some dispersion around the trend line. A small number of observations fell beyond 3 standard deviations from the mean on one or the other variable. Sensitivity analyses excluding these extreme observations produced results consistent with the full sample, so the primary analyses reported include all cases. The primary correlation analysis between locus of control and self-esteem yielded a Pearson correlation coefficient of r = -0.42. This correlation was statistically significant with p < .001. The 95% confidence interval for the correlation ranged approximately from -0.55 to -0.27, indicating a moderate negative association and reasonable precision in the estimate. A Spearman rank-order correlation produced a similar coefficient (rho = -0.39), confirming the robustness of the association to nonparametric assumptions. The negative correlation indicates that higher external locus of control scores were associated with lower self-esteem scores in this sample. To further examine the relationship while accounting for potential demographic confounds, a hierarchical multiple regression was conducted with self-esteem as the dependent variable. In the first step, locus of control was entered as a predictor and accounted for approximately 17.6% of the variance in self-esteem (R^2 = .176, F(1,130) = 27.9, p < .001). In the second step, age and gender were added as covariates. The addition of these demographic variables resulted in a small and non-significant increase in explained variance (ΔR^2 = .012, p = .18), indicating that the association between locus of control and self-esteem was not meaningfully explained away by age or gender differences. In the final model, locus of control remained a significant predictor of self-esteem (standardized beta = -0.41, p < .001). One-sample t-tests were conducted to compare sample means to the normative reference values selected a priori. For the self-esteem scale, the sample mean of 18.5 was compared to the normative reference value of 20.0. The one-sample t-test indicated that the sample mean was significantly lower than the reference norm (t(131) = -3.43, p = .001). The effect size for this difference was Cohen’s d = -0.35, indicating a small-to-moderate difference below the normative mean. For the locus of control scale, the sample mean of 14.8 was compared to the normative reference value of 12.0. The one-sample t-test indicated that the sample mean was significantly higher than the reference norm (t(131) = 8.25, p < .001). The effect size for this comparison was Cohen’s d = 1.00, indicating a large difference in the direction of greater externality relative to the normative value. These results suggest that the Russell Group student sample was characterized by slightly lower global self-esteem and substantially higher external locus of control relative to the selected normative benchmarks. Exploratory group comparisons by gender revealed small differences. Women had a mean self-esteem score of 18.2 (SD = 5.0) while men had a mean self-esteem score of 19.0 (SD = 4.7). This gender difference was not statistically significant (t(129) = -1.01, p = .31). For locus of control, women had a mean score of 15.0 (SD = 3.8) and men had a mean score of 14.6 (SD = 3.6), which was also not a significant difference (t(129) = 0.59, p = .56). Given the nonsignificant gender differences, the principal findings regarding the overall association between locus of control and self-esteem appear robust across genders in this sample. Additional exploratory analyses categorized participants into three groups based on self-esteem (low, medium, high) using conventional cutoffs. Analysis of variance indicated that mean locus of control scores differed across these groups (F(2,129) = 15.7, p < .001). Post hoc comparisons showed that the low self-esteem group had significantly higher locus of control scores than the medium and high self-esteem groups, consistent with the correlation findings. Effect size estimates for these group differences were in the moderate range. Assumption checks for the t-tests and regression analyses did not reveal serious violations of homoscedasticity or linearity. Residual plots from the regression model appeared randomly distributed, suggesting an adequate fit of the linear model. Taken together, the results provide consistent evidence of a moderate negative association between externality on the locus of control scale and global self-esteem among Russell Group university students. The sample also differed from normative reference values in the direction of greater external control beliefs and somewhat lower self-esteem.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between locus of control and self-esteem in a sample of students from Russell Group universities and evaluated whether scores on each construct differed from commonly cited normative values. The primary finding was a statistically significant and moderate negative association between locus of control and self-esteem, such that higher external locus of control scores were associated with lower self-esteem. This finding was robust to nonparametric checks and persisted after accounting for age and gender. The secondary finding was that the sample mean for self-esteem was modestly lower than the reference normative value, while the sample mean for locus of control was substantially higher than the normative reference. These combined findings offer several theoretical and practical implications. The negative association between external locus of control and lower self-esteem is consistent with theoretical accounts that link perceived lack of control to diminished self-worth. From a theoretical perspective, the sense that outcomes are determined by external forces can undermine the perception that one’s actions are effective and meaningful. Over time, such perceptions may erode global evaluations of the self. The moderate magnitude of the association suggests that locus of control represents an important correlate of self-esteem but not the sole determinant. That is, while beliefs about control are related to global self-regard, other factors such as social feedback, personality traits, and situational successes and failures also contribute appreciably to self-esteem. The robustness of the association across genders in this sample suggests that the underlying psychological processes linking control beliefs and self-regard operate broadly in young adult student populations. Importantly, the finding that the sample displayed higher externality than the normative reference is notable and warrants careful interpretation. One possible explanation is that the elite academic environment itself engenders perceptions of external constraints. Students in highly competitive universities may experience heightened workload, institutional demands, and reliance on external evaluations (for example, grading by external examiners), all of which could foster an increased sense that outcomes are determined by forces beyond individual control. Alternatively, selective admission processes may attract individuals whose prior experiences and background have shaped more external attributional styles, though this explanation seems less likely given the expectation that selective processes often favor candidates with demonstrated agency. Another possibility is that the normative references used for comparison may not perfectly match the demographic characteristics of the present sample, and differences could in part reflect such sampling variation. Nonetheless, the large effect size observed for the locus of control difference suggests a substantive deviation from the reference benchmark that merits further investigation. The modestly lower self-esteem observed relative to the normative reference might be related to the same environmental pressures hypothesized to account for elevated externality. High-achieving academic environments can generate persistent social comparison, imposter feelings, and stress that may erode global self-worth despite objective successes. The combination of increased externality and lower self-esteem in this sample could thus reflect a profile in which students attribute outcomes to external forces and experience diminished confidence in their own abilities. This profile may have practical consequences for academic persistence, help-seeking behavior, and mental health. For example, students who perceive limited control over outcomes and who also hold lower self-esteem may be less likely to seek academic support proactively or to engage in adaptive coping following setbacks. They may also be more vulnerable to stress-related decline in well-being. These practical implications suggest potential avenues for targeted interventions within university settings. Interventions aimed at enhancing perceived control, such as training in study skills, time management, and problem-solving, could help shift attributional patterns toward greater internality. Complementary interventions aimed at building self-esteem, such as strengths-based feedback and cognitive restructuring to challenge self-critical beliefs, may be particularly beneficial if implemented in tandem. Such programs could be incorporated into student orientation activities or counseling services, with attention to the specific demands of elite academic environments. The cross-sectional design of the current study precludes causal inference about the directionality of the association between locus of control and self-esteem. Although theoretical accounts suggest that external control beliefs can lead to reduced self-esteem, it is also plausible that lower baseline self-esteem predisposes individuals to perceive events as beyond their control. It is further plausible that the relationship is reciprocal and dynamic over time. Longitudinal research that tracks students across their university careers would be useful for elucidating the temporal ordering and causal mechanisms underlying the observed association. Future longitudinal studies could examine whether changes in perceived control predict changes in self-esteem or vice versa, and whether interventions that manipulate control beliefs lead to corresponding shifts in self-esteem. Another limitation is that both constructs were measured using self-report instruments, which can be susceptible to response biases including social desirability and mood-congruent reporting. Although the measures used have established psychometric properties, incorporating multimethod assessments in future research—such as behavioral measures of control-related decision making or informant reports of self-esteem—could strengthen causal inference and reduce method variance. The sample, while focused on an academically selective population, was drawn from a limited number of institutions and may not fully represent the diversity of students within the Russell Group. In particular, the sample included more women than men and may have underrepresented certain academic disciplines. Replication in larger and more diverse elite student samples would help establish the robustness and generalizability of the findings. There is also the question of cultural context. The current study was conducted within a specific national higher education system, and cultural factors influence both attributional styles and self-esteem. Cross-cultural research comparing similar student populations across countries would be valuable to determine whether the observed pattern of externality and lower self-esteem is specific to this context or reflects a broader phenomenon in elite academic environments internationally. The psychometric properties observed in this sample were generally acceptable, with high internal consistency for the self-esteem measure and acceptable consistency for the locus of control inventory. Nonetheless, forced-choice formats for locus of control measures can limit variance and affect reliability. Future research might consider using alternative locus of control instruments that use Likert-type response formats to facilitate comparison and potentially yield higher internal consistency. From a theoretical standpoint, the present findings underscore the importance of integrating attributional constructs and self-concept in models of student functioning. The moderate negative correlation between externality and self-esteem suggests that interventions or educational practices that promote a sense of agency could have beneficial effects on students’ global self-evaluations. Application of attributional retraining paradigms, which encourage individuals to attribute outcomes to controllable factors when appropriate, might be a promising avenue for enhancing both academic persistence and self-esteem. It would also be informative to examine mediators that link locus of control to self-esteem. For example, academic feedback, mastery experiences, and perceived competence in specific domains may mediate the relationship between control beliefs and overall self-worth. Testing mediation models in longitudinal datasets would provide insight into the processes through which control beliefs influence self-esteem and downstream outcomes. Another fertile area for future research is the exploration of moderators that may buffer or amplify the association between locus of control and self-esteem. Social support, mentoring relationships, and institutional resources may buffer the impact of external control beliefs on self-esteem. Conversely, high-stakes evaluative contexts, such as rigorous grading systems, may exacerbate the negative association. Identifying such moderators could inform targeted interventions and help universities prioritize resources for students most at risk. The findings also raise questions about the role of selection versus socialization in elite academic environments. Longitudinal studies that begin prior to university entry could disentangle whether students arrive with preexisting externality and lower self-esteem or whether these characteristics develop or intensify during their time at university. Such research could also examine the contribution of high school experiences, socioeconomic background, and early life stressors to the development of control beliefs and self-esteem. In practical terms, university administrators and student support services may consider routine assessment of control beliefs and self-esteem as part of wellbeing monitoring. Early identification of students with high externality and low self-esteem could enable timely provision of supportive interventions. Importantly, any such monitoring should be implemented in a manner that preserves student confidentiality and autonomy, and that is sensitive to cultural and individual differences in self-presentation. The current study’s findings also suggest potential implications for academic advising and pedagogy. Instructors can promote students’ sense of agency by providing clear, actionable feedback, opportunities for mastery experiences, and scaffolding that helps students link effortful study strategies to outcomes. Such pedagogical approaches may not only improve learning but also bolster students’ perceived control and self-esteem. Finally, future research should examine whether the observed relationship between locus of control and self-esteem translates into differences in objective academic outcomes, mental health indicators, and adaptive coping behaviors. For example, do students with higher externality and lower self-esteem demonstrate lower persistence, greater withdrawal from challenging courses, or higher rates of anxiety and depressive symptoms? Integrating self-report, behavioral, and administrative outcome data would enable a richer understanding of the practical significance of the constructs studied here. In summary, this study provides evidence that, among Russell Group university students, greater external locus of control is associated with lower self-esteem, and that students in this context exhibit higher externality and modestly lower self-esteem compared with selected normative benchmarks. These findings highlight the potential role of perceived control in shaping global self-regard and point to opportunities for intervention through educational practices, skills training, and counseling services. Further research using longitudinal designs, multimethod measurement, and diverse samples is needed to clarify causal mechanisms and to translate these insights into effective support strategies for students in high-pressure academic environments.
