Introduction

Color is a pervasive element of human environments and it can influence perception, emotion, and behavior in subtle and overt ways. Humans learn to associate particular colors with objects, events, and meanings over the course of development, and these learned associations can shape cognitive processes. In addition to cultural learning, there are hypotheses that some color responses may be rooted in evolved sensitivities to environmental cues, such as the association of red with danger or blood and green with growth and safety. Empirical work in cognitive and social psychology has increasingly explored how color cues, presented incidentally, might influence performance on tasks that require cognitive resources. One strand of this research has focused specifically on the consequences of perceiving particular hues for achievement-related outcomes, such as performance on tests of reasoning, memory, and arithmetic. A consistent pattern reported in a number of investigations is that exposure to red impairs performance on certain types of cognitive tasks when the color is made salient just prior to task performance. This body of work has led to theoretical accounts that invoke learned associations between red and failure or threat, which may induce a prevention-focused cognitive set and narrow attention, thereby reducing performance on tasks requiring analytic processing. At the same time, the literature on color effects is heterogeneous, and not all studies have reported consistent or large effects, which suggests that boundary conditions and methodological factors matter. For instance, task characteristics such as difficulty level, the stakes associated with performance, and the type of cognitive process required may moderate whether color has an observable effect. Some researchers have argued that color influences operate more reliably in evaluative contexts or where feedback about correctness is implied, whereas others contend that the perceptual salience and proximity of color to task stimuli are crucial. Another consideration in this field is that different colors are associated with different affective and semantic connotations in everyday life. Yellow, for example, is commonly associated with warmth, optimism, energy, and attention in many Western cultural contexts. These associations could plausibly lead to facilitative effects on cognitive performance, if increased arousal or positive affect broadens attentional scope or enhances motivation. Conversely, some aspects of yellow such as high luminance and its tendency to attract attention might also create distraction or visual fatigue under certain conditions, which could counteract any beneficial associations. Thus, the net effect of perceiving yellow on cognitive performance is theoretically ambiguous and likely dependent on how the color is presented, the task context, and individual differences in sensitivity to color cues. Given the theoretical plausibility of facilitative effects of yellow and the reported detrimental effects of red, a direct empirical comparison among hues and a neutral baseline is warranted. Beyond general expectations about red and yellow, the broader question of whether incidental color cues exert reliable influences on objective performance remains unsettled. Several meta-analytic summaries have suggested that color effects on cognition are typically small and subject to publication bias, and they emphasize the need for larger, preregistered replications using well-controlled manipulations. Critics of early color-effect research have pointed to issues such as unclear manipulations, lack of control over luminance and saturation, and failure to account for participant awareness of the manipulation. The methodological heterogeneity across studies complicates synthesis and indicates the importance of rigorous experimental designs. Another important factor is that many prior studies have used student samples and particular types of tests, leaving open questions about generalizability to other populations and task domains. When studies have manipulated color via background paper, stimuli frames, or ambient lighting, the spatial relation of the color to the task stimuli and the degree to which the color is processed peripherally versus centrally appear to influence outcomes. A further complication is that color associations can vary with culture, language, and individual differences in affective reactivity, which suggests that simple main-effect predictions may not hold universally. Despite these complexities, the potential practical significance of color influences on performance is considerable, given that everyday contexts such as examinations, workplace materials, and interfaces often involve incidental color cues. For example, if certain colors impair exam performance, this could have implications for test design and fairness. Conversely, if some colors modestly enhance performance, these cues could be harnessed to support learning and productivity. Therefore, careful experimental work that compares different hues under controlled conditions can help clarify whether and when color cues matter for math performance. The present study was motivated by the need to extend research beyond the frequently studied red hue and to examine yellow, a color with largely positive everyday associations, as a possible facilitator of performance. We opted for a between-subjects design to avoid carry-over effects that might occur if participants encountered multiple color conditions sequentially. We included a white paper control condition to provide a neutral baseline against which to compare red and yellow. Our primary outcome was the number of correct responses on a mathematical task, chosen because mathematical problem-solving is a well-established domain for assessing analytic cognitive performance and because prior work on color effects has often used achievement-style tasks. We operationalized the color manipulation by having participants complete identical math items printed on paper of the respective colors. We were guided by the hypothesis that perceiving yellow, relative to a neutral white baseline, would be associated with better performance due to positive affective associations and possible increases in approach motivation. We also hypothesized, in line with prior research, that perceiving red would be associated with poorer performance relative to the neutral baseline due to associations with caution and threat and the potential to induce a prevention-focused cognitive orientation. In addition to these directional hypotheses, we recognized that the effect sizes might be small and that a null outcome would nonetheless be informative for refining theoretical accounts. Accordingly, we designed the study to include checks for potential confounds, such as group differences in baseline math ability and demographic characteristics, and to assess whether the color manipulation produced changes in self-reported mood or task-related anxiety as exploratory mediators. We also planned to inspect assumptions of statistical tests and to conduct sensitivity analyses that could quantify evidence for the absence of an effect if results were non-significant. Our approach was therefore intended to provide a clear test of whether simple exposure to colored paper in a low-stakes math task affects objective performance in a sample of convenience. In doing so, we aimed both to attempt a replication of the red effect and to extend the literature by testing yellow, thereby contributing to a more nuanced understanding of color-performance relations.

Methods

Participants and recruitment

Ninety undergraduate students participated in the study. Participants were recruited from introductory psychology courses and through campus flyers and were offered course credit or a small monetary compensation for participation. All participants were between 18 and 30 years of age. We screened participants to ensure normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of color vision deficiencies, relying on self-report during the intake. Participants who reported color blindness or severe visual impairments were excluded from participation. The final sample included 90 participants after exclusions for noncompliance or incomplete data, with 30 participants assigned to each experimental condition. Random assignment to the three conditions was implemented using a computerized random number generator prior to the start of the data collection period. The study was approved by the institutional review board and all participants provided informed consent before participation. Demographic information collected included age, self-reported gender, and year in college. There were no significant differences across the three groups in terms of age or gender distribution based on preliminary checks after assignment. The study was conducted in a single testing room under standardized lighting conditions to limit variability in ambient illumination across participants. The room lighting was fluorescent and set to the same level for all sessions during the data collection window.

Materials

The primary cognitive task was a set of 20 arithmetic and algebra items designed to require moderate analytic processing without being excessively difficult. Items included a mixture of basic arithmetic computations, simple algebraic manipulations, and numerical reasoning questions, and were presented in a fixed order for all participants. The items were pilot-tested on a separate sample to ensure that the average accuracy was neither near ceiling nor floor, with a target mean accuracy of approximately 60–70% under neutral conditions. The stimuli were printed in black ink using a standard serif font at a legible size on paper of one of three color conditions: red, yellow, or white. We used commercially available colored paper stock for the manipulations and selected shades that were commonly used in offices and classrooms; the red paper was a standard medium red, the yellow paper was a medium warm yellow, and the white paper was standard office white. Prior to the main study, we measured approximate surface luminance of each paper using a handheld photometer to assess gross differences. The white paper had the highest measured luminance, the yellow paper was slightly lower, and the red paper had the lowest luminance. Because we used commercially available paper, exact matching of luminance across conditions was not achieved. In addition to the cognitive items, we included a brief self-report mood questionnaire administered after the task. This questionnaire consisted of a few items assessing current mood valence and arousal on Likert scales, as well as two items assessing task-related anxiety and motivation. These measures were intended as exploratory checks to determine whether the color manipulation produced subjective effects that might mediate any performance differences. Finally, participants completed a brief demographic form and a manipulation check item at the end of the session in which they were asked whether they noticed the paper color and whether they believed it influenced their performance.

Procedure

Participants arrived at the testing room in small cohorts but were tested individually to prevent contamination. After informed consent was obtained, participants were seated at a desk with the experimenter present to provide standardized instructions. The experimenter provided written instructions that were identical across conditions, explaining that participants would complete a series of math problems and that accuracy was important. Participants were told that they had 20 minutes to complete the set of 20 items, which allowed for slightly more than one minute per item. No feedback was provided on item correctness during the task. Each participant received a packet corresponding to their assigned condition; the packet included the math items printed on the appropriate paper color along with the answer sheet. Participants completed the math items within the allotted time and then turned in the materials to the experimenter. Immediately after completing the math items, participants completed the brief mood and anxiety questionnaire and the manipulation check. The experimenter debriefed participants at the end of the session and provided course credit or payment. The entire session lasted approximately 25 minutes per participant.

Scoring and outcome variables

The primary dependent variable was the total number of correct answers out of 20 on the math task. Responses were scored dichotomously and summed to yield an overall accuracy score for each participant. Secondary variables included self-reported mood valence, arousal, and task-related anxiety, each on a 1–7 Likert scale. The manipulation check responses were coded dichotomously for whether participants noticed the paper color and whether they believed it influenced their performance.

Statistical analysis plan

We planned to conduct a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test for differences in mean accuracy across the three color conditions. Prior to running the ANOVA, we inspected the distributions of scores for normality and tested for homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. If assumptions were violated, we had planned to use nonparametric alternatives. Planned pairwise comparisons were defined a priori to test yellow versus white, red versus white, and yellow versus red, with Holm-Bonferroni corrections applied to control for multiple comparisons. We reported effect sizes for the omnibus test (partial eta-squared) and for pairwise comparisons (Cohen’s d computed from pooled standard deviations). We also conducted exploratory analyses to examine whether mood or anxiety ratings differed by condition and whether these measures correlated with task performance, which could suggest mediation. Finally, because the literature contains reports of small effect sizes, we planned to conduct a post hoc sensitivity analysis to estimate the minimum effect size detectable with the collected sample at 80% power and to consider the implications of the observed effect sizes.

Results

Preliminary checks and descriptive statistics

All 90 participants completed the study and provided usable data. There were 30 participants in each of the red, yellow, and white conditions. The mean age for the sample was 19.8 years (SD = 1.7). The sample consisted of a majority of women; across the three groups the gender distribution did not differ significantly. Mean scores on the math task for each group were computed. Participants in the white condition had a mean of 12.8 correct answers (SD = 3.2). Participants in the yellow condition had a mean of 13.1 correct answers (SD = 3.0). Participants in the red condition had a mean of 12.4 correct answers (SD = 3.5). These descriptive means suggested only minor differences between conditions. The distributions of scores for each group were inspected visually using histograms and quantile-quantile plots. There were no extreme outliers in any condition, and skewness values were modest and within acceptable bounds for parametric testing. A Shapiro-Wilk test for normality applied to the residuals from the ANOVA model did not indicate a significant departure from normality. Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances across the three groups returned a non-significant result, indicating that the assumption of equal variances was tenable for the ANOVA.

Primary analyses: ANOVA and pairwise comparisons

We conducted a one-way ANOVA to test for differences in mean accuracy across the three color conditions. The omnibus test did not approach statistical significance, F(2,87) = 0.45, p = .64. The partial eta-squared was .01, indicating a very small proportion of variance in task scores accounted for by condition. Given the non-significant omnibus result and the small effect size estimate, pairwise comparisons were nevertheless examined to provide descriptive contrasts. The mean difference between yellow and white was 0.3 correct answers in favor of yellow, with a pooled standard deviation of 3.1, yielding Cohen’s d = 0.10. The uncorrected t-test for this contrast was t(58) = 0.36, p = .72, and after Holm-Bonferroni correction it remained non-significant. The mean difference between red and white was -0.4 correct answers in favor of white, with Cohen’s d = -0.13, t(58) = -0.50, p = .62. The contrast between yellow and red showed a mean difference of 0.7 correct answers in favor of yellow, Cohen’s d = 0.23, t(58) = 1.12, p = .27. None of the pairwise contrasts reached statistical significance. Confidence intervals for the pairwise mean differences were wide relative to the small point estimates; for example, the 95% confidence interval for the yellow versus white contrast ranged from approximately -1.6 to 2.2 correct answers, indicating uncertainty about small positive or negative effects.

Bayesian and sensitivity analyses

Given the increasing emphasis on assessing evidence for the null, we conducted a Bayesian ANOVA comparing models with and without the condition factor. The Bayes factor comparing the null model to the model including condition was approximately 4.0, indicating that the data were about four times more likely under the null model than under the alternative model with condition effects. This Bayes factor provides moderate evidence in support of no effect of paper color on math performance in this sample and under these experimental conditions. We also conducted a post hoc sensitivity analysis to determine the smallest effect size that our sample could detect with reasonable power. For an omnibus ANOVA with three groups, alpha set at .05, and power at .80, the minimum detectable effect size f was approximately .25, which corresponds to a medium effect size and translates to a Cohen’s d of roughly .50 for pairwise contrasts. Our observed pairwise effects were much smaller than this threshold; hence, the study was underpowered to detect small effects (for example, d = .20) with high confidence.

Exploratory analyses of mood and manipulation checks

We examined the self-reported mood and anxiety ratings collected immediately after the task to check for any subjective effects of color. Mean mood valence scores were similar across conditions and did not differ significantly by ANOVA, F(2,87) = 0.63, p = .54. Arousal ratings also did not differ across groups, F(2,87) = 0.97, p = .38. Task-related anxiety scores were low overall and did not differ by condition, F(2,87) = 0.82, p = .44. Correlations between these subjective measures and math performance were small and non-significant; for instance, the correlation between valence and accuracy was r = .08, p = .45. The manipulation check indicated that most participants noticed the paper color (approximately 85% across groups). However, only a small proportion of those who noticed the color reported believing that it had influenced their performance (around 10%), and this belief was not associated with actual performance. In short, the color manipulation was perceptible to participants, but it did not appear to induce substantial changes in self-reported mood or task anxiety that might explain differences (or lack thereof) in performance.

Checks for potential moderators

We explored whether gender moderated any condition effects by including a gender-by-condition interaction term in a 2 x 3 ANOVA model. The interaction was non-significant, F(2,84) = 0.21, p = .81, and effect size estimates were negligible. Likewise, age and year in college did not meaningfully correlate with scores or interact with condition. An inspection of item-level performance across the 20 problems did not reveal systematic patterns suggesting that certain types of items were differentially affected by color; performance trends across items were parallel across conditions.

Summary of results

Overall, participants’ accuracy on the mathematical task did not differ meaningfully across the red, yellow, and white paper conditions. The omnibus ANOVA was non-significant and effect size estimates were small. Bayesian analyses favored the null model, and exploratory measures of mood and anxiety did not differ by condition or predict performance. These findings indicate that, under the conditions of this study, perceiving yellow did not enhance mathematical performance relative to a neutral baseline, and perceiving red did not impair performance in a reliable way.

Discussion

The present study set out to investigate whether incidental exposure to colored paper would influence performance on a mathematical task, with specific hypotheses that yellow would facilitate performance and red would impair it relative to a white control. In contrast to these hypotheses, the results provided no evidence that paper color affected the number of correct answers participants produced. Descriptive differences among the three conditions were small and non-significant, the omnibus test did not indicate a color effect, and Bayesian analysis provided moderate support for the null. Also, self-reported mood and task-related anxiety did not differ across conditions and were not associated with performance. Taken together, the findings suggest that a simple manipulation of paper color in a low-stakes testing environment does not have a detectable impact on math performance in a typical undergraduate sample. These null results contribute to an emergent picture in the literature in which color effects on cognition are not uniformly observed and appear contingent on methodological and contextual factors. One important implication is that the effects reported in prior research on red may not generalize across all manipulations and task contexts. The theoretical account that associates red with threat and prevention-focused cognitive states remains plausible, but the causal chain from color exposure to altered cognition likely requires particular contextual cues to be activated. For example, red may produce stronger effects when it is associated with evaluative feedback or when it appears in contexts that signal assessment, failure, or social judgment. If red appears only as part of incidental background material with no evaluative connotation, it may be too weak a prime to induce measurable changes in analytic performance. Likewise, the anticipated facilitative effect of yellow, presumed to operate through positive affect or heightened approach motivation, may be subtle and easily swamped by other task demands or by individual differences. The present study’s null finding for yellow implies that merely presenting math problems on yellow paper does not reliably induce a sufficient positive affective shift to influence performance on a timed arithmetic task. This outcome aligns with theoretical positions that prioritize task relevance and motivational framing over incidental perceptual cues as determinants of performance. The absence of mood differences across conditions in our data supports the notion that the color manipulation did not generate substantial affective changes. It is also notable that most participants noticed the paper color, yet relatively few believed it had influenced their performance, and this subjective belief was unrelated to actual performance. This pattern suggests that conscious awareness of color is not synonymous with an effect on cognition, and it highlights the potential distinction between attention to a cue and the activation of meaningful associations that alter cognitive processes.

Methodological reasons for the null findings deserve careful consideration. First, the color manipulation used commercially available colored paper, and although we measured surface luminance roughly, we did not exactly match luminance and saturation across conditions. This imbalance could produce competing effects; for example, the lower luminance of the red paper might reduce strain and thus counteract any negative association effects, or the higher luminance of the white paper could have inadvertently provided an advantage. Second, the spatial relation of the color to the task stimuli was limited to the paper background, which may be a relatively weak manipulation compared with ambient lighting, large color fields surrounding stimuli, or salient colored frames that directly highlight correctness cues. Third, the task was low-stakes and consisted of only 20 items; participants may have been insufficiently motivated for color-induced subtle changes in approach or avoidance orientation to affect performance. Fourth, our sample size, although typical for experimental studies in this domain, lacked power to detect small effect sizes that are plausible for incidental color cues; our post hoc sensitivity analysis showed that only medium-sized effects could be detected with reasonable power in the present design. Consequently, it remains possible that color exerts small effects on math performance that our study was not equipped to detect.

Beyond methodological limitations, theoretical considerations suggest additional reasons why effects may be absent or inconsistent. The color-in-context framework emphasizes that color effects depend on the interaction between color cues and contextual meanings. In our study, the context was neutral and did not invoke evaluative feedback or social comparison, which may have been necessary to activate the negative associations of red. Moreover, cultural and individual differences in color associations could attenuate average effects in a heterogeneous sample; while yellow is often associated with positivity in Western contexts, this association is not universal, and individual experiences could moderate responses. Personality factors such as trait anxiety, sensitivity to punishment or reward, and baseline affective state may also moderate responsiveness to color, yet we did not measure these systematically. A further theoretical point concerns the mechanisms by which color might influence cognition; if the pathway involves attentional narrowing or broadening, then task features that test attentional scope directly might be more sensitive to color manipulations than the arithmetic task we used. In other words, color might affect certain cognitive processes (for example, creative divergent thinking or global-local attention) more reliably than analytical calculation.

The present findings thus indicate both constraints on the generality of color effects and directions for future work. First, future studies should employ more potent manipulations of color salience, such as ambient lighting changes, full-screen color presentations, or larger colored stimuli that are central rather than peripheral to the task. These manipulations could be combined with careful control of luminance and chromaticity to ensure that the hue, rather than brightness, drives any observed effects. Second, researchers should sample larger and more diverse participant pools to increase statistical power and to examine moderating variables such as culture, personality, and baseline mood. Third, tasks that vary in stakes and in cognitive process demands should be used to map which task domains are most susceptible to color influences; for example, alternating between evaluative test contexts and exploratory, low-evaluation tasks could reveal context-dependent effects. Fourth, mechanistic measures such as eye-tracking, physiological arousal (heart rate, skin conductance), and process-tracing methods could help to elucidate whether color manipulations alter attention, arousal, or strategy use. Fifth, preregistration and multi-lab replications would strengthen inferences and address concerns about publication bias and underpowered findings in this field. Sixth, experimental designs that manipulate color within subjects in counterbalanced ways, while carefully controlling for carry-over effects, could increase sensitivity to detect subtle within-person changes.

Our study also raises practical considerations for educators and test designers. Given the null findings, there is no immediate basis for recommending altering paper color to improve math test outcomes in low-stakes contexts. However, given the possibility of context-dependent effects, it would be premature to conclude that color is never relevant for assessment settings, especially those involving explicit evaluation or high stakes. Test administrators might therefore prioritize ensuring that color choices for examination materials do not introduce inadvertent variability; for instance, avoiding colors that are highly salient or that might be differentially perceived by examinees with visual impairments remains prudent.

Several specific follow-up experiments could build on the present work. One useful study would manipulate color both as background paper and as ambient lighting in a factorial design to probe whether central versus peripheral presentation of color cues interacts to produce effects. Another experiment could introduce evaluative framing, such as telling participants their performance would be compared to peers or that scores would be used for selection, to test whether red’s purported negative effects emerge under evaluation. A third approach would measure immediate attentional effects using visual search or global-local tasks while presenting color cues, to determine whether color reliably narrows or broadens attention in a way that could plausibly affect complex problem solving. Finally, a large-scale, preregistered multi-site replication testing both red and yellow effects under high control for luminance and saturation would be valuable for settling whether small but reliable effects exist.

In interpreting the present null results, it is important to emphasize that null findings are informative. They highlight the boundaries of prior claims and encourage refinement of theoretical models. The absence of an effect of paper color on math performance in our study suggests that any influence of color is neither robust across all implementations nor sufficiently large to be detected in modest samples using subtle manipulations. This outcome should not be construed as definitive evidence that color never matters, but rather as evidence that the relationship is conditional and that careful attention to context, manipulation strength, and statistical power is essential. In conclusion, while color remains an intriguing factor that may sometimes influence cognition and behavior, the present study suggests that simple exposure to yellow or red paper does not alter math performance in a low-stakes undergraduate testing context. Future research that systematically varies context, manipulation strength, and sample characteristics will be necessary to delineate when and how colors like red and yellow can be expected to influence cognitive performance.
