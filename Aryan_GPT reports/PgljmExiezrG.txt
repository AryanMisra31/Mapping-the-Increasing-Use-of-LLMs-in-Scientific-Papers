Introduction

Empathy is a complex and multi-faceted psychological construct that has been the subject of extensive theoretical and empirical investigation. Empathy broadly refers to the ability to understand and share the feelings of others and is implicated in social functioning, prosocial behavior, and interpersonal relationships. Many researchers have sought to identify activities and experiences that can enhance empathy, both because of the theoretical interest in its malleability and because of potential applied benefits for education, clinical practice, and social policy. One body of literature has argued that engagement with fictional narratives, such as novels and stories, can increase empathy. The proposed mechanism in that literature is that immersive narrative experiences promote perspective-taking and emotional transportation into the lives of characters, which in turn strengthens empathic responding to others. Several studies using self-report and experimental designs have found positive correlations or causal effects between reading fiction and measures of trait empathy or short-term empathic response. These findings have been taken to suggest that fiction can function as a form of social simulation that provides practice in imagining mental states and emotional responses of people different from oneself.

Despite a growing consensus about reading fiction, comparatively less attention has been paid to other modes of engaging with fictional narratives, such as viewing fiction via film, television, or streamed drama. While film and television contain many of the same narrative elements that appear in printed fiction, such as character arcs, emotional conflict, and opportunities for identification, the media differ in important cognitive and experiential dimensions. Viewing media is often temporally paced by the medium and guided by audiovisual cues, while reading is a more active and generative process that relies on mental imagery and inner speech to construct characters and scenes. These modality differences raise the empirical question of whether exposure to fictional narratives through viewing produces effects on empathy similar to those observed for reading. It is plausible that viewing could be as effective as reading because it still exposes viewers to a character’s subjective experience. It is also plausible that viewing could be less effective because the sensory richness of the medium may reduce the need for active imaginative construction, or because viewing often occurs in contexts that promote multitasking and attenuated engagement.

Another dimension that is relevant to the association between fiction and empathy is gender. Across a large number of studies, women have generally been found to score higher than men on self-reported empathy measures. Several explanations have been offered for this gender difference, including socialization that encourages greater interpersonal attention in women, as well as potential differences in emotion recognition and expression. In the context of fiction, it has also been suggested that women may consume more fiction than men on average, particularly literary and character-driven fiction, and that this pattern of consumption could help explain part of the empathy difference. If women both read or view more fiction and have higher trait empathy, then gender may function as a confound in observational studies that link fictional exposure and empathic dispositions. Alternatively, gender may interact with media mode so that the empathy effects of various narrative media differ by sex.

The existing literature, however, leaves several gaps. First, most of the evidence linking fiction to empathy derives from studies of reading rather than viewing. The assumption that viewing will have equivalent effects has not been thoroughly tested. Second, much of the reading fiction literature relies on correlational designs or brief laboratory manipulations; there is a need for converging evidence using diverse samples and measures of media exposure. Third, gender differences have been treated as covariates in many studies rather than as potential moderators, leaving open the question of whether the association between fiction and empathy differs by gender.

Given these theoretical and empirical considerations, the present study was designed to investigate the association between fictional viewing frequency and trait empathy in a community sample, and to examine whether this association differs by gender. The study also sought to replicate the robust finding that women score higher on trait empathy than men. The primary rationale was to test whether the positive correlations reported for reading extend to viewing. If narrative engagement operates similarly across media, then greater exposure to fiction via viewing should be associated with higher trait empathy. Alternatively, if viewing and reading differ in how they engage imaginative processes, then viewing might show a weaker or nonexistent association with empathy. Moreover, gender could further shape the relation. For instance, if men and women differ in typical viewing styles, genre preferences, or levels of transportation when watching, then the viewing-empathy association might be moderated by gender.

To address these questions, the study measured self-reported empathy using a widely used trait instrument and obtained a quantitative measure of fictional viewing in the past fortnight. The past fortnight was chosen to balance recall accuracy with capturing recent behavior. Fictional viewing per fortnight was operationalized as the total number of hours that participants reported spending watching fiction across television, film, and streaming platforms during the prior two-week period. The decision to use hours rather than frequency of sessions was based on the desire to capture both the duration and intensity of exposure. Gender was collected as a self-reported binary variable in order to examine group differences consistent with prior literature. The participant sample was recruited from a broad adult population to maximize variability in both media use and empathy.

Analytic choices were pre-specified: the primary analysis was a Pearson correlation between total fictional viewing hours per fortnight and trait empathy across the whole sample. Secondary analyses included independent-samples t-tests comparing empathy scores between men and women and comparing viewing hours between men and women, as well as separate correlations within each gender. Exploratory analyses included multiple regression to test whether interaction effects between viewing and gender predicted empathy, and robustness checks such as nonparametric correlations, control for age, and inspection of influential cases.

It is important to note that the observational design allows only for the identification of associations and not causal effects. Nevertheless, observational associations can be informative about whether the patterns observed for reading generalize to viewing, and about whether gender moderates these relationships. The present investigation therefore set out to provide an initial empirical test of the viewing-empathy relationship, with an eye toward informing subsequent experimental or longitudinal studies.

The main hypothesis registered prior to analysis was that there would be a positive relationship between fictional viewing per fortnight and trait empathy, mirroring the positive associations reported in reading fiction studies. A secondary hypothesis was that women would score higher on trait empathy than men, consistent with prior epidemiological and psychological findings. Given uncertainty about the media-modality question, no directional hypothesis was specified for differences between genders in the viewing-empathy association, though the study planned to explore potential gender-specific associations.

Finally, several potential confounds and alternative explanations were considered. Age can correlate with both media consumption habits and empathy, with younger adults possibly consuming more visual media and older adults showing different empathic profiles. Socioeconomic status, education, and cultural background can also influence media habits and empathic dispositions. The study, therefore, included basic demographic data to enable covariate analyses and robustness checks. Measurement limitations inherent in self-reports were acknowledged in advance, and the discussion section was planned to address them in detail.

Methods

Participants were 200 adults recruited through a combination of online advertisements, community postings, and a university research participation pool. The recruitment materials invited adults aged 18 and older to participate in a short online questionnaire about media use and social cognition. The final sample comprised 200 participants after quality checks and removal of incomplete responses; 120 identified as women and 80 identified as men. The sample age ranged from 18 to 65 years with a mean age of 29.3 years and a standard deviation of 9.8 years. Participants were predominantly English-speaking and represented a range of educational backgrounds. The sample was heterogeneous but not strictly representative of any particular population; convenience sampling was used to ensure a sufficiently large sample for correlational analyses.

Ethics approval for the study was obtained from the host institution’s human research ethics committee. All participants provided informed consent via an online consent form before beginning the questionnaire. Participants were informed that their responses would be anonymous, that they could withdraw at any time without penalty, and that the study was for research purposes only. To encourage thoughtful responses, attention checks were embedded in the survey, and participants who failed two or more attention checks were excluded from the final dataset.

Measures included a self-report trait empathy measure and a fictional viewing measure. Trait empathy was measured with the Empathy Quotient (EQ), a standard instrument consisting of 40 items that assess cognitive empathy, affective responses, and social skills, along with 20 filler items, producing scores ranging approximately from 0 to 80. Items were answered on a 4-point response scale and summed according to the scale’s scoring rules. The scale has been widely used in prior personality and clinical research and is well suited for capturing inter-individual differences in empathy. In this sample the EQ showed acceptable internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha of .88.

Fictional viewing per fortnight was measured with a brief media diary question that asked participants to estimate the total time, in hours, they had spent watching fictional content during the previous two weeks. The prompt provided examples of fictional content including film, television dramas, narrative web series, and scripted streaming content, and explicitly excluded documentary, news, sports, and non-narrative content. Participants entered a numeric value; very large values were flagged and followed up with a confirmation question to reduce the impact of entry errors. The distribution of viewing hours was inspected for extreme skewness; values above three standard deviations were examined, and one extreme outlier was winsorized to the 99th percentile value to reduce undue influence on parametric tests. The final variable used in analyses was fictional viewing hours over the past fortnight.

Demographic variables included age, gender (self-reported as man or woman in this dataset; participants selecting other or prefer not to say were too few to permit group analyses and were therefore not included in gender comparison tests), educational attainment (categorized), and employment status. Participants were also asked whether they had been diagnosed with a mental health condition as a broad health screener; these responses were used only for descriptive purposes and not as exclusionary criteria.

Procedure involved participants completing the survey online via a secure platform. After informed consent, participants completed demographic items followed by the empathy questionnaire and the media use items. The order of empathy and media questions was counterbalanced across participants to reduce order effects; half the sample completed the EQ first and half completed the media items first. The questionnaire required approximately 15 to 20 minutes to complete. Data collection took place over a six-week period and was conducted in English. Responses were anonymized and stored in encrypted form.

Data screening and preparation were conducted prior to hypothesis testing. Missing data were minimal; cases with missing values on core variables (EQ and viewing hours) were excluded. The final analytic sample of 200 participants had complete data on the primary variables. Variable distributions were inspected for normality using histograms, skewness and kurtosis indices, and Shapiro-Wilk tests. The EQ scores were approximately normally distributed. Viewing hours showed a moderate positive skew, so both parametric analyses with the raw hours and supplementary nonparametric analyses using Spearman’s rho were planned.

Statistical analyses were conducted in a standard statistical software environment. The primary test was a Pearson correlation between fictional viewing hours per fortnight and EQ total scores across the whole sample. This analysis was chosen for its direct parallel to previous correlational studies in the fiction-empathy literature. Secondary analyses included independent-samples t-tests comparing mean EQ scores for women and men and comparing mean viewing hours for women and men. Separate Pearson correlations between viewing hours and EQ within the subgroups of women and men were also computed to examine whether the association differed by gender.

Further exploratory analyses included a multiple linear regression in which EQ was regressed on viewing hours (centered), gender (coded as 0 = woman, 1 = man), and the interaction term between viewing and gender. Age was included as a covariate in supplementary regressions to test robustness to potential age confounds. Regression diagnostics were examined including residual plots, variance inflation factors, Cook’s distance, and tests for heteroscedasticity. Nonparametric Spearman correlations were computed as robustness checks given the skew in viewing hours. Where appropriate, effect sizes such as Cohen’s d for group differences and r for correlations were reported. All statistical tests were two-tailed and significance was judged at alpha = .05. Adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied to the set of pre-specified primary and secondary tests but were considered in discussing the pattern of exploratory findings.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are reported first. Across the full sample of 200 participants, the mean Empathy Quotient (EQ) score was 42.1 with a standard deviation of 12.0. For fictional viewing hours per fortnight, the mean was 9.6 hours with a standard deviation of 8.1 hours. Women (n = 120) had a mean EQ score of 45.0 (SD = 11.0), whereas men (n = 80) had a mean EQ score of 38.0 (SD = 12.0). Women reported a mean of 8.4 viewing hours (SD = 7.2) per fortnight, while men reported a mean of 11.8 viewing hours (SD = 8.9) per fortnight. Age had a mean of 29.3 years (SD = 9.8) and showed limited variance related to the primary variables. The distribution of viewing hours showed mild positive skewness (skew = 1.1), prompting supplementary nonparametric analyses.

The primary analysis was a Pearson correlation between fictional viewing hours per fortnight and EQ scores across all participants. The correlation coefficient was r = -0.05, with a p-value of .48. This correlation explained less than 1% of the variance in empathy and was not statistically significant. A scatterplot inspection did not suggest a robust linear association across the full sample. Given the skew in viewing hours, a Spearman rank-order correlation was also computed and yielded rho = -0.04, p = .60, consistent with the non-significant Pearson result.

Independent-samples t-tests were conducted to compare EQ scores between women and men. The t-test indicated a significant difference: t(198) = 4.18, p < .001. The mean difference was 7.0 points on the EQ scale, with women scoring higher than men. The pooled standard deviation was approximately 11.5, yielding a Cohen’s d of 0.61, which is conventionally interpreted as a medium effect size. Thus, the well-established gender difference in self-reported empathy was replicated in this sample.

A t-test comparing fictional viewing hours between women and men was also conducted. Men reported significantly more viewing hours than women: t(198) = 3.02, p = .003. The mean difference was 3.4 hours per fortnight, with men watching more fictional content. Cohen’s d for this difference was approximately 0.43, a small-to-medium effect size.

Given the theoretical interest in whether gender moderated the viewing-empathy association, separate correlations within each gender were computed. For women, the Pearson correlation between viewing hours and EQ was r = 0.05, p = .57, indicating no evidence of an association among women. For men, the correlation was r = -0.29, p = .009, indicating a modest but statistically significant negative association whereby men who reported more fictional viewing in the prior fortnight tended to report lower empathy scores. The correlation for men accounted for approximately 8.4% of the variance in EQ within the male subgroup.

To examine whether the gender difference in the viewing-empathy association was statistically reliable, a multiple regression analysis was conducted in which EQ was regressed on centered viewing hours, gender (0 = woman, 1 = man), and an interaction term between viewing and gender. The overall regression model accounted for R^2 = .072 of variance in EQ, F(3,196) = 5.07, p = .002. Within this model, gender was a significant predictor (β = -0.41 for men relative to women, p < .001), indicating lower EQ scores among men after accounting for viewing. Viewing hours as a main effect was not a significant predictor (β = -0.04, p = .52). Critically, the interaction between viewing hours and gender was statistically significant (β = -0.24, p = .01), supporting the inference that the association between viewing and empathy differed by gender in the sample.

Supplementary regressions that included age as a covariate produced substantively similar results. In these models, age was not a significant predictor of EQ (p = .34), and the viewing-by-gender interaction remained significant (p = .01). Variance inflation factors for the predictors were below 1.5, indicating low multicollinearity. Residual plots suggested approximate homoscedasticity, and Cook’s distance indicated that no single case exerted undue influence on the interaction effect. One extreme viewing hours outlier had been winsorized prior to the main analyses; analyses without the winsorization produced comparable but slightly attenuated results for the male subsample correlation (r = -0.27, p = .02).

Exploratory nonparametric analyses reinforced the parametric findings. Spearman correlations within gender groups yielded rho = 0.04, p = .65 for women, and rho = -0.25, p = .02 for men, again indicating a significant negative association for men but not for women. Partial correlations controlling for age also produced similar patterns: partial r = 0.03, p = .72 for women and partial r = -0.28, p = .01 for men.

Additional exploratory checks considered whether the order of questionnaire presentation affected results. Approximately half the sample completed the EQ first and half completed the media items first. An independent-samples analysis comparing correlation coefficients across order groups revealed no significant differences (Fisher’s z test, p = .84), suggesting that order effects did not account for the pattern of results.

Because the male negative correlation was an unexpected finding relative to the primary hypothesis, further analyses assessed whether educational attainment, employment status, or self-reported mental health diagnoses might account for the association. Including educational attainment and employment status as covariates in the regression did not meaningfully change the viewing-by-gender interaction (interaction p = .012). Similarly, excluding participants who reported a diagnosed mental health condition did not eliminate the male negative correlation (r = -0.27, p = .02), indicating that these factors did not account for the observed association.

To quantify the practical significance of the male-specific finding, the correlation coefficient of r = -0.29 in men corresponds to a Cohen’s d of approximately -0.61 when contrasted with a median split approach, indicating that the effect would be considered in the medium range. Nonetheless, the effect explained only about 8% of variance, suggesting that viewing hours per fortnight is only one of many factors associated with empathy.

Overall, the principal findings were: no significant correlation between fictional viewing hours per fortnight and trait empathy across the entire sample, a robust gender difference in empathy with women scoring higher, men reporting more viewing hours than women on average, and a significant, negative correlation between viewing hours and empathy within the male subsample. These results were robust to several analytic checks including nonparametric tests, covariate adjustment, and outlier handling.

Discussion

The present study tested whether recent fictional viewing was associated with trait empathy and whether this relation differed by gender. The principal prediction, based on prior literature linking reading fiction with empathy, was that greater exposure to fictional narratives via viewing would be positively associated with trait empathy. Contrary to that hypothesis, no significant positive association emerged across the full sample. Instead, the data revealed a notable gender pattern: women scored higher on trait empathy than men, men reported more viewing hours than women, and within men there was a modest but significant negative association between viewing hours and empathy. The regression analysis confirmed that gender moderated the viewing-empathy association. These findings have several implications for theories about narrative engagement, for the generalizability of reading-based findings to viewing, and for future research on media effects and empathy.

First, the replication of a gender difference in trait empathy aligns with a substantial literature indicating higher self-reported empathy among women. This study’s medium-sized effect underscores the reliability of this difference across diverse samples and measurement contexts. The gender difference remained robust after accounting for viewing hours, and it accounted for a meaningful portion of variance in empathy scores in the regression models. It is therefore important in future research to treat gender as a potential moderator rather than merely as a covariate, particularly when examining media exposures that may be patterned by gender.

Second, the lack of a positive correlation between fictional viewing and empathy for the overall sample suggests that the association between narrative exposure and empathic disposition may not generalize straightforwardly from reading to viewing. In the reading literature, active imaginative construction and depth of mental simulation are theorized to mediate empathy-enhancing effects. Reading requires extensive generation of imagery and internal representation, and it typically necessitates more focused attention on textual cues. Viewing, by contrast, provides rich audiovisual information that can constrain imagination and reduce the need for active inferential processing. If active imagination is a key driver of empathy gains, then modalities that reduce imaginative effort could be less effective at promoting trait empathic change. Consequently, the null finding for the full sample may reflect modality-specific differences in cognitive engagement.

Third, the negative association observed among men raises intriguing possibilities about differential media selection, viewing practices, and socialization. One interpretation is that men who report low levels of trait empathy may preferentially seek out fictional media in greater quantities as a form of entertainment that does not require or encourage empathic engagement. This would be a selection effect in which lower empathy leads to greater consumption of certain kinds of media rather than the reverse. Alternatively, greater exposure to particular types or styles of fictional viewing among men — for example, action-oriented or violence-heavy content that emphasizes spectacle over interiority — could be associated with reduced empathic orientation if such content fosters dehumanizing or desensitizing attitudes. A third possibility is that men may be more likely to engage in viewing while multitasking, thereby attenuating narrative transportation and limiting the types of reflective perspective-taking that could strengthen empathy.

It is also possible that the negative association in men is driven by unmeasured third variables. For instance, certain personality traits such as low agreeableness or high sensation-seeking may correlate with both lower empathy and higher consumption of fictional visual media. Social factors including peer norms about media use and emotional expression could likewise shape both media engagement and self-reported empathic capacities. The present study’s cross-sectional design cannot disentangle these possibilities, and thus causation cannot be inferred from the observed associations.

Another consideration is measurement specificity. The measure of viewing captured total hours of fictional content without discriminating genre, narrative complexity, interactivity, or the degree of character-focused content. Fictional viewing hours is therefore a coarse measure that aggregates diverse experiences. It is plausible that certain types of fictional content — such as character-driven dramas with rich interiority and complex social dilemmas — promote empathy more than action-oriented or plot-driven content. If men are more likely to consume genres less conducive to perspective-taking, aggregated viewing hours could mask positive effects of specific content types and yield an overall null or negative association. Future studies should therefore include genre-specific measures and assess narrative features such as character perspective, moral ambiguity, and emotional intensity.

Theoretical work on narrative transportation and identification provides a useful framework for understanding why reading and viewing might differ in their relations to empathy. Transportation theory posits that immersion in a narrative mediates the effect of consumption on empathy and attitude change. If transportation is achieved differently across media — for example, if reading fosters deeper and more sustained transportation for certain individuals — then the pathways from narrative exposure to empathy may be modality dependent. Additionally, identification with characters involves cognitive and affective processes that may be more readily invoked during reading because readers supply their own mental imagery and character voices. Measuring transportation and identification directly would be a valuable addition to future research aimed at clarifying whether and how viewing influences empathic development.

The gender moderation found in this study also invites examination of gendered patterns in narrative engagement. Prior work has suggested that women, on average, prefer fiction that foregrounds internal states and social relationships, while men may prefer narratives emphasizing action and external conflict. These preferences could both reflect and reinforce gendered socialization regarding emotional openness and interpersonal focus. If empathy is cultivated most effectively by narratives emphasizing interiority and nuanced social interaction, women’s preferences for such narratives could partially account for higher empathy scores. However, it is important not to essentialize preferences or to assume rigid gender binaries; individual differences within genders are large, and cultural variation can shift patterns of media preference and empathic expression.

Several methodological limitations of the present study warrant discussion and point to directions for future work. The primary limitation is the correlational, cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference. Longitudinal or experimental designs would be needed to test directionality. A randomized experiment in which participants are assigned to read or view the same narrative content, or to content with differing empathic affordances, would allow stronger claims about causal effects and about modality differences. Pre- and post-test measures of empathy, along with measures of transportation, identification, and physiological indices of emotional responding, would provide richer data on mechanisms.

Another limitation relates to measurement. The use of a single aggregate measure of viewing hours captures quantity but not quality of narrative exposure. More nuanced instruments that capture genre preference, narrative complexity, character focus, mode of consumption (binge-watching vs episodic viewing), and situational context (alone vs with others, multitasking) are likely to reveal more fine-grained relations between viewing and empathy. Additionally, self-report measures of empathy can be subject to social desirability and introspective limitations; complementing self-report with behavioral tasks, informant reports, or physiological measures would strengthen the validity of inferences.

Sample characteristics also constrain generalizability. The sample was a convenience sample and not representative of any broader population. Cultural differences in media ecosystems and in norms around empathy and emotion may shape the relations observed. Replication in more diverse and representative samples is therefore essential before drawing broad conclusions about the general population.

Despite these limitations, the study’s findings point to several productive avenues for future research. A priority should be to directly compare reading and viewing of equivalent narratives in experimental designs, measuring both short-term changes in empathic responding and longer-term effects on trait empathy. Such studies should include manipulation checks for transportation and identification and should vary content features systematically to determine which narrative elements are most conducive to empathy enhancement. Another promising direction would be longitudinal studies tracking media consumption patterns and empathic development over months or years to assess cumulative effects and temporal ordering.

Research should also probe the role of gender as a moderator in greater depth. Qualitative work exploring how men and women describe their engagement with fiction could illuminate the subjective processes that underlie quantitative patterns. Additionally, studies that examine intersectional identities, including non-binary and transgender participants, and that consider cultural and socioeconomic moderators, would broaden understanding of how narrative media interact with social identities to shape empathy.

From an applied perspective, the distinction between reading and viewing has implications for interventions aimed at fostering empathy. Fiction-based interventions in educational or therapeutic settings often rely on written narratives; if viewing does not produce the same effects, then program designers should be cautious about substituting film or television without empirical support. Conversely, if certain types of viewing can be shown to promote empathy under specific conditions (for example, when viewing is interactive, accompanied by guided reflection, or focused on character psychology), then viewing could be harnessed as a scalable empathy-enhancement tool.

In sum, the present study contributes to the literature by interrogating the assumption that narrative exposure in any modality will have similar associations with empathy. The null overall correlation and the gender-specific negative association among men suggest that media modality, content, and social factors interact in complex ways to shape empathic dispositions. The findings underscore the importance of specifying media modality and content features in research on narrative effects and point to gender as a meaningful moderator. Future work using experimental and longitudinal designs, more nuanced measurement, and broader samples will be required to unpack the causal mechanisms and to determine how best to leverage narratives for empathy promotion.

Concluding remarks emphasize the tentative nature of these findings and the need for replication and extension. The study suggests that viewing fiction, as typically practiced in naturalistic settings, may not confer the same empathic correlates documented for reading fiction. Interventions and theories that assume equivalence across media should be re-examined empirically. At the same time, the negative association found among men opens a line of inquiry into how different narrative forms may differentially engage viewers and how social and individual differences shape these processes. Addressing these issues will advance both theoretical understanding of narrative effects and practical efforts to foster empathy in diverse populations.
