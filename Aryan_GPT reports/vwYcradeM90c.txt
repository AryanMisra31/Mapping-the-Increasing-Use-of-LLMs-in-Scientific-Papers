Introduction

Gender stereotypes are pervasive cognitive schemas that influence how people perceive and evaluate others in social and occupational contexts. These schemas shape expectations about characteristics, roles, and behaviors that are seen as typical or appropriate for men and women. Research across social psychology has shown that gender stereotypes can affect hiring decisions, performance evaluations, promotion opportunities, and everyday interpersonal interactions. One influential formulation of gendered occupational expectations is the observation that people tend to associate managerial roles and leadership qualities more strongly with men than with women. This pattern has been articulated in classic work on the "think manager think male" phenomenon, which captures a tendency to align prototype managerial attributes with stereotypically male traits. The implication of this association is that women may be judged as less fit for managerial roles simply because their gender conflicts with prototypical leader characteristics, and that men may experience social pressure to conform to leadership expectations because managerial prototypes align with male stereotypes.

Schein’s early work on perceived managerial characteristics documented the alignment between manager prototypes and male stereotypes by showing that respondents typically rated items thought to describe managers as more congruent with descriptions of men than with descriptions of women. This line of inquiry opened a substantial body of research concerned with the content, strength, and consequences of gendered leadership prototypes. Subsequent empirical investigations examined how these prototypes persist across cultures, organizational contexts, and over time. Many studies have documented that, even when women achieve high levels of competence and occupy leadership positions, evaluations of their fit for managerial roles remain influenced by gendered expectations. These findings have stimulated theoretical work on role congruity theory, which argues that prejudice toward female leaders may arise because the communal qualities associated with women are seen as incongruent with the agentic traits expected of leaders.

At the same time, research has shown that stereotypes are not immutable. Stereotypes can be attenuated through exposure to counterstereotypic exemplars, changing social norms, or persuasive messages that challenge prevailing associations. Experimental work designed to manipulate exposure to counterstereotypic information has shown that presenting people with counterstereotypic exemplars or narratives can shift their implicit and explicit stereotypes, at least temporarily. For example, exposure to successful women in leadership roles has been used as an intervention to reduce bias in subsequent judgments about women’s competence. Such interventions have sometimes yielded reductions in stereotypic associations on implicit measures and attenuated biased judgments on explicit measures, especially when exposures are vivid, repeated, or accompanied by normative framing that highlights the legitimacy and typicality of female leadership.

The effectiveness of counterstereotypic exposure, however, appears to be moderated by several factors. First, the persistence and depth of an individual’s prior beliefs influence how readily new counterevidence is incorporated. Long-standing, culturally reinforced stereotypes can be resilient and require repeated or strong interventions to shift. Second, motivational and identity-related factors matter; individuals who perceive threats to their ingroup or who hold strong gender-typical identities may resist counterstereotypic evidence. Third, the manner in which counterstereotypic information is presented can critically shape its impact. Messages that highlight exceptionalism or frame female success as anomalous may fail to generalize and may even reinforce stereotypes by positioning female leaders as outliers rather than as prototypical alternatives. In contrast, exposures that imply typicality or normalize female leadership are more likely to produce generalized reductions in stereotype endorsement.

There is also an emerging body of work suggesting that cultural change and cohort effects may lead to shifts in stereotype content over time. Younger generations often endorse less traditional gender-role beliefs than older cohorts. Trends in media representation, organizational diversity initiatives, and public discourse about gender equality have likely influenced the social salience and content of gender stereotypes. Given this dynamic context, it is plausible that the strength of the "think manager think male" association has weakened in some populations, and that under certain conditions women might even be perceived as equal to or more managerial than men on explicit measures.

Despite these advances, there remain unresolved questions about the robustness of counterstereotype interventions and the conditions under which counterevidence translates into changed evaluative judgments of individuals. Prior work has typically relied on either implicit association measures or on aggregate explicit ratings, but the relationship between short-term experimental exposures and explicit trait attributions across a range of managerial characteristics deserves additional exploration. In particular, it is important to examine whether brief, single-exposure interventions that portray a successful female manager can shift evaluative judgments on multi-item managerial measures adapted from classic paradigms, such as those introduced by Schein. Such tests are valuable because they assess the extent to which counterstereotypic messages can influence explicit beliefs about traits directly relevant to hiring and promotion decisions.

Moreover, investigations that include within-subject comparisons of male and female target evaluations provide a rigorous test of whether exposure to counterstereotypic exemplars differentially affects perceptions of same-behavior targets when gender is explicitly manipulated. A within-subjects comparison can reveal whether people adjust their application of leadership criteria across male and female targets after exposure to counterevidence. Conversely, a between-subjects control condition allows assessment of whether observed differences reflect the experimental manipulation or are instead attributable to baseline patterns in the sample.

Another important consideration pertains to stimulus design. The way that character descriptions are written—including subtle wording choices that convey warmth, agency, competence, or decisiveness—can shape how gender information interacts with trait attributions. If descriptions of male and female targets differ in tone or phrasing, even slightly, they may produce patterns that do not purely reflect gender-stereotypic associations. In addition, language that emphasizes communal qualities may be interpreted differently depending on whether it accompanies a male or female target, which could produce unexpected main effects if participants interpret the same descriptors differently when paired with gendered names.

There are also reasons to suspect that contemporary respondents might show patterns that depart from the classic "think manager think male" finding. For instance, awareness of gender bias and social desirability concerns might lead participants to explicitly rate women as more managerial in order to appear egalitarian. Alternatively, women respondents might demonstrate in-group favoritism that elevates evaluations of female targets. On the other hand, persistent implicit biases could still lead to lower ratings of female targets despite explicit egalitarian attitudes. These diverse theoretical possibilities make empirical testing essential.

The current study set out to evaluate whether exposure to a brief news-style article describing a successful female manager would reduce stereotypic associations in explicit ratings on an adapted Schein-style managerial traits questionnaire. To do so, we presented participants with five fictional character descriptions, two of which were focal targets that varied only in name-based gender (Simon vs Sally) and three of which served as distractors. Participants rated each fictional character on 15 managerial qualities adapted from Schein’s set. The main planned analysis was a 2 (target gender: Simon vs Sally; within-subject) × 2 (condition: counterstereotype article vs control article; between-subject) mixed factorial design. The primary dependent variable was the composite managerial rating derived from the 15 items, with higher values indicating stronger perceived managerial fit.

Based on prior research showing that counterstereotypic exemplars can reduce stereotype endorsement, the hypothesis was that participants exposed to the article about a successful female manager would reduce gendered differences in managerial ratings relative to participants in the control condition. Specifically, we predicted that in the counterstereotype condition, ratings for the female target, Sally, would increase relative to ratings for the male target, Simon, producing a reduced gender gap or even a reversal. We further expected a significant interaction between target gender and condition indicating that the article would differentially affect evaluations of female versus male targets. Alternative outcomes were possible given contemporary social norms and potential phrasing artifacts. For instance, if social desirability pressures lead to elevated ratings for women across conditions, a main effect favoring the female target could emerge without a specific effect of the counterstereotypic article. Similarly, if article exposure was insufficiently potent or too subtly framed, no effect of condition would materialize.

The present investigation thus aimed to test whether brief counterstereotypic exposure could shift explicit managerial attributions in a within-subject evaluation of male and female targets. The design allowed us to examine both the main effect of target gender and whether introducing counterevidence in the form of a successful female manager would alter the relative perception of male and female managerial fit. The study also permitted exploratory inspection of potential influences of participant gender on responses. Finally, the research provided an opportunity to discuss how contemporary framing and wording of character descriptions may interact with participant beliefs to produce patterns that inform theoretical accounts of stereotype change.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and from a local community through online advertisement. A total of 78 participants completed the study. All participants provided informed consent and were debriefed at the end of the session. The study was approved by the university’s institutional review board prior to data collection. Participants were told that the study concerned impressions of fictional individuals described in short vignettes and that their responses would be used for research purposes. Participation was voluntary and participants received course credit or a small monetary reimbursement for their time.

The final sample included 78 participants ranging in age from 18 to 62 years. The mean age was 24.7 years (SD = 7.6). Fifty participants identified as female, twenty-seven identified as male, and one participant identified as non-binary; one participant declined to specify gender. Because the dominant comparisons concerned target gender manipulations and condition assignment, analyses focused on the full sample. Participant gender was included as an exploratory factor in secondary analyses, but the primary analyses did not hinge on participant gender subgroups.

Materials included five short character descriptions adapted for this study. Two descriptions were focal targets and were identical in all respects except for the name; one was labeled "Simon" and the other "Sally." Both target descriptions included information about a fictional person's recent work achievements, decision-making style, communication behaviors, and supervisory responsibilities. The three remaining descriptions served as distractors and varied in content and gender in order to obscure the manipulation. The content of the focal descriptions was carefully drafted to be gender-neutral in trait content and to avoid overt gendered language other than the names themselves. The 15 managerial qualities used for rating were adapted from the set used by Schein and included items such as "decisive," "assertive," "confident," "cooperative," "interpersonally skilled," and other traits commonly associated with managerial prototypes. Each item was presented as a single adjective or short phrase.

Participants rated each character on the 15 managerial qualities using a 7-point Likert scale. The scale endpoints were labeled 1 = "Not at all characteristic" and 7 = "Very characteristic." For each character, an overall composite managerial score was computed by averaging the 15 item ratings. As a result, the dependent measure reflected mean managerial endorsement per character, with higher scores indicating greater perceived managerial fit. Internal consistency for the 15-item scale was assessed with Cronbach’s alpha and found to be acceptable in the combined sample.

The manipulation involved presenting participants with either a counterstereotype article or a control article prior to the vignette ratings. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the two conditions using an online randomization routine. In the counterstereotype condition, participants read a short, news-style article about a woman who had achieved notable success as a manager in a mid-sized company. The article emphasized her strategic vision, decisive leadership, ability to turn-around a struggling department, and the respect she garnered from colleagues and subordinates. It was framed as a descriptive piece rather than as an advocacy text. The control condition read a neutral article of similar length and format about a non-gendered organizational topic (e.g., changes in workplace coffee procurement) that did not mention individuals or leadership. Both articles were matched for word count and readability.

After reading the assigned article, participants completed a brief reading comprehension check to encourage engagement and to confirm attention. The comprehension check asked two multiple-choice questions about factual details in the article. Participants who failed both comprehension questions were excluded from the primary analyses; however, no participants were excluded on this basis in the final sample because all participants answered at least one comprehension item correctly.

Participants then read the five character descriptions, presented in randomized order for each participant to control for order effects. For each description, they rated the 15 managerial items using the 7-point scales. After rating the characters, participants completed a short demographic questionnaire that included age, gender, education level, and prior work experience in supervisory roles. They also completed exploratory items asking about their own familiarity with workplace leadership and their explicit beliefs about gender equality in management. These exploratory items were used only for supplementary analyses.

Data preparation involved computing composite scores for each character by averaging the 15 item ratings. Prior to conducting inferential analyses, data were inspected for missingness and normality. Missing item responses were rare and were handled by person-mean imputation when fewer than 10% of items were missing for a given character. Composite scores were normally distributed by visual inspection and by skewness and kurtosis criteria within acceptable limits.

The primary statistical test was a 2 (target gender: Simon vs Sally; within-subject) × 2 (condition: counterstereotype vs control; between-subject) mixed ANOVA on the composite managerial ratings. The mixed-design ANOVA allowed us to evaluate the main effect of target gender, the main effect of condition, and the interaction between target gender and condition. Because the within-subject factor had two levels, sphericity was not a concern. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared for ANOVA tests and Cohen’s d for paired and independent t-tests. In addition to the omnibus ANOVA, planned paired-samples t-tests compared Sally and Simon ratings within each condition. Independent-samples t-tests compared mean ratings across conditions for each target separately as an exploratory step.

Secondary analyses examined whether participant gender moderated the effects. These analyses added participant gender as an additional between-subjects factor in follow-up mixed ANOVAs and tested simple effects where appropriate. Finally, exploratory item-level analyses inspected whether particular managerial qualities showed differential patterns for target gender or condition. All inferential tests used a two-tailed alpha of .05. Statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the composite managerial scores are presented first. Across the entire sample, the mean composite rating for Sally was 4.85 (SD = 0.66). Across the entire sample, the mean composite rating for Simon was 4.61 (SD = 0.71). Thus, on average, Sally was rated higher on the managerial composite than Simon. Inspection of distributions showed no extreme skew or kurtosis, and visual checks of histograms suggested approximately normal distributions for both target scores. Internal consistency of the 15-item scale was high, with Cronbach’s alpha = .92 for the combined items across all targets, which supported the use of an averaged composite score.

Participants were evenly distributed across experimental conditions due to random assignment. Thirty-nine participants were assigned to the counterstereotype article condition and thirty-nine were assigned to the control condition. In the counterstereotype condition, Sally’s mean composite rating was 4.88 (SD = 0.69). In the same condition, Simon’s mean composite rating was 4.57 (SD = 0.74). In the control condition, Sally’s mean composite rating was 4.82 (SD = 0.62). In the control condition, Simon’s mean composite rating was 4.65 (SD = 0.69). These descriptive patterns suggested that Sally was rated higher than Simon in both the counterstereotype and control conditions.

The primary inferential analysis was the 2 × 2 mixed ANOVA. The within-subjects factor was target gender (Simon vs Sally) and the between-subjects factor was condition (counterstereotype vs control). The ANOVA yielded a significant main effect of target gender. Specifically, there was a main effect indicating that Sally was rated higher than Simon, F(1,76) = 7.12, p = .009, partial eta-squared = .086. This effect showed that, collapsing across conditions, participants on average perceived the female-named target as more managerial than the male-named target.

The between-subjects main effect of condition was not significant. There was no significant overall difference between participants who read the counterstereotype article and those who read the control article, F(1,76) = 0.98, p = .326, partial eta-squared = .013. This finding indicated that the brief article manipulation did not produce a detectable shift in overall composite managerial ratings across participants.

Critically, the interaction between target gender and condition was not significant. The interaction term yielded F(1,76) = 0.44, p = .509, partial eta-squared = .006. This result implied that the effect of target gender on managerial ratings did not differ as a function of whether participants were exposed to the counterstereotype article or to the control article. In other words, the pattern of Sally being rated higher than Simon was evident in both conditions and was not meaningfully altered by reading about a successful female manager.

Planned paired-sample t-tests were conducted within each condition to further probe the within-subject gender difference. In the counterstereotype condition, participants rated Sally higher than Simon, t(38) = 2.20, p = .033, Cohen’s d = 0.35. In the control condition, participants also rated Sally higher than Simon, t(38) = 2.15, p = .037, Cohen’s d = 0.34. These within-condition t-tests corroborated the overall main effect of target gender and showed that the female target was rated higher than the male target regardless of article exposure.

Independent-samples t-tests examined whether the counterstereotype article changed ratings for each target separately. For Sally, the difference between conditions was not significant, t(76) = 0.45, p = .653, Cohen’s d = 0.10. For Simon, the difference between conditions was also not significant, t(76) = 0.74, p = .463, Cohen’s d = 0.17. These tests reinforced the ANOVA conclusion that article condition did not significantly alter evaluations of either target.

Exploratory analyses considered participant gender as a potential moderator. A follow-up mixed ANOVA including participant gender as an additional between-subjects factor revealed no significant three-way interaction among target gender, condition, and participant gender, F(1,73) = 0.29, p = .593, partial eta-squared = .004. The two-way interaction between participant gender and target gender was not significant either, F(1,73) = 0.84, p = .363, partial eta-squared = .011. These null findings suggested that the pattern of favoring the female target was not strongly dependent on the gender of the rater in this sample.

Item-level analyses assessed whether particular managerial qualities drove the composite effect in favor of Sally. Paired t-tests on individual items indicated that the largest differences favoring Sally were on items such as "interpersonally skilled" and "cooperative," with smaller and non-significant differences for items such as "decisive" and "assertive." However, after correcting for multiple comparisons, no single item-level difference remained statistically significant. Thus, the composite effect appeared to reflect a modest, distributed set of small differences rather than a single, dominant trait discrepancy.

Additional exploratory checks examined whether participants’ prior supervisory experience or education level correlated with differential ratings. Correlation analyses indicated no reliable associations between years of supervisory experience and the differential rating of Sally versus Simon, r = .07, p = .545. Similarly, education level showed no significant relationship with the gender difference, r = -.05, p = .663. These results suggested that prior experience and education did not account for the pattern of findings.

Finally, attention checks and manipulation checks confirmed that participants engaged with the materials. Mean accuracy on the article comprehension check was 85% correct across items, indicating that participants generally read and understood the articles. There was no evidence that comprehension accuracy systematically differed by condition and thus no indication that differential attention explained the absence of a condition effect.

In sum, the results indicated a modest but statistically significant main effect favoring the female target, Sally, on composite managerial ratings. There was no evidence that the brief counterstereotype article altered this pattern. The hypothesized interaction between target gender and article exposure was not supported, and exploratory moderators offered little explanation for the unexpected directionality favoring the female target.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether brief exposure to a counterstereotypic exemplar—a news-style article about a successful female manager—would alter explicit managerial ratings of male and female targets. The design adapted Schein’s classic approach by having participants rate fictional character descriptions on 15 managerial qualities and by manipulating the target gender through names that differed only in gender. We anticipated that the counterstereotypic article would reduce traditional "think manager think male" associations and either eliminate or reverse any male-favoring bias in leader attributions. Contrary to that expectation, the principal finding was that across conditions participants rated the female-named target, Sally, as slightly more managerial than the male-named target, Simon. Importantly, the counterstereotypic article did not significantly change this pattern, and no interaction between article condition and target gender emerged.

This pattern of results contributes to the literature in several ways. First, it suggests that explicit managerial attributions in contemporary samples may not always conform to the classic "think manager think male" template. The finding that Sally was rated more highly than Simon could reflect shifts in social norms and increased public visibility of women in leadership roles. Over time, greater representation of women in managerial positions, media portrayals of female executives, and educational efforts to combat gender bias may have altered explicit evaluations so that people do not automatically favor male-named targets on average.

Second, the lack of effect of the counterstereotype article indicates that brief, single-exposure interventions may not be sufficient to produce measurable changes in explicit judgments above and beyond baseline tendencies in the sample. This absence of a condition effect is consistent with a growing appreciation that stereotype change often requires repeated, task-relevant, or deeply processed exposures rather than brief vignettes. It is possible that the article provided information that was either too general or too brief to shift the application of trait criteria when participants evaluated specific character descriptions immediately afterward.

Third, the reversal in directionality relative to classic findings invites careful consideration of methodological details, stimulus design, and social desirability influences. One plausible account is that participants engaged in impression management and deliberately elevated ratings for the female target because they are now more sensitized to gender bias and wish to avoid appearing prejudiced. In such a case, participants might over-correct on explicit measures even if implicit associations remain unchanged. Social desirability pressures can lead to asymmetric effects where explicit measures show egalitarian or even pro-female biases while implicit measures capture persistent male-favoring associations.

Another possible explanation rests on the phrasing and content of the character descriptions. Although the descriptions were constructed to be identical in trait content other than name, subtle connotations of specific words can interact with gendered name cues in complex ways. For instance, descriptors that emphasize interpersonal skills or cooperation might be interpreted as managerial strengths when attached to a woman but as weaknesses when attached to a man, or vice versa. In this dataset, item-level analyses suggested slightly larger differences on interpersonal and cooperative items favoring Sally. That pattern raises the possibility that contemporary respondents value a broader set of managerial attributes—ones that include communal competencies—and that these attributes are now seen as central to good management irrespective of gender. If participants associate modern effective management with relational skills as much as with assertiveness, then female targets may benefit in explicit ratings.

It is also possible that sample characteristics contributed to the unexpected pattern. The sample in this study was relatively young and included a preponderance of women. Younger cohorts and women raters may be particularly likely to endorse egalitarian views or to exhibit in-group preferences, which could tilt explicit ratings in favor of women. Notably, the exploratory moderator analyses did not find a strong interaction with rater gender, but the sample size may have been insufficient to detect nuanced moderating effects. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples could examine whether the pattern holds across age cohorts, cultural contexts, and occupational backgrounds.

Methodological considerations about the strength and framing of the counterstereotype intervention are also relevant. The article used here was descriptive and framed as a news report rather than an overtly normative message about gender equality. Prior work indicates that persuasive framing that normalizes female leadership or that situates female success as typical rather than exceptional may be more effective in producing generalized stereotype change. By contrast, an article that singularly highlights an exceptional woman without contextualizing her success as commonplace may inadvertently reinforce the idea that she is an exception to a male-dominated norm. Thus, future interventions should manipulate not only the presence of counterstereotypic exemplars but also the framing—whether the exemplar is presented as typical, one of many, or as a singular standout—to test differential effects.

Another implication of the findings concerns the measurement of managerial perceptions. Using an aggregate composite of 15 managerial qualities, as in Schein’s tradition, has strengths in capturing a multifaceted view of managerial fit. However, composite scores may obscure differential effects across trait clusters. The exploratory item analyses here suggested that communal items may have driven the female advantage, while agentic items showed smaller differences. This suggests that contemporary perceptions of managerial competence may be more complex than a simple alignment with male-stereotypic agentic traits. Researchers may benefit from factor-analyzing managerial trait sets to identify underlying dimensions (e.g., agentic vs communal vs strategic) and examining how gender interacts with these dimensions.

The lack of significant moderation by condition raises practical questions about interventions aimed at reducing gender bias in organizational contexts. If brief exposures to counterstereotypic exemplars do not reliably change explicit trait attributions, organizations should be cautious in assuming that occasional diversity messaging is sufficient to alter evaluative judgments in hiring and promotion contexts. Instead, multi-faceted approaches that combine increased representation, structural change, and repeated exposure to counterstereotypic role models may be necessary to produce durable change in both implicit and explicit evaluations.

Limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. The sample size, while adequate for detecting moderate effects, may have limited power to detect small interaction effects. The participant sample was composed largely of university-affiliated individuals and local community members, which may limit generalizability to working adults in varied organizational settings. The character descriptions, though carefully drafted, remain hypothetical and might be interpreted differently in real-world assessments where additional contextual information is available. Moreover, the study used a single brief article for the counterstereotype manipulation; stronger manipulations involving multiple exemplars, images, or behavioral simulations might produce different outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the study contributes to the evolving understanding of gendered leadership perceptions. The finding that the female target was rated slightly more managerial than the male target suggests that explicit stereotypes favoring men in managerial roles are not universal and may be attenuated or reversed in certain samples and contexts. The null effect of the counterstereotype article cautions against over-reliance on brief exposure interventions for changing explicit evaluations. Importantly, the pattern observed raises productive theoretical questions about how the content of managerial prototypes may be shifting to valorize communal competencies and how this shift interacts with gendered expectations.

Future research could extend the current work in multiple directions. First, longitudinal or repeated-measures designs could test whether repeated counterstereotypic exposures produce cumulative changes in both implicit and explicit measures. Second, researchers could manipulate the framing of counterstereotypic exemplars to test whether portraying female leaders as typical rather than exceptional leads to stronger stereotype attenuation. Third, studies could disentangle the agentic and communal dimensions of managerial prototypes to see how gender interacts with different trait clusters. Fourth, samples drawn from employed populations and from varied organizational levels would enhance ecological validity and could reveal whether workplace context moderates responses.

Additionally, combining explicit trait ratings with behavioral measures—such as hiring decisions, promotion recommendations, or simulated performance evaluations—would provide a more direct test of whether altered perceptions translate into consequential decisions. Incorporating implicit association measures alongside explicit ratings could clarify whether explicit pro-female biases coexist with implicit pro-male associations, thereby revealing dissociations between controlled and automatic processes. Finally, qualitative methods might illuminate how participants interpret descriptor language and how names and gender cues activate different interpretations of identical content.

In conclusion, the present experiment provides novel evidence that explicit managerial perceptions can favor a female-named target and that a single brief counterstereotypic article did not meaningfully alter this pattern. The results highlight important shifts in how managerial competence may be construed and underscore the need for robust, theoretically informed interventions to reduce gender bias in leadership evaluations. Continued research is required to delineate when and how counterstereotypic information can produce generalized stereotype change and to identify organizational practices that reliably promote equitable evaluations of men and women in managerial roles.
