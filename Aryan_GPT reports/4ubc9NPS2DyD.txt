Introduction

Facebook currently occupies a unique position in the landscape of digital communication and social interaction. The platform is used by a substantial proportion of the global population for a range of activities that include keeping in touch with family and friends, organizing social events, following news and informational content, and participating in communities of practice. Because of its pervasiveness, Facebook is also a central focus for research into how online social networks influence psychological outcomes. One outcome of interest for psychologists and policy makers is overall life satisfaction. Life satisfaction is a cognitive evaluation of one’s life as a whole and has been linked to mental and physical health, social functioning, and longevity. Understanding how modern forms of mediated sociality like Facebook relate to life satisfaction is therefore of both theoretical and practical importance.

Empirical research on social networking sites and subjective well-being has produced a complex and sometimes contradictory pattern of findings. Some studies report that use of social networking sites is associated with improved social capital, greater perceived social support, and enhanced opportunities for social bridging and the maintenance of weak ties. These studies emphasize the ways that online platforms can supplement offline relationships and can provide resources, information, and opportunities that contribute positively to life outcomes. In contrast, other lines of research have associated social media use with negative outcomes, such as increased social comparison, envy, loneliness, and depressive symptoms. These negative effects are often argued to arise from features of social media that encourage selective self-presentation, upward comparisons, and the measurement of social feedback through likes and comments.

One factor that may help reconcile these divergent findings is the nature and intensity of use. Frequency of checking a platform is not necessarily equivalent to the degree to which the platform is integrated into a user’s social life. The construct referred to as intensity of use attempts to capture how central a platform is to a person’s everyday interactions, how emotionally invested they are, and the extent of the site’s incorporation into their social routines. Measures of intensity typically assess both behavioral aspects, such as time spent and number of friends, and attitudinal aspects, such as emotional connectedness to the platform. Some evidence suggests that intensity of use is more strongly related to social outcomes than simple frequency metrics because intensity reflects the role the platform plays in supporting social relationships.

Given that intensity is theoretically tied to the quality of users’ social ties, it may be particularly relevant for life satisfaction. Users who rely on Facebook as a central hub for maintaining and building relationships may experience greater feelings of social integration and access to social resources. These benefits could plausibly translate into higher evaluations of life satisfaction. Conversely, intense use might also facilitate more frequent upward comparisons or greater exposure to curated content, which could erode life satisfaction over time. The balance between these processes may depend on user motivations and the social contexts in which the platform is used.

Gender differences in social media use and effects have also been investigated, with mixed results. Some research indicates that women may use social networking platforms more for social connection and disclosure, whereas men may be more likely to use them for information or self-promotion. These differences in use patterns could modulate the relationship between platform intensity and life satisfaction, producing gender-specific associations. Other studies, however, have failed to find meaningful gender differences in the psychological outcomes associated with social media. The question of whether gender moderates the association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction therefore remains open.

Adolescence is often highlighted as a particularly sensitive period for social media effects because of developmental changes in social cognition, self-concept, and peer relationships. A sizable portion of the literature has expressed concern that social media may be detrimental to adolescents’ well-being through mechanisms such as cyberbullying, social comparison, and sleep disruption. Yet there are also arguments that adolescents might stand to benefit from the social affordances of platforms like Facebook, particularly when they use such platforms to maintain and extend supportive networks. The net effect for adolescents may thus vary according to the ways in which the platform is used and the contexts in which use occurs.

Measurement issues are important when assessing the association between Facebook use and life satisfaction. The Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS) is a commonly used measure of global life satisfaction that assesses the cognitive evaluative component of subjective well-being. The Facebook Intensity Scale (FBI) is a self-report instrument designed to capture the degree of integration of Facebook into a person’s daily life and the emotional significance attached to the platform. Using psychometrically sound measures can help clarify the nature of the relationship between platform engagement and life satisfaction.

Another consideration is the extent to which the association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction is robust across samples and statistical controls. Age is an important potential confound because both life satisfaction and patterns of social media use vary with age. Younger users tend to spend more time on social platforms and may integrate them differently into their social lives than older users. Socioeconomic status, offline social network size, and personality traits such as extraversion and neuroticism have also been proposed as potential third variables that could influence both platform use and subjective well-being.

Previous studies have produced divergent effect sizes for the association between social media use and life satisfaction, with some reporting small negative associations, some reporting null associations, and others reporting positive associations particularly when measures of social support or social capital are taken into account. These mixed findings underscore the need for continued empirical work that carefully measures intensity of use and life satisfaction in diverse samples while examining the role of potential moderators and the stability of associations across demographic groups.

The present study sought to contribute to this literature by examining the association between intensity of Facebook use and overall life satisfaction in a cross-sectional sample. We focused on intensity rather than mere frequency because intensity captures the emotional and integrative aspects of platform use that are theoretically linked to social resources and subjective well-being. Participants completed the Facebook Intensity Scale and the Satisfaction With Life Scale. We also collected basic demographic information including age and gender to examine potential moderating effects and to control for possible confounds in exploratory analyses.

Based on theoretical considerations about social integration and access to social resources, we hypothesized that higher intensity of Facebook use would be associated with higher levels of life satisfaction. At the same time, given the conflicting literature that emphasizes possible negative consequences of social media through social comparison and decreased offline engagement, we acknowledged an alternative hypothesis that intensity could be negatively associated with life satisfaction. Therefore, we treated the direction of association as an empirical question with a primary prediction favoring a positive association due to the social capital and social support mechanisms tied to intensity.

We also examined gender differences in both the intensity of Facebook use and life satisfaction. Given mixed findings in prior work, we did not formulate a strong directional hypothesis about gender differences; instead, we tested for the presence of mean differences and for possible gender moderation of the association between intensity and life satisfaction. Finally, because adolescence is often flagged as a vulnerable period but also a time when online social resources may be especially valuable, we explored whether associations differed substantially for younger participants compared to older participants.

In addition to testing the primary correlational association, we planned to perform robustness checks by controlling statistically for age and gender. We also intended to examine the distributional properties of the measures and to report internal consistency estimates to provide an indication of measurement reliability. Given the cross-sectional nature of the design, we emphasized that causal inferences would be inappropriate and that any mediational processes such as social support would remain speculative without direct measurement or longitudinal data.

The study therefore contributes to the literature by focusing on the often-overlooked construct of intensity of Facebook use, by using validated measures of life satisfaction and Facebook engagement, and by providing empirical data that bear on whether intensive engagement with Facebook is associated with higher or lower life satisfaction in a general sample. This empirical clarity is important for informing debates about the risks and benefits of social networking platforms and for guiding future longitudinal and experimental research aimed at isolating causal mechanisms.

Overall, the introduction moves from the general importance of understanding social media’s relationship to well-being, to an integration of competing theoretical perspectives, to a clear statement of the present study’s aims and hypotheses. It highlights the relevance of measuring intensity as opposed to frequency, notes the mixed literature on gender and developmental differences, and sets reasonable empirical expectations. The methodological emphasis and the planned analytic approach are designed to maximize clarity in interpreting observed associations. By situating the study within the broader research landscape and by acknowledging limitations inherent in cross-sectional work, the introduction provides a balanced rationale for the investigation that follows.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a combination of university student pools and community advertisements. The sample comprised four hundred twelve individuals who provided complete data on the primary measures. Participants ranged in age from 13 to 65 years. The mean age of the sample was 24.8 years with a standard deviation of 8.3 years. Participants self-identified their gender; 247 identified as female and 165 identified as male. No participants identified a non-binary gender in this sample, although recruitment materials invited participants of all gender identities. The sample included individuals from a variety of educational backgrounds and employment statuses, reflecting the broad recruitment approach that combined student and community outreach.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the relevant institutional review board prior to participant recruitment. Participants under the age of 18 provided assent and parental consent was obtained in accordance with institutional guidelines. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary, that they could withdraw at any time without penalty, and that their responses would be anonymized for analysis. After completing the survey, participants received either course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time.

Measures included two primary self-report scales. The first was the Facebook Intensity Scale (FBI). The FBI is a multi-item self-report instrument designed to measure the degree to which Facebook is integrated into a person’s social and emotional life. Items assess behavioral aspects such as hours spent on Facebook per day and number of Facebook friends as well as attitudinal components such as the extent to which Facebook is considered part of daily routines and emotional connectedness to the platform. Items are rated on Likert-type scales and are combined to form a total intensity score. For the present study, we used a version of the scale that yields scores on a 1 to 5 metric for ease of interpretation, with higher scores indicating greater intensity.

The second primary measure was the Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS). The SWLS is a five-item instrument that assesses global cognitive judgments of one’s life satisfaction. Participants rate their agreement with statements that reflect overall life evaluation, rather than momentary affect or domain-specific satisfactions. Items are typically scored on a 1 to 7 Likert scale, and total scores indicate higher life satisfaction with larger values. The SWLS was selected for its brevity, psychometric properties, and widespread use in research on subjective well-being.

Administration of the measures occurred online through a secure survey platform. After providing informed consent, participants completed demographic questions followed by the FBI items and then the SWLS items. The survey order was kept constant across participants to limit variability introduced by counterbalancing. At the end of the survey participants were thanked for their time and provided debriefing information about the aims of the research and contact details for the investigators.

Data screening procedures were conducted prior to primary analyses. Responses were examined for missingness, completion time, and response patterns indicative of low engagement. Participants who failed attention checks or whose completion times were less than one-third of the sample median completion time were flagged and reviewed. Fourteen participants were excluded from the final analytic sample due to incomplete data or implausible completion times, resulting in the final sample of 412 participants. Missing data for individual items within the retained sample were minimal and handled using mean imputation for participants missing fewer than 10 percent of items on a given scale. No participant had more than one item missing per scale.

Scale reliabilities were computed using Cronbach’s alpha to assess internal consistency. The FBI items were examined for dimensionality and consistency prior to aggregation into a single intensity score. The SWLS was scored in the customary manner by summing item scores to produce a total life satisfaction score. Descriptive statistics including means, standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis were computed for the primary variables. Graphical inspection of distributions was conducted to assess normality and to identify potential outliers.

The main statistical test for the primary hypothesis was Pearson’s product-moment correlation between the FBI total score and the SWLS total score. Given the possibility of non-normality, Spearman rank correlations were also computed as robustness checks. We also computed partial correlations controlling for age and gender to assess whether the association was robust to those covariates. To further examine the association, a linear regression model predicting life satisfaction from Facebook intensity was estimated with and without covariates (age and gender). Standardized regression coefficients were reported to facilitate interpretation.

To test for gender differences in intensity and life satisfaction, independent-samples t-tests were conducted comparing male and female participants on FBI and SWLS scores. Effect sizes were estimated using Cohen’s d. In addition, an interaction term between Facebook intensity and gender was included in a regression model to test whether the association between intensity and life satisfaction differed by gender. Given the potential heterogeneity across age groups, an exploratory subgroup analysis compared associations among adolescents (defined here as participants aged 13 to 17) and older participants (aged 18 and above).

Assumptions for parametric tests were evaluated. Linearity between the FBI and SWLS was inspected using scatterplots and locally weighted smoothing. Homoscedasticity of residuals in regression models was evaluated visually and via Breusch-Pagan tests. Multicollinearity was not expected given the simplicity of the models but was assessed using variance inflation factors. All statistical tests used two-tailed significance criteria with an alpha level of .05. Confidence intervals for key estimates were provided to aid interpretation.

Power considerations were addressed during study planning. With a target sample size of approximately 400 participants, the study had sufficient power to detect small to medium effect sizes in correlational analyses. Specifically, with N = 412, the study had over 80 percent power to detect a correlation of approximately r = .14 or larger at the conventional alpha level. Thus, the design was reasonably well powered to detect effects of modest magnitude that are typical in social media and well-being research.

Finally, all analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Variables were inspected for accuracy, and code used for the analyses was archived for reproducibility. Results are reported following the planned analytic strategy with additional exploratory analyses noted as such.

Results

Descriptive statistics are presented first to provide an overview of the sample and the primary measures. The mean Facebook Intensity Scale score in the sample was 3.21 on the 1 to 5 metric, with a standard deviation of 0.78. The distribution of FBI scores was approximately symmetric with a skewness of -0.12 and a kurtosis of -0.35, indicating no gross departures from normality. The mean score on the Satisfaction With Life Scale was 23.5, on the typical 5 to 35 summative metric, with a standard deviation of 5.9. The SWLS distribution showed a slight positive skew of 0.24 and a kurtosis of -0.10, again indicating distributions amenable to parametric analysis.

Internal consistency estimates for both scales suggested acceptable reliability. The Cronbach’s alpha for the FBI items was 0.83 in the present sample, indicating good internal consistency for the intensity measure. The SWLS yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86, which is consistent with established reliability levels for that instrument. Item-level statistics revealed no items that appreciably decreased the scales’ internal consistencies when omitted.

The primary analysis examined the association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction. Pearson’s correlation between FBI score and SWLS total was positive and statistically significant at r = .21, p < .001. The 95 percent confidence interval for the Pearson correlation ranged from .12 to .29, indicating that a small but reliable positive association was present in the sample. A Spearman rank correlation was also computed as a nonparametric check and yielded a similar result, rho = .19, p < .001, supporting the robustness of the association to rank-based assumptions.

Because age and gender could potentially confound the association, partial correlations controlling for age and gender were computed. The partial correlation between FBI and SWLS, controlling for age and gender, remained positive and statistically significant at r_partial = .19, p < .001. These results indicate that the observed positive association between intensity and life satisfaction was not simply due to age or gender differences in the sample.

A simple linear regression predicting life satisfaction from Facebook intensity further characterized the association. In the unadjusted model, FBI score was a significant predictor of SWLS total with an unstandardized coefficient of 2.31 (SE = 0.45), t(410) = 5.13, p < .001. This coefficient indicates that a one-point increase in FBI score was associated with an approximate 2.31 point increase in SWLS total score on the 5-35 metric. The standardized beta was .21, consistent with the correlation. The unadjusted model explained about 4.4 percent of the variance in life satisfaction (R^2 = .044).

An adjusted regression model including age and gender as covariates showed that FBI remained a significant predictor. In this model, the FBI unstandardized coefficient was 2.05 (SE = 0.46), t(408) = 4.45, p < .001, and the standardized beta was .19. Age was modestly positively associated with life satisfaction in the adjusted model, b = 0.08 (SE = 0.03), t = 2.67, p = .008, suggesting that older participants tended to report slightly higher life satisfaction. Gender was not a significant predictor in the adjusted model, b = 0.23 (SE = 0.38), t = 0.61, p = .54. The adjusted model explained 6.1 percent of the variance in life satisfaction (adjusted R^2 = .061).

Tests of the assumptions underlying the regression analyses were satisfactory. Scatterplots and locally weighted smoothing indicated a roughly linear association between FBI and SWLS scores across the observed range. Residuals from regression models were approximately normally distributed with no clear systematic patterns. The Breusch-Pagan test for heteroscedasticity did not yield evidence of heteroscedasticity (p = .28), supporting the use of standard errors from ordinary least squares estimates. Variance inflation factors were all close to 1, indicating no problem with multicollinearity in the simple models.

Gender comparisons on the primary variables were conducted using independent-samples t-tests. Female participants had a mean FBI score of 3.25 (SD = 0.77) and male participants had a mean FBI score of 3.14 (SD = 0.79). The difference was not statistically significant, t(410) = 1.19, p = .235. Cohen’s d for the gender difference in FBI was 0.14, indicating a small and non-significant effect size. For life satisfaction, female participants had a mean SWLS score of 23.7 (SD = 6.0) and male participants had a mean of 23.2 (SD = 5.7). This difference was also not statistically significant, t(410) = 0.76, p = .45, with a Cohen’s d of 0.09. Therefore, there was no evidence of meaningful gender differences in either Facebook intensity or life satisfaction in this sample.

To examine whether gender moderated the association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction, an interaction term between FBI and gender was included in the regression model. The interaction coefficient was small and non-significant, b = 0.35 (SE = 0.62), t = 0.56, p = .57, indicating that the association between intensity and life satisfaction did not differ significantly by gender. These moderation analyses are consistent with the t-test results showing no main effect of gender on the primary variables.

Given theoretical interest in adolescence, an exploratory subgroup analysis compared associations among adolescents aged 13 to 17 and participants aged 18 and older. The adolescent subgroup consisted of 48 individuals. In the adolescent subgroup, the correlation between FBI and SWLS was r = .27, p = .064, which was numerically larger than the correlation in the adult subgroup but did not reach conventional statistical significance, likely due to the small adolescent sample size. In the adult subgroup, the correlation was r = .20, p < .001. A formal test of the difference in correlation coefficients between the two subgroups did not reach statistical significance, z = 0.41, p = .68. Thus, while the pattern is suggestive of possibly stronger associations in younger users, the evidence was not definitive.

Outlier diagnostics were conducted to determine whether extreme scores unduly influenced the results. A few participants had particularly high FBI scores and high SWLS scores, but removing those cases did not materially change the pattern of results. When the top and bottom 2.5 percent of FBI scores were trimmed, the correlation between FBI and SWLS remained positive and significant, r = .19, p < .001. This robustness check suggests that the positive association was not driven by a few extreme observations.

In sum, the results demonstrated a small but statistically reliable positive association between intensity of Facebook use and life satisfaction in the sample. This association persisted after controlling for age and gender and was robust to nonparametric checks and to the exclusion of outliers. No meaningful gender differences were observed in either mean levels of the primary measures or in the strength of the association between intensity and life satisfaction. Exploratory analyses suggested a numerically larger association among adolescents, but this result was not statistically significant in the present sample.

Discussion

The present study examined the association between intensity of Facebook use and overall life satisfaction in a cross-sectional sample of participants drawn from university and community settings. The primary finding was a small but reliable positive association between the Facebook Intensity Scale and the Satisfaction With Life Scale. This association remained significant after controlling for age and gender and was robust when examined with nonparametric methods and when outliers were removed. In addition, the study found no evidence of meaningful gender differences in either Facebook intensity or life satisfaction, and no evidence that gender moderated the association between intensity and life satisfaction. Exploratory evidence suggested that the association might be numerically larger among adolescents, though the small adolescent subsample limited definitive conclusions.

These findings contribute to the ongoing conversation about the psychological correlates of social media use. The positive association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction aligns with theoretical perspectives that highlight the social benefits of online platforms. When Facebook serves as an integrated component of daily life, it may facilitate the maintenance of social ties, provide opportunities for social support, and enhance access to social capital. Such social resources are well-established correlates of higher subjective well-being and life satisfaction. In this light, the present results suggest that intensive engagement with Facebook may, at least in some contexts, be associated with greater overall life evaluations.

It is important to situate these findings within the broader, mixed literature on social media and well-being. Some prior work has documented negative associations between aspects of social media use and subjective well-being, often emphasizing processes like upward social comparison, envy, and displacement of offline interactions. The present results do not negate the existence of those processes. Instead, they suggest that the net association between platform intensity and life satisfaction can be positive in general population samples, perhaps because the social support and connectivity benefits outweigh harmful comparison effects for many users. The balance of positive and negative influences likely depends on individual differences, motivations for use, the nature of online networks, and the broader social context.

One reason that intensity, as operationalized in the FBI, may relate positively to life satisfaction is that the intensity construct captures emotional investment and integration of the platform into social routines rather than mere time spent online. Users who report higher intensity may do so because Facebook successfully supports meaningful relationships and facilitates frequent, reciprocal interactions. By contrast, someone who spends large amounts of time passively browsing but does not derive social connection from the platform might not report high intensity in the same way, and might experience different well-being outcomes. Thus, the psychological quality of engagement may be a crucial determinant of whether social media use is beneficial or harmful.

The lack of gender differences observed in this study is noteworthy in light of mixed prior findings. Some literature suggests that women and men use social networking sites differently, but differences in how those usage patterns translate into well-being outcomes have been inconsistent. The present study’s null gender findings suggest that, at the level of intensity and global life satisfaction, similar processes may operate across genders. It is possible that gender differences, when present, are more likely to emerge in domain-specific outcomes (for example, appearance-related distress) or when assessing particular features of platform use rather than overall intensity.

The exploratory suggestion that the intensity–life satisfaction association might be larger among adolescents deserves careful consideration. Adolescence is a period characterized by heightened sensitivity to peer relationships and a strong reliance on social networks for identity development and social support. If Facebook and similar platforms are effectively used by adolescents to maintain friendships and receive social affirmation, they could serve as important resources that bolster life satisfaction. However, adolescents also face unique risks related to social media, including cyberbullying, peer pressure, and heightened vulnerability to social comparison. The net effect for adolescents will therefore likely depend on the balance of these forces, and on contextual factors such as parental guidance, the quality of offline relationships, and individual coping strategies.

Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. First and foremost, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. While we observed a positive association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction, it is equally plausible that individuals with higher life satisfaction are more inclined to use Facebook intensively, or that third variables account for the association. Longitudinal and experimental designs are necessary to disentangle directionality and to test mediational hypotheses such as whether social support mediates the association between intensity and life satisfaction.

Second, the study relied on self-report measures, which can be subject to response biases and inaccuracies in recall. Although the FBI and SWLS are established measures with good internal consistency, objective behavioral measures of Facebook use and more detailed assessments of the nature of online interactions would complement self-report data and help clarify which aspects of use are most beneficial or harmful.

Third, the generalizability of the findings is limited by the sample composition. The recruitment strategy included university students and community members, but the sample was not nationally representative. In particular, cultural and socioeconomic factors that shape both platform use and well-being were not systematically sampled. Future research should examine more diverse and representative samples across different cultural contexts to determine whether the positive association observed here is robust across populations.

Fourth, the study did not directly measure potential mediators such as perceived social support, social capital, or mechanisms related to social comparison and envy. While the discussion has emphasized social support as a plausible mediator that could explain why intensity correlates positively with life satisfaction, this remains speculative in the absence of direct measurement. Future studies that include validated measures of social support and social comparison could test mediation models to identify processes that underlie the observed association.

Fifth, the rapid evolution of social media platforms means that findings tied to Facebook at a particular point in time may not generalize to the platform as it changes or to other platforms with different affordances. Platforms that emphasize ephemeral content, visual presentation, or algorithmically curated feeds may yield different relationships with well-being. Comparative studies across platforms can provide insight into which design features and social affordances are associated with positive versus negative outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the present results have several implications for research and practice. From a research perspective, the findings point to the value of focusing on intensity and qualitative aspects of online engagement rather than solely on time-based metrics. Understanding how users integrate platforms into their social lives, and how those integrations relate to access to social resources, is essential for building explanatory models. Longitudinal research that follows users over time and assesses changes in social networks and well-being can test causal pathways and identify potential critical periods or thresholds of use that are especially consequential.

From a practical standpoint, the results suggest that policies or recommendations that adopt a blanket stance against social media use may overlook important benefits that platforms can provide for social connectivity and life satisfaction. Interventions and guidance that help users harness the social support potential of platforms—such as encouraging active and reciprocal engagement rather than passive consumption, fostering constructive online communities, and providing tools for managing adverse experiences—may be more effective than simplistic prescriptions to reduce screen time.

For adolescents in particular, the results indicate that social media, including Facebook, can be part of a supportive social ecology when used in ways that promote connection and mutual support. Educational efforts that teach digital literacy, promote positive online behaviors, and provide resources for dealing with negative experiences may maximize the benefits and mitigate harms for younger users.

Future research could profitably pursue several lines of inquiry. First, longitudinal studies that measure intensity, social support, social comparison, and well-being across multiple time points would permit tests of mediation and directionality. Second, experimental designs that manipulate the quality or type of engagement (for example, encouraging users to engage with close friends vs. passively browse public feeds) could clarify causal effects of different kinds of use. Third, research that combines self-report with objective behavioral data, such as time-stamped logs of interactions and content analyses of posts, could identify which features of online behavior are most strongly linked to positive life outcomes. Fourth, studies that compare different platforms and take into account algorithmic exposure to content could help determine how platform design influences psychological processes.

In conclusion, the current study found a small but robust positive association between the intensity of Facebook use and overall life satisfaction, with no evidence of gender differences in mean levels or in the association between the constructs. The findings are consistent with models that emphasize the social benefits of integrated online engagement, such as increased social support and access to social capital. However, the cross-sectional and self-report nature of the data limits causal conclusions, and further research is required to test mediational mechanisms and to explore how these associations play out across developmental stages and diverse populations. By focusing on intensity and the social quality of engagement, future work can better capture the nuanced ways in which social platforms contribute to or detract from subjective well-being.
