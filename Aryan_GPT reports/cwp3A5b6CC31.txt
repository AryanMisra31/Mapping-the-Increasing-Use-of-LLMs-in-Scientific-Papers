Introduction

Social media use has become a pervasive feature of contemporary life, particularly among young adults. The growth of platforms that enable users to create, curate, and consume social content has been rapid and global. Instagram, a visually oriented social media platform, has become especially popular among young adults who use it for sharing photos, following peers, and engaging with content creators. The prevalence of Instagram use in this demographic has led to concern, curiosity, and intense scholarly interest about its psychological effects. One of the central questions in this line of inquiry is whether social media use has a net positive or negative effect on subjective well-being. Subjective well-being is often operationalized as life satisfaction, which reflects a cognitive evaluation of one's overall quality of life. The relationship between social media use and life satisfaction is complex and remains contested in the literature. Some studies report negative associations that are often attributed to mechanisms such as upward social comparison, envy, and displacement of face-to-face interactions. Other studies find neutral or even positive associations that are explained by mechanisms such as social support, identity expression, and increased opportunities for social connection. Differences in findings across studies may be attributable to a range of methodological and conceptual factors. These include variability in how social media use is measured, differences in the particular platforms studied, the age and cultural background of samples, and whether studies account for individual difference variables that may confound the association. For example, the distinction between active and passive social media use has been identified as an important moderator, with active use often linked to more positive outcomes and passive consumption linked to worse outcomes. The photographic and curated nature of Instagram may amplify certain processes compared to text-based platforms, especially those related to appearance, lifestyle, and aspirational content. Young adults are also at a life stage where social identity and peer relations are especially salient, and therefore the psychological effects of platforms like Instagram may be heightened. Given this complexity, it is important to examine Instagram use with attention to potential confounding individual differences. One such individual difference that is relevant to both social media behavior and subjective well-being is locus of control. Locus of control reflects the degree to which individuals perceive outcomes in their life as contingent on their own actions (internal locus of control) versus external forces such as luck, fate, or powerful others (external locus of control). Locus of control has long been linked to a variety of psychological outcomes, including life satisfaction, coping strategies, and social behavior. Individuals with a more internal locus of control tend to report higher life satisfaction and engage in more proactive social behaviors. Conversely, those with a more external locus of control often report lower life satisfaction and may engage in behavior that reflects passivity or dependence on external validation. Importantly, locus of control may also relate to social media use patterns. For instance, individuals with an internal locus of control may use Instagram in a goal-directed and expressive way, whereas those with an external locus of control may use the platform in ways that seek external validation or that reflect passive watching of others. Locus of control could therefore operate as a confound or moderator of the relationship between Instagram use and life satisfaction. Despite this theoretical plausibility, relatively few empirical studies have explicitly controlled for locus of control when examining social media outcomes. Another methodological issue that complicates interpretation of past findings is the heterogeneity of measures used to capture social media use. Some studies rely on single-item measures of time spent, while others use multi-item questionnaires that tap into frequency, intensity, emotional involvement, and different types of engagement. The multifaceted nature of social media behavior suggests that simple measures may miss important nuances. Furthermore, studies differ in their analytic approaches; some report simple bivariate correlations, while others use multivariate models that control for a range of covariates. Without consistent control for relevant individual differences such as locus of control, it is difficult to isolate the unique contribution of Instagram use to life satisfaction. There are also plausible mechanisms by which Instagram use could positively influence life satisfaction. Active use that involves communicating with friends, sharing personal achievements, and receiving supportive feedback may bolster feelings of connectedness and competence, thereby increasing life satisfaction. Visual media may allow for creative expression and identity exploration, which are important developmental tasks in young adulthood. Moreover, for some users, Instagram serves as a source of social support, community identification, and informational resources that can enhance their perceived quality of life. In contrast, mechanisms for potential negative effects include social comparison, exposure to idealized images, and the potential for cyberbullying or exclusion. The balance between these mechanisms may depend on the user’s patterns of engagement and their broader psychological profile. Considering these competing pathways, the current study sought to contribute to the literature by examining the association between Instagram use and life satisfaction among young adults while statistically controlling for locus of control. By accounting for locus of control, the study aims to clarify whether any observed relationship between Instagram use and life satisfaction is robust to adjustment for this stable individual difference. The design of the study is correlational and cross-sectional, which means that the analyses can identify associations but cannot establish causal direction. Nonetheless, cross-sectional data remain valuable for elucidating the patterns of covariation that can inform future longitudinal and experimental work. The sample for the present study was drawn from a population of young adults, a segment of the population for whom Instagram use is particularly prevalent and developmentally salient. Selecting a young adult sample helps to increase the relevance of the findings to a demographic where social media may have especially strong psychological effects. The study employed a multi-measure approach. Life satisfaction was assessed with a nine-item Life Satisfaction Questionnaire that captures a cognitive evaluation of one’s satisfaction with life as a whole. Instagram use was measured with a multi-item Instagram Use Questionnaire designed to capture frequency, intensity, and behavioral patterns such as active versus passive engagement. Locus of control was assessed using a well-established Rotter-style locus of control scale to capture the degree to which individuals perceive their lives as controlled by internal versus external forces. The combination of these measures allows for a focused test of the primary hypothesis. The primary hypothesis guiding the study was that higher Instagram use would correlate negatively with life satisfaction scores in young adults. This hypothesis was informed by a body of literature that emphasizes risks associated with passive social media consumption and social comparison processes. At the same time, given the mixed pattern of findings in prior work and theoretical arguments highlighting potential benefits of active engagement, alternative outcomes were plausible. Therefore, the study was explicitly designed to test the hypothesized negative association and to examine whether this association persisted when controlling for locus of control. A secondary objective was to explore whether patterns of Instagram engagement (active versus passive) showed different associations with life satisfaction, as suggested by prior theoretical work. A tertiary objective was to consider whether locus of control moderated any observed associations, although the primary analytic strategy focused on statistical control for locus of control. The study also incorporated checks for common statistical issues such as non-normality, multicollinearity, and influential outliers to ensure the robustness of results. In addition, exploratory subgroup analyses by gender were planned given observed gender differences in social media use in prior descriptive research. Ultimately, the study sought to provide a clearer picture of the association between Instagram use and life satisfaction among young adults and to contribute to theoretical discussions about the conditions under which social media may support versus undermine subjective well-being. The rationale for focusing on locus of control as a covariate was grounded in its documented association with life satisfaction and its plausible connection to social media behavior. Controlling for locus of control allows for a more precise assessment of the unique variance in life satisfaction that may be associated with Instagram use. By including a multi-item measure of Instagram behavior, the study also aimed to capture differences between active and passive engagement that might help explain inconsistent results in the literature. Although causal conclusions would not be possible from the data, identifying robust associations in a controlled analysis can inform hypotheses for longitudinal and experimental follow-ups. In sum, the present study was designed to examine the relationship between Instagram use and life satisfaction among young adults with statistical control for locus of control, to test the hypothesis that higher Instagram use would be associated with lower life satisfaction, and to explore the role of engagement patterns and potential interactions with locus of control and gender.

Methods

The study employed a cross-sectional correlational design implemented via an online survey. Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and from social media advertisements targeted at young adults aged 18 to 30. In total, 207 individuals initiated the survey. After removing incomplete responses and those who failed attention checks, a final sample of 200 participants was retained for analysis. The final sample size of 200 was selected to provide adequate power to detect small-to-medium correlations; power calculations indicated that with N = 200 the study had approximately 0.80 power to detect a correlation of r = 0.20 at alpha = .05. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 30 years with a mean age of 22.4 years (SD = 2.8). The sample included 115 women, 82 men, and 3 participants who identified as non-binary or another gender identity. Participants were offered course credit or entry into a raffle for an online gift card as compensation for their time. Inclusion criteria required participants to be between 18 and 30 years of age and to report having an active Instagram account used at least once in the preceding month. Participants with incomplete surveys or inconsistent responses on embedded attention checks were excluded from analyses. All participants provided informed consent electronically prior to beginning the survey. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee in accordance with standard ethical guidelines for human subject research. Measures included a nine-item Life Satisfaction Questionnaire (LSQ-9), an Instagram Use Questionnaire (IUQ), and a locus of control scale modeled after Rotter’s approach. The LSQ-9 is a self-report questionnaire designed to assess global life satisfaction across nine items. Items on the LSQ-9 were rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Five of the nine items were positively worded and four were reverse-scored; total scores were obtained by summing item responses after appropriate reverse scoring, yielding a possible range from 9 to 63, with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction. In the present sample, the LSQ-9 demonstrated good internal consistency with Cronbach’s alpha = .88. The Instagram Use Questionnaire (IUQ) consisted of 16 items designed to capture multiple dimensions of Instagram engagement. The IUQ included items assessing time spent on the platform per day (reported in minutes), frequency of visits per day, number of posts per week, and subjective intensity of engagement. Importantly, the IUQ also contained an 8-item subscale intended to differentiate active engagement (e.g., posting content, commenting, direct messaging) from passive consumption (e.g., scrolling, viewing others’ stories without interacting). These items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (never) to 5 (very often). Scores for overall Instagram use were computed by summing relevant IUQ items after converting time and frequency items to standardized z-scores to place them on a similar metric; the overall IUQ score was then rescaled to a 0–100 range to facilitate interpretation. Subscale scores for active and passive use were calculated by averaging the respective items, with higher scores indicating greater active or passive engagement. Internal consistency for the IUQ overall score was acceptable with Cronbach’s alpha = .81; the active and passive subscales had alphas of .79 and .74, respectively. Locus of control was assessed using a 23-item Rotter-style scale with forced-choice items that present an internal versus external option. Scoring followed standard conventions where higher scores indicate a more external locus of control. The locus of control scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the current sample with Cronbach’s alpha = .72. Demographic information including age, gender, year in school, employment status, and socioeconomic indicators was also collected. The survey included two embedded attention-check items and one item assessing whether participants had been honest in their responses; participants failing the attention-check items or indicating dishonesty were excluded. Procedure details included that participants completed the survey online in a single session that took approximately 15 minutes. The order of the three primary questionnaires was counterbalanced across participants to reduce order effects. The majority of participants completed the survey on a laptop or desktop computer, with a minority completing it on a mobile device. Before conducting primary analyses, data were inspected for missingness, normality, and influential observations. Missing data were minimal (<2% for any single item) and were handled by mean imputation for the relevant scale when less than 10% of items on that scale were missing. Univariate distributions of key variables were assessed via skewness and kurtosis statistics and visual inspection of histograms; deviations from normality were modest and within acceptable bounds for the planned parametric analyses. Leverage and influence were assessed using Cook’s distance and standardized residuals; no cases exceeded conventional cutoffs to suggest undue influence on regression estimates. The analytic strategy pre-specified Pearson correlation coefficients to examine bivariate associations among Instagram use, life satisfaction, and locus of control. Partial correlations controlling for locus of control were planned to test the robustness of any observed association between Instagram use and life satisfaction. Hierarchical multiple regression analyses were also conducted with life satisfaction as the dependent variable. In the regression analyses, demographic covariates (age, gender) and locus of control were entered in the first step, and overall Instagram use was entered in the second step to assess incremental predictive value. Additional exploratory regressions examined active and passive subscales of Instagram use as simultaneous predictors. Interaction terms to test potential moderation by locus of control were examined in follow-up models by entering the centered product of locus of control and Instagram use after the main effects. Assumptions for regression analysis were checked including linearity, homoscedasticity, independence of residuals (Durbin-Watson test), and multicollinearity (variance inflation factors). Statistical significance was evaluated at alpha = .05, two-tailed. Effect sizes were reported for correlations and standardized regression coefficients, and 95% confidence intervals were computed for key estimates. Supplementary sensitivity analyses included nonparametric Spearman correlations and a robust regression approach to confirm the stability of results. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The mean life satisfaction score on the LSQ-9 was 41.7 with a standard deviation of 9.2. The rescaled overall IUQ score had a mean of 52.3 and a standard deviation of 18.6 on the 0–100 metric. The mean locus of control score indicated a modest tendency toward internality with a mean of 8.6 on the 23-item scale where higher scores reflect greater externality, and the standard deviation was 4.1. The distributional properties of the primary variables were inspected and were not markedly skewed; LSQ-9 skewness was -0.41 and kurtosis was 0.21; IUQ skewness was 0.12 and kurtosis was -0.35; locus of control skewness was 0.38 and kurtosis was -0.10. Internal consistency estimates were reported above in the Methods and were sufficient to support the use of the scales in subsequent analyses. Bivariate Pearson correlations among the three primary variables were examined. Contrary to the preregistered hypothesis that higher Instagram use would correlate negatively with life satisfaction, the bivariate correlation between the rescaled overall IUQ score and LSQ-9 life satisfaction was positive and statistically significant, r(198) = .21, p = .004. The 95% confidence interval for this correlation ranged from .07 to .34, indicating a small but reliable positive association. Locus of control showed a significant negative correlation with life satisfaction, r(198) = -.28, p < .001, which reflects that more external locus of control was associated with lower life satisfaction in this sample. The correlation between Instagram use and locus of control was small and not statistically significant, r(198) = -.05, p = .45, suggesting that overall Instagram use did not covary strongly with locus of control in the sample. Given the theoretical importance of locus of control, a partial correlation was computed between the IUQ overall score and LSQ-9 life satisfaction controlling for locus of control. The partial correlation remained positive and statistically significant, r_partial(197) = .19, p = .01, indicating that the positive association between Instagram use and life satisfaction persisted after adjusting for locus of control. Confidence intervals for the partial correlation (95% CI = .05 to .32) suggested the effect was modest but not trivially null. Hierarchical multiple regression analyses were then conducted with life satisfaction as the dependent variable. In the first step, demographic covariates (age and gender) and locus of control were entered. This model accounted for R^2 = .09 of the variance in life satisfaction, F(3,196) = 6.40, p < .001. Within this model, locus of control was a significant predictor (standardized beta = -.27, t = -4.15, p < .001) whereas age and gender were not significant predictors at conventional thresholds. In the second step, the overall IUQ score was entered. The addition of Instagram use resulted in a statistically significant increase in explained variance, ΔR^2 = .03, ΔF(1,195) = 7.56, p = .006, bringing the total model R^2 to .12. In the full model, locus of control remained a significant predictor (beta = -.25, t = -3.85, p < .001) and overall Instagram use was also a significant positive predictor (beta = .20, t = 2.75, p = .007). The standardized regression coefficient for Instagram use indicated a small effect size according to conventional benchmarks. The 95% confidence interval for the standardized coefficient for Instagram use was .06 to .34, indicating precision around the positive effect estimate. Diagnostic tests suggested the regression assumptions were adequately met. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) for predictors were all below 1.3, indicating no problematic multicollinearity. The Durbin-Watson statistic was 1.96, suggesting the independence of residuals. Visual inspection of residual plots did not indicate pronounced heteroscedasticity or nonlinear patterns that would undermine the linear model. Supplementary analyses examined the active and passive IUQ subscales as simultaneous predictors of life satisfaction. In a model including locus of control and demographics in the first step and both active and passive subscales in the second step, active Instagram use emerged as a significant positive predictor of life satisfaction (beta = .24, t = 3.30, p = .001), whereas passive Instagram use was not a significant predictor (beta = -.06, t = -0.82, p = .41). The inclusion of the two subscales resulted in a similar increase in explained variance relative to the model with overall IUQ, and the active subscale appeared to drive the positive association. The difference in predictive value between the active and passive subscales was tested and the contrast was significant, indicating that active engagement was more strongly related to higher life satisfaction than passive engagement. An additional set of analyses tested whether locus of control moderated the association between Instagram use and life satisfaction by including an interaction term between centered IUQ score and centered locus of control in the regression. The interaction term was not significant (beta = -.07, t = -1.05, p = .29), and models including the interaction did not exhibit improved fit, suggesting that locus of control did not moderate the relationship in this sample. Nonparametric Spearman correlation analyses produced the same pattern of results, with a positive association between Instagram use and life satisfaction (rho = .18, p = .01), lending robustness to the findings against distributional concerns. Robust regression analyses that down-weighted potential influential points produced similar parameter estimates for the main predictors, further suggesting the observed associations were not driven by a few extreme cases. Exploratory subgroup analyses by gender examined whether the positive association between Instagram use and life satisfaction held for men and women separately. The correlation between IUQ and LSQ-9 was r = .22, p = .02 for women (n = 115) and r = .19, p = .08 for men (n = 82), indicating a similar pattern across genders although the effect for men did not reach conventional statistical significance, likely due to reduced power in the smaller male subsample. Tests of equality of correlation coefficients across gender did not indicate a statistically significant difference. Finally, a median split on overall IUQ scores was used to create low and high Instagram use groups for a follow-up t-test comparison of life satisfaction. Participants in the high Instagram use group reported higher mean life satisfaction (M = 43.1, SD = 8.7) than those in the low use group (M = 40.4, SD = 9.4), t(198) = 2.54, p = .01, Cohen’s d = .29, again indicating a small positive difference. Across the various analyses, the pattern of results consistently indicated a small but reliable positive association between Instagram use—particularly active engagement—and life satisfaction, and this association remained after controlling for locus of control.

Discussion

The present study examined the association between Instagram use and life satisfaction among young adults while statistically controlling for locus of control. Contrary to the preregistered hypothesis that higher Instagram use would correlate negatively with life satisfaction, the primary analyses revealed a small but statistically significant positive association between overall Instagram use and life satisfaction. This positive association remained significant after adjusting for locus of control, and hierarchical regression analyses indicated that Instagram use explained incremental variance in life satisfaction beyond locus of control and basic demographic covariates. Importantly, analyses that decomposed Instagram behavior into active and passive engagement suggested that active forms of use—such as posting content, commenting, and direct messaging—were positively associated with life satisfaction, whereas passive consumption did not show a significant association. The interaction analyses provided no evidence that locus of control moderated the Instagram–life satisfaction link. The results therefore suggest that, at least in this sample of young adults, Instagram use is not uniformly detrimental to global life satisfaction and may even be associated with slightly higher life satisfaction when engagement involves active social interaction. These findings add nuance to the ongoing debate about the psychological effects of social media by highlighting the importance of how users engage with platforms rather than simply how much time they spend on them. The observed positive correlation between Instagram use and life satisfaction is consistent with theoretical accounts that emphasize the social benefits of active engagement. Active use may facilitate social connectedness, social support, and meaningful interactions that contribute to an enhanced sense of well-being. For young adults, the ability to share experiences, receive feedback, and maintain relationships via a platform that is visually rich and socially oriented may serve adaptive functions. Instagram’s affordances for self-expression and community-building may support identity exploration and social integration during a life stage when these processes are salient. Moreover, for some users, Instagram may provide access to communities of interest and informational resources that improve perceived quality of life. The stronger positive association for active versus passive use underscores the relevance of engagement style and aligns with prior theoretical and empirical work suggesting that interactive behaviors have different psychological correlates than solitary consumption. Conversely, the lack of a negative association between overall use and life satisfaction in this study does not negate evidence from other studies that document risks associated with social comparison and unhealthy patterns of use. The balance of potential benefits and harms may depend on multiple contextual and individual factors, including the nature of the content encountered, the user’s motivations, concurrent mental health status, and the broader social environment. For example, exposure to highly curated, idealized images could still trigger negative social comparison processes for some users, particularly those who are vulnerable or who habitually engage in upward comparison. The present study’s findings suggest that, on average, Instagram use in this young adult sample was associated with small positive differences in life satisfaction, but this aggregate pattern conceals heterogeneity in individual experiences. Locus of control emerged as a robust predictor of life satisfaction in the expected direction, with more external locus of control associated with lower life satisfaction. However, locus of control did not account for the positive association between Instagram use and life satisfaction, nor did it moderate the effect. This suggests that the beneficial association of active Instagram engagement with life satisfaction operates independently of the extent to which individuals perceive control over life outcomes. One interpretation is that active engagement provides psychosocial rewards that are available across different orientations toward control. Another interpretation is that the variance in Instagram use relevant to life satisfaction is not strongly patterned by locus of control in this sample. The lack of moderation should be interpreted cautiously, however, because moderation effects can be difficult to detect in samples of this size, and the operationalization of locus of control in a single scale may not capture all relevant nuances. Several methodological considerations and limitations temper the conclusions that can be drawn from the current study. First and foremost, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. While the data show an association, they cannot establish whether greater active Instagram use leads to higher life satisfaction, whether individuals with higher life satisfaction are more likely to engage actively on Instagram, or whether both are influenced by an unmeasured third variable. Longitudinal and experimental designs are necessary to disentangle directionality and temporal dynamics. Second, the reliance on self-report measures introduces potential biases including social desirability and inaccurate recall. Although efforts were made to include multiple items and attention checks, objective measures of Instagram behavior such as passive logging of app use would provide a complementary and potentially more precise perspective. Third, the sample was drawn largely from a university-adjacent population and recruited online, which limits the generalizability of the findings to broader populations and to older or younger age groups. Young adults in other cultural or socioeconomic contexts may show different patterns. Fourth, the Instagram Use Questionnaire, while multi-dimensional, is a self-report instrument developed for the study and thus may have limitations in terms of validity relative to established behavioral tracking methods. Nevertheless, internal consistency for the IUQ and its subscales was acceptable. Fifth, there are many potential confounds that were not measured in this study, such as baseline depressive symptoms, anxiety, interpersonal stressors, offline social network size, and personality traits beyond locus of control, any of which could influence both Instagram use patterns and life satisfaction. Future research would benefit from more comprehensive measurement of these covariates. Notwithstanding these limitations, the study suggests several directions for future work that could deepen understanding and resolve apparent inconsistencies in the literature. First, longitudinal studies that track individuals over time can clarify whether active Instagram engagement predicts subsequent increases in life satisfaction or whether the relationship is reciprocal. Experience sampling methods could capture within-person fluctuations in mood and momentary social media behaviors to determine proximal effects. Second, experimental interventions that manipulate type of engagement—encouraging active versus passive behaviors—could provide causal tests of whether changes in engagement style produce changes in well-being. Third, integrating objective behavioral data from app logs with self-report measures would permit examination of actual patterns of use, such as time of day, session duration, and content categories, and how these relate to subjective outcomes. Fourth, research should examine moderators such as baseline mental health, trait vulnerability to social comparison, and the valence of feedback received on the platform, to identify for whom Instagram use is likely to be beneficial versus harmful. Fifth, qualitative studies could explore subjective experiences of Instagram users to provide richer contextual understanding of how the platform is used to build social capital, seek support, or navigate identity development. Sixth, given the platforms’ evolving features and algorithmic curation, longitudinal research across cohorts could illuminate whether changing platform affordances alter the nature of psychological associations. Theoretically, the findings contribute to a more nuanced perspective on social media and well-being that emphasizes heterogeneity in effects. Rather than conceptualizing social media as uniformly good or bad, the data point toward a conditional model in which user motivations, patterns of engagement, and contextual factors determine outcomes. This view aligns with frameworks that distinguish between active and passive engagement and that highlight the social functions of media use. In applied terms, the results suggest potential avenues for promoting well-being in social media contexts. Encouraging active, socially engaged behaviors that foster connection and reciprocity may be a fruitful strategy for users and designers who wish to maximize psychological benefits. Educational interventions could teach users about the different implications of active versus passive use and provide strategies for leveraging platforms to maintain relationships and access supportive communities. Platform designers might also consider features that facilitate genuine social interaction rather than merely prolonged consumption. The present study also underscores the importance of considering individual differences like locus of control as robust predictors of life satisfaction, deserving attention in both research and practice when assessing digital behaviors. In conclusion, this correlational study of young adults found a small but reliable positive association between Instagram use and life satisfaction, particularly for active engagement, and this association persisted after controlling for locus of control. The results caution against simplistic assumptions that increased Instagram use necessarily undermines life satisfaction and instead point to a more complex pattern in which the nature of engagement matters. Future longitudinal, experimental, and multi-method research will be needed to establish causal pathways, identify moderators and mediators, and translate findings into interventions that help users derive the benefits of social media while minimizing potential harms.
