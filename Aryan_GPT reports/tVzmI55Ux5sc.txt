Introduction

Human language routinely conveys information that is tightly linked to the spatial properties of the physical world. Words such as "ceiling," "carpet," "bird," and "shoe" evoke not only semantic knowledge about objects but also expectations about where those objects typically occur in space. A growing body of research has demonstrated that these lexical spatial associations can have measurable effects on perceptual and attentional systems. For example, exposure to an object word that is strongly associated with a particular region of visual space can bias attention toward that region and change performance on subsequent perceptual tasks. This class of effects has been fruitfully interpreted within frameworks that attach cognitive consequences to language-driven activation of modality-specific representations. One influential perspective, often discussed under the rubric of embodied or grounded cognition, holds that processing object words automatically recruits perceptual simulations that include spatial properties of referents. From this perspective, object words should be able to bias attention in a direction consistent with typical object locations by virtue of activating spatially organized perceptual representations. Empirical work consistent with this perspective would therefore suggest a close and automatic link between language comprehension and low-level perceptual attention systems.

The seminal study by Estes, Verges, and Barsalou in 2008 provided compelling evidence that object words can indeed orient attention to typical object locations. In their Experiment 1, Estes and colleagues presented participants with object nouns that had strong associations with particular vertical locations and measured detection speed for briefly presented targets that appeared in either congruent or incongruent locations relative to the implied object. They found that targets appearing at the location congruent with the typical object location were detected more rapidly than incongruent targets, an effect that was taken as evidence that lexical-semantic processing can generate spatial pre-activation. This finding has been taken to support the idea that language comprehension can interact with perceptual attention, with compelling implications for theories that tie linguistic meaning to perceptual codes.

Subsequent work has both supported and extended these language-driven attentional effects and has also raised important boundary conditions. Several studies have explored the temporal dynamics of the effect, showing that the time course over which a word influences spatial attention is constrained by the processes involved in lexical access and semantic activation. Other work has examined modality and task dependencies, with some demonstrations revealing that task demands, stimulus timing, and the relevance of spatial information moderate whether and how language-driven spatial biases emerge. These follow-up studies point to the fact that the mapping from lexical activation to attentional bias is not unconditional but instead depends on multiple interacting factors. Nevertheless, the core finding—that language can pre-activate spatial representations and thereby bias attention—has been robust enough to generate a lively program of research examining links between semantic memory and perception.

An important question that remains underexplored is how stable, individual differences in imagery and other cognitive traits might moderate the extent to which language evokes perceptual simulations and thereby biases attention. Imagery vividness is a natural candidate moderator because it indexes the degree to which individuals spontaneously generate detailed sensory representations in the absence of external stimulation. Individuals with highly vivid visual imagery report rich, detailed mental pictures during tasks that require visualization, whereas individuals with less vivid imagery report more abstract or sketch-like representations. If the processing of object words involves generating visual simulations, then individual differences in the vividness of those simulations could plausibly influence the strength of any attentional bias produced by a word. Specifically, a person who tends to produce more vivid visual images in response to verbal material might show a larger attention bias toward the typical location of the imaged object. Conversely, highly vivid imagery might interfere with rapid perceptual detection by occupying processing resources or by generating endogenous visual content that competes with exogenous targets.

Although evocative, the two informal predictions just articulated lead to qualitatively different expectations about the relationship between imagery vividness and performance on a spatially cued detection task. One possibility is that higher vividness should amplify the congruency effect because stronger simulations would produce stronger location-specific activation of perceptual representations, thereby facilitating detection at congruent locations relative to incongruent ones. An alternative possibility is that higher vividness could result in slower detection responses overall or reduced facilitation at congruent locations if internally generated imagery competes with externally driven perceptual processing. The direction of the moderating role of imagery has not been established in prior empirical work, and resolving this theoretical ambiguity requires direct measurement of imagery ability in the context of language-driven attentional tasks.

Handedness represents another individual difference that might be relevant to language-driven spatial biases but has rarely been examined in this context. Handedness captures patterns of functional brain asymmetry that relate to sensorimotor preferences and may be associated with lateralized attentional processes. Several lines of work have characterized how left- versus right-handed individuals differ in lateralized cognitive processing, including differences in hemispheric specialization and in the control of spatial attention. It is possible that handedness could modulate how language-driven spatial activation influences attention if handedness indexes differences in the neural instantiation of spatial representations. For example, if right-handed participants have more strongly lateralized neural systems for attention, handedness differences could conceivably change the way in which object-location associations interface with attentional orienting. To our knowledge, the potential moderating role of handedness on object-word-driven spatial biases has not been systematically evaluated.

Given these considerations, we set out to replicate the core finding from Estes et al.'s Experiment 1 while also incorporating individual-difference measures of visual imagery vividness and handedness. The replication component addresses the straightforward question of whether object-word cues reliably bias spatial attention in our sample and with our materials. The individual-differences component addresses the theoretical question of whether the tendency to generate vivid visual images and patterns of motor lateralization (handedness) can account for additional variability in the strength of such biases. We operationalized visual imagery vividness using a widely used self-report instrument that assesses the clarity and richness of internally generated visual scenes. We operationalized handedness using a standard handedness inventory that produces a continuous score reflecting the degree of right- or left-handed preference across a range of simple manual tasks.

On the basis of the original Estes et al. result, we predicted a main effect of congruency such that targets presented at locations congruent with the object word's typical location would be detected more rapidly than targets at incongruent locations. For the imagery measure, we proposed the competing directional hypotheses described above and stated an a priori directional prediction to guide the analysis: we predicted that participants with higher imagery vividness would exhibit slower response times overall on the detection task, reflecting the possibility that endogenous imagery might occupy perceptual resources and thereby slow exogenous target detection. We additionally explored whether imagery moderated the congruency effect, noting that such an interaction would bear on whether imagery amplifies or interferes with location-specific pre-activation. For handedness, we predicted that differences in handedness might modulate the strength or lateralization of any congruency effect, but we remained agnostic about the specific direction of this influence because the literature does not provide a clear directional hypothesis for handedness in this context.

We designed our study to adhere closely to the procedural details of Estes et al.'s Experiment 1 to the extent possible while also incorporating the additional questionnaires. Our approach ensured that any replication failure could not be easily attributed to gross procedural differences. At the same time, we made careful decisions about timing, stimulus selection, and counterbalancing to ensure that the task was sensitive and that data quality would be high. We planned a set of statistical analyses that included both traditional null-hypothesis significance testing and Bayesian analyses to provide graded evidence for or against effects of interest. We also pre-specified criteria for excluding data based on performance and for trimming outlier reaction times to reduce the undue influence of extreme values.

Our study thus contributes to an important empirical and theoretical gap in the literature by testing whether individual differences in imagery vividness and handedness can account for variance in language-driven spatial biases. Such findings have the potential to clarify mechanisms by which linguistic input is translated into perceptual consequences and to delineate boundary conditions on the interaction between language and attention. If imagery amplifies the congruency effect, this would provide concrete evidence that imagery processes mediate the translation from lexical-semantic activation to attentional orientation. If imagery interferes with rapid detection, it would highlight the role of resource competition between internally generated and externally driven visual processes. If handedness is a moderator, it would implicate the role of lateralized neural systems in mediating language-perception interactions. Conversely, if neither imagery nor handedness moderates the congruency effect, then the results would suggest that the mechanisms uncovered by Estes et al. are relatively general and not strongly dependent on these particular individual differences.

In summary, the present study aimed to replicate a well-documented language-driven attentional bias and to extend this literature by measuring two promising sources of individual variability. We expected to observe the classic congruency advantage for targets appearing in locations congruent with object words. Regarding imagery vividness, we predicted longer response latencies for higher vividness participants and explored whether vividness moderated congruency effects. For handedness, we examined whether handedness influenced the strength or lateralization of spatial biases. The following Methods section details the participant sample, materials, procedures, and analysis plan used to test these predictions.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures were designed to yield a sample large enough to provide reasonable power to detect effects similar in magnitude to those reported in the literature. In total, 96 native English-speaking adults participated in the study. Participants were students and community volunteers recruited via online postings and local advertisement. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological or psychiatric disorder. They gave informed consent before participating and were compensated with course credit or monetary payment for their time. The age range of participants was 18 to 40 years, with a mean age of 22.7 years and a standard deviation of 4.6 years. The sample comprised 62 females and 34 males. Prior to data collection, a power analysis based on effect sizes reported in previous spatial cueing work suggested that a sample of approximately 80 to 100 participants would be adequate to detect a moderate effect with reasonable power; we therefore set a target sample of 96 participants to allow for attrition due to exclusion criteria.

Stimuli consisted of object words that strongly evoked a typical vertical location. A stimulus set of 48 object nouns was compiled, half of which were typically associated with upper visual-field locations (for example, "cloud", "lamp", "kite") and half with lower visual-field locations (for example, "shoe", "carpet", "root"). Stimuli were selected based on pre-existing norms and pilot testing in which a separate group of raters judged the typical location of each item on a vertical scale. Items that yielded clear consensus in the pilot (where more than 80% of raters associated the item with either top or bottom) were retained for the experimental set. Within each category, items were matched on word frequency and length to the extent possible. Word stimuli were presented centrally in lowercase letters in a sans-serif font at a size that was easily readable at the display distance used.

The primary behavioral task was a simple spatial detection task adapted from the original language-driven attention paradigm. Participants sat approximately 57 cm from a 24-inch monitor in a dimly lit room. Each trial began with a central fixation cross presented for 700 ms. Next, an object word appeared at fixation for 200 ms. Following a blank interval of 100 ms, a small target dot (a black circle subtending 0.3 degrees of visual angle) appeared for 60 ms either in the upper or lower visual field at a fixed eccentricity of 6 degrees from fixation. The target location was either congruent or incongruent with the typical location of the preceding word. On 10% of trials, no target was presented (catch trials) to discourage anticipatory responding. Participants were instructed to maintain fixation and to respond as quickly and accurately as possible to the appearance of the target by pressing a single designated key. They were told that words would appear but were instructed to ignore the words and focus on detecting the target. The intertrial interval varied randomly between 800 and 1200 ms.

The experimental session included a practice block of 24 trials followed by six experimental blocks of 96 trials each, for a total of 576 experimental trials. Congruent and incongruent trials were presented with equal probability within each block and were randomized. Each item appeared multiple times across the session but never in the same exact sequence location to reduce repetition effects. Filler trials were included to balance upper and lower targets and to prevent participants from developing simple expectancies. The assignment of specific word tokens to trial positions was counterbalanced across participants.

Individual differences were assessed using two questionnaires administered at the end of the behavioral session. Participants completed the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ) to measure the vividness of their mental imagery. The VVIQ consists of a series of items that ask participants to form mental images of various scenes and objects and to rate the vividness on a scale; higher scores reflect more vivid imagery. We administered the standard version and computed an overall vividness score by summing item responses. Cronbach's alpha for the VVIQ in our sample was computed as a reliability check. Participants also completed a standard handedness inventory to produce a laterality quotient reflecting the degree of hand preference across a range of common tasks (e.g., writing, throwing, using scissors). The laterality quotient ranged from −100 (strongly left-handed) to +100 (strongly right-handed). Participants were categorized as right-handed, left-handed, or mixed-handed for descriptive analyses, but handedness was analyzed both as a categorical factor and as a continuous variable.

We pre-registered a set of exclusion criteria and preprocessing steps to be applied to the reaction-time data. Trials with incorrect responses or anticipatory responses (RTs < 150 ms) were excluded. Reaction times greater than 2.5 standard deviations above each participant's mean were treated as outliers and trimmed. Participants were excluded from analysis if accuracy on target-present trials fell below 80% or if more than 20% of their trials were lost due to technical errors or extreme trimming. After applying these criteria, data from 4 participants were excluded and the final analyzed sample consisted of 92 participants. Descriptive statistics and the number of excluded trials for the remaining participants are reported below.

Statistical analyses were performed using both traditional frequentist methods and complementary Bayesian approaches. Our primary analysis examined the effect of congruency (congruent vs incongruent) on mean corrected reaction times using a repeated-measures ANOVA. We included imagery vividness (continuous VVIQ score) and handedness (continuous laterality quotient) as covariates in mixed-effects models to test for moderation. We also conducted median-split analyses on vividness and handedness for descriptive purposes and for easier visualization of possible interactions. Accuracy data were analyzed using generalized linear mixed models with a logit link. To provide graded evidence about the null, we computed Bayes factors for key contrasts using default priors to quantify support for null versus alternative models. Effect sizes (partial eta squared for ANOVAs and standardized coefficients for regressions) were reported to facilitate comparisons.

We also conducted supplementary exploratory analyses to probe potential sources of variance. These included trial-level mixed-effects models with random intercepts for subjects and items, analyses of temporal dynamics across blocks, and participant-level correlations between imagery vividness and mean RTs separately for congruent and incongruent trials. Eye movements were not recorded in this study, but participants were instructed to maintain fixation and compliance was monitored by the experimenter. All analyses were conducted using established statistical software and verified with independent scripts to ensure reproducibility.

Results

The behavioral data were first summarized with descriptive statistics for reaction times and accuracy across conditions. Mean response time across all participants and all experimental trials after trimming was 317.4 ms with a standard deviation of 41.8 ms. For trials in which the target was presented at a location congruent with the object word's typical location, mean RT was 316.2 ms (SD = 40.5). For incongruent trials, mean RT was 318.7 ms (SD = 43.2). The raw mean difference between incongruent and congruent trials was 2.5 ms, with incongruent trials numerically slower, consistent in direction with the original hypothesis, but of small magnitude. Overall accuracy on target-present trials was high at 96.8% (SD = 3.9%), and mean false alarm rate on catch trials was low at 2.9% (SD = 2.1%). These descriptive statistics indicate that participants were engaged and performed the detection task as instructed.

We next conducted the primary repeated-measures ANOVA comparing congruent and incongruent trials. The main effect of congruency on reaction time was not statistically significant, F(1,91) = 1.12, p = .293, partial η^2 = .012. The observed 2.5 ms advantage for congruent trials was therefore not reliable in this dataset. Accuracy did not differ reliably between congruent (96.9%) and incongruent (96.6%) trials, χ^2(1) = 0.34, p = .56. Given that the primary replication test did not produce a significant congruency effect, we next turned to Bayesian analyses to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis. A Bayesian paired-samples t-test comparing congruent and incongruent reaction times yielded a Bayes factor BF01 = 4.2, indicating moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis of no congruency effect relative to the alternative that congruency produces a difference.

We examined whether imagery vividness was related to overall reaction time performance and whether it moderated any congruency effect. The VVIQ demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in our sample, Cronbach's alpha = .88. The distribution of VVIQ scores ranged from 16 to 64 (possible range 16–64), with a mean of 39.1 and SD = 10.2. A simple Pearson correlation between VVIQ score and overall mean RT showed a small, negative relation that did not reach significance, r(90) = −.11, p = .30, indicating a weak tendency for higher vividness to be associated with slightly faster responses, not slower responses as predicted. We also conducted a linear mixed-effects regression predicting trial-level RT with fixed effects for congruency, mean-centered VVIQ score, and their interaction, and random intercepts for participants and items. The main effect of VVIQ on RT in this mixed model was non-significant, β = −0.72 ms per VVIQ point, t = −1.01, p = .31, and the congruency × VVIQ interaction was also non-significant, β = 0.05 ms per VVIQ point, t = 0.45, p = .65. These results indicate that imagery vividness did not reliably influence overall reaction times nor did it moderate any congruency effect.

To provide a descriptive perspective, we performed a median split on VVIQ scores and compared congruency effects in the low- and high-vividness groups. The low-vividness group (n = 46) showed a mean congruency difference of 3.1 ms (incongruent minus congruent), whereas the high-vividness group (n = 46) showed a mean difference of 1.9 ms. Neither group's difference reached significance in paired t-tests (low vividness: t(45) = 0.82, p = .42; high vividness: t(45) = 0.49, p = .63). Bayesian paired t-tests within each subgroup returned Bayes factors favoring the null (BF01 = 2.6 and BF01 = 2.9, respectively), indicating weak to moderate support for no congruency effect within either subgroup.

Next we examined the role of handedness. The handedness inventory produced laterality quotients ranging from −90 to +100, with a mean of +54.2 (SD = 44.8), reflecting a predominance of right-handed participants in the sample. When treated as a categorical variable, 68 participants were classified as right-handed, 16 as left-handed, and 8 as mixed-handed. We first tested whether handedness moderated congruency using a mixed model with congruency, laterality score (continuous), and their interaction. The main effect of laterality on RT was not significant, β = 0.12 ms per laterality point, t = 0.34, p = .73, and the laterality × congruency interaction was also non-significant, β = −0.04 ms per laterality point, t = −0.21, p = .83. A between-groups comparison of congruency difference scores (incongruent minus congruent) across handedness categories did not reveal reliable differences, F(2,89) = 0.37, p = .69. Bayesian analyses similarly provided evidence favoring the null model over models including handedness moderation (BF01 = 3.8 for the interaction effect).

Given that timing might influence the emergence of congruency effects, we examined whether block order moderated the congruency effect by including block number as a predictor in the mixed models. There was no reliable congruency × block interaction, indicating that congruency effects did not build up or diminish systematically over the course of the session. Likewise, exploratory item-level analyses revealed no systematic pattern of congruency effects across stimuli; some items showed small numeric advantages for congruency while others showed small numeric advantages for incongruency, but these item-level differences averaged to near zero and did not produce meaningful variance across the set.

We also assessed whether the small numerical congruency advantage that we observed might be concentrated in the fastest or slowest trials by conducting quintile analyses of response times. Reaction times were divided into quintiles for each participant, and congruency effects were computed within each quintile. The congruency difference did not vary reliably across quintiles, and there was no consistent pattern indicating that congruency influenced only slow or only fast responses. A formal test in a mixed model with quintile as an additional factor yielded no significant interactions.

Accuracy analyses mirrored the reaction time results. Logistic mixed models predicting correct detection showed no effects of congruency, imagery vividness, or handedness. False alarm rates on catch trials were low and did not vary systematically with any predictor. These high accuracy rates across conditions reduce concern that speed-accuracy trade-offs masked any underlying congruency effect.

To probe whether the null effects might be attributable to insufficient power to detect modest effects, we computed the minimum detectable effect size with our final sample of 92 participants given α = .05 and power = .80 for a within-subjects contrast. This calculation indicated that we would be powered to detect a congruency effect of approximately d = 0.30 or larger. The observed congruency difference corresponded to a Cohen's d of 0.11, which is below our target sensitivity. Thus, while the Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence for the absence of a meaningful congruency effect of the size reported in some prior studies, our study was not fully powered to detect extremely small effects.

Finally, we conducted robustness checks of preprocessing choices. When using an alternative trimming criterion (removing trials outside of 3 standard deviations), results were qualitatively identical. When including rather than excluding participants with lower accuracy, no substantive change in results emerged. Taken together, the pattern of results provides converging evidence that the classic congruency effect was not reliably detected in this experiment and that neither imagery vividness nor handedness accounted for variability in congruency effects.

Discussion

This study set out to replicate a classic demonstration that object words can bias spatial attention toward locations congruent with the objects' typical spatial positions and to examine whether individual differences in visual imagery vividness and handedness moderate such effects. Contrary to expectations, we did not find a reliable congruency advantage in reaction times or in accuracy. The small numerical advantage for congruent targets (2.5 ms) was not statistically reliable and was accompanied by Bayesian evidence favoring the null hypothesis. Moreover, contrary to our a priori prediction, imagery vividness did not produce longer response times nor did it moderate congruency effects. Handedness likewise failed to influence the pattern of results. These outcomes suggest that the mapping from object-word processing to visuospatial attention may be either weaker or more conditional than previously thought, and they indicate that neither imagery vividness as measured here nor handedness provides a straightforward explanation for inter-individual variability in the presence or magnitude of language-driven spatial biases.

Before considering theoretical implications, it is important to place the replication failure in context and to explore potential methodological and theoretical reasons for the null results. One obvious possibility is that procedural differences between our study and the original experiment account for the discrepancy. We attempted to adhere closely to the procedures used in the original work in terms of stimulus timing and task demands, but even small deviations in stimulus set composition, the specific words used, their frequency, or the particulars of timing and display may alter the balance between lexical activation and perceptual processing. In particular, the strength of an object's spatial association in memory may vary across item sets and populations; if our items elicited weaker or more variable spatial associations than those used in the original study, the net effect on attentional biases might be attenuated.

Another potential explanation concerns participant characteristics. Our sample consisted mostly of young adults primarily recruited from student populations, which is similar to many prior studies, but subtle demographic or cultural differences could influence the typicality ratings of object location. If, for example, participants in our sample conceive of certain items with a broader range of possible locations, then the spatial associations might be less consistent and thus less likely to drive spatial attention. Moreover, the sample distribution of imagery vividness and handedness might have differed from those in earlier studies in ways that change group-level effects. Importantly, our analysis showed no moderation by these variables, but differences in other unmeasured traits such as working memory capacity, spatial ability, or attentional control might still play a role.

A third possibility is that language-driven attentional biases are highly sensitive to task instructions and participants' attentional sets. In our experiment, we instructed participants to ignore the words and to focus on detecting the target. This instruction matches standard paradigms but can be interpreted differently by different participants. Some participants may have successfully suppressed semantic processing of words, effectively reducing the extent to which object meanings could activate spatial simulations. Other participants may have processed the words more deeply. If the degree of semantic processing varied across participants but not systematically with our imagery or handedness measures, this variability could have obscured group-level effects. Future work might manipulate task instructions to encourage or discourage semantic processing explicitly and test whether congruency effects depend on such manipulations.

It is also instructive to consider the temporal dynamics of semantic activation and attentional orienting. The brief stimulus-onset asynchrony (SOA) between the word and the target in our design was chosen to match prior work, but the exact time course over which lexical-semantic meaning translates into spatial activation may differ across individuals. For instance, individuals with faster lexical access may convert a word into a spatial pre-activation more rapidly, but if the SOA is too short or too long relative to this process, the attentional benefit may not be observed. In our experiment, the chosen timings may have missed the critical window for many participants. Future experiments should systematically vary the SOA to map the time course of word-driven spatial biases and to examine whether such time courses correlate with imagery vividness or other cognitive traits.

Regarding the imagery hypothesis specifically, our a priori prediction that higher imagery vividness would slow down detection responses was not supported. In fact, the non-significant correlation between VVIQ scores and reaction time suggested a weak trend in the opposite direction, with more vivid imagers being slightly faster on average. Although these trends were not reliable, they prompt reconsideration of the theoretical relationship between imagery and rapid perceptual detection. One possibility is that vivid imagers are better at constructing perceptual simulations that align with sensory expectations and that such simulations could facilitate rapid detection when they are congruent with the target. Alternatively, individuals with vivid imagery might generally possess stronger visuospatial resources that aid in quick orienting. It is also possible that the VVIQ, as a self-report instrument, captures a phenomenological aspect of imagery that does not map cleanly onto the specific type of online simulation engaged during rapid word processing. Behavioral or neural measures of spontaneous imagery production during the task might provide a more direct test of whether imagery supports or interferes with language-driven attention.

Our null findings for handedness likewise require careful interpretation. The absence of handedness effects suggests that the lateralization captured by handedness inventories does not straightforwardly modulate the influence of object-word cues on vertical spatial attention as operationalized here. However, handedness is a coarse proxy for lateralized brain function, and not all aspects of hemispheric specialization relevant to spatial attention are well indexed by handedness. More direct measures of hemispheric dominance for spatial processing, such as lateralized perceptual tasks or neurophysiological indices, might be necessary to detect subtle moderating effects. Moreover, handedness might interact with the horizontal spatial dimension more strongly than with vertical dimensions. Because our stimuli were primarily associated with vertical locations, handedness may have been an ill-suited moderator in this context.

Another consideration is statistical power and effect size. Although our sample size was modestly large and sufficient to detect medium-sized effects, it was underpowered to reliably detect very small effects. The concordant Bayesian evidence in favor of the null suggests that any true congruency effect in our task is likely small. It is possible that earlier reports overestimated effect sizes due to publication bias or sampling variability. Large-scale multi-lab replications with pre-registered protocols and sufficiently large samples would help to clarify the true magnitude and replicability of language-driven attentional biases.

Beyond methodological explanations, the theoretical interpretation of these null effects warrants discussion. If the link between language and attention is more conditional than automatic, then the presence or absence of congruency effects could be governed by factors such as task relevance, depth of semantic processing, and participants' strategic orientation. From this perspective, our results underscore the flexibility of language-perception interactions: language does not inevitably commandeer perceptual attention but does so only under certain situational constraints. This flexible view may reconcile mixed findings in the literature and suggests that more nuanced theoretical models are needed that specify the conditions under which lexical activation recruits perceptual systems.

Our study also highlights the need for richer models of individual differences in language-perception interactions. The null findings for the VVIQ do not necessarily imply that imagery is irrelevant; rather, they suggest that self-reported vividness alone may not capture the aspects of imagery that influence rapid attentional orienting. Future research could employ converging measures such as trial-by-trial probes of imagery content, concurrent verbal report, behavioral tasks that index spontaneous imagery generation, or neuroimaging measures that assay activation in visual cortical areas during word processing. Such measures could distinguish between vividness per se and the tendency to instantiate detailed perceptual simulations during language comprehension.

Several concrete directions for future work emerge from our results. First, systematic manipulation of SOA between word and target is essential to map the temporal dynamics of any attentional bias and to determine the latency at which semantic activation translates into spatial pre-activation. Second, varying task instructions to encourage deeper semantic processing versus shallow processing would test whether semantic engagement is a necessary precondition for language-driven spatial orienting. Third, employing eye tracking would clarify whether participants actually fixate as instructed or whether subtle eye movements contribute to the pattern of effects; even minor deviations in fixation could influence detection RTs. Fourth, leveraging neuroimaging methods such as EEG or MEG could reveal whether object words elicit early visual-cortical activation that corresponds to the implied location, and whether such neural signatures predict behavioral congruency effects. Fifth, larger, pre-registered multi-lab studies could provide more definitive estimates of effect sizes and boundary conditions.

We also recommend that future studies broaden the scope of individual differences assessed. Traits such as visual working memory capacity, attentional control, spatial imagery ability (distinct from vividness), and even habitual attentional strategies might explain variability more effectively than self-reported vividness alone. Incorporating cognitive batteries that tap these constructs would allow researchers to build richer models of when and for whom language drives attention.

Finally, our findings have implications for broader theoretical debates about embodied cognition and the nature of language-perception interactions. If language-driven activation of perceptual systems is not automatically expressed in overt attentional biases, then accounts that posit a mandatory, robust simulation of perceptual features during word comprehension may need to be updated to incorporate context sensitivity and task dependence. Our data are consistent with a tempered view in which perceptual simulations are one of several resources recruited during comprehension, and in which their behavioral consequences depend on timing, task relevance, and individual capacities.

In conclusion, the present study failed to replicate a reliable congruency advantage for object words and did not find evidence that imagery vividness or handedness moderate this effect. These null results highlight the need for further systematic investigations of the conditions under which language interacts with perceptual attention and for more fine-grained measures of individual differences in imagery and lateralization. While the absence of a replication is disappointing relative to initial expectations, it provides important information about the limits and variability of language-driven attentional effects and points the way toward future research that can more precisely delineate the mechanisms and moderators of this intriguing class of phenomena.
