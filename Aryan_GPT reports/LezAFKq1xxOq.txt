Introduction

Faces are among the most important visual stimuli that humans encounter in their daily social environments. They convey a wealth of information about identity, intention, and affect, and they play a central role in social communication. From infancy, humans are tuned to detect faces rapidly and to extract socially relevant information from them. This rapid extraction supports immediate behavioral decisions in social contexts, such as approach, avoidance, or affiliative responses. One class of information that is extracted quickly from faces is emotional expression, and emotional expressions are known to capture attention in ways that neutral faces do not. Emotional expressions can act as attention-guiding cues that orient observers toward particular individuals within a cluttered visual scene. The ability to find an emotional face in a crowd has been studied extensively using visual search paradigms in which observers are asked to locate a face with a particular expression among distractor faces. A robust set of findings indicates that certain expressions are detected more efficiently than others under visual search. For example, some research has reported an advantage for angry faces, often interpreted as a threat detection advantage that is adaptive for survival. The threat advantage account proposes that angry or fearful faces are prioritized by the attentional system because they signal potential danger in the environment. Other research, however, has demonstrated a detection advantage for happy faces, particularly in tasks in which configural or coarse processing is beneficial. Happy faces are often more visually salient in terms of exposed teeth, contrast, and characteristic feature configurations that can be efficiently processed in a glance. Thus, the literature on which expressions are advantaged in visual search is mixed and suggests that multiple factors determine search efficiency for emotional faces. Task demands, stimulus properties, number of distractors, and the visual characteristics of the emotional expressions themselves can all influence whether a threat or a positive advantage emerges.

Beyond the static characteristics of stimuli and tasks, the performance of observers can be shaped by learning and practice. Perceptual learning and repeated task exposure typically lead to faster and more efficient visual search performance across many domains. Practice can reduce response times, enhance accuracy, and flatten search slopes that index search efficiency. In the domain of face perception, practice can sharpen the tuning of mechanisms that extract identity and expression, and it can facilitate the use of diagnostic features that distinguish targets from distractors. Repeated exposure to specific visual tasks can lead to more automatic deployment of attention, improved target-template formation, and more efficient use of visual short-term memory. These improvements could plausibly operate differentially across emotional expressions, because expressions differ in their diagnostic features and the degree to which they rely on configural versus featural processing. If practice allows observers to learn diagnostic featural combinations that define a particular expression, then expressions that are more featurally distinct might benefit more from practice. Conversely, if practice benefits arise primarily from general improvements in attentional deployment, then similar improvements might be expected across expressions.

Another factor that has been proposed to shape attention to emotional faces is the observer’s current mood state. Mood is known to influence cognitive processing broadly, producing mood-congruent biases in memory, judgment, and attention in many contexts. When people are in a positive mood, they may be more likely to attend to positive stimuli and to interpret ambiguous events in a positive light. Conversely, negative mood states can bias attention toward negative or threatening information and can increase sensitivity to potential dangers. Mood-congruency effects in attention have been demonstrated in various tasks, including dot-probe paradigms, memory search, and gaze-cuing tasks. Given that mood can bias the allocation of attention, it seems plausible that mood could modulate visual search for emotional faces, leading observers to find mood-congruent expressions more quickly. However, findings on mood effects on visual attention are also mixed. Some studies find robust mood-congruent attentional biases, while others find negligible or context-dependent mood effects. Factors that may determine whether mood exerts a detectable influence include the strength and ecological validity of the mood induction, the salience of the target stimuli, individual differences in affective reactivity, and the cognitive demands of the task.

Against this background, the question of how practice and mood interact to shape the speed and efficiency of finding an emotional face in a crowd remains open. Practice and mood are both plausible modulators of search performance, but they may operate at different levels and through different mechanisms. Practice effects may reflect changes in perceptual encoding, target-template specificity, or attentional deployment that accrue with repeated exposure to the search task. These changes are likely to be relatively stimulus-specific and to depend on the features that define the target expression. Mood effects, by contrast, likely reflect top-down motivational and affective biases that influence the priority assigned to different kinds of stimuli. If practice effects are strong and stimulus-driven, they may override subtle mood biases, whereas if mood effects are strong they may interact with practice to produce differential learning rates for mood-congruent versus incongruent targets.

To date, relatively few studies have directly tested whether practice modulates the search advantage for particular emotional expressions and whether mood moderates these practice effects. A direct test requires a design in which observers perform repeated blocks of a visual search task that includes targets of different emotional valences, while their mood is measured or manipulated. Such a design can reveal whether practice leads to overall speed-ups in detection times, whether the magnitude of improvement differs across emotional valences, and whether mood interacts with these learning trajectories. It can also reveal how practice influences search efficiency in terms of search slopes across different set sizes, which provides a measure of how attention is being deployed as a function of the number of distractors. Moreover, assessing accuracy alongside response times can clarify whether speed-accuracy trade-offs account for any observed practice effects.

The present study was designed to address these issues by examining practice effects on visual search for emotional faces and testing whether observer mood relates to search performance. We selected two emotionally distinct target expressions that have both been implicated in prior search literature: happy and angry expressions. These expressions offer a contrast between positive and negatively valenced cues that are commonly used to study attentional biases. We presented these targets among neutral distractors in search arrays of varying set sizes to derive search slopes and to estimate changes in efficiency with practice. We implemented a practice regimen that consisted of multiple blocks of trials to allow learning-related changes to manifest across the experimental session. To probe mood influences, we measured participants’ state mood before the task using a well-established self-report affect scale and also included a between-subjects mood induction manipulation to enhance mood variability. This combined measurement-and-manipulation approach was intended to provide a sensitive test of mood effects on emotional face search.

We derived several empirical predictions from prior literature and from theoretical considerations. First, following general perceptual learning findings, we predicted an overall practice-related decrease in response times and an increase in search efficiency across blocks. Second, we considered two alternative predictions about valence-specific practice effects. One possibility, based on threat-detection priorities, is that negative expressions such as anger would show a strong and perhaps immediate search advantage that may leave less room for practice-related improvement. An alternative possibility, based on featural distinctiveness and prior happy-face advantages, is that happy faces would benefit more from practice because their characteristic features are amenable to rapid feature-based learning. Third, with regard to mood, we hypothesized that participants in a positive mood would show relatively faster detection of happy faces and participants in a negative mood would show relatively faster detection of angry faces, consistent with mood-congruent attentional bias accounts. Finally, we expected that mood might interact with practice, such that mood-congruent advantages would either emerge early and be sustained or would interact with learning dynamics to produce differential rates of improvement.

The present investigation thus sought to provide a detailed characterization of how practice alters the search for emotional faces and whether mood exerts an additional modulatory influence. By combining repeated blocks of visual search with mood assessment and manipulation, we aimed to separate stimulus-driven learning effects from top-down affective biases. If practice dominates, then learning-related improvements should be observed irrespective of mood, and their magnitude may differ across target expressions based on stimulus properties. If mood plays a role, then we should observe mood-congruent performance differences or interactions with practice. These outcomes have implications for understanding the mechanisms by which social attention is tuned through experience and how affective states shape attention in social contexts. They also bear on applied concerns, such as training regimes for professions that require rapid detection of emotional cues and interventions that might modify affective biases in clinical populations.

In addition to testing these primary hypotheses, the present study aimed to provide diagnostic measures of search efficiency by estimating search slopes across set sizes and by examining accuracy rates and potential speed-accuracy trade-offs. These measures make it possible to assess whether practice leads to more automatic detection (as indexed by flatter slopes) or simply to faster responses without changes in underlying search processes. They also allow us to test whether improvements reflect better discrimination between target and distractor expressions or merely changes in response criterion. The inclusion of set size manipulations and target-absent trials is therefore critical to interpreting the locus of any practice-related changes and their relationship to mood.

Finally, the study was designed with methodological control and ecological considerations in mind. We used standardized facial expression stimuli to ensure consistency across trials, randomised target positions to avoid location learning effects, and counterbalanced mood induction conditions to avoid systematic confounds. The sample size and trial numbers were chosen to provide sufficient statistical power to detect medium-sized effects and interactions among block, valence, and mood factors. Overall, the study was positioned to contribute to debates about the relative roles of stimulus-driven learning and state mood in shaping the rapid detection of social signals in complex visual environments.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from local community advertisements. Eighty participants completed the study in exchange for course credit or a small monetary payment. The sample included both male and female participants and reflected a range of ethnic backgrounds typical of the university community. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological disorders. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation, and the study was approved by the institutional review board. The experiment took place in a quiet laboratory room, and stimuli were presented on a calibrated computer display. Participants were seated approximately 60 cm from the screen and were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible.

Facial stimuli were drawn from a standardized and validated face database that includes multiple identities expressing prototypical emotions. We selected images of 24 young adult faces (12 male, 12 female) each showing three expressions: happy, angry, and neutral. Images were grayscale and cropped so that only the face and hairline were visible, eliminating extraneous context. All images were normalized for luminance and contrast to minimize low-level visual differences across expressions. Happy expressions were selected to include open-mouth smiles with visible teeth when present in the original stimuli, while angry expressions included furrowed brows and tightened lip regions. Neutral faces exhibited relaxed features.

Search arrays contained either 4, 8, or 12 faces arranged on an invisible circle around fixation to minimize overlap and to control spacing. On target-present trials, one face displayed the target expression (happy or angry) and the remaining faces displayed neutral expressions. On target-absent trials, all faces displayed neutral expressions. Target-valence was manipulated within participants such that blocks included trials with happy targets and blocks with angry targets. Target presence (present vs absent) and set size were counterbalanced across trials to allow computation of search slopes and to prevent participants from adopting simple response heuristics based on set size.

The procedure consisted of a mood manipulation followed by the visual search task divided into three equal practice blocks. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two mood induction groups: positive mood induction or neutral control. Participants in the positive mood condition watched a two-minute film clip that had been pretested to induce mild positive affect; control participants watched a neutral clip of similar duration. Immediately before and after the clip, participants completed a brief state affect questionnaire to assess the effectiveness of the mood induction. The state affect questionnaire assessed both positive and negative affect on Likert-style scales and was used to quantify participants’ mood at the time of the search task.

Following the mood induction and mood assessment, participants completed the visual search task. Each participant completed 360 trials in total, organized into three practice blocks of 120 trials each. Within each block, trials were pseudorandomized so that each combination of set size (4, 8, 12), target presence (present, absent), and target valence (happy, angry) occurred with approximately equal frequency. Target valence was not signalled explicitly as a block property; instead, happy and angry target trials were intermixed within blocks to avoid simple task switching effects and to keep participants engaged. Trial sequence began with a fixation cross for 500 ms, followed by the search array which remained on screen until response or for a maximum of 2500 ms. Participants responded by pressing one key for "target present" and another key for "target absent." Feedback on accuracy and reaction time was not provided during the main task to avoid influencing practice effects through external reinforcement.

Before the main task, participants completed 20 practice trials with different faces to familiarize them with the response mapping and timing. The main task proceeded with the three experimental blocks and short rest breaks between blocks to minimize fatigue. At the end of the session, participants completed a demographic questionnaire and an additional dispositional affect inventory to assess trait mood tendencies. The entire session lasted approximately 45 minutes.

Data preprocessing involved several standard steps. Reaction times (RTs) from correct target-present trials were the primary dependent variable for speed analyses. Trials with RTs below 200 ms or above 2500 ms were excluded as anticipatory or noncompliant responses. Additionally, RTs exceeding 2.5 standard deviations from a participant’s condition mean were excluded to reduce the influence of outliers. These trimming criteria were applied symmetrically across valence and block conditions. Accuracy was analyzed for both target-present and target-absent trials to check for speed-accuracy trade-offs.

We computed mean RTs for each participant as a function of block (1–3), target valence (happy, angry), and set size (4, 8, 12) on target-present trials. To quantify search efficiency, we computed search slopes by fitting a linear regression of RT on set size for each participant within each block and valence condition. Slopes (ms/item) provided an index of how much additional time was required for each extra distractor and served as a measure of search efficiency. Lower slopes indicate more efficient search.

Statistical analyses were conducted using repeated-measures ANOVA with block (3 levels) and valence (2 levels) as within-subject factors and mood group (positive vs neutral) as a between-subject factor. Additional analyses included Block × Valence × Set Size mixed ANOVAs to test whether practice effects differed across set sizes. We also tested for interactions between mood and the primary within-subject factors. When Mauchly’s test indicated violations of sphericity, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared for ANOVA effects. Post hoc comparisons used Bonferroni corrections for multiple tests. For the search-slope analyses, we conducted paired t-tests and repeated-measures ANOVAs on the slope estimates to test whether slopes changed significantly across blocks and whether changes differed between valence conditions. Accuracy rates were analyzed using analogous ANOVA models to verify that improvements in RT were not accompanied by declines in accuracy.

All data processing and statistical analyses were performed using standard statistical software. We verified assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance for primary analyses and used nonparametric tests where assumptions were substantially violated. Outlier participants were identified based on low accuracy (below 60% correct) or excessive mean RTs (more than 3 standard deviations above the sample mean) and were excluded from analyses; this led to the exclusion of two participants, leaving a final sample of 78 participants for the reported analyses. Reported degrees of freedom reflect this final sample size.

Results

Descriptive statistics showed that participants were generally accurate on the task, with mean accuracy across all conditions of 92.3% (SD = 4.7%). Accuracy did not differ substantially across blocks, valence, or mood groups, and no speed-accuracy trade-offs were evident. Mean target-present RTs across the entire experiment were 793 ms (SD = 145 ms). The data preprocessing exclusions described in the Methods removed 3.8% of trials, leaving a robust dataset for analysis. Initial checks indicated that the mood induction produced the expected moderate increase in self-reported positive affect for the positive mood group relative to the neutral group, although the manipulation effect was modest in magnitude. Specifically, on the state affect scale, positive affect increased by an average of 1.2 points (on a 7-point scale) in the positive induction group and did not change appreciably in the neutral group.

The primary repeated-measures ANOVA on target-present RTs with within-subject factors Block (1–3) and Valence (happy, angry) and between-subject factor Mood (positive, neutral) revealed a robust main effect of Block. Mean RTs decreased significantly across the three blocks, indicating a practice effect. The Block main effect was significant, F(2,154) = 45.31, p < .001, partial η^2 = .37. Mean RTs by block were 845 ms (SD = 160 ms) in Block 1, 790 ms (SD = 150 ms) in Block 2, and 744 ms (SD = 142 ms) in Block 3. Pairwise comparisons indicated that RTs in each block were significantly faster than in the previous block (Block 1 > Block 2, p < .001; Block 2 > Block 3, p = .004), indicating continuous improvement over the session.

The ANOVA also revealed a main effect of Valence, F(1,77) = 9.87, p = .002, partial η^2 = .11. Overall, happy targets were detected faster than angry targets, with mean RTs of 777 ms (SD = 148 ms) for happy targets and 809 ms (SD = 151 ms) for angry targets. This overall happy advantage was consistent across blocks, but importantly, there was also a significant Block × Valence interaction, F(2,154) = 6.82, p = .002, partial η^2 = .08. This interaction reflected differential rates of improvement for happy versus angry targets across practice blocks. Follow-up analyses showed that RTs for happy targets decreased from 884 ms in Block 1 to 720 ms in Block 3, a reduction of 164 ms, whereas RTs for angry targets decreased from 806 ms in Block 1 to 768 ms in Block 3, a reduction of 38 ms. Paired comparisons confirmed that the reduction for happy targets was significant across each block transition (Block 1 > Block 2, p < .001; Block 2 > Block 3, p = .001), whereas reductions for angry targets were smaller and did not reach significance between Block 2 and Block 3 (Block 1 > Block 2, p = .03; Block 2 > Block 3, p = .12).

The between-subjects Mood factor did not produce a significant main effect on RTs, F(1,77) = 0.74, p = .39, and there were no significant interactions between Mood and Block or Mood and Valence. The Block × Valence × Mood interaction was also non-significant, F(2,154) = 0.65, p = .52. Bayesian follow-up analyses on key contrasts provided moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis for mood effects, suggesting that the absence of mood effects was not merely a consequence of low power in this dataset.

Search slope analyses provided further insight into how practice affected search efficiency. We computed linear slopes (ms per item) for each participant, valence, and block by regressing RT on set size. For happy targets, mean slopes decreased markedly across blocks, from 34.7 ms/item (SD = 12.4) in Block 1 to 22.1 ms/item (SD = 10.8) in Block 3. This reduction in slope for happy targets was significant in a repeated-measures ANOVA on slopes, F(2,154) = 21.45, p < .001, partial η^2 = .22, and paired t-tests confirmed significant reductions from Block 1 to Block 2 and from Block 2 to Block 3 (p < .01 for both). In contrast, slopes for angry targets showed a smaller and non-significant reduction, from 30.2 ms/item (SD = 11.7) in Block 1 to 26.8 ms/item (SD = 11.2) in Block 3; the omnibus test for angry-target slopes across blocks was marginal, F(2,154) = 2.31, p = .10. A direct comparison of slope reductions between happy and angry targets yielded a significant Valence × Block interaction on slope values, F(2,154) = 8.11, p < .001, partial η^2 = .09, indicating that search efficiency improved more for happy targets with practice.

Examination of target-absent trials revealed that RTs on absent trials also showed practice-related decreases, but the magnitude of the decrease was smaller than on present trials. Target-absent RTs decreased from a mean of 1254 ms in Block 1 to 1180 ms in Block 3. The pattern of absent-trial changes did not differ significantly by valence because valence is undefined on absent trials, but the fact that absent-trial RTs decreased modestly suggests that some general task familiarity contributed to overall speed improvements. Importantly, absent-trial accuracy remained high and stable across blocks, arguing against a liberal shift in response criterion as the primary driver of speed-ups.

Accuracy analyses on target-present trials showed no significant main effects of Block or Valence, and no interactions involving Mood. Mean accuracy for target-present trials was 93.6% in Block 1, 92.9% in Block 2, and 92.4% in Block 3. The small decrease in accuracy over blocks was not statistically significant, F(2,154) = 1.08, p = .34, and did not correlate with the reductions in RT. Correlation analyses indicated that individual differences in mood change scores (post-induction minus pre-induction positive affect) were not significantly associated with RTs for either happy or angry targets in any block (all r values between −.08 and .12, p > .20).

Additional analyses examined whether the initial magnitude of valence-related differences in Block 1 predicted subsequent slopes of improvement. Participants who exhibited larger happy-vs-angry RT advantages in Block 1 tended to show larger practice-related reductions for happy targets (r = .31, p = .006), suggesting that initial stimulus distinctiveness or salience predisposed some participants to benefit more from practice. Conversely, initial angry advantages, when present, were not predictive of subsequent improvement, r = .09, p = .42.

Exploratory analyses considered whether gender of the face stimuli or gender of participants modulated the observed effects. We found no consistent interactions involving participant gender or face gender that accounted for the valence-related practice improvements. Both male and female participants showed similar patterns of faster RTs for happy targets and greater practice-related slope reductions for happy targets. Similarly, faces of either gender produced comparable effects.

In sum, the results demonstrated robust practice effects on visual search for emotional faces, with continuous decreases in RT across blocks and significant improvements in search efficiency as indexed by flatter search slopes. Crucially, these practice-related improvements were larger for happy targets than for angry targets, resulting in a growing happy-face advantage across practice. Observer mood, as manipulated and measured in the present study, did not exert significant effects on RTs, accuracy, or the dynamics of practice-related improvement.

Discussion

The present study investigated how practice and mood influence the speed and efficiency of finding an emotional face in a crowd. The core findings were clear: practice produced substantial improvements in search speed and efficiency, and these improvements were especially pronounced for happy targets. By contrast, the mood induction and state mood measures did not have a detectable impact on search performance. These results offer several insights into the processes that tune social attention and clarify how stimulus-driven learning and affective state interact in the context of visual search for faces.

First and foremost, the strong practice effects observed across the session are consistent with a large body of work showing that perceptual learning and task repetition lead to faster responses and more efficient performance on visual tasks. The reduction in mean RT across blocks, together with flatter search slopes for happy targets, indicates that repeated exposure to the search task facilitated not only faster responding but also more efficient attentional deployment. This pattern suggests that practice led to improvements in the mechanisms that enable observers to find targets among distractors, such as the formation of sharper target templates, enhanced feature weighting, or more effective guidance of attention to diagnostic facial regions.

The valence-specific nature of the practice benefits is particularly informative. Happy faces showed markedly greater reductions in RT and steeper improvements in search slopes than angry faces. This finding is inconsistent with a simple, immutable threat-superiority account that would predict a persistent advantage for negative expressions that leaves little room for practice-driven improvement. Instead, the data suggest that the happy advantage either emerges over time due to learning or becomes amplified with practice because happy faces are more amenable to perceptual learning in this task context. There are several reasons why happy faces might benefit more from practice. Happy expressions often include salient featural cues, such as exposed teeth or upward mouth curvature, that can be readily learned and used as efficient search features. Such featural markers are particularly well-suited to rapid detection strategies that develop with practice. In contrast, angry expressions may rely more heavily on subtler configural cues such as brow furrowing, which may be less distinctive in brief peripheral views or more variable across identities, making them harder to abstract into a stable search template with limited practice.

The search-slope data provide further mechanistic clues. The notable reduction in slope for happy targets across blocks suggests that practice led to more efficient attention allocation per distractor item for happy faces. This pattern is consistent with a transition toward more parallel or more effectively guided search for happy targets after practice. In contrast, the relatively modest and non-significant slope reductions for angry targets indicate that practice did not substantially change how additional distractors impacted search for angry faces. Put differently, observers appeared to become better at identifying happy cues irrespective of the number of distractors, which points to improved feature-based guidance rather than an overall speed-up that would affect slopes for both expressions equally.

The lack of mood effects in the present study is notable given theoretical expectations of mood-congruent attentional biases. Several explanations are possible for this null effect. First, the mood induction produced only a modest change in self-reported positive affect, and the induced mood may have been insufficiently strong or durable to influence perceptual processes in a demanding visual search task. Mood manipulations often vary in their potency, and transient affective changes may dissipate rapidly, particularly when participants engage in a cognitively engaging task. Second, mood effects on attention may be more likely to emerge in tasks that involve ambiguity, judgment, or sustained evaluation rather than rapid detection under time pressure. Visual search for discrete expressions among neutral distractors may simply be too constrained and stimulus-driven for mood to exert a measurable influence. Third, individual differences in affective reactivity and baseline mood may moderate mood effects; a between-subjects design that randomly assigned participants to induction conditions may not capture these individual differences adequately. Fourth, it is possible that practice effects are sufficiently strong to overshadow subtle mood biases; as participants become more proficient at the task, top-down mood influences may become relatively less influential compared to learned, stimulus-driven templates.

The interaction between initial salience and learning also merits consideration. Our exploratory finding that participants who initially showed larger happy-face advantages tended to derive larger practice gains suggests that initial perceptual sensitivity to certain expressions may bootstrap further learning. This pattern is compatible with accounts of perceptual learning in which early stimulus salience facilitates the extraction of diagnostic features that can then be reinforced through repeated exposure. It also implies that individual differences in baseline sensitivity to facial cues may predict the extent to which individuals benefit from practice, which could be relevant for training programs that aim to improve social cue detection.

There are several implications of these findings for theories of attentional priority for emotional faces. The present results argue against a simple, universal threat primacy in attentional capture and instead support a more nuanced view in which the relative detectability of emotional expressions depends on stimulus properties, task demands, and the observer’s experience with the task. Happy faces may be preferentially enhanced by practice when their diagnostic features are stable and salient, while negative expressions may enjoy priority in scenarios where rapid threat detection is paramount or when expressions are more exaggerated and unambiguous. The differential impact of practice on different expressions underscores the importance of considering dynamic learning processes when evaluating emotion-attention interactions.

These results also suggest practical applications. For occupational roles that require rapid detection of positive social cues, such as customer service or therapeutic settings, brief targeted practice could substantially improve detection speed and efficiency. Conversely, training that aims to enhance detection of negative cues (for security or safety personnel) may require different or more intensive regimens if such cues are less amenable to rapid feature-based learning. The absence of a mood effect suggests that short-term mood induction is unlikely to undermine or enhance such training benefits in straightforward detection tasks, although more ecologically valid or emotionally engaging conditions could yield different outcomes.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The mood induction used here produced only modest affective changes, and stronger or more sustained mood manipulations may yield different results. The laboratory nature of the task and the use of static images limit the generalizability to real-world social perception where dynamic expressions and contextual cues interact. The stimuli, though standardized, include variability across identities that might modulate the clarity of angry expressions more than happy expressions, and this could contribute to the differential practice effects. Moreover, the trial set was relatively brief and confined to a single session; it remains unclear how extended training across days or weeks would impact the balance between expression advantages and whether mood effects might emerge over longer timescales.

Future research can address these limitations in several ways. Studies could employ more potent mood manipulations or take advantage of naturally occurring mood variation to test mood-congruency effects in larger and more diverse samples. Investigations could include dynamic facial stimuli and more varied social contexts to test whether the valence-specific practice effects generalize to more ecologically valid settings. Longitudinal designs that examine training across multiple sessions could determine whether angry-target benefits can be achieved with extended practice and whether initial happy advantages persist or attenuate over time. Neuroimaging approaches could elucidate the neural mechanisms underlying the observed learning effects, such as changes in fusiform face area tuning, amygdala responsivity, or frontoparietal attentional networks. Additionally, studies could manipulate the featural diagnosticity of expressions experimentally (for example by digitally exaggerating mouth or brow features) to test whether feature salience causally drives the greater learnability of certain expressions.

Another avenue for future work is to examine individual differences in trait affect, emotion recognition ability, and cognitive control, which may moderate both baseline detection and practice-related gains. For instance, individuals high in social anxiety may exhibit different baseline sensitivity to angry faces and may show distinct learning trajectories, which has relevance for clinical interventions. Cross-cultural research could explore whether cultural norms in expression production and perception influence which expressions are most amenable to practice-based improvements.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that practice robustly enhances the speed and efficiency of finding emotional faces in a crowd and that these practice effects are particularly pronounced for happy expressions. Observer mood, as manipulated and measured here, did not influence search performance. These findings highlight the importance of experience and stimulus properties in shaping social attention and suggest that perceptual learning can selectively tune attentional systems toward particular emotional signals. Understanding the mechanisms and boundary conditions of these effects will be important for theories of social perception and for practical efforts to train or remediate emotion detection abilities.
