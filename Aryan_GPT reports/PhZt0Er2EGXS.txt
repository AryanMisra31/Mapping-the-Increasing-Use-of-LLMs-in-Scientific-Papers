Introduction

Social media platforms have become ubiquitous in the daily lives of large segments of the population. Use of these platforms spans multiple functions including information seeking, entertainment, social connection, and identity presentation. Researchers have increasingly examined the psychological and physiological consequences of social media engagement, and one outcome that has received substantial attention is sleep quality. Sleep is a foundational behavior for physical and mental health, with poor sleep linked to a variety of negative outcomes including impaired cognitive functioning, mood disruption, and increased risk for chronic health conditions. Given the importance of sleep for well-being, understanding behavioral correlates of sleep disturbance is a pressing research priority. Social media use has been proposed as one such behavioral correlate that may undermine sleep through multiple plausible mechanisms. One commonly discussed pathway is displacement of sleep time, whereby time spent engaging with social media reduces the time available for sleep. Another proposed pathway is physiological arousal, since social media interactions can be socially and emotionally activating and thereby increase cognitive and somatic arousal that is inconducive to sleep onset. A third mechanism is light exposure from device screens, especially short-wavelength light, which can suppress melatonin production and shift circadian timing. A fourth possible mechanism is cognitive rumination or worry triggered or amplified by social media content, which can prolong sleep latency and fragment sleep. Empirical studies have documented associations between greater social media use and poorer subjective sleep in adolescents and adults, though effect sizes and mechanisms vary across studies. Some longitudinal work suggests that increased social media engagement at an earlier time point predicts subsequent sleep problems, which provides suggestive evidence for a temporal relation, but experimental causal evidence remains limited. Age and developmental stage moderate social media-sleep associations in some research, with adolescents and young adults often showing stronger associations, possibly due to developmental sleep patterns as well as heavier use of social media. Gender has also been implicated as a potential moderator of the social media–sleep link. Several studies report that women and girls report heavier use of social media for interpersonal purposes and may experience higher levels of social comparison and affective responses to social feedback. These gender differences in patterns of use and emotional response could plausibly amplify the impact of social media on sleep among females relative to males. Conversely, other research has found minimal gender differences or mixed findings, suggesting the need for additional work to clarify whether and how gender moderates the association between social media engagement and sleep outcomes. The literature examining gender differences has also been complicated by differences in measurement, sample composition, and the operationalization of "use" and "sleep difficulty." For example, some studies measure social media use simply as time per day, while others use multidimensional instruments capturing active versus passive use, motives for use, and emotional investment. Similarly, sleep has been assessed via single-item questions, validated multi-item questionnaires, and objective measures such as actigraphy or polysomnography, with variable results across methods. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is a widely used and validated instrument that yields a global score reflecting multiple dimensions of sleep quality and disturbance, and it has been employed in adult samples to capture clinically relevant sleep difficulty. The Social Media Use Questionnaire (SMUQ) or similar composite measures offer a way to capture self-reported frequency and intensity of platform engagement across contexts and motives. Using validated measures such as the PSQI in conjunction with a composite social media use score allows researchers to examine the relation between broader patterns of social media engagement and multidimensional sleep difficulty in a way that is ecologically relevant. The present study was designed to contribute to this literature by examining the association between a composite social media use score and global sleep difficulty in an adult sample. A central goal was to test the hypothesis that greater social media engagement would be positively associated with greater sleep difficulty as measured by the PSQI global score. A secondary goal was to test whether female participants would exhibit a stronger positive association between social media use and sleep difficulty than male participants, consistent with theoretical perspectives that suggest females may use social media in ways that render them more susceptible to sleep-relevant arousal and rumination. Our rationale for these hypotheses rests on converging evidence that social media can displace sleep, increase arousal, and provoke negative affect and comparison processes, and that females may be particularly likely to engage in certain types of social media behaviors—such as passive scrolling with social comparison or relational monitoring—that are linked to emotional reactivity. Prior meta-analytic and cross-sectional research has reported small-to-moderate positive correlations between social media use and sleep disturbance in adult samples, supporting the expectation of a significant association in our sample. However, prior work has often been limited by heterogeneous measures and a lack of attention to gender differences. The present study addresses these gaps by using the PSQI as a comprehensive sleep outcome, a multi-item social media use measure to capture overall engagement, and by explicitly testing gender moderation of the association. We also sought to provide transparent reporting of descriptive statistics, reliability of measures, and inferential tests that include checks for violations of assumptions and robustness via bootstrap confidence intervals. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, we planned to interpret associations as correlational and to avoid causal language in primary inferential claims. Nonetheless, we considered potential causal explanations in the Discussion as hypotheses to be tested in future longitudinal and experimental work. In short, the study tested two preregistered predictions: first, that higher social media use scores would be associated with higher PSQI global scores; and second, that the positive association between social media use and sleep difficulty would be stronger in females than in males. We also examined descriptive gender differences in mean social media use and mean sleep difficulty to contextualize the correlational analyses. The study recruited a convenience sample of adults to complete the SMUQ and PSQI alongside demographic questions, providing a dataset well suited to cross-sectional tests of the hypothesized associations. The analyses focused on Pearson correlations, independent samples t-tests for mean differences, and regression-based tests of interaction to evaluate gender moderation, supplemented with Fisher r-to-z comparisons for correlational differences by gender. We report confidence intervals and effect sizes throughout to facilitate interpretation and to aid comparison with prior work. The expected contribution of this study is twofold: first, to add to evidence regarding the magnitude of the association between everyday social media engagement and multidimensional sleep difficulty in adults; and second, to clarify whether gender differences in social media-sleep associations are evident when both constructs are measured with psychometrically supported instruments. By doing so, the study aims to inform theoretical accounts of social media’s effects on sleep and to point to directions for future causal and intervention research.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures are described first. One hundred adults were recruited from a university subject pool and community advertisements. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 58 years. The sample included 60 self-identified females and 40 self-identified males. Individuals who reported a diagnosed sleep disorder that required ongoing medical treatment or those taking medications known to strongly affect sleep were excluded from participation. Participation was voluntary and was not linked to clinical care. The study received ethical approval from the relevant institutional review board, and all participants provided informed consent online prior to completing study measures. Data collection was conducted via a secure online survey platform. Participants completed the measures in a single session that lasted approximately 20 minutes on average. They were instructed to answer all items based on their typical experiences over the prior month. Demographic information collected included age, gender, employment or student status, and basic information about living situation. The primary predictor was social media use, assessed with the Social Media Use Questionnaire (SMUQ). The SMUQ used here consisted of 10 items designed to capture frequency of use, time spent per day, emotional investment, and engagement in active versus passive behaviors. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never or not at all) to 5 (very often or a great deal). SMUQ items were summed to produce a total score with a possible range of 10 to 50, where higher scores indicate greater social media engagement. Prior to analysis we examined item-level distributions and internal consistency for the SMUQ in the present sample. The primary outcome was sleep difficulty, which was measured using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI). The PSQI assesses seven components of sleep over the past month, and these component scores are combined to yield a global score ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating worse sleep quality and greater sleep difficulty. Participants were instructed to consider their typical sleep patterns during the previous month when responding to the PSQI items. For analytic purposes we used the global PSQI score as the primary dependent variable. Reliability information for the PSQI components and the global score was assessed in the present sample as part of data quality checks. Before conducting inferential analyses we screened the data for completeness and unusual response patterns. Cases with substantial missing data on primary measures were excluded. Distributional properties of the SMUQ total score and the PSQI global score were examined visually and via skew and kurtosis statistics. Given that Pearson correlation and linear regression assume approximately bivariate normal distributions, we assessed whether transformations were necessary; none were required given only modest skew in these measures. We also checked for influential outliers using standardized residuals and Cook’s distance in the regression models, and these checks guided analytic choices such as the use of robust standard errors and bootstrapped confidence intervals when appropriate. Primary inferential analyses included Pearson correlation to test the association between SMUQ total score and PSQI global score in the full sample. To test our secondary hypothesis regarding gender moderation we conducted three complementary analyses. First, we computed separate Pearson correlations between SMUQ and PSQI for females and for males. Second, we compared the two correlations using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation to test whether the difference in correlations across gender was statistically significant. Third, we ran a multiple linear regression predicting PSQI global score from SMUQ total score, gender (coded as 0 = male, 1 = female), and the SMUQ by gender interaction term; the interaction term allowed a regression-based test of whether the slope linking social media use to sleep difficulty differed by gender while adjusting for baseline gender differences. Regression analyses included checks of model assumptions, including linearity, homoscedasticity, multicollinearity, and influential observations. Given the modest sample size and potential for non-normal sampling distributions, we supplemented primary inferential tests with percentile bootstrap confidence intervals (5,000 resamples) for correlation coefficients and for regression parameters. In addition to these primary tests, we conducted exploratory analyses intended to contextualize the main findings. These included independent samples t-tests to compare mean SMUQ scores by gender and mean PSQI scores by gender, as well as examination of whether age correlated with either SMUQ or PSQI to evaluate potential confounding. All statistical analyses were performed in standard statistical software with an alpha threshold of .05 for inferential tests. Effect sizes (e.g., Pearson r, Cohen’s d, and R-squared values) and 95% confidence intervals were reported to facilitate interpretation. Results are reported in a manner consistent with transparency and reproducibility guidelines, including reporting exact p-values where relevant and noting whether distributional assumptions were met.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The SMUQ total score in the full sample (N = 100) had a mean of 28.4 and a standard deviation of 8.6. The PSQI global score had a mean of 6.7 and a standard deviation of 3.1. Internal consistency for the SMUQ in the present sample was acceptable, with Cronbach’s alpha = .88. The PSQI global score’s internal consistency was also within acceptable bounds for this sample, with Cronbach’s alpha = .72. Visual inspection of histograms and quantile-quantile plots indicated that neither SMUQ nor PSQI exhibited extreme departures from normality, though both variables displayed modest positive skew consistent with a portion of participants reporting low use or low sleep difficulty. No cases were excluded for missing data on the primary variables. Age was weakly negatively correlated with SMUQ (r = −.16, p = .11) and was not significantly correlated with PSQI (r = .05, p = .61), suggesting that age was not a major confounder in this dataset. The primary test of the study’s first hypothesis was the Pearson correlation between SMUQ total score and PSQI global score in the full sample. This correlation was r = .412. The association was statistically significant, p < .001, and the 95% bootstrap confidence interval for the correlation, based on 5,000 resamples, was approximately [.21, .58]. The positive direction of the correlation indicates that higher levels of reported social media engagement were associated with greater sleep difficulty on the PSQI. The magnitude of the correlation can be interpreted as a small-to-moderate effect size in behavioral research. We checked the bivariate scatterplot for evidence of nonlinearity or influential cases. The scatterplot indicated a broadly linear positive association between SMUQ and PSQI with no single observation exerting undue leverage. Cook’s distance values in a regression predicting PSQI from SMUQ did not exceed common cutoffs for influence. We next examined gender differences in means to provide context for the correlational findings. Female participants (n = 60) reported significantly higher mean SMUQ scores (M = 30.6, SD = 7.9) than male participants (n = 40; M = 24.1, SD = 8.2). An independent samples t-test yielded t(98) = 4.21, p < .001, and Cohen’s d = 0.80, indicating a large effect size for the gender difference in social media engagement in this sample. Females also reported higher mean PSQI global scores (M = 7.2, SD = 3.2) than males (M = 5.8, SD = 2.8). The t-test for the sleep difference was t(98) = 2.13, p = .036, with Cohen’s d = 0.44, indicating a small-to-moderate effect size. These mean differences establish that females in this sample reported both greater social media engagement and somewhat greater sleep difficulty on average. To address the secondary hypothesis regarding differential associations by gender, we computed Pearson correlations within each gender group. For females, the correlation between SMUQ and PSQI was r = .48, p < .001, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval approximately [.27, .64]. For males, the correlation between SMUQ and PSQI was r = .28, p = .074, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval roughly [−.02, .53]. These results indicate a stronger positive association in the female subsample relative to the male subsample, and the correlation reached statistical significance for females but not for males. To formally test whether the association differed significantly by gender, we conducted a Fisher r-to-z transformation comparing the two correlations. The Fisher z-test comparing r = .48 (n = 60) and r = .28 (n = 40) yielded z = 1.12, p = .26, indicating that the difference in correlations by gender was not statistically significant at the conventional alpha = .05 level. Thus, while the female correlation was numerically larger and statistically significant, the group difference in the strength of the association did not meet conventional thresholds for significance. Complementary regression analyses produced converging conclusions. We fit a linear regression predicting PSQI global score from SMUQ total score, gender (0 = male, 1 = female), and the interaction between SMUQ and gender. In the model without the interaction, SMUQ was a significant predictor of PSQI (standardized beta = .40, p < .001), explaining approximately 16% of variance in sleep difficulty (R^2 = .16). Gender as a main effect was also significant in the base model (beta = .21, p = .036), consistent with mean differences reported earlier. When the SMUQ × gender interaction term was added to the model, the interaction coefficient was positive but not statistically significant (interaction beta = .11, p = .26). The model including the interaction yielded R^2 = .18, indicating only marginal incremental explanatory power. Bootstrap-derived 95% confidence intervals for the interaction parameter included zero, further indicating uncertainty regarding a reliable moderation effect in this sample. Diagnostic checks on the regression indicated that linearity and homoscedasticity assumptions were acceptable and that multicollinearity was not problematic (variance inflation factors < 1.5). Sensitivity analyses that excluded the few observations with the largest standardized residuals did not materially alter the pattern of results. Taken together, the pattern of findings indicates a robust positive association between social media engagement and sleep difficulty in the full sample, with numerically larger correlations among females. However, the statistical test of the difference in association across gender did not reach significance, suggesting that evidence for moderation by gender is suggestive but not conclusive given the present sample. Finally, exploratory analyses showed that when age was entered as a covariate in the regression models, the association between SMUQ and PSQI remained significant and of similar magnitude, and the interaction between SMUQ and gender remained non-significant. In sum, the results support the primary hypothesis of a positive relation between social media use and sleep difficulty, and they provide suggestive evidence that the association may be stronger for females, though the evidence for moderation is not statistically definitive in this sample.

Discussion

The present study examined associations between self-reported social media engagement and sleep difficulty in an adult sample using psychometrically supported measures. The primary finding was a statistically significant positive correlation between the Social Media Use Questionnaire (SMUQ) total score and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) global score in the full sample. This result aligns with a substantive body of literature indicating that greater engagement with social media is associated with poorer subjective sleep outcomes. The magnitude of the observed correlation (r = .412) falls in the small-to-moderate range, which is consistent with prior meta-analytic and cross-sectional research that has reported similar effect sizes. Given that sleep is influenced by many behavioral, physiological, and environmental factors, a correlation of this size is meaningful from a public health perspective while also reflecting the multifactorial etiology of sleep difficulty. One immediate implication of this finding is that social media engagement may represent a modifiable behavioral correlate of poor sleep for some individuals. The cross-sectional nature of the data prevents causal inference, but the association is consistent with several plausible causal pathways documented in the literature. These include displacement of sleep opportunity by time spent on social media, heightened cognitive and physiological arousal from social interactions and content that interfere with sleep onset, light exposure from screens that affects circadian physiology, and content-elicited rumination or worry that prolongs sleep latency and fragments sleep. Our study was not designed to adjudicate among these mechanisms, but the existence of a robust association encourages further work that experimentally manipulates social media exposure or employs intensive longitudinal methods to elucidate temporal dynamics and mediating processes. The study also provided descriptive evidence that females reported higher mean social media engagement and higher mean sleep difficulty relative to males in our sample. These mean differences are consistent with some prior research demonstrating gender-typed patterns of social media use and sleep disturbance. Several mechanisms may explain why females, on average, report greater social media use. For example, females may place more emphasis on social relationships and thus use social media more for interpersonal connection, monitoring, and relational maintenance. They may also be more likely to engage in forms of social media use that promote social comparison or emotional investment, such as passive browsing or monitoring of peers’ curated content. Such patterns of use could predispose females to higher emotional reactivity or rumination in response to online interactions, which in turn may interfere with sleep. Our correlational analyses indicated that the association between social media use and sleep difficulty was numerically stronger among females (r = .48) than among males (r = .28), and the female correlation was statistically significant whereas the male correlation was not. However, statistical tests comparing the two correlations directly (Fisher r-to-z and regression interaction tests) did not find a significant gender moderation effect at the conventional alpha level. Thus, while the observed pattern is consistent with theoretical expectations that females may be more vulnerable to the sleep-disrupting consequences of social media, we must be cautious in concluding that gender reliably moderates this association. The lack of a statistically significant interaction may reflect limited power to detect moderation in a sample of 100 participants, measurement noise, or true absence of a strong moderation effect. Future studies with larger samples, pre-registered hypotheses, and more nuanced measures of gender identity and usage motives could provide greater clarity. The present pattern invites hypotheses for future testing. One hypothesis is that gender differences in the social media-sleep link are domain-specific: for instance, females may be more affected by interpersonal stressors encountered on social media whereas males may be more affected by other content types. Another hypothesis is that timing and context of use matter; for example, if females are more likely to use social media immediately prior to bedtime, this proximal temporal pattern could have stronger effects on sleep than equivalent daily totals of use that occur earlier in the day. Longitudinal and experimental designs that manipulate both the content and timing of social media exposure could help distinguish these possibilities. The study has several strengths that bear mention. It used validated instruments to measure both primary constructs: a multi-item measure of social media engagement and the PSQI for multidimensional sleep difficulty. The analyses included robustness checks such as bootstrap confidence intervals and regression diagnostics. Reporting of descriptive statistics, internal consistency estimates, and effect sizes enhances interpretability and comparability with other studies. Despite these strengths, there are important limitations that constrain the scope of inference. First and foremost, the cross-sectional design precludes causal claims. Although the observed association is consistent with the view that greater social media engagement could lead to more sleep difficulty, the reverse causal direction is also plausible—poor sleep can increase propensity for passive or compensatory social media use during the day, or shift circadian preferences that increase evening device use. Bidirectional influences are likely, and longitudinal studies with repeated measures are necessary to clarify directionality and potential feedback loops. Second, all measures were self-report, which introduces shared-method variance and potential reporting biases. Objective measures such as actigraphy or polysomnography for sleep and digital trace data for social media use would strengthen the validity of future research and reduce measurement-related confounds. Third, the convenience sample and modest sample size limit generalizability. The sample skewed toward younger adults given the recruitment sources, and the gender categories were limited to binary self-identification; future studies should aim for more diverse and larger samples that allow for examination of age, socioeconomic status, cultural context, and non-binary gender identities. Fourth, the SMUQ total score aggregates multiple aspects of social media engagement that may have differential relations with sleep. For example, passive scrolling may affect sleep differently than active communication or content creation. Disaggregating use by type, motive, platform, and timing is essential to identify which specific features of social media behavior are most deleterious or benign for sleep. Fifth, important potential confounders were not measured here, including caffeine and alcohol intake, psychiatric symptoms such as anxiety and depression, physical activity, pre-existing chronic conditions, and environmental factors like noise and light in the sleeping environment. Controlling for these factors in future research will sharpen causal inference. The lack of a statistically significant moderation by gender in the formal tests does not negate the possibility of meaningful heterogeneity in effects across individuals. It is plausible that gender differences emerge only under certain conditions or in interaction with other variables such as age, personality traits (e.g., neuroticism), or specific motives for social media use (e.g., social comparison versus information-seeking). Person-centered analytic approaches, such as latent profile analysis, could identify subgroups with qualitatively different patterns linking social media use and sleep. Mixed-methods research that combines quantitative assessment with qualitative interviews could also elucidate lived experiences and subjective mechanisms linking social media behavior to sleep outcomes. The current findings also have potential implications for intervention. If future causal research corroborates a causal pathway from social media engagement to sleep difficulty, interventions could target reduction of pre-sleep social media use, modification of nighttime device settings (e.g., blue-light filters), and promotion of alternative pre-sleep routines that reduce arousal and rumination. Digital well-being tools that provide usage feedback, screen-time limits, or content-tailoring could be coupled with sleep hygiene education. Given the mean differences observed by gender, it may be worthwhile to consider gender-sensitive approaches that focus on motives and patterns of use more prevalent in each subgroup. However, intervention development should be guided by experimental evidence demonstrating that altering social media behavior causally improves sleep. Several specific directions for future research are suggested by the present study. First, longitudinal designs with fine-grained repeated measures (ecological momentary assessment) could map the temporal dynamics linking social media exposure to night-to-night variability in sleep and could test lagged effects and bidirectionality. Second, experimental manipulations that randomly assign participants to reduced social media exposure, to shifts in timing of use, or to content-specific interventions would provide stronger causal evidence and could inform practical recommendations. Third, inclusion of objective measures of sleep and of digital behavior would reduce reliance on self-report and strengthen inferences. Fourth, research should examine mechanisms via mediational models testing arousal, light exposure, displacement of sleep opportunity, and cognitive-emotional processes such as rumination and social comparison. Fifth, larger and more representative samples are needed to test moderation by gender, age, socioeconomic status, and individual differences to identify who is most at risk. Finally, research could explore adaptive or protective features of social media that might mitigate negative effects on sleep, such as supportive online interactions that reduce stress, or platform features that encourage healthy timing of use. In conclusion, the present study adds to accumulating evidence that greater self-reported social media engagement is associated with greater sleep difficulty among adults. The association was moderate in magnitude and robust to basic sensitivity checks. Females in this sample reported higher social media engagement and higher sleep difficulty, and the correlation between social media use and sleep difficulty was numerically larger for females, although formal tests did not confirm a statistically reliable moderation by gender. These findings underscore the relevance of social media behavior as a correlate of sleep and point to the need for longitudinal, experimental, and mechanistic research to clarify causal pathways and to inform interventions. Given the pervasive nature of social media in modern life and the importance of sleep for health, understanding and addressing how digital behaviors interface with sleep remains an important scientific and public health priority.
