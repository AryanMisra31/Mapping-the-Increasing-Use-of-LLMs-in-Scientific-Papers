Introduction

The relation between the sounds of words and their perceived referents has long intrigued philosophers, linguists, and psychologists. Sound symbolic effects, where phonetic material maps non-arbitrarily to semantic or perceptual properties, challenge the traditional Saussurean assumption of arbitrary sign form. One of the most robust demonstrations of sound symbolism in the laboratory is the “bouba/kiki” effect, in which adults reliably associate rounded shapes with soft-sounding pseudowords such as “bouba” and jagged shapes with sharp-sounding pseudowords such as “kiki.” This effect has been replicated across many languages and cultures and has prompted investigation into the perceptual and cognitive mechanisms that underpin cross-modal mapping. Maurer, Pathman, and Mondloch provided an influential demonstration that newborns and adults show similar biases, suggesting early-emerging predispositions in mapping sounds to visual form. Their work and subsequent studies have raised questions about whether sound-symbolic mappings reflect simple global shape attributes, more local shape features, or higher-level semantic or affective correlates of the stimuli. Resolving which aspects of visual form are most consequential for name assignment in sound symbolism has implications for theories of multimodal representation, language acquisition, and the evolution of language.

One open question concerns the relative contribution of external versus internal visual features to sound-symbolic mapping. External visual features refer to the global contour or silhouette of a shape, whereas internal features comprise internal line segments, textures, spots, or cavities that occur within the outline. Studies in visual perception and face recognition suggest that different classes of visual information may be weighted differently depending on the task. For example, in face perception research, adults often rely heavily on internal facial features for recognition tasks and on external features for tasks involving gender or age judgments. These results imply that there may be task-dependent prioritization of internal versus external information in complex shapes. Extrapolating this to sound symbolism, one might ask whether people assign names to complex shapes based primarily on their outer contour or whether internal structural details shift the mapping.

Previous experiments on the bouba/kiki effect have typically manipulated overall shape in relatively simple ways, contrasting rounded versus spiky outlines without systematically varying internal features. A handful of studies have suggested that local edge curvature and pointy elements are critical cues, but these studies often confound internal and external elements or do not isolate their independent contributions. Other lines of work have explored cross-modal correspondences more broadly, linking acoustic properties such as spectral tilt and consonant voicing to perceptual dimensions such as size, brightness, and weight. These studies indicate that the mapping is probably not a unitary phenomenon but rather a constellation of multiple correspondences that may draw on different visual subfeatures. Testing the relative roles of internal and external features therefore represents an important extension of existing paradigms.

Reaction times provide another informative window into the process of name assignment. If a mapping taps into a strong, automatic association, one expects faster decision times when stimulus features and labels are congruent with that mapping. Conversely, when competing cues are present, such as conflicting internal and external features, we might expect slower responses as participants resolve the competition. Some prior research has demonstrated facilitation in reaction times for congruent sound-shape pairings relative to incongruent pairings, while other studies have not consistently observed speed advantages for congruence. Mixed findings could result from differences in stimulus design, the presence of competing cues, or participant instructions. Careful manipulation of congruency between internal and external content and measurement of both choice and response latency can thus illuminate whether sound-shape mappings are primarily driven by a single dominant cue or by a dynamic integration of multiple cues.

The present study aimed to extend the work of Maurer, Pathman, and Mondloch by explicitly manipulating internal and external content in complex shapes used in a bouba/kiki paradigm. Two interrelated questions guided the research. First, when internal and external features of a shape are set in opposition, which source of information primarily determines name assignment? Second, does congruence or incongruence between internal and external features affect the speed of name assignment, and if so, in what direction? These questions bear on broader debates about the relative roles of global configuration versus local detail in cross-modal correspondences and about the cognitive architecture that supports rapid multimodal mapping.

The study was designed to test a specific prediction derived from face recognition research. Because adults show a predisposition to attend to internal features when recognizing complex patterns such as faces, one might predict that participants would base name assignments on internal features when internal and external cues conflict. This prediction implies that when a shape has a rounded external contour but contains jagged internal elements, participants would tend to choose the name associated with jaggedness (e.g., “kiki”), whereas a rounded internal configuration in a jagged contour would similarly bias responses toward “bouba.” An alternative hypothesis is that external contours dominate in sound-symbolic mapping because the general silhouette provides an immediate global cue that aligns strongly with phonetic shapes and is processed rapidly. Testing these competing hypotheses requires a within-subjects design that systematically contrasts congruent shapes (rounded external + rounded internal; jagged external + jagged internal) with incongruent shapes (rounded external + jagged internal; jagged external + rounded internal).

The current research also probed whether congruent trials produce speeded responses relative to neutral or single-feature trials. If congruence produces facilitation, then congruent trials should elicit faster name assignments than incongruent or single-feature trials. Conversely, if congruent trials merely reduce ambiguity but do not confer additional processing speed benefits, reaction times may not differ from other trial types. Additionally, incongruent trials were expected to slow responses if participants needed to resolve conflicting cues, which would show up as longer latencies in those conditions.

Beyond the main hypotheses, the study incorporated methodological controls to ensure that any observed effects were attributable to the intended manipulations. Stimuli were computer-generated to allow precise control of contour curvature and internal features while minimizing unintended low-level confounds such as differential luminance or texture density. The experimental task used forced-choice assignment of two pseudowords, “bouba” and “kiki,” which have become standard labels in the literature and have robust mappings to rounded and jagged forms, respectively. Reaction times were collected with millisecond resolution, and outlier trials were defined a priori to reduce noise from lapses or attentional slips. Participant selection criteria excluded individuals with self-reported color-blindness or uncorrected vision issues and excluded those with formal psychology training to reduce expectation effects in the sample.

The sample size was determined by a pragmatic combination of available participant resources and sensitivity to detect medium-sized effects typical in the literature. A final sample of 42 participants provided acceptable power for within-subject contrasts. Participants were primarily university students drawn from non-psychology courses to minimize exposure to rubric designs and expectations about the bouba/kiki phenomenon.

Analytic approaches included both categorical choice-based analyses and continuous reaction time analyses. Logistic regression models tested whether internal or external content predicted the probability of selecting “bouba” versus “kiki.” Repeated-measures analyses of variance examined reaction time differences across trial types, with planned contrasts to test specific hypotheses about congruent and incongruent trials. Complementary nonparametric tests were also used to ensure robustness against violations of normality assumptions.

The study also explored secondary questions related to learning and consistency across trials. For example, whether participants’ mapping tendencies strengthened or weakened over the experimental session was examined by inspecting trial order effects and by including trial number as a covariate in regression analyses. These auxiliary analyses can shed light on whether mappings are stable or whether exposure and feedback-free repetition could shift the reliance on internal versus external cues.

Finally, the research aimed to situate findings in terms of broader theoretical implications. If external contours prove to dominate name assignment, the results would suggest that global silhouette processing is privileged in cross-modal mapping tasks, supporting a view of sound symbolism as rooted in coarse perceptual correspondences. If internal features are dominant, the findings would align with face-based observations and suggest that local structural detail can overrule global contour in cross-modal mapping. The reaction time patterns would further inform whether conflict resolution processes play a meaningful role during name assignment, which has implications for models of multimodal integration and for applied domains such as branding, language teaching, and assistive communication design.

In summary, this study implemented an extension of Maurer, Pathman, and Mondloch’s work to determine whether internal or external content is the more essential factor in name assignment in the bouba/kiki effect and to assess whether congruency between internal and external features affects speed of assignment. The design, analyses, and controls were tailored to provide a clear test of these competing hypotheses and to contribute to an improved mechanistic understanding of sound-symbolic mapping between phonetic form and visual shape.

Methods

Participants were 42 adults recruited from a university campus, none of whom were enrolled in psychology programs. The sample comprised 22 males and 20 females, and the age range was 18 to 34 years. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and had no known hearing impairments. None of the participants reported neurological disorders or history of a language disorder. Participants provided informed consent in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines prior to taking part in the study. They were compensated with course credit or a small monetary reimbursement for their time. Data from all 42 participants were included in the analyses after pre-registered exclusion criteria were applied. These exclusion criteria would have removed participants who had accuracy rates less than 50 percent on control trials or who had more than 20 percent of trials with reaction times faster than 200 ms or slower than 3000 ms, but no participant met these exclusion thresholds.

Stimuli were created using vector graphic software to permit tight control over contour curvature and internal structure. Four classes of stimuli were generated, corresponding to the crossing of external and internal shape types: congruent-rounded (rounded external contour with rounded internal elements), congruent-jagged (jagged external contour with jagged internal elements), incongruent-rounded-external (rounded external contour with jagged internal internal elements), and incongruent-jagged-external (jagged external contour with rounded internal internal elements). External contours were manipulated by varying the curvature of the silhouette; rounded contours were composed of smooth arcs with minimal angular points while jagged contours contained multiple sharp angles and points. Internal elements consisted of either smooth pebble-like blobs for rounded internal content or sharp angular spikes and cross-hatches for jagged internal content. The size, density, and overall area occupied by internal elements were held constant across conditions to avoid confounding overall visual complexity with internal content type. Stimulus luminance and the proportion of black-to-white area were equated across stimuli. In total, 40 unique shapes were generated, with 10 exemplars per condition, ensuring that participants saw multiple instantiations of each condition.

Stimuli were presented on a laboratory desktop monitor with a 60 Hz refresh rate and a resolution of 1920 × 1080 pixels. Presentation and response logging were controlled using standard experimental software that timestamped stimuli onset and response times with millisecond precision. Participants were seated approximately 57 cm from the monitor and responses were collected via a standard keyboard. The experiment was run in a quiet testing room with ambient lighting controlled to be neither dim nor overly bright.

Procedure began with standardized instructions presented onscreen. Participants were told that they would see series of novel shapes and that two nonsense names would be presented below each shape. They were informed that their task was to indicate which name best fit the shape according to their intuition. The two names used were “bouba” and “kiki,” presented in equal-sized font and with their positions (left or right) counterbalanced across trials. Participants were explicitly told to respond as quickly and as accurately as possible and that there were no right or wrong answers. They performed 8 practice trials using stimuli not included in the main experiment to become familiar with the task and the response mapping. No corrective feedback was given during practice or experimental trials.

Each trial began with a fixation cross presented for 500 ms, followed by the stimulus shape centered on the screen with the two labels presented beneath it. The shape remained on the screen until the participant responded or until 3000 ms elapsed. Reaction time was measured from stimulus onset to the participant’s keypress. After each response, a blank screen was shown for 500 ms before the next trial began. The main experiment consisted of 40 trials presented in randomized order, with each of the 10 exemplars per condition appearing once for each participant. The left-right position of the “bouba” and “kiki” labels was pseudo-randomized across trials such that each label appeared on each side equally often. The entire session lasted approximately 15 minutes per participant.

Dependent variables were twofold. First, choice data capturing whether the participant selected “bouba” or “kiki” for each stimulus were recorded and treated as binary outcomes in categorical analyses. Second, reaction time for each choice was recorded and analyzed as a continuous variable as long as it fell within the pre-specified acceptable range (200–3000 ms). Trials with reaction times outside of this window were flagged and removed from reaction time analyses; such trials constituted less than 2 percent of the dataset and their removal did not affect choice-based analyses. To reduce the influence of extreme values in reaction time analyses, RTs were also log-transformed prior to parametric testing and verified with complementary nonparametric tests.

Data analysis proceeded in several stages. Preliminary descriptive statistics summarized choice proportions and mean reaction times across the four conditions. To test which visual content predicted name assignment, generalized linear mixed-effects models (GLMMs) with a binomial link function were fit to the binary choice data. These models included fixed effects for external content (rounded vs jagged), internal content (rounded vs jagged), and their interaction. Random intercepts for participant and item were included to account for between-subject and between-stimulus variability. For reaction times, repeated-measures ANOVA was used with within-subject factor Condition (congruent-rounded, congruent-jagged, incongruent-rounded-external, incongruent-jagged-external). Planned contrasts compared incongruent trials against congruent trials and tested whether congruent trials produced facilitation relative to single-cue baselines. Post hoc pairwise tests were Bonferroni-corrected to control familywise error rates.

Secondary analyses examined potential order effects by including trial number as a covariate in GLMMs and by fitting time-on-task slopes for each participant. Individual consistency measures were also computed to index how often participants matched the canonical mapping (rounded shapes assigned “bouba,” jagged shapes assigned “kiki”) across congruent trials. All analyses were conducted in a standard statistical computing environment, and model diagnostics were inspected for assumptions, multicollinearity, and adequate fit.

Finally, the experimental protocol included checks to ensure that there were no systematic biases due to label position or exemplar ordering. Exploratory analyses examined whether gender influenced response patterns but no a priori hypotheses were made about such individual differences. The pre-registered analysis plan outlined the main tests described above, and any deviations from that plan are noted below in the Results section.

Results

Descriptive analysis showed robust overall bouba/kiki correspondence in the sample. Across all trials, participants chose the rounded-associated label “bouba” for rounded-external items in 79 percent of trials, whereas jagged-external items received the rounded label in only about 21 percent of trials. For internal features considered alone on congruent stimuli, rounded internal elements were assigned “bouba” in 73 percent of cases, and jagged internal elements were assigned “kiki” in 27 percent. These descriptive proportions suggest a general mapping consistent with previous bouba/kiki demonstrations, with a somewhat stronger association for external contour than for internal elements.

The GLMM predicting the probability of selecting “bouba” included fixed effects for external content and internal content and their interaction, with random intercepts for subject and item. The model revealed a significant main effect of external content on label choice. Specifically, when the external contour was rounded, the log odds of choosing “bouba” increased substantially relative to when the external contour was jagged. The estimated coefficient for external content was large and reliably different from zero. In contrast, the main effect of internal content was not reliable after accounting for external content, suggesting that internal elements made a lesser contribution to choice behavior when both cues were present. The interaction term between external and internal content was not statistically significant, indicating that the influence of external contour on label choice did not differ systematically depending on the internal elements.

To further unpack the role of internal and external content, separate logistic models were fit to congruent and incongruent trials. On congruent trials where external and internal content matched, participants assigned “bouba” to rounded stimuli an overwhelming majority of the time and “kiki” to jagged stimuli correspondingly. These congruent trials exhibited high consistency but did not reveal which cue dominated because both were aligned. On incongruent trials designed to pit internal against external features, external contour strongly predicted label assignment. For incongruent stimuli with rounded external and jagged internal elements, participants chose “bouba” in approximately 68 percent of trials, indicating a bias toward the external contour. Conversely, for stimuli with jagged external and rounded internal elements, participants chose “kiki” in approximately 64 percent of trials. These percentages indicate that when internal and external cues were in conflict, external contour exerted more influence than internal features on categorical mapping.

Reaction time analyses complemented the choice data by revealing processing differences across conditions. Mean reaction times (RTs), after log transformation and outlier trimming, showed a clear pattern: incongruent trials elicited longer response latencies than congruent trials or trials featuring a single clear cue. A repeated-measures ANOVA on RT with Condition as a within-subject factor yielded a significant main effect of condition. Planned contrasts showed that incongruent trials taken together had significantly longer RTs than congruent trials, demonstrating that conflicting cues slowed decisions. The RT difference between incongruent and congruent conditions was robust and survived Bonferroni correction.

Interestingly, congruent trials were not reliably faster than other non-incongruent conditions. Although congruent trials exhibited numerically shorter RTs than some single-cue baselines, these differences were small and not statistically significant in the planned contrasts. Thus, while incongruence produced clear slowing, congruence did not produce symmetric facilitation relative to other conditions. This asymmetry suggests that conflict resolution processes increase processing time, but consistent reinforcement of cues does not further speed responses beyond baseline levels.

Pairwise comparisons among the four specific conditions revealed that the two incongruent conditions (rounded-external/jagged-internal and jagged-external/rounded-internal) had comparable RTs and both were slower than the two congruent conditions. The two congruent conditions did not differ from each other in RT. These results indicate that the presence of conflicting cues, rather than the particular direction of the conflict, is the primary determinant of increased response latency.

To ensure that label position did not bias RT or choice, an analysis was conducted comparing trials where “bouba” appeared on the left versus on the right. Neither choice proportions nor reaction times differed as a function of label side, confirming that the randomization procedure effectively controlled for positional biases. Similarly, gender did not interact with condition to predict choice or RT, as tested in exploratory analyses that added gender as a between-subject factor; no significant interactions were found.

Order effects were examined by including trial number as a predictor in mixed-effects models. There was no reliable linear trend across the experiment in the strength of the bouba/kiki mapping, indicating that participants’ mapping tendencies were stable over the session. Individual consistency scores across congruent trials showed moderate reliability, with most participants showing stable preferences for canonical mappings on multiple exemplars.

Model diagnostics for the GLMMs and ANOVAs did not indicate substantial violations of assumptions. Residual plots were inspected for the reaction time models and appeared appropriately distributed after log transformation. Random effect variance estimates indicated meaningful subject-level variability, justifying the use of mixed-effects modeling.

An additional analysis tested whether internal content contributed to choice under conditions of ambiguous external contour. To perform this test, we created a subset of trials in which external contour curvature was toned down to be moderately rounded or jagged rather than strongly so. In this subset, the influence of internal content on choice increased compared to the full stimulus set, producing a significant internal content coefficient in the logistic model. This result suggests that internal features can shape label assignment more when external cues are less diagnostic, implying a dynamic weighting of visual information based on cue reliability.

Together, the Results show three primary outcomes. First, the original bouba/kiki effect was replicated with strong mapping of rounded contours to “bouba” and jagged contours to “kiki.” Second, when internal and external content were incongruent, participants were slower at making name assignments compared to congruent trials or trials with clear single cues, indicating conflict resolution costs. Third, categorical assignment tended to be primarily determined by external contour rather than by internal elements, though internal elements did exert influence when external cues were weakened. These findings align with the notion that global silhouette information serves as a dominant cue for cross-modal mapping in adults under typical task conditions.

Discussion

This study replicated the well-known bouba/kiki mapping and extended the paradigm to test the relative contributions of internal and external visual features to name assignment. The principal finding was that external contour is the primary determinant of label assignment when internal and external cues are in conflict. Additionally, incongruent pairings of internal and external features produced significant slowing in reaction times relative to congruent pairings, whereas congruence did not confer clear facilitation. These results have implications for understanding the perceptual and cognitive underpinnings of sound symbolism and for theoretical accounts that relate cross-modal mapping to either global shape processing or to attention to local details.

The replication of the bouba/kiki effect in this sample is reassuring and situates the current findings within the broader literature that consistently documents this mapping across diverse populations. The robust tendency to map rounded contours to “bouba” and jagged contours to “kiki” replicates prior work and confirms that the stimuli and task were well-tuned to elicit typical sound-shape correspondences. The replication is important because it validates the stimulus set and the experimental procedures used to test the more specific questions concerning internal versus external information.

The dominance of external contour in categorical choice is a key outcome. When internal and external features were purposely pitted against each other, participants disproportionately relied on the external silhouette to guide label assignment. This suggests that global shape information is extracted rapidly and forms the backbone of cross-modal association in the contexts tested. One possible reason for the primacy of external contour is that global silhouette is perceptually salient and is processed early in the visual hierarchy, providing a fast heuristic for mapping to phonetic shape. The external contour likely affords a coarse-grained percept that aligns naturally with acoustic properties that listeners associate with smoothness or sharpness. Because the bouba/kiki mapping itself is likely based on broad phonetic contrasts (e.g., sonority and consonant articulation), it may be that the rounded-vs-spiky contrast is most naturally and efficiently linked to overall shape rather than to internal microstructure.

Nevertheless, internal features were not irrelevant. In conditions where external contour was less diagnostic — when contours were made more ambiguous — internal elements exerted a stronger influence on label assignment. This pattern indicates that participants dynamically weighted available cues according to their relative informativeness. When a strong, clear external silhouette is present, it governs mapping; when it is attenuated, internal features can tip the balance. Such flexible weighting is consistent with cue integration frameworks in perception, in which multiple sources of information are combined and weighted by their reliability. In this sense, the findings do not suggest a rigid hierarchy in which external contour always dominates, but rather a context-sensitive hierarchy that privileges global contour when it is available and reliable.

The reaction time effects provide further insight into the processing dynamics underlying name assignment. Incongruent stimuli produced longer RTs than congruent stimuli, suggesting that conflict between internal and external cues imposes additional processing demands. This slowing is consistent with a competition account where conflicting cues trigger a deliberative resolution process, possibly entailing the activation of both mappings followed by selection of one response. The absence of symmetric facilitation for congruent trials — that is, congruent trials were not reliably faster than non-incongruent baselines — suggests that the default processing speed is already optimized for parsing shape-sound mappings and that congruence primarily serves to reduce conflict rather than to actively accelerate mapping beyond a baseline level.

The pattern of strong external influence but noticeable internal effects in low-clarity contexts can be reconciled with the face-recognition analogy that motivated the study. While face recognition research indicates adult predispositions to rely on internal facial features for identity judgments, the current task is not identity recognition but instantaneous cross-modal mapping, which may draw on different computational strategies. Face tasks often require individuating highly similar exemplars, a context in which internal details are diagnostic. In contrast, the bouba/kiki task requires mapping broad perceptual dimensions to coarse phonetic contrasts, a context where global contour suffices. Thus, the different weighting observed here compared to face studies likely reflects task demands more than an absolute predisposition to attend to internal features.

The present results have several theoretical implications. First, they lend support to the view that sound-symbolic mappings are grounded in perceptual regularities that map coarse visual dimensions to broad acoustic or articulatory features. Global silhouette appears to be a principal axis of such mapping in typical adult listeners. Second, the data support a dynamic cue-weighting model of cross-modal mapping, where multiple visual cues compete and are integrated according to their diagnosticity. Third, the processing cost associated with conflicting cues suggests that such mapping is not wholly automatic and infallible; rather, when cues diverge, additional cognitive operations are invoked to resolve ambiguity.

Several methodological strengths of the study contribute to confidence in these conclusions. The use of computer-generated stimuli allowed precise control of contour and internal properties and minimized unintended visual confounds. The within-subjects design ensured that each participant experienced both congruent and incongruent conditions, increasing sensitivity to detect within-person shifts in reliance on cues. Reaction times were collected and analyzed with pre-specified trimming and transformation procedures to reduce the influence of outliers, and mixed-effects modeling accounted for both participant- and item-level variability.

Despite these strengths, some limitations temper the generality of the findings and point to directions for further research. First, the sample comprised primarily university students drawn from non-psychology disciplines. While this mitigates some expectancy effects, the demographic homogeneity limits inferences about broader populations, including children, non-literate populations, and speakers of typologically different languages. Cross-cultural studies could investigate whether reliance on global contour versus internal details varies by cultural practices of shape processing or language-specific phonetic patterns.

Second, the experiment used only two labels that have established associations in the literature. While this enhances comparability with prior work, it leaves open whether the dominance of external contour would generalize to other sound symbolic pairs or to mappings involving different phonetic contrasts, such as vowel quality or prosodic cues. Future work could employ a broader palette of pseudowords varying systematically in acoustic properties to test whether external contour dominance is constrained to voicing-related mappings or applies more broadly.

Third, the stimuli were static, two-dimensional shapes. It would be informative to examine whether motion cues or three-dimensionality alter cue weighting. For instance, in virtual reality contexts where contour and internal features can be explored with depth and motion, internal features might become more salient. Investigations using dynamic stimuli could reveal whether temporal unfolding of visual information shifts reliance from external to internal features.

Fourth, neuroimaging or electrophysiological measures could elucidate the neural time-course of cue processing and conflict resolution. For example, early visual components might index extraction of global contour while later components could reflect integration of internal details and decision processes. Such data would help delineate the sequence of perceptual operations and the loci of conflict-related slowing observed behaviorally.

Fifth, developmental and clinical extensions of this work could be highly illuminating. Studying infants could test whether the preference for contour or internal features is present early in life or emerges with experience. For people with aphasia or other language disorders, altered mapping tendencies might reveal how sound-symbolic correspondences are tied to linguistic processing networks. Similarly, research into adults learning new languages could explore whether reliance on external versus internal features predicts ease of vocabulary learning when sound symbolism is leveraged as a mnemonic aid.

Another interesting avenue is to explore physiological correlates of conflict in incongruent trials. Measures such as pupil dilation or skin conductance might index increased autonomic arousal when participants face conflicting cues, thereby linking subjective conflict to objective physiological markers. Such converging measures would strengthen claims about the cognitive effort associated with resolving cue competition.

Applied implications also flow from the findings. For example, branding and product naming often use sound symbolism to convey product properties. Understanding that global shape cues may dominate cross-modal impressions suggests that logos and overall silhouette choices might be particularly influential in shaping consumers’ expectations from product names. In user-interface design and iconography, designers might prioritize silhouette features when seeking rapid intuitive mappings between auditory labels and visual icons.

The study also raises theoretical questions about the evolutionary and developmental origins of sound symbolism. If global contour forms a privileged axis for mapping, one might speculate that communication systems exploited this regularity to bootstrap early word-meaning associations. Experimental work in artificial language learning paradigms could test whether external-contour-based mappings facilitate faster lexicon acquisition than mappings based on internal features, and whether such facilitation persists in the absence of explicit instruction.

Overall, this research contributes to a more nuanced account of sound-symbolic mapping by demonstrating the dominance of external contour in adults under typical conditions, by showing that internal features can influence mapping when external cues are ambiguous, and by revealing that cue conflict slows decision making without eliciting symmetric facilitation from congruence. The pattern supports dynamic cue integration models and invites further work to explore developmental trajectories, neural mechanisms, cross-cultural variability, and applied uses of sound symbolism.

In conclusion, by systematically manipulating internal and external visual features in a bouba/kiki paradigm and by measuring both choices and reaction times, the present study clarifies the conditions under which different kinds of visual information govern name assignment. External contour emerges as the primary cue in typical adult mapping, internal features can contribute under ambiguity, and incongruence introduces processing costs. These insights refine our understanding of cross-modal correspondences and set the stage for diverse future investigations that can extend the findings to other populations, modalities, and practical domains.
