Introduction

Religion is a pervasive aspect of human history and culture. It has shaped social structures, moral codes, and individual life choices across societies. Religion has been defined in many ways, but a common thread is belief in and practice oriented toward supernatural agents or transcendent realities. Religion often involves ritual practices, communal gatherings, and narratives that provide explanations for why the world is as it is. Over time, religious systems have changed, with new movements emerging and older traditions evolving. In some regions and demographics, religious influence has declined while in others it remains robust and central to daily life. The social role of religion has long been a subject of debate among scholars, policymakers, and practitioners. One prominent line of inquiry concerns the relationship between religion and psychological well-being. That relationship is theoretically complex because religion can provide both resources and constraints for individuals. On one hand, religion can provide social support, meaning, and coping strategies that can enhance well-being. On the other hand, religion can be associated with guilt, intergroup conflict, and restrictive norms that may undermine well-being for some people. Empirically, studies have produced evidence for both beneficial and adverse relations between religion and various indicators of mental health. Some studies have documented positive associations between religious involvement and life satisfaction, lower depressive symptoms, and reduced anxiety in certain populations. Other investigations have described null or even negative associations when religious commitments are rigid, when religious identity is in conflict with other aspects of identity, or when institutional religion is associated with social exclusion. The divergent findings in the literature suggest that the relationship between religion and well-being is conditional on multiple factors including the form of religious engagement, cultural context, and individual differences. One dimension of well-being that has been linked to religion in prior research is optimism. Optimism is commonly understood as a generalized expectancy that good things, rather than bad things, will happen in the future. Optimism has been associated with better coping, persistence in the face of difficulties, and a host of beneficial health outcomes. Researchers have examined whether religion promotes optimism by providing frameworks for hope, narratives that foreground ultimate justice, and practices that foster a sense of control or trust. Some lines of research have reported positive correlations between religious involvement and measures of optimism. Those studies often argue that religious frameworks encourage positive expectations either through doctrinal assurances or through social reinforcement in religious communities. However, other research has found that the link between religion and optimism is more nuanced. For example, the content of religious beliefs may matter, such that beliefs emphasizing divine benevolence may promote optimism, whereas beliefs emphasizing punishment or judgment may not. Similarly, the distinction between private religious practice and organizational participation may affect psychological outcomes differently. Participation in communal religious practices can provide social reinforcement of hopeful narratives, whereas solitary ritual may yield less of this social uplift. Another important distinction in the literature is between religious belief and religious practice. Belief refers to the cognitive-associative acceptance of doctrines or spiritual propositions, while practice refers to the behavioral enactment of prayer, attendance, and ritual. Some studies find that practice is more strongly associated with social integration and support, and that these social processes, not belief per se, account for links to well-being. Other research suggests that intrinsic religious orientation, in which religion is integrated into the core of an individual's identity, is more beneficial than extrinsic orientation, where religion serves more instrumental functions. The diversity of constructs and measures across studies complicates attempts to draw general conclusions. The secularization thesis predicts that in some societies religion might become less influential over time, and this social change could moderate relationships between religion and psychological variables. In contexts where religious practice is normative, religiosity might be associated with broad social networks that support optimism. Conversely, in more secular contexts a highly religious individual might experience social dissonance that could reduce well-being. Another issue that arises in the literature is measurement. Some prior research uses single-item indicators of religiosity, such as frequency of attendance, while other studies use multidimensional scales that capture depth, intensity, and content of belief. Optimism measures also vary, with some instruments designed to capture dispositional optimism and others focused on situational expectations. These methodological inconsistencies make comparisons across studies challenging. In light of this complexity, it is useful to ask a focused question about the relation between a specific operationalization of religiosity and a specific operationalization of optimism. If religious practice is meaningfully linked to optimism, then individuals who engage more frequently in religious practices should, on average, report higher levels of dispositional optimism. If no relation exists, then the theorized benefits of religious practice for generalized future expectations may need to be qualified. Given the mixed findings in the literature, we sought to contribute to ongoing debate by examining the relation between religious practice and optimism in a community sample. We focused on religious practice rather than broad religious belief because practice is an observable and behaviorally grounded aspect of religiosity that has been linked to social integration and support. The construct of optimism was operationalized as dispositional future-oriented positive expectancies, measured by a brief standardized instrument. Prior evidence suggests a positive association between various aspects of religiosity and optimism, although effect sizes reported in the literature vary and are often small to moderate. We anticipated that the pattern of results might be contingent on demographic factors such as age and gender as well as on measurement properties of the scales used. Based on theoretical grounds and previous empirical findings indicating an association between religiosity and optimistic outlook, our primary hypothesis was that higher levels of religious practice would be associated with greater dispositional optimism. We expected a positive linear correlation when both constructs were measured on continuous scales. We also planned secondary, exploratory analyses to test whether the relation held after controlling for basic demographic variables and whether different facets of religious practice, such as private prayer frequency versus communal attendance, showed differential relations with optimism. The study employed a correlational design that allowed us to examine associations within a cross-sectional framework. Although such an approach does not permit causal inference, it permits a direct test of the hypothesized association within a well-defined sample. We submitted the study to an institutional ethics review to ensure that procedures conformed to ethical standards for human subjects research. The survey was administered online to reach a broad cross-section of participants and to ensure anonymity, which can reduce social desirability bias on sensitive items. We preregistered the primary analysis plan to evaluate the a priori hypothesis of a positive association between religious practice and dispositional optimism. Secondary and exploratory analyses were clearly delineated as such. This careful design allowed us to investigate the specific question of whether a behavioral index of religiosity is associated with a standardized measure of optimism in a mid-sized convenience sample. By focusing on practice rather than belief and by using multiple analytic approaches to evaluate robustness, the study aimed to add clarity to an otherwise heterogeneous literature. The present investigation thus addresses a clear gap by providing a straightforward test of a theoretically motivated hypothesis in an accessible community sample. Our analytic approach prioritized transparency and included checks for normality, reliability, and potential outliers. We also considered nonparametric alternatives and effect size estimates to interpret the practical significance of any observed relations. Overall, the study was designed to provide an informative test of the specific question: does religious practice predict higher dispositional optimism in a community sample?

Method

Participants were 118 adults who completed an online survey that included measures of religious practice, dispositional optimism, and demographic information. Recruitment occurred through online advertisements posted on community forums and social media platforms over a period of four weeks. Eligible participants were required to be at least 18 years old and able to read English. The sample included participants from a variety of socioeconomic backgrounds and geographic locations, although it was a convenience sample rather than a probability sample. Participants provided informed consent prior to beginning the survey and were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without penalty. The survey took approximately 15 minutes to complete on average. Participants were not compensated monetarily, but were entered into a voluntary raffle for a small number of gift cards as an incentive. Demographic questions included age, gender, level of education, self-described religious affiliation, and whether participants regularly attended religious services. The religious practice measure was a composite questionnaire developed to assess behavioral aspects of religiosity. It included items assessing frequency of attendance at religious services, frequency of private prayer or meditation, frequency of participation in religious rituals, and self-reported importance of religious practice in daily life. Each item was scored on a Likert-type scale with higher scores indicating greater frequency or importance. The items were combined into a single composite religiosity score by averaging across standardized items after confirming internal consistency. The optimism measure used in the study was a widely employed brief instrument designed to capture dispositional optimism. The instrument consists of a series of statements about expectations for the future that participants rate on a Likert scale. Items assessing optimism are summed or averaged to produce a single score where higher values indicate greater dispositional optimism. The measures were administered in a fixed sequence, with demographics first, followed by the religious practice items, followed by the optimism items. The order was selected to reduce the possibility that responding to optimism items could prime religious responses. To guard against careless responding, the survey included two attention-check items embedded within the questionnaires that instructed participants to select a particular response. Responses that failed both attention checks were flagged for exclusion. No participants were excluded for failing attention checks in the final sample of 118. Data were inspected for completeness and anomalous response patterns prior to analysis. Missing data were minimal, with less than 1% missing at the item level, and were handled using pairwise deletion for descriptive analyses and listwise deletion for primary inferential tests. All scale scores were computed according to prescribed scoring procedures; items that required reverse coding were rescored prior to computation of composite scores. Internal consistency reliability for the religiosity composite was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha and was examined to ensure that the items cohered sufficiently to justify aggregation. Internal consistency for the optimism measure was also computed to assess reliability in this sample. Prior to conducting primary analyses, basic descriptive statistics were computed for all primary variables, including means, standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis. Distributional properties were inspected visually using histograms and Q-Q plots to determine whether parametric assumptions were reasonable. Outliers were identified using boxplots and influence statistics in regression models; decisions about retaining or removing outliers were made with reference to both statistical criteria and substantive considerations. The primary planned analysis was a Pearson correlation between the continuous measure of religious practice and the optimism score, conducted two-tailed with an alpha level of .05. In addition to the Pearson correlation, a Spearman rank-order correlation was also computed as a nonparametric check in case of violations of linearity or sensitivity to outliers. Secondary analyses included a linear regression predicting optimism from religiosity while controlling for age and gender, entered simultaneously. Gender was coded as a binary variable for these analyses, reflecting participant self-report of male or female; participants who selected other categories were few in number and were included in descriptive statistics but were not treated as a separate dummy-coded group in regression due to limited sample size. An exploratory median-split analysis was also planned, in which participants were divided into higher and lower religiosity groups and compared on optimism using an independent-samples t-test. Effect size estimates were provided for key tests, including Pearson’s r for correlations, Cohen’s d for means comparisons, and standardized beta coefficients for regression analyses. Confidence intervals at the 95% level were computed for primary effect size estimates to aid interpretation. Given the correlational design, causal interpretations were avoided and analyses focused on the magnitude and direction of associations. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Statistical decisions and thresholds for inference were specified a priori, and the full dataset and analytic code were retained for reproducibility. The sample size of 118 was recognized as providing adequate power to detect moderate-sized effects but limited power for small effects, a point that was considered in interpreting null results.

Results

Descriptive statistics indicated that the sample of 118 participants had a mean age of 34.7 years with a standard deviation of 11.4 years. The sample included 72 individuals who identified as female, 44 who identified as male, and 2 who selected another or preferred not to say; the small number in the latter category limited subgroup analyses by gender beyond a male-female comparison. Educational attainment varied, with participants reporting high school completion through postgraduate degrees. A variety of religious affiliations were represented including no affiliation, Christian denominations, and other faith traditions, although the largest subgroup reported either Christian or no affiliation. The composite religiosity score ranged from the minimum to the maximum of the scale, indicating that there was variability in reported practice among participants. The mean religiosity score was 3.12 on the scale used, with a standard deviation of 1.04. The optimism score also showed a broad range; the mean optimism score was 14.8 with a standard deviation of 4.2, consistent with past applications of the scale in community samples. Internal consistency reliability for the religious practice composite was acceptable, with a Cronbach’s alpha of .83. Internal consistency reliability for the optimism measure was also acceptable, with a Cronbach’s alpha of .76. Skewness and kurtosis values for both primary variables were within acceptable ranges for parametric analyses, and Q-Q plots did not reveal extreme deviations from normality. Visual inspection of scatterplots suggested no obvious nonlinear relation between religiosity and optimism. The primary analysis, a Pearson product-moment correlation between religiosity and dispositional optimism, yielded a small positive correlation that was not statistically significant. The Pearson r was .06 and the associated two-tailed p-value was .52. The 95% confidence interval for the correlation ranged from approximately -.13 to .24, indicating that the observed data were compatible with small negative or small positive true effects, but did not provide evidence for a meaningful positive relation. A nonparametric Spearman rank-order correlation produced a similar null result, with rho = .07 and p = .48. These convergent findings across parametric and nonparametric tests reduced concern that the null finding was an artifact of distributional violations. Given the nonsignificant correlation, an exploratory median-split analysis was performed to compare mean optimism scores between participants classified as higher versus lower in religious practice. The independent-samples t-test revealed no significant mean difference in optimism between the higher religiosity group (M = 15.02, SD = 4.16) and the lower religiosity group (M = 14.58, SD = 4.24). The t statistic was 0.52 with 116 degrees of freedom and the two-tailed p-value was .60. The corresponding Cohen’s d was .10, indicating a very small effect size. A multiple linear regression predicting optimism from religiosity, age, and gender indicated that religiosity did not explain significant unique variance in optimism when controlling for the demographic covariates. In that model, religiosity had a standardized beta of .05 with a p-value of .62. Age had a small negative association with optimism (beta = -.12, p = .19) and gender coded as female versus male had a small positive association with optimism (beta = .09, p = .30), but neither covariate reached statistical significance in this sample. The full regression model did not account for a statistically significant portion of variance in optimism, with an R-squared of .02 and an adjusted R-squared near zero. Influence diagnostics in the regression analyses did not identify any individual cases with leverage or residuals large enough to materially influence the parameter estimates. To explore whether specific aspects of religious practice might be differentially related to optimism, we examined correlations between individual religious practice items and optimism. Frequency of communal attendance had a correlation with optimism of r = .04 (p = .65), frequency of private prayer correlated r = .08 (p = .40), and self-reported importance of practice correlated r = .03 (p = .74). None of these item-level correlations approached statistical significance. We also examined whether religious affiliation categories differed in mean optimism using a one-way ANOVA. Mean optimism did not differ significantly across major affiliation groups in this sample, F(3,114) = 0.87, p = .46. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were not pursued given the nonsignificant omnibus test and the modest subsample sizes within some categories. Because the observed correlation between religiosity and optimism was small, we conducted a post-hoc power analysis to estimate the sample size that would be required to detect an effect of the observed magnitude with conventional power. To detect a correlation of r = .06 with 80% power at alpha = .05 would require a sample size well in excess of 1,000 participants, indicating that the present sample of 118 had limited ability to detect effects of this size. However, the present sample would have had adequate power to detect moderate-sized correlations in the range of r = .25 to r = .30. Accordingly, the null result in the present study cannot rule out small effects but does indicate that there is not a moderate or large positive association between religiosity as measured here and optimism in this sample. In light of the null findings, the possibility of Type II error was considered, and it was noted that measurement error or restricted range in religiosity could reduce power to detect relations. Nevertheless, the internal consistencies of the measures were acceptable, and the distribution of religiosity scores suggested reasonable variability. Taken together, the results revealed no reliable association between religious practice and dispositional optimism in this convenience sample.

Discussion

The present study examined whether religious practice is associated with dispositional optimism in a community sample of adults. The primary hypothesis, that higher levels of religious practice would be associated with greater optimism, was not supported. Across multiple analytic approaches including Pearson and Spearman correlations, regression analyses controlling for demographic covariates, and group comparisons based on median splits, the data failed to reveal a reliable positive relation between the behavioral index of religiosity and dispositional optimism. The effect sizes were uniformly small and confidence intervals encompassed zero. These findings contribute to a nuanced understanding of the religion-well-being literature by suggesting that religious practice, at least as operationalized in the present study, does not inevitably correspond to higher levels of generalized optimistic expectations. One immediate implication of the null findings is that the putative benefits of religious engagement for mental states characterized by hopeful future expectations cannot be assumed across samples. While prior research has found associations between religiosity and certain dimensions of well-being, the relationship appears to be conditional on a range of factors. The present study adds to the literature by demonstrating a lack of association in a mid-sized, geographically varied convenience sample when religiosity is conceptualized and measured in terms of frequency and importance of practice. There are several plausible explanations for the lack of a detectable relation in our data. One possibility is that it is not behavioral practice per se that fosters optimism, but rather the content and interpretation of religious beliefs. For instance, beliefs that frame the future in terms of a benevolent deity or eventual justice may foster optimism more than ritual attendance alone. If this is the case, measures that capture belief content or intrinsic religiosity may show different patterns of association with optimism. Another possibility is that social context moderates the relation; in environments where religious practice is normative and reinforced by community networks, practice may bolster optimism through social support. In more secular or pluralistic contexts, frequent religious practice could be associated with minority status or social distance that offsets any optimism-promoting effects. The present sample likely included participants across such contexts, which could attenuate aggregate associations. Measurement and operationalization choices may also account for the current null results. The religiosity composite used in this study aggregated several practice-oriented items, and although the scale displayed acceptable internal consistency, it may not have captured the dimensions of religiosity most relevant to optimism. For example, measures of religious coping, perceived spiritual support, or doctrinal assurance might show stronger relations with optimism. Similarly, the optimism measure captured dispositional generalized expectancies, which may be less sensitive to domain-specific hopeful beliefs that religious practice nurtures, such as hope about spiritual matters or communal outcomes. Thus, future work might examine both generalized optimism and religiously specific expectancies to elucidate potential differential patterns. Another consideration is the cross-sectional and correlational design of the study. Even if religion and optimism are related in some contexts, the direction of causality could vary. Optimistic individuals might be more inclined to seek communal or spiritual practices, or conversely, engaging in religious practices could foster optimism over time. Longitudinal or experimental approaches would be necessary to disentangle such temporal dynamics. The present design cannot adjudicate between these possibilities. Statistical power and sampling considerations also warrant attention. The sample size of 118 afforded reasonable power to detect moderate effect sizes but was underpowered for detecting very small effects. The observed correlation of r = .06 was small, and a much larger sample would be required to detect such an effect reliably. Therefore, the null finding should be interpreted as evidence against moderate or large effects in this context, but it cannot completely exclude the existence of small associations. Moreover, the convenience sampling strategy and online recruitment may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Participants who self-select into online research panels may differ systematically from the general population in both religiosity and optimism. Social desirability and self-report biases may also influence responses on sensitive items about religious practice; although anonymity was emphasized to mitigate these concerns, such biases cannot be entirely ruled out. The null findings highlight the importance of considering moderators and mechanisms in the religion-optimism relation. It is plausible that religious practice enhances optimism indirectly through social support, meaning-making, or enhanced perceived control. Such mediation effects would not be detectable in simple bivariate correlations unless the mediating pathways are explicitly modeled and measured. Future research could include measures of perceived social support, sense of meaning, and control beliefs to test mediation models. Additionally, moderators such as cultural context, intrinsic versus extrinsic religious orientation, and the valence of doctrinal content (e.g., benevolence versus punitive themes) could shape whether and how practice relates to optimism. Testing interactive models across diverse cultural samples would help clarify boundary conditions. Another fruitful direction for future research is to examine the interplay of private and communal religious practices. In the present study, neither private prayer nor communal attendance showed significant associations with optimism, but the sample size may have limited the ability to detect subtle differences. Qualitative methods could also provide rich, contextualized insights into how individuals interpret their religious practices and whether those interpretations include hopeful expectations. Such mixed-method approaches could identify nuances that standardized questionnaires might miss. The theoretical implications of the null finding are noteworthy. It suggests that while religion can be associated with some aspects of psychological well-being, it is not a monolithic influence that uniformly enhances generalized positive expectancies. Theories that posit a straightforward pathway from religious involvement to optimism must be tempered to account for complexity and heterogeneity. The present results are consistent with a model in which religious practice is one among many social and cognitive resources that may contribute to optimism for some individuals but not others. Methodologically, researchers should be cautious about equating frequency of practice with the psychological qualities that confer hopefulness. Future measurement development could aim to more precisely capture the psychological mechanisms by which religion might influence optimism, such as perceived benevolence of a transcendent agent, narratives of redemption, or communal reinforcement of hopeful frames. Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged explicitly. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, the convenience sample limits external validity. Third, the measures, while reliable, may not have encompassed the full conceptual breadth of religiosity and optimism. Fourth, subgroup analyses by specific faith traditions or minority identities were not feasible due to limited cell sizes. Fifth, potential confounding variables such as mental health status, socioeconomic adversity, or major life events were not included and could influence both religiosity and optimism. Despite these limitations, the study has strengths. The analytic approach was transparent and included both parametric and nonparametric checks. Measures showed acceptable internal consistency. Multiple analytic perspectives converged on a consistent conclusion of no meaningful bivariate relation in this sample. In conclusion, the current investigation found no evidence that religious practice is positively associated with dispositional optimism in a community sample. The lack of a detectable relation suggests that the benefits of religious practice for generalized optimistic outlooks are not automatic and may depend on other factors such as belief content, social context, and individual differences. Future research should employ longitudinal designs, more nuanced measures of religiosity and optimism, and larger, more diverse samples to clarify the conditions under which religion and optimism are linked. Understanding these conditions has theoretical importance for models of religion and well-being and practical implication for interventions that seek to harness religious resources to promote psychological resilience.
