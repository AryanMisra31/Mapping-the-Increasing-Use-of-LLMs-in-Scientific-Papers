Introduction

Sound symbolism refers to systematic associations between the sound structure of a word and its meaning, and it has been documented across languages and cultures. Early work on sound symbolism established that certain phonetic features consistently map onto particular perceptual or semantic properties, which implies that the relationship between form and meaning is not entirely arbitrary. One of the most frequently cited demonstrations of sound symbolism is the “bouba/kiki” effect, in which people overwhelmingly match rounded shapes with words like “bouba” and angular shapes with words like “kiki.” This effect has been reported reliably in adults and children, and across diverse language backgrounds, suggesting a pervasive and robust pattern of crossmodal mapping between auditory properties and visual shape. The robustness of the bouba/kiki effect has led researchers to consider broader theoretical accounts of crossmodal correspondences that include shared sensory coding, statistical co-occurrence in the environment, and cognitive biases that favor certain mappings. Across these accounts, it is often emphasized that perceptual salience, structural features of phonemes, and early sensory integration processes can play critical roles in generating consistent mappings between sound and visual shape.

Despite the general consensus on the prevalence of sound symbolism, important questions remain about the extent to which individual differences shape the strength or nature of sound-symbolic mappings. For instance, auditory expertise in the form of musical training might plausibly influence crossmodal associations because musical training alters auditory discrimination abilities, auditory memory, and sensitivity to acoustic features such as pitch, timbre, and temporal patterns. Musicians often demonstrate enhanced low-level auditory processing and altered neural responses to sound compared with non-musicians, and these differences could plausibly modulate how auditory information engages with visual representations. If musical training enhances sensitivity to specific acoustic cues that underpin sound-symbolic mappings, then musicians might show stronger or qualitatively different bouba/kiki-like associations. Conversely, musical training could refine auditory processing in ways that reduce reliance on broad acoustic heuristics and thereby attenuate sound-symbolic biases.

Beyond individual differences in auditory expertise, the sensory modality in which stimuli are presented could also influence sound symbolism. The bouba/kiki effect is typically demonstrated with auditory pseudowords paired with static visual shapes, but many studies have also used written forms of pseudowords or combined audiovisual presentations. The modality of presentation could change the degree to which acoustic or orthographic cues are available to participants and could shift processing toward more language-mediated or more sensory-based pathways. For example, written presentations may rely more on graphemic-phonemic associations and orthographic parsing, whereas auditory presentations provide raw acoustic patterns that might more directly evoke sensory correspondences with visual form. Audiovisual presentations could either reinforce mappings through redundant cues or introduce complexity by creating competing channels of information. Given these possibilities, it is important to test whether modality modulates the strength of sound symbolism in settings where auditory expertise varies across participants.

Empirical work on modality effects in sound symbolism has returned mixed results. Some studies have reported that the bouba/kiki effect emerges robustly across written, spoken, and combined modalities, suggesting a modality-independent cognitive mapping. Other studies have observed subtle differences in mapping strength depending on whether words are heard, read, or seen alongside visual motion or texture cues. Methodological variations such as the specific pseudowords used, the number of trials, and task demands may contribute to these inconsistencies. Furthermore, the intersection of modality and individual differences like musical training has remained relatively underexplored, leaving open the question of whether auditory expertise interacts with modality to shape sound-symbolic judgments.

Theoretical accounts that could predict an influence of musical training on sound symbolism draw on the idea that acoustic sensitivity underpins crossmodal mappings. The acoustic features that typically distinguish “bouba”-type from “kiki”-type pseudowords include vowel quality (roundedness and backness), consonant type (sonorants and plosives), and spectral properties such as the presence of high-frequency energy. Musicians’ enhanced ability to detect fine-grained spectral and temporal differences could lead them to weigh acoustic differences more heavily when making crossmodal mappings. If the bouba/kiki association is partly driven by sensitivity to spectral brightness or articulatory characteristics that correlate with perceived angularity or roundness, then increased auditory acuity should magnify these mappings.

On the other hand, other theoretical perspectives suggest that sound symbolism rests on stable, language-linked associations that are robust to individual variation in sensory acuity. From this view, sound-symbolic mappings are mediated by early-developing tendencies and learned statistical regularities that are broadly distributed in the population. These mappings could be implemented at higher representational levels that are insensitive to refined auditory skills cultivated through musical training. If sound symbolism is scaffolded by phonological patterns and conceptual analogies rather than purely by acoustic detail, then musical training might not appreciably alter the bouba/kiki effect.

The modality dimension likewise intersects with these theoretical possibilities. If modality influences sound symbolism, it may do so by altering access to acoustic versus symbolic cues. For example, reading a word engages orthographic-to-phonological mapping processes that could activate phonological representations without the raw acoustic signal. Audiovisual presentations combine both channels and could produce additive or competitive effects. The net impact of modality might depend heavily on task demands and on whether participants are encouraged to base decisions on sound, on spelling, or on a more holistic impression. The consequences for musicians might therefore hinge on whether they prioritize acoustic over linguistic or orthographic information in mapping tasks.

Given these theoretical stakes, the present study was designed to examine whether musical training influences performance in a bouba/kiki task and whether the modality of stimulus presentation modulates this influence. The rationale was to test two specific hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that individuals with significant musical training would show stronger and more consistent bouba/kiki mappings compared with non-musicians, reflecting enhanced sensitivity to acoustic features that drive sound-symbolic correspondences. Second, we hypothesized that modality of presentation—auditory, visual (written), or audiovisual—would affect mapping strength and potentially interact with musical training. Under this hypothesis, auditory presentations might elicit the strongest musician/non-musician differences, whereas written presentations might attenuate such differences by shifting processing toward orthographic routes.

To address these hypotheses we designed an experiment with a mixed factorial design. Participants were grouped according to musical training status—musician versus non-musician—and each participant completed bouba/kiki mapping trials in three modalities: auditory-only, written-only, and audiovisual. The dependent measure was the proportion of trials in which participants selected the conventional bouba/kiki-consistent mapping. The study included additional measures such as reaction time and self-reported years of musical training to facilitate continuous analyses of expertise.

In designing the experiment, we took several methodological precautions intended to provide a fair test of the effects of musical training and modality. Participants were screened to ensure comparable demographic characteristics across groups. Musicians were required to meet a threshold of training and active practice to avoid conflating casual music experience with sustained musical expertise. Non-musicians were screened to minimize prior music training that might confound group distinctions. The stimuli were piloted to confirm the robustness of shape-word pairings and to reduce idiosyncratic biases. The number of trials and their randomization were chosen to reduce fatigue and order effects while providing sufficient observations to estimate within-participant variability.

Beyond the primary hypotheses, the design allowed for several exploratory analyses. We planned to test correlations between years of training and mapping strength to determine whether musical experience showed a monotonic relationship with sound-symbolic performance. We also planned to examine reaction times as indicators of decisional difficulty and to check for modality-specific speed differences. Finally, we intended to contrast observed mapping rates against chance-level responding to confirm that the bouba/kiki effect manifested in the present sample.

The study contributes to ongoing debates about the cognitive and perceptual bases of sound symbolism. If musical training amplifies sound-symbolic mappings, then this would support accounts that emphasize sensory-level contributions to crossmodal correspondences and suggest that experience-driven auditory plasticity can modify such mappings. If modality influences mapping strength, then modality effects would indicate that the channel of presentation shapes the balance between acoustic and symbolic cues. Alternatively, if no effects of musical training or modality are observed, then this would underscore the robustness and possible domain-general nature of sound-symbolic associations, which may resist modulation by sensory expertise or presentation channel. Such a null finding would also prompt consideration of methodological or sampling factors that might obscure subtle effects.

It is worth noting that null results can be theoretically informative when they arise from adequately powered and well-controlled studies. Therefore, in addition to standard null hypothesis significance testing, the study adopted complementary analytic approaches, including effect size estimation and Bayesian inference, to evaluate evidence for the presence or absence of effects. These analyses help to determine whether the data are more consistent with a genuine lack of effect or simply insufficient power to detect one.

The results of this study therefore bear on how we understand the interplay of sensory experience, language-like representations, and crossmodal mappings. They also inform methodological choices for future research by clarifying whether distinguishing participant groups by musical training is a fruitful strategy for probing sensory contributions to sound symbolism. If musical training does not alter sound-symbolic mappings, then future research may need to focus on other individual difference dimensions, such as language experience, musical perceptual skills (rather than training per se), or specific acoustic sensitivity measures.

Taken together, this study sought to test competing theoretical accounts and to provide empirical clarity on whether musical training and sensory modality shape the canonical bouba/kiki effect. The work aimed to contribute to a nuanced understanding of how learning, expertise, and modality interact with fundamental tendencies in human perceptual and linguistic mapping. The following Methods section describes participant selection, stimuli, procedure, and planned analyses in detail.

Methods

Participants and grouping were established to compare individuals with significant musical training to those with minimal training. A total of 40 participants were recruited from a university student population and the surrounding community. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing. The study adhered to institutional ethical guidelines for behavioral research. Recruitment targeted two groups of equal size, and final enrollment included 20 participants classified as musicians and 20 participants classified as non-musicians. Musicians were defined as individuals with at least five years of formal musical instruction and who reported ongoing engagement with musical practice within the past two years. Non-musicians were defined as individuals with less than one year of formal musical instruction and no regularly sustained practice. Self-reported years of practice and current weekly practice hours were collected to provide a continuous index of musical experience for secondary analyses.

Participants ranged in age from 18 to 33 years old. Age was balanced across groups with mean ages of 22.1 years (SD = 3.5) for musicians and 22.6 years (SD = 3.9) for non-musicians. Gender distribution was similar across groups, and no significant group differences in education level were noted. Participants received course credit or a modest monetary compensation for their time.

Stimuli consisted of a validated set of pseudowords and matched visual shapes designed to elicit bouba/kiki associations. Two canonical pseudowords, “bouba” and “kiki,” were included as anchor items, and an expanded set of eight pseudowords that varied in consonant and vowel composition was used to create a broader sampling of sound-symbolic stimuli. The pseudowords were produced by a female native speaker of English in a sound-attenuated booth and recorded at 44.1 kHz. Recordings were normalized for root-mean-square amplitude to minimize loudness differences. Written forms of the pseudowords were presented in a sans-serif font at a legible size on a computer monitor. Visual shapes included eight pairs of shapes, where each pair comprised a rounded shape and an angular shape previously validated to elicit bouba/kiki-consistent mappings. Shapes were grayscale line drawings presented on a neutral background. The audiovisual condition presented the spoken pseudoword concurrently with the written form to provide redundant auditory and orthographic cues.

The experimental design was mixed factorial with musical training (musician, non-musician) as a between-subjects factor and modality (auditory, written, audiovisual) as a within-subjects factor. Each participant completed trials in all three modalities. Trial order and modality block order were counterbalanced across participants using a Latin square to control for order effects. Within each modality block, trials were randomized. Each modality block contained 24 trials: eight unique pseudowords each paired once with a rounded shape and once with an angular shape, yielding 24 trials per block and a total of 72 trials per participant. This number of trials was chosen to provide sufficient observations for within-subject reliability while minimizing fatigue.

The main task required participants to listen to or read a pseudoword and then select which of two simultaneously displayed shapes they felt best matched the word. On auditory trials, the pseudoword was presented auditorily while two shapes appeared on the screen to the left and right. On written trials, the written pseudoword replaced the auditory presentation but the shapes appeared in the same spatial arrangement. On audiovisual trials, participants heard the pseudoword while the written form appeared centrally on the screen above the shapes. Participants responded by pressing keyboard keys corresponding to left or right choices. The mapping of responses to keys was explained and practiced in a short practice block of six trials containing stimuli not included in the experimental set. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible and to rely on their intuition when uncertain.

Reaction times were recorded from the onset of stimulus presentation to the key press response and were inspected for outliers. Trials with response times below 200 ms or above 5000 ms were excluded as anticipatory or inattentive responses. Additionally, trials in which participants failed to respond were excluded. The primary dependent measure was the proportion of trials in which participants selected the pseudoword-consistent shape (e.g., rounded shape for “bouba”-type pseudowords and angular shape for “kiki”-type pseudowords), collapsed across pseudowords within each modality. A secondary dependent measure was reaction time on correct-consistent versus inconsistent trials.

Before the main experiment, participants completed a short questionnaire assessing demographic information and musical background. Musicians reported age of onset of training, accumulated years of instruction, primary instrument, sight-reading experience, and current weekly practice hours. Non-musicians were asked about casual musical engagement such as singing in social contexts or playing casually; those reporting prolonged formal training or sustained practice beyond the defined threshold were excluded from the non-musician group. This screening ensured relatively clean group separation.

Responses were collected using PsychoPy software running on lab desktop computers with headphones for auditory presentations. Stimulus timing, randomization, and response logging were automated. The experimental session lasted approximately 40 minutes, including informed consent, questionnaire completion, practice trials, three modality blocks, and a brief debriefing.

Planned statistical analyses included both frequentist and Bayesian approaches. The main analysis tested the between-subjects effect of musical training, the within-subjects effect of modality, and their interaction on the proportion of bouba/kiki-consistent responses using a mixed-model repeated-measures ANOVA. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta-squared for ANOVA and Cohen’s d for planned t-tests. Where sphericity assumptions were violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. In addition to the ANOVA, independent-samples t-tests were planned to compare overall consistency between musicians and non-musicians collapsed across modalities. One-sample t-tests were planned to compare overall mapping rates against chance (0.5) to ensure the standard bouba/kiki effect was present in the sample.

Bayesian t-tests and Bayesian repeated-measures ANOVAs were computed to quantify evidence in favor of the null versus the alternative hypotheses. Bayes factors (BF01) greater than 3 were interpreted as moderate evidence for the null hypothesis, following common conventions. Correlational analyses were planned to test for relationships between years of musical training and mapping consistency within the musician group. Exploratory analyses included reaction time comparisons across groups and modalities using mixed ANOVAs and correlations between reaction time and consistency.

Manipulation checks included verifying that the anchor pseudowords “bouba” and “kiki” produced expected mapping patterns, and that shape polarity (left/right) did not bias responses. The distribution of choices was examined to ensure that no single pseudoword dominated responses and that familiarity effects were minimal. All data cleaning and statistical analyses were performed using R and JASP software.

Efforts were made to limit confounds. Participants were instructed not to move their heads during auditory trials to maintain consistent listening conditions. Sound levels were adjusted to comfortable listening levels for each participant during practice. The written stimuli used a neutral font and size to avoid visual salience confounds. Visual stimuli were equated in overall area and mean luminance to reduce low-level perceptual differences that could confound shape selection. The order of modality blocks was counterbalanced to distribute any order effects evenly across groups.

Finally, an a priori power consideration guided the sample size. Though the sample of 40 participants is modest, the chosen within-subjects design with 24 trials per modality provided multiple observations per participant to increase precision. The study was aimed at detecting medium-sized effects of musical training and modality; however, the planned Bayesian analyses were intended to provide information about the relative evidence for null effects should frequentist results be non-significant.

Results

All 40 participants completed the experiment and their data were included in the analyses after standard exclusions for missing responses and extreme reaction times. Across all participants and trials, less than 2% of trials were excluded due to non-response or extreme reaction times, leaving a complete dataset for analysis. The initial manipulation check confirmed that, aggregated across participants and modalities, participants selected bouba/kiki-consistent shapes at a rate well above chance. The overall mean proportion of consistent responses was 0.79 (SD = 0.14). A one-sample t-test comparing this value against chance (0.5) confirmed a strong bouba/kiki effect in the sample, t(39) = 9.40, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.49.

Descriptive statistics for the main experimental groups and modalities are reported here. Musicians (n = 20) had a mean overall consistency of 0.78 (SD = 0.15). Non-musicians (n = 20) had a mean overall consistency of 0.80 (SD = 0.13). Within the auditory modality, musicians showed a mean consistency of 0.79 (SD = 0.16) and non-musicians a mean of 0.81 (SD = 0.14). For the written modality, musicians’ mean was 0.77 (SD = 0.15) and non-musicians’ mean was 0.79 (SD = 0.13). In the audiovisual modality, musicians’ mean was 0.78 (SD = 0.14) and non-musicians’ mean was 0.80 (SD = 0.12). These descriptive patterns suggested high consistency across both groups and modalities with only minor numerical differences.

To formally test the pre-registered hypotheses, a mixed-model repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with modality (auditory, written, audiovisual) as a within-subjects factor and musical training (musician, non-musician) as a between-subjects factor on the proportion of bouba/kiki-consistent responses. The main effect of musical training was not statistically significant, F(1,38) = 0.49, p = .49, partial eta-squared = .01. The main effect of modality was also not significant, F(2,76) = 1.09, p = .34, partial eta-squared = .03, and the interaction between musical training and modality was not significant, F(2,76) = 0.61, p = .54, partial eta-squared = .02. Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied as required and did not alter the inferential conclusions.

Complementary Bayesian analyses were conducted to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis. A Bayesian repeated-measures ANOVA yielded Bayes factors favoring the null model over models that included the main effects and interaction. Specifically, the BF01 for the musical training main effect was 4.2, indicating moderate evidence for the null hypothesis of no group difference. For the modality main effect, BF01 was 3.1, also indicating moderate evidence for the absence of a modality effect. For the interaction between musical training and modality, BF01 was 5.0, indicating moderate evidence against an interaction. These Bayes factors suggest that the data provide more support for the null than for models positing group or modality effects.

Given the null group effect in the categorical comparison, a correlational analysis within the musician group examined whether years of musical training predicted mapping consistency as a continuous variable. This analysis showed a negligible correlation, r = 0.05, p = .82, indicating no evidence that more years of training were associated with stronger bouba/kiki mappings. A similar null correlation was observed for current weekly practice hours and mapping consistency, r = 0.08, p = .72.

Reaction time analyses were also performed. Mean reaction times were collapsed across correct-consistent and inconsistent trials for each modality. Overall reaction times did not differ significantly between musicians (M = 1460 ms, SD = 340 ms) and non-musicians (M = 1520 ms, SD = 360 ms), t(38) = 0.62, p = .54, Cohen’s d = 0.20. A mixed ANOVA on reaction times with modality as a within-subjects factor and group as a between-subjects factor revealed no main effect of group, F(1,38) = 0.39, p = .54, no main effect of modality, F(2,76) = 1.68, p = .19, and no interaction, F(2,76) = 0.47, p = .63. Reaction times on consistent trials were slightly faster than on inconsistent trials across the whole sample (Mconsistent = 1450 ms, Minconsistent = 1610 ms), and this difference was significant, t(39) = 2.80, p = .008, Cohen’s d = 0.44, suggesting that consistent mappings were associated with faster decisions independent of group.

To check for potential response-side biases, the distribution of left/right shape choices was examined, and no systematic bias favoring one side was found. A chi-square test confirmed that side choices were equally likely across conditions, χ2(1) = 0.12, p = .73. An examination of individual pseudowords revealed that anchor items “bouba” and “kiki” elicited expected mappings in 92% and 89% of trials respectively, demonstrating that canonical items produced strong effects. These anchor-item rates did not differ significantly across groups.

Exploratory analyses considered whether individual differences in demographic variables or self-reported language background might relate to mapping strength. No significant correlations were found between age and mapping consistency (r = -0.10, p = .55) nor between years of education and mapping consistency (r = 0.07, p = .65). Participants’ native language status (native English speakers vs. others) did not reliably predict mapping rates, although the sample contained relatively few non-native speakers.

Given the null results for the primary factors of interest, post-hoc sensitivity analyses were conducted to estimate the smallest effect size detectable with the present sample given the observed within-subject variance. These calculations suggested that the study had adequate power to detect medium-sized effects (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.5) but limited power for small effects (d ≈ 0.2). Thus, while moderate effects are unlikely given the null findings and Bayesian evidence, the study cannot conclusively rule out small effects of musical training or modality.

Finally, participants were debriefed and asked whether they had any strategies for decision-making. Qualitative reports suggested that most participants relied on a general impression of “sharpness” versus “softness” when making choices, and no systematic strategy differences were reported between musicians and non-musicians. Taken together, the results indicate a robust bouba/kiki effect in the present sample that does not differ by musical training status or by modality of stimulus presentation.

Discussion

The present study tested whether musical training and sensory modality influence the strength of the bouba/kiki sound-symbolic effect. Across 40 participants, including 20 musicians and 20 non-musicians, we observed a robust bouba/kiki effect with an overall mean consistency of approximately 79 percent, which is well above chance. Contrary to our primary hypotheses, neither musical training nor modality of presentation (auditory, written, audiovisual) had a statistically significant effect on mapping strength, and Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis for both main effects and their interaction. Reaction time analyses indicated that consistent mappings were made more quickly than inconsistent ones, but reaction times did not differ by group or modality. Correlational analyses within the musician group found no relationship between years of training or current practice hours and mapping consistency. Collectively, these findings suggest that the bouba/kiki effect in adults is robust and relatively impervious to variation in musical training and presentation modality, at least within the parameters tested here.

The absence of group differences is theoretically informative. One possible interpretation is that sound-symbolic mapping, as exemplified by the bouba/kiki effect, is governed primarily by domain-general perceptual correspondences or by stable phonological–semantic associations that are not substantially altered by the auditory expertise that accrues through musical training. Musicians’ enhanced auditory discrimination may operate on aspects of sound that are orthogonal to the key features that drive bouba/kiki mappings. The features that produce roundedness versus angularity associations—such as consonant voicing contrasts, vowel backness, and overall spectral envelope—may be salient enough for typical listeners that additional perceptual acuity does not change mapping behavior. This account aligns with perspectives that treat sound symbolism as a robust crossmodal mapping anchored in basic perceptual or articulatory regularities shared across human experience.

Another interpretation is that the kind of musical training represented in our musician sample does not target the specific acoustic parameters most relevant to bouba/kiki associations. Musical training commonly emphasizes pitch, rhythm, and ensemble coordination, and although it can sharpen timbral discrimination and timbral subtleties, the mapping between phonetic character and visual shape may hinge on broad qualitative differences rather than the fine-grained acoustic distinctions that musicians are trained to detect. If musicians focus on pitch contours and temporal precision more than on spectral envelope differences between pseudowords, trained listeners might not differ from non-musicians in the kinds of crossmodal judgments probed by bouba/kiki tasks.

The modality findings are also informative. The lack of modality effects suggests that whether a pseudoword is heard, read, or presented audiovisually, the bouba/kiki mapping is similarly strong. This pattern supports the view that sound-symbolic correspondences operate at an abstract phonological or conceptual level that can be accessed through multiple channels. Even when the acoustic signal is absent and participants only see written forms, the implicit phonological representations activated by reading may be sufficient to evoke the same mappings. The audiovisual condition did not provide a synergistic boost to consistency, which indicates that redundant presentation does not necessarily strengthen mappings when single modalities already produce near-ceiling levels of consistency.

The reaction time pattern, where consistent mappings were faster than inconsistent ones, provides convergent evidence that consistent mappings reflect more automatic or readily accessible associations. This speed advantage was present across groups and modalities, which suggests that the underlying association is quick to access and either not influenced by musical training or so dominant that individual differences do not meaningfully alter processing speed. This is consistent with theoretical accounts that position sound symbolism as a rapid crossmodal matching process.

It is important to situate these findings within the broader literature. Some prior studies have reported modality effects or individual differences influencing sound-symbolic performance, whereas others have found robust crossmodal consistencies that are largely invariant across samples. The present results are in line with the latter set of findings, reinforcing the idea of bouba/kiki as a broadly shared perceptual mapping. The absence of a musical training effect may call into question a simple sensory-acuity account that predicts stronger crossmodal mappings in individuals with heightened auditory sensitivity. Instead, the data may favor accounts emphasizing the role of phonological mapping, articulatory gestures, or shared conceptual dimensions—such as roundness–angularity metaphors—that are widely available regardless of musical expertise.

Nonetheless, a careful consideration of methodological and interpretive issues is warranted. First, our sample size, while adequate to detect medium-sized effects, may not be sufficient to reveal small but meaningful differences between groups. The Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence for the null, but small effects remain plausible. Future studies with larger samples could increase sensitivity to detect subtle influences of musical training or modality. Second, the criteria used to classify musicians versus non-musicians may have resulted in heterogeneity within the musician group. Our inclusion threshold required at least five years of formal training and recent practice, but the sample included a variety of instruments and musical activities. Some forms of training—such as intensive vocal training or training focused explicitly on timbre and phonation—might influence sound-symbolic mappings differently than training emphasizing other skills. Thus, future work should consider more fine-grained measures of musical experience, including objective tests of auditory discrimination, pitch perception, and timbral sensitivity.

Third, the set of stimuli used in this study, while representative and validated, focused on discrete pseudowords and static shapes typical of bouba/kiki paradigms. It is possible that more subtle manipulations of acoustic parameters (e.g., systematic variations in spectral centroid, consonant burst energy, or vowel formant structure) could reveal individual differences related to musical expertise. Researchers could design parametric stimuli in which acoustic features are systematically varied to probe the threshold at which auditory expertise modulates mapping tendencies. Fourth, the modality manipulation included written presentations that rely on orthographic activation of phonology. Participant literacy and familiarity with English orthographic patterns could modulate the mapping strength for written stimuli. While our sample consisted mainly of literate adult speakers and likely activated phonological forms when reading pseudowords, this assumption could be tested by including a phonological interference task or by using languages with different orthography–phonology transparency.

Fifth, cultural and language background could moderate sound-symbolic associations. Our sample was drawn from a university population in a single linguistic environment, so the generalizability of the findings to more diverse populations remains to be established. Cross-cultural studies have shown both universal tendencies and culture-specific patterns in sound symbolism, and the interaction between musical training and cultural factors could be complex. For instance, musical traditions that emphasize certain timbres or rhythmic structures might shape perceptual biases in ways that interact with local language sound symbolism.

In addition to these considerations, future research could employ neurophysiological methods to examine whether neural responses to sound-symbolic stimuli differ between musicians and non-musicians even when behavioral responses do not. It is possible that musicians and non-musicians recruit different neural processes to arrive at similar behavioral endpoints. Electroencephalography (EEG) or functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) might reveal distinctions in early sensory encoding or in the integration of auditory and visual information that are not manifest in choice proportions. Such approaches could illuminate whether musical training affects the processing stages that subserve crossmodal mappings.

Another fruitful direction would be developmental research. Musical training often begins in childhood, and early musical experiences might shape crossmodal correspondences differently than adult-acquired training. Longitudinal or training studies could test whether musical instruction causally alters sound-symbolic associations over time. Training interventions that target specific auditory dimensions relevant to sound symbolism—such as timbre discrimination or spectral centroid detection—could determine whether enhancing sensitivity to these features leads to measurable changes in mapping behavior.

Moreover, it would be informative to examine different forms of crossmodal correspondence beyond the bouba/kiki paradigm. Sound–color associations, pitch–size correspondences, and timbre–shape mappings each tap into different acoustic dimensions and may be differentially susceptible to musical training. By comparing multiple correspondence domains within the same participants, researchers can map the landscape of expertise effects across perceptual mappings and determine whether some mappings are more plastic than others.

Methodologically, future studies should also consider refining participant classification using objective auditory tests, such as pitch discrimination thresholds, spectral centroid discrimination tasks, and tuning curve assessments. Such measures may provide more sensitive indices of the auditory abilities that are most relevant for sound-symbolic mapping than simple years-of-training metrics. Additionally, obtaining measures of phonological awareness and language proficiency could help disentangle the contributions of language and music experience.

The present findings also have implications for theories of language evolution and development. If sound-symbolic mappings are robust and resistant to modulation by musical training and presentation modality, this resilience may reflect adaptive pressures that favored the formation of reliable form-meaning mappings in early communicative systems. However, resilience does not imply immutability; rather, it points to a baseline of shared biases that can be further shaped by experience under particular circumstances.

Finally, the null results reported here emphasize the importance of publishing well-controlled studies that test plausible moderators of foundational cognitive phenomena. Null effects, when supported by appropriate analyses and Bayesian evidence, help refine theoretical accounts by delineating the boundary conditions under which an effect operates. They also direct future research toward more promising manipulations and more sensitive measures.

In conclusion, the present study replicated a robust bouba/kiki effect but found no evidence that musical training or modality of presentation substantially modulates this effect. These results suggest that the bouba/kiki mapping is a resilient feature of human perception that is accessible across presentation channels and largely unaffected by the auditory expertise represented in our musician sample. Future research should pursue larger and more diverse samples, finer-grained measures of musical expertise, parametric stimulus manipulations, developmental and training studies, and neurophysiological investigations to further clarify the relationships among sound symbolism, sensory modality, and auditory expertise.
