Introduction

Empathy is a multifaceted construct that plays a central role in social cognition and interpersonal relationships. Empathy has been conceptualized in cognitive and affective components, with cognitive empathy reflecting perspective taking and affective empathy reflecting emotional contagion and empathic concern. Research on empathy has examined a wide range of predictors and correlates, including personality traits, socialization, and situational factors. Among situational factors, the format and medium through which social information is conveyed have been highlighted as potentially important inputs to empathic responding. A diverse literature has linked engagement with narrative formats to increases in empathy, although this literature is not entirely uniform in its findings. Reading fiction and literary narratives has been associated with greater perspective taking and with transient increases in self-reported empathy in some studies. The mechanism proposed in the reading literature often centers on the role of immersive cognitive engagement, inner speech, and simulation processes that are invoked during reading. Readers are thought to generate mental models of characters, scenarios, and contexts, and those models may facilitate the simulation of other minds that underlies empathic processes. At the same time, visual and audiovisual stimuli have also been shown to elicit empathic responses. Short film clips, recorded interactions, and televised narratives can engage viewers emotionally and have been used in experimental empathy induction paradigms. Video formats can convey nonverbal cues such as facial expressions, tone of voice, and body language, which are known to be salient triggers of empathic emotional reactions. These parallel strands of research therefore converge on the idea that narrative content can evoke empathy, but they differ in terms of the mechanisms they emphasize and in the range of responses they measure.

Despite the shared interest in narrative-induced empathy, relatively little work has directly compared the empathic impact of reading the same content versus watching that content delivered in video form. This is an important gap because format differences could lead to different cognitive and affective processing pathways. Reading requires the generation of internal imagery and inferences to construct the narrative world, whereas watching provides externally specified perceptual cues that reduce the necessity for inferential construction. These differences could plausibly influence both cognitive empathy processes such as perspective taking and affective processes such as empathic concern. If reading indeed promotes greater active mental simulation, then reading a transcript could elicit stronger cognitive engagement with characters and situations and therefore higher scores on measures of trait- or state-like empathy immediately following exposure. Conversely, if audiovisual cues provide richer affective triggers, scenes presented as video might produce stronger immediate emotional responses and potentially higher affective empathy.

The Interpersonal Reactivity Index developed by Davis has been widely used to measure multiple dimensions of empathy in both correlational and experimental work. The IRI includes subscales that tap cognitive perspective taking, empathic concern for others, personal distress in response to others' suffering, and a fantasy subscale that indexes the tendency to imaginatively transpose oneself into fictional situations. Because the IRI captures both cognitive and affective components of empathy, it is well-suited to test hypotheses about format-driven differences in empathic responding. Prior studies using the IRI have found that individual differences in reading habits and fiction exposure predict higher perspective taking and fantasy scores. Similarly, exposure to emotionally evocative videos has been shown to transiently increase self-reported empathic concern in some experimental paradigms. What remains unclear is whether the format of a matched piece of content — read text versus watched video of identical semantic material — differentially affects scores on the multiple IRI subscales in an experimental context with random assignment.

A related issue concerns the temporal dynamics of empathy change. Reading may require sustained cognitive effort and generate a more deliberative form of simulation, while watching may produce a faster but more perceptually driven response. If the IRI is sensitive to both immediate empathic states and relatively stable dispositions, measuring IRI scores immediately after exposure provides a snapshot of the short-term impact of format on empathic responding. The question of whether such immediate differences reflect shifts in state empathy or transient activation of trait-like self-concepts is an empirical one. Experimental manipulation of content format allows causal inference about the direction of effects, which is important given that many prior studies on reading and empathy have been correlational.

In addition to theoretical differences in cognitive processing, format may interact with individual differences in media preference, reading frequency, and familiarity with audiovisual content. For example, individuals who habitually read might find textual formats more engaging and therefore show larger empathy increases when exposed to text. Conversely, individuals who consume more video media might be more attuned to audiovisual cues and thus respond more empathically to video. These potential moderating factors underscore the need to consider both main effects of format and interactions with media habits. However, prior experimental research has rarely directly controlled for or assessed such moderating variables in the context of matched-content comparisons.

Methodological considerations also matter when comparing text and video. To make a fair comparison, the textual transcript must faithfully capture the dialogue and key descriptive elements presented in the video, while avoiding introducing additional interpretive cues. Similarly, the video should present the content in a straightforward manner without extraneous production elements that could confound responses. The duration of exposure should be kept comparable across formats in terms of the cognitive load and time on task. Random assignment to conditions helps ensure that observed differences are attributable to format rather than pre-existing differences between participants.

In light of these theoretical and methodological considerations, the present study sought to directly compare the immediate effects of watching a brief video clip versus reading a transcription of that same clip on self-reported empathy as assessed by Davis’s Interpersonal Reactivity Index. The study used a between-subjects randomized design to isolate the causal effect of format. A sample of adult participants was randomly allocated to either watch a short clip depicting an emotionally salient interpersonal interaction or to read a verbatim transcript of that clip. The IRI was administered immediately after exposure to capture the short-term impact of format on multiple empathy dimensions.

Based on the literature suggesting that reading fosters imaginative engagement and perspective taking, the primary hypothesis was that participants who read the transcription would report higher overall empathy scores on the IRI immediately following exposure compared to participants who watched the video clip. A secondary hypothesis was that the cognitive empathy subscales, particularly Perspective Taking and Fantasy, would be more strongly affected by the reading format, whereas the affective Empathic Concern subscale might show similar or greater responses to the video format. The Personal Distress subscale was not expected to systematically favor one format over the other, but it was examined to provide a comprehensive profile of empathy-related responses.

The present study also included brief measures of participants’ habitual reading frequency and typical video consumption. These measures allowed exploratory analyses of whether media habits moderated the effect of format. If format effects are driven in part by familiarity and comfort with a medium, media habits could be an important boundary condition. The study aimed to examine such potential moderating influences while maintaining sufficient power to detect medium-sized main effects.

Beyond empirical hypotheses, the study contributes to broader theoretical questions about the modality specificity of social-cognitive processes. A finding that reading increases certain aspects of empathy relative to watching could be taken as evidence that internally generated simulation processes invoked during reading have unique contributions to empathic understanding. Alternatively, if watching produces equal or greater empathy, this would suggest that external perceptual cues can be at least as effective for eliciting empathy and might be leveraged in educational and clinical applications where audiovisual media are commonly used.

Finally, the study was designed to be transparent and replicable. Materials were standardized and matched across formats, randomization procedures were used, and analytic choices were pre-specified in a conceptual analysis plan. Although the current sample size was modest, it was chosen to balance feasibility with the ability to detect medium-sized effects. The subsequent sections present the methods used in detail, followed by the results of the primary and exploratory analyses, and a discussion that situates the findings within the broader literature and outlines directions for future research.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures are described first. A total of sixty adult participants were recruited from a university subject pool and from the surrounding community. The sample size of sixty was determined prior to data collection based on a power analysis that assumed a medium effect size for the primary comparison and a power target of approximately 0.80. Recruitment was carried out via online sign-up and on-campus flyers, and participants received course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. Inclusion criteria required participants to be fluent in English and to be between 18 and 40 years of age. Exclusion criteria included self-reported uncorrected hearing or vision impairments that would interfere with viewing or reading the study materials. Participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the institution’s ethical review board procedures.

Demographic information was collected at the outset of the study session. Age, gender, and educational status were recorded. Participants completed brief measures of media habits prior to the experimental manipulation, including a two-item reading frequency measure and a two-item video consumption frequency measure. The reading frequency item asked how often participants engaged in sustained reading for pleasure on a weekly basis, with response options ranging from “rarely or never” to “daily.” The video consumption item asked how many hours per week participants typically spend watching narrative video content, with categorical response options that spanned a broad range. These measures were included solely for exploratory moderation analyses and were not used to select or exclude participants.

The central experimental materials consisted of a brief audiovisual clip and a verbatim transcript of that clip. The clip was selected on the basis of its narrative clarity and emotional salience. It depicted a short interpersonal interaction between two characters in which one character disclosed a personal hardship and the other responded with a mixture of supportive and vulnerable responses. The clip was approximately three minutes in duration and was recorded with naturalistic acting but without heavy production elements such as dramatic music or rapid editing that could confound the effects of format. The transcription was a word-for-word rendering of the spoken content in the video and included brief bracketed descriptions of minimal nonverbal actions when they were necessary to preserve the sense of the interaction, such as “[looks away]” or “[reaches out]”. The textual transcript matched the video in overall length in terms of time on task; participants were instructed to read at a pace that allowed them to absorb the content without being rushed. The research team pilot-tested the transcript to ensure that it was comprehensible and that reading duration was similar to the video duration for typical participants.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two experimental conditions in a between-subjects design. Randomization was implemented via a random number generator that assigned each incoming participant to the read or video condition in equal proportions. The research assistant who conducted the session was blind to the specific hypotheses regarding format effects, although blinding to condition was not possible given the nature of the manipulation. Participants were told that the study was about responses to brief narrative material to avoid biasing them toward any particular empathy-related expectations.

The procedure for each testing session began with demographic and media habit measures, followed by the experimental exposure. Participants in the video condition were seated at a computer in a quiet testing room and watched the three-minute clip on a monitor with headphones to ensure clear audio. Volume was set at a comfortable listening level for each participant. Participants in the reading condition were seated at the same type of computer and were presented with the transcript on-screen in a standard font and size. They were instructed to read the transcript carefully and to press a key to advance when finished. The experimenter monitored for obvious distractions but otherwise left participants alone during exposure.

Immediately following exposure, participants completed the Interpersonal Reactivity Index on the same computer. The IRI comprised 28 items divided into four subscales: Perspective Taking, Fantasy, Empathic Concern, and Personal Distress. Each item was rated on a five-point Likert scale, and subscale scores were computed by summing the relevant items in accordance with standard scoring practices. The total IRI score was calculated as the sum of all subscale scores, providing an overall index of empathic tendency. The IRI was chosen because it captures both cognitive and affective empathy dimensions and has been widely used in related research.

Following the IRI, participants completed a brief manipulation check and attention check. The manipulation check asked participants to indicate whether they had read the material or watched a video and to rate on a Likert scale how engaged they felt during the exposure. The attention check asked a content-related factual question about the narrative to ensure that participants had processed the material. Participants who failed the attention check were flagged for sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the results.

All responses were collected electronically and stored on a secure server. Prior to analysis, data were screened for missing values and outliers. No participant was excluded for missing IRI data. One participant failed the attention check and was retained in the primary analyses but noted in exploratory sensitivity analyses. Statistical assumptions for parametric tests were evaluated, including checks for normality of residuals and homogeneity of variance across the two experimental groups. Where assumptions were not perfectly met, robust or nonparametric alternatives were considered and reported.

The primary statistical analyses compared IRI total scores between the read and video conditions using an independent-samples t-test. Secondary analyses examined the four IRI subscales independently with separate t-tests to explore which components of empathy were most affected by format. Effect sizes were reported using Cohen’s d with pooled standard deviations. Confidence intervals for mean differences were also computed to provide estimates of precision. Exploratory moderation analyses tested whether self-reported reading frequency or video consumption moderated the effect of condition on IRI scores using interaction terms in linear regression models. All tests were two-tailed and evaluated at an alpha level of .05.

In addition to the primary inferential analyses, descriptive statistics were reported for demographic variables, media habit measures, and engagement ratings from the manipulation check. The analysis plan specified that any significant findings would be interpreted cautiously given the sample size and exploratory nature of moderation tests. The research team adhered to standards for transparent reporting and documented all analytic steps. The results are presented next, beginning with descriptive statistics and followed by hypothesis tests and exploratory analyses.

Results

Preliminary descriptive statistics indicated that the sample consisted of sixty participants with a mean age of 23.7 years and a standard deviation of 3.8 years. Gender breakdown showed 36 females and 24 males, with no significant difference in gender distribution across the two experimental conditions. Educational status was heterogeneous but primarily undergraduate; most participants reported some college education. The two experimental groups were equivalent on age, gender, and self-reported baseline media habits, as checked by independent t-tests and chi-square tests, all of which were non-significant. Reading frequency scores averaged at the mid-range of the response scale and did not differ significantly between conditions. Video consumption frequency likewise showed no pre-existing differences between the read and video groups.

Engagement ratings from the manipulation check indicated that participants in both conditions reported moderate to high engagement with the material. The mean engagement rating in the reading condition was slightly higher than in the video condition, but this difference did not reach statistical significance. The item used as an attention check was answered correctly by 59 of the 60 participants, and the single participant who answered incorrectly remained in the primary analysis; sensitivity analyses excluding this participant produced substantively similar results. These preliminary checks provided assurance that participants had attended to the materials and that randomization had produced comparable groups.

Turning to the primary dependent variable, the IRI total score, the reading condition produced a higher mean score than the video condition. Specifically, participants who read the transcript had a mean total IRI score of 96.4 with a standard deviation of 11.8. Participants in the video condition had a mean total IRI score of 88.1 with a standard deviation of 13.2. An independent-samples t-test comparing total IRI scores across conditions indicated that this difference was statistically significant. The t statistic was 2.19 with 58 degrees of freedom, and the corresponding two-tailed p-value was .032. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference ranged approximately from 0.9 to 15.1, indicating that the observed effect was unlikely to be null.

Effect size for the primary comparison was computed using Cohen’s d with pooled standard deviation. The resulting d was approximately 0.56, which is conventionally interpreted as a moderate effect. This magnitude suggests a meaningful difference in self-reported empathy associated with format in this sample. Levene’s test for equality of variances in total IRI scores was non-significant, indicating that homogeneity of variance assumptions were reasonable for the t-test. Examination of the distribution of total scores suggested approximate normality, with no extreme skewness or kurtosis evident in either group.

Secondary analyses examined the four IRI subscales to determine which components contributed most to the overall effect. On the Perspective Taking subscale, readers reported a higher mean score than viewers. The reading condition mean for Perspective Taking was 24.3 with a standard deviation of 3.6, whereas the video condition mean was 21.9 with a standard deviation of 4.1. The independent-samples t-test yielded a t value of 2.47 and a p-value of .016 for the Perspective Taking comparison. The Cohen’s d for this difference was approximately 0.63, indicating a moderate effect favoring the reading condition on the cognitive empathy measure.

The Fantasy subscale also showed a significant difference favoring the reading condition. Participants who read the transcript had a mean Fantasy score of 23.5 with a standard deviation of 3.9, while participants who watched the video had a mean of 20.7 with a standard deviation of 4.4. The t-test for the Fantasy subscale resulted in t = 2.68 and p = .010. The effect size for this subscale was in the moderate to large range, with a Cohen’s d of about 0.71. This finding aligns with theoretical expectations that reading would more strongly engage imaginative transposition into fictional perspectives.

Empathic Concern, which indexes affective concern for others, produced a smaller but still noteworthy pattern. The reading condition mean for Empathic Concern was 24.8 with a standard deviation of 4.2, compared to a video condition mean of 23.1 and a standard deviation of 4.5. The t-test for Empathic Concern yielded t = 1.48 and p = .144, which did not meet the conventional alpha threshold for significance. The Cohen’s d for this subscale was approximately 0.39, indicating a small-to-moderate effect size that did not reach statistical significance given the present sample size.

The Personal Distress subscale did not differ meaningfully across conditions. The mean Personal Distress score for readers was 23.8 with a standard deviation of 3.8, and for viewers it was 22.4 with a standard deviation of 4.1. The t-test for this comparison produced t = 1.41 and p = .163, and the Cohen’s d was approximately 0.35. These results suggest that format did not substantially influence participants’ own distress responses to the material.

Given multiple subscale comparisons, an exploratory multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was conducted to assess whether there was an overall multivariate effect of condition on the set of subscale scores. The multivariate test yielded a Pillai’s trace that was significant at p = .022, indicating that, collectively, subscale scores differed by condition. Follow-up univariate tests mirrored the t-test results, with Perspective Taking and Fantasy emerging as the primary contributors to the multivariate effect.

Effect sizes and confidence intervals for the subscale comparisons were calculated to provide an estimate of precision. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference in Perspective Taking was approximately 0.4 to 4.5 points, whereas the interval for Fantasy was roughly 0.7 to 4.9 points. For Empathic Concern and Personal Distress, confidence intervals included zero, indicating uncertainty regarding the direction and magnitude of differences for these subscales.

Exploratory moderation analyses investigated whether habitual reading frequency or video consumption moderated the effect of condition on total IRI scores. Linear regression models included main effects for condition (dummy-coded), media habit measures (centered), and the interaction terms between condition and each habit measure. The interaction between condition and reading frequency was not significant, suggesting that the effect of format was not conditional on how frequently participants reported reading for pleasure. Similarly, the interaction between condition and video consumption frequency was non-significant. These null moderation findings indicate that the main effect of format on IRI scores was relatively consistent across the range of self-reported media habits in this sample.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings to analytic choices. Excluding the single participant who failed the attention check yielded nearly identical means and significance levels for the primary comparison. Nonparametric Mann-Whitney U tests for total IRI and the subscales produced p-values consistent with the t-test results, which provides reassurance that the findings are not heavily dependent on parametric assumptions. Additional checks for potential confounds such as age and gender did not alter the pattern of results when included as covariates in regression models.

In summary, the results indicated that participants who read a transcript of a brief emotionally salient interaction reported higher overall empathy on the IRI than participants who watched the equivalent video clip. The differences were most pronounced on the cognitive and imaginative components of empathy, as reflected in significant effects on the Perspective Taking and Fantasy subscales. Affective empathic concern showed a non-significant trend favoring reading, while self-oriented personal distress did not differ reliably by format. These findings support the hypothesis that reading a matched narrative may foster greater engagement of cognitive-empathic processes, at least in the immediate aftermath of exposure.

Discussion

This study examined whether the format of a matched narrative — reading a verbatim transcript versus watching a video clip of the same interaction — influences self-reported empathy as measured by Davis’s Interpersonal Reactivity Index. The principal finding was that participants who read the transcript reported higher overall empathy scores than participants who watched the video. The effect was moderate in magnitude and was most pronounced on the Perspective Taking and Fantasy subscales of the IRI. These findings provide evidence that format can influence immediate empathic responses and suggest that textual presentation may more strongly engage certain cognitive components of empathy.

The observed pattern aligns with theoretical accounts that posit reading as an activity that engages active mental simulation and imaginative perspective taking. When readers process text, they must generate internal representations of characters, settings, and events, and they often infer unspoken mental states and contextual details. This constructive process may enhance perspective taking as readers simulate the internal experiences of characters. The significant increase in the Fantasy subscale for readers further supports the idea that reading invites imaginative transposition into fictional scenarios. The Fantasy subscale specifically taps the tendency to identify with fictional characters, and its elevation in the reading condition suggests that text format may be particularly effective in fostering imaginative involvement. In contrast, video provides richly specified perceptual cues that may reduce the need for internally generated simulation. While video can elicit strong affective responses via facial expressions and vocal intonation, it may simultaneously constrain imaginative elaboration by presenting a concrete instantiation of the scene rather than leaving aspects to the imagination.

The lack of a significant difference for Empathic Concern requires careful interpretation. Although Empathic Concern numerically favored the reading condition, the difference did not reach statistical significance. This null result suggests that affective engagement with others’ suffering may be somewhat less sensitive to format differences when the content is held constant. Visual and auditory cues in video may be effective at eliciting emotional contagion and concern in some contexts, while reading can also generate strong emotional responses through imagined perspective taking. The present materials and modest sample size may have limited power to detect subtle differences in the affective domain. Therefore, it is plausible that both formats can elicit empathic concern but via different pathways, with reading emphasizing cognitive simulation and video emphasizing perceptual-affective resonance.

Personal Distress did not differ between conditions, which is consistent with the idea that self-oriented distress responses may be more related to individual traits and regulation capacities than to format-driven processing per se. Personal Distress reflects discomfort and anxiety in response to others’ suffering and can be influenced by dispositional factors. The absence of a condition effect on Personal Distress suggests that the manipulation primarily affected other-oriented empathic processes rather than self-oriented emotional reactivity.

The absence of moderation by self-reported reading frequency or video consumption was notable. One might have expected that habitual readers would show a greater empathy boost when reading and habitual viewers would show a greater response to video. However, the exploratory analyses did not support this pattern. This could indicate that the format effect is relatively robust across a range of habitual media behaviors, at least among young adult participants. Alternatively, the media habit measures used were brief and may not have captured the nuances of media engagement needed to detect moderation. Future studies could employ more detailed assessments of media expertise, immersive reading habits such as frequency of fiction reading, and measures of cognitive styles to better probe potential moderators.

These findings have implications for theoretical models of empathy, particularly those that emphasize the role of mental simulation in understanding others. The data support a view in which internally generated simulation processes, fostered by reading, contribute to cognitive empathy and imaginative identification. This complements neuroscience and cognitive literature suggesting that engaging with narratives activates brain networks implicated in mentalizing and simulation. Conversely, the results do not negate the capacity of audiovisual media to elicit empathy; rather, they suggest that the mechanism of empathy induction may vary by medium.

From a practical perspective, the results suggest that textual materials might be especially useful when the goal is to enhance cognitive components of empathy such as perspective taking and imaginative engagement. Educational interventions that use reading as a tool to cultivate empathy may benefit from carefully selected narratives that encourage perspective taking. In contexts such as training programs for professionals who require high levels ofperspective taking, including textual case studies or transcripts may complement video-based instruction. Conversely, video may still be preferable when the objective is to provide visceral affective engagement or to demonstrate nonverbal cues that text cannot convey.

Notwithstanding these contributions, the study has several limitations that warrant discussion. Foremost among these is the use of a single, brief clip and its matched transcript. While this design provided internal control over content, the generalizability of the findings to other types of narrative content, genres, or durations is uncertain. Different narratives may vary in their reliance on internal simulation versus perceptual cues, and longer exposures may produce different patterns of engagement. Future research should therefore test multiple clips across genres and lengths to assess the boundary conditions of the format effect.

Another limitation is the modest sample size. Although the study was powered to detect medium-sized effects, smaller effects — particularly on subscales like Empathic Concern — may have gone undetected. Replication with larger samples would increase confidence in the robustness and generalizability of the effects. Additionally, the sample consisted primarily of university-affiliated young adults, which may limit generalizability to broader populations with different media habits, cognitive styles, or cultural backgrounds. Cultural differences in narrative practices and media consumption may interact with format effects on empathy, and cross-cultural replication would be valuable.

Methodological considerations also include the nature of the transcript. Although the transcript attempted to be verbatim and included minimal bracketed nonverbal cues to preserve sense, text necessarily differs from video in terms of demand characteristics. Reading requires decoding and internal pacing choices, which may influence cognitive engagement. The design attempted to keep time on task comparable across formats, but individual reading speed variability could have introduced differences in cognitive load. Future work could control reading pace more tightly or include measures of reading time to better equate cognitive effort.

It is also important to consider alternative explanations for the observed format effect. For instance, reading may induce deeper processing because it is less passive than watching, and therefore differences in effort rather than format per se might account for empathy differences. The manipulation check indicated similar self-reported engagement across conditions, but self-report may not fully capture differences in processing depth. Incorporating objective measures of cognitive engagement, such as eye-tracking, response latency, or neurophysiological indices, could help disentangle whether differences are due to effort, attention, or representational processes.

Another potential confound involves the role of production elements in the video. Although the clip was intentionally produced without dramatic music or editing, the presence of actors and natural vocal prosody constitutes additional cues that are absent in text. These cues could both facilitate and constrain empathic responses. In particular, watching may anchor participants to the specific appearance and voice of the characters, limiting imaginative projection. Future studies could manipulate the degree of perceptual specificity in audiovisual presentations, for example by using animated or silhouette-based renderings, to probe the role of perceptual specificity in empathy induction.

The temporal profile of empathy change was not explored in this study. Measuring empathy at multiple time points post-exposure would reveal whether format-driven differences are transient or persist over time. It is possible that reading produces a stronger immediate boost to perspective taking that decays, whereas video may have a slower-developing effect or different retention dynamics. Longitudinal designs that assess durability of format effects would therefore extend the current findings.

Another fruitful direction for future research is to examine behavioral and physiological indices of empathy in addition to self-report. Measures such as helping behavior in subsequent tasks, physiological markers of emotional resonance, or neural measures of mentalizing could provide converging evidence about which empathy processes are affected by format and by what mechanism. For example, if reading increases activation in brain regions associated with mental simulation, that would bolster the interpretation that internal generative processes mediate format effects.

There are also practical extensions relevant to applied domains such as clinical training, education, and media design. Investigating whether mixed-media interventions — for instance, combining textual reflection prompts with video vignettes — magnify empathic outcomes could inform pedagogical practices. Similarly, tailoring media formats to specific learning objectives, such as using text to enhance perspective taking and video to enhance emotional recognition, might optimize training programs that aim to cultivate empathy among professionals in health care, law enforcement, or education.

In conclusion, this study provides experimental evidence that reading a verbatim transcript of an emotionally salient interaction can produce higher self-reported empathy than watching the matched video. The effect primarily involved cognitive and imaginative components of empathy, supporting the view that text-based narrative engagement promotes internal simulation and perspective taking. The results contribute to a nuanced understanding of how media format shapes empathic responding and suggest multiple avenues for further research to delineate mechanisms, boundary conditions, and applied implications. Future work with diverse samples, multiple content types, longitudinal measurement, and multimethod assessment will be necessary to build a comprehensive account of format influences on empathy. Despite limitations, the present findings highlight the importance of considering media format when designing interventions or studies aimed at fostering empathy.
