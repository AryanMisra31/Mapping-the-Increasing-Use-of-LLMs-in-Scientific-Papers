Introduction

Human attention is not evenly distributed across visual stimuli but is guided by the functional relevance of those stimuli to the observer. One prominent influence on attentional allocation is emotional expression, and particularly the valence of facial expressions. Faces that convey negative emotions such as anger, fear, or disgust have been reported in many studies to capture attention more rapidly than positive or neutral faces, an effect often interpreted through evolutionary reasoning: rapid detection of potential threats confers survival advantages. The idea that negative emotional stimuli, and particularly threatening faces, enjoy privileged access to attention and awareness has motivated a large body of experimental work in visual search, attentional blink, and dot-probe paradigms. These findings have been taken to suggest that emotional valence modulates early perceptual and attentional processes and that negative valence can lead to faster detection and prioritization in complex visual scenes.

At the same time, the literature contains notable heterogeneity. Some studies find a robust “threat advantage” in which angry or fearful faces are found more rapidly among neutral distractors, while other investigations report null effects or even advantages for positive expressions such as happiness. Discrepancies in observed effects have been attributed to a variety of methodological and theoretical factors, including the nature of the stimuli (static vs. dynamic faces), the congruence between target and distractor emotional content, the perceptual load of the visual array, individual differences in anxiety and mood, and the operationalization of response measures (reaction time, accuracy, eye movements). Consequently, while the threat-detection account provides a coherent framework for understanding many findings, the overall picture is nuanced rather than univocal.

One important but underexplored factor in this domain is the potential for habituation or attenuation of emotional effects with repeated exposure. Habituation is a fundamental property of biological systems in which responses to repeated, unchanging stimuli decline over time. If the rapid detection of negatively valenced faces is primarily a result of their status as potential threats, then repeated exposure to the same category of negative faces in a context where they are task-relevant and non-threatening may result in a diminished attentional advantage. Put differently, the functional significance of negative faces might be expected to decrease with repeated, predictable exposure, reducing their ability to capture attention relative to initial presentations. This possibility has not been systematically examined in visual search paradigms where the same participants perform repeated sessions over days or weeks.

Another complicating factor in the literature is the role of mood and affective state. Because mood can bias attention and influence the processing of emotional stimuli, changes in mood across testing sessions could masquerade as habituation or practice effects. If participants become more positive or more negative over the course of a study, their overall sensitivity to positive or negative targets might change independently of perceptual habituation. Consequently, studies that track the effect of repetition on emotion-driven search advantages should adequately monitor and control for mood fluctuations.

Practice effects represent an additional mechanism that must be considered in repeated testing paradigms. Repeated performance on the same visual search task commonly yields improvements in speed and sometimes accuracy, independent of the emotional content of the stimuli. Such improvements can arise from procedural learning, increased familiarity with task demands, improved motor execution, or refined search strategies. If reaction times to emotional targets decrease across sessions, these declines could reflect general practice rather than habituation of emotional value. Disentangling these possibilities requires designs that assess both the valence-specific pattern of change across sessions and the overall practice-related improvements that apply to all target types.

Given the mixed findings concerning valence-specific search advantages and the potential influence of habituation, mood, and practice, it is important to directly test whether a negative-valence search advantage persists with repeated exposure when the same participants perform a face-search task across multiple sessions spaced over time. In the current investigation we sought to address this question by having participants complete a visual-face search task once a week for three consecutive weeks. Targets on the task were faces showing either positive (happy) or negative (angry) expressions embedded among neutral distractor faces. The core hypothesis was that a negative-face search advantage would be present on the first exposure, consistent with the threat-detection account, but that this advantage would reduce across repeated weekly sessions as the negative expressions became less threatening or less salient through habituation.

We also explicitly measured and controlled for participants’ mood using the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) before each session. If mood changed systematically across sessions, mood shifts might confound any observed changes in search performance. By measuring mood at each session we could test whether mood changes accounted for any reductions in emotional search advantages. We predicted that mood would not appreciably change across the three weekly sessions and thus would not explain changes in reaction times. That assumption was based on the short and regular weekly schedule, and the lack of any special mood-inducing manipulations in the experimental procedure.

Beyond testing for habituation of the negative-face advantage, the design also enabled us to quantify the magnitude and time course of any practice effects. If reaction times for both positive and negative targets decline across sessions at similar rates, this pattern would point to practice as the primary driver of improvements in performance. Conversely, if negative targets show a greater decline relative to positive targets, that pattern would be more consistent with habituation to threat-related salience. To achieve sufficient sensitivity to detect such differences, the task employed a relatively large number of trials per session, yielding a stable estimate of individual reaction time distributions across valence conditions.

We designed the stimuli to be standardized facial expressions drawn from a validated set of photographs that balanced identity, gender, and ethnicity across valence conditions. This approach minimized low-level visual confounds and ensured that any valence-driven effects were unlikely to be attributable to gross perceptual differences between stimulus categories. The visual search arrays were arranged to preserve attentionally demanding conditions by using multiple distractors such that participants had to perform an active search rather than simply detect a singleton pop-out.

The present study therefore pursued three main goals. First, it tested for the presence and direction of any valence effects in a face-search task administered longitudinally across three weekly sessions. Second, it examined whether any observed valence effect—particularly a putative negative-face advantage—diminishes across sessions in a manner consistent with habituation. Third, it assessed the contributions of mood and practice to changes in performance by measuring affective state across sessions and characterizing overall reaction-time improvements. Based on the threat-detection hypothesis and prior findings, our a priori expectation was that negative faces would be detected faster than positive faces in the initial session and that this advantage would attenuate across sessions. The competing expectation, derived from some prior work that finds a positivity advantage, was that positive faces might be detected faster than negative ones, in which case the study would reveal whether this pattern is robust across repeated testing and whether it is subject to habituation or practice effects.

By combining repeated-measures testing, mood monitoring, and careful stimulus selection, the study aimed to provide a clearer picture of how emotional valence and repeated exposure interact to shape visual attention. If negative-face advantages are robust and resistant to habituation, that finding would support accounts emphasizing the evolutionary priority of threat detection. If, by contrast, valence effects shift or diminish with repetition, this outcome would underscore the plasticity of attentional biases and the importance of experience. Finally, demonstrating that changes reflect practice rather than mood would clarify the mechanisms that drive improvements in emotion-driven search performance across time.

Methods

Participants and recruitment
We recruited 48 adult volunteers from the local university community through advertisements placed on campus bulletins and a participant recruitment system. All participants were required to be between the ages of 18 and 35, have normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and report no history of neurological or psychiatric conditions that would interfere with visual perception or task performance. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation and received monetary compensation for completing all three sessions. The final sample consisted of 48 participants (28 female, 20 male) with a mean age of 22.9 years and a standard deviation of 3.2 years. Demographic data and compliance with inclusion criteria were collected via a brief screening questionnaire during recruitment.

Stimuli
Face stimuli were selected from a standardized set of facial expressions that included posed happy (positive), angry (negative), and neutral expressions. We selected 36 unique identities (18 female, 18 male) balanced across ethnicities represented in the available database to minimize any systematic confound between valence and identity. For each identity, we used three photographs showing happy, angry, and neutral expressions. Stimuli were grayscale, cropped to exclude hair and non-facial cues, and equated for overall luminance and contrast using image processing software. Each face subtended approximately 3.5 degrees of visual angle when presented on the display and was presented on a mid-gray background. The use of standardized, validated faces was intended to reduce idiosyncratic differences in expressive intensity across stimuli.

Apparatus
The experiment was run on desktop computers with 24-inch monitors (resolution 1920 x 1080 pixels) and standard keyboards for responses. Stimulus presentation and data collection were controlled using experimental software that recorded response times with millisecond resolution. Participants were seated approximately 60 cm from the monitor and used a chinrest to stabilize head position for the duration of each session. The testing room was dimly lit and quiet to minimize distractions.

Task design and trial structure
The primary task was a visual search in which participants reported whether a target face of a designated emotional valence was present among neutral distractors. Each trial presented a 3 x 3 grid of nine faces arranged on a square with equal spacing. On target-present trials, one of the nine faces displayed an emotional expression (happy or angry) and the remaining eight faces displayed neutral expressions. All targets and distractors were of different identities within a trial to avoid simple identity matching. Target type (positive or negative) was manipulated within each session in a randomized order so that participants alternated between trials where the target was a happy face and trials where the target was an angry face.

Each session consisted of 240 trials in total. Within each session there were 120 positive-target trials and 120 negative-target trials. Each session also contained a short practice block of 12 trials at the start with feedback to ensure comprehension of task instructions. On each trial, stimuli appeared after a 500 ms fixation cross and remained visible until the participant made a response or until 3000 ms elapsed. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible by pressing one key to indicate that they detected the target and another key to indicate that they did not see the target. Target-absent trials were not the focus of this study; however, to maintain attention and uncertainty, 12% of trials were target-absent trials where all faces were neutral. Target presence on the remaining 88% of trials was balanced across positive and negative targets. Feedback was provided only during the practice block; no feedback was given during experimental trials.

Procedure and session schedule
Participants completed three sessions spaced exactly one week apart (Session 1: Week 1; Session 2: Week 2; Session 3: Week 3). Each session began with completion of the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) to assess current mood. The PANAS comprises two scales: positive affect (PA) and negative affect (NA). Participants completed the PANAS on paper before starting the task in each session. After PANAS completion, participants were reminded of the task instructions, completed the short practice block, and then performed the 240 experimental trials, divided into four blocks of 60 trials with short breaks between blocks. The entire session lasted approximately 45 minutes.

Instructions emphasized speed and accuracy equally. The experimenter stayed in the room during the session to ensure compliance with the protocol but did not interact with participants except to provide standard reminders and to enforce breaks at block transitions.

Data preprocessing and exclusion criteria
Reaction time (RT) data were recorded for all trials. Preprocessing excluded trials with incorrect responses from RT analyses. Trials with RTs below 200 ms or above 2000 ms were considered anticipatory or excessively slow and were discarded. Additionally, for each participant, RTs that exceeded ±3 standard deviations from that participant’s mean were removed as outliers. Across the dataset fewer than 5% of trials were removed for these reasons. Participants with overall accuracy below 70% across a session would have been excluded; however, no participant fell below this threshold in any session, so no participants were excluded from analyses.

Statistical approach
The primary dependent variable was mean RT for correct target-present trials in each condition (target valence x session). Accuracy rates were considered as a secondary dependent variable. For the main analyses we conducted repeated-measures analyses of variance (ANOVAs) with within-subject factors of Valence (positive, negative) and Session (Week 1, Week 2, Week 3). Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied where sphericity assumptions were violated. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta-squared (ηp^2) for ANOVA effects and Cohen’s d for paired t-tests.

To test whether mood changes could account for differences in RT, we ran repeated-measures ANOVAs on PANAS PA and NA scores across sessions. We also conducted exploratory analyses including PA and NA scores as covariates in the RT ANOVA (ANCOVA) to determine whether mood accounted for variance in RTs. In addition to the session-level analyses, we fitted linear mixed-effects models to trial-level RTs with trial number as a continuous predictor and random intercepts for participants to quantify practice-related learning slopes across the sessions. All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software with an alpha level of .05 for significance.

Ethics and data handling
The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics review board prior to data collection. All participants provided written informed consent and were debriefed at the end of their final session. Data were anonymized and stored on secure servers in accordance with data protection guidelines. Summary data used for statistical analyses are presented below.

Results

Descriptive statistics and preprocessing outcomes
After data cleaning and exclusion of outliers as described in the Methods, the dataset included RTs from all 48 participants across the three sessions. On average, participants contributed 110 correct positive-target trials and 108 correct negative-target trials per session after removing invalid trials and errors; the small difference reflects the removal of trials for excessive RTs and occasional errors. Across participants, the mean RTs (ms) for correct target-present trials were as follows: positive targets — Week 1: M = 612 ms, SD = 78 ms; Week 2: M = 571 ms, SD = 70 ms; Week 3: M = 548 ms, SD = 65 ms. For negative targets the means were: Week 1: M = 651 ms, SD = 85 ms; Week 2: M = 605 ms, SD = 76 ms; Week 3: M = 574 ms, SD = 69 ms. Overall mean RT across all sessions and participants was 594 ms for positive targets and 610 ms for negative targets.

Accuracy rates were generally high. Mean accuracy for positive-target trials was 96.2% (SD = 3.5%) in Week 1, 97.0% (SD = 2.8%) in Week 2, and 97.6% (SD = 2.0%) in Week 3. For negative-target trials, mean accuracy was 95.1% (SD = 4.2%) in Week 1, 96.2% (SD = 3.6%) in Week 2, and 97.1% (SD = 3.0%) in Week 3. Error rates were low and showed a small improvement across sessions for both valence conditions.

Primary ANOVA on reaction time
A 2 (Valence: positive, negative) x 3 (Session: Week 1, Week 2, Week 3) repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted on mean RTs for correct target-present trials. The main effect of Valence was statistically significant, F(1,47) = 18.64, p < .001, ηp^2 = .284, indicating that positive targets were detected faster overall than negative targets across sessions. The main effect of Session was also highly significant, F(2,94) = 45.12, p < .001, ηp^2 = .490, reflecting the overall reduction in reaction times across sessions for both valence conditions.

The Valence x Session interaction was not statistically significant, F(2,94) = 2.10, p = .13, ηp^2 = .043. The non-significant interaction indicates that the pattern of RT reductions across sessions did not differ reliably between positive and negative targets; both valence conditions showed similar magnitudes of improvement with practice.

Post-hoc paired comparisons
Planned paired-sample t-tests examined valence differences within each session and session-to-session changes within each valence. In Week 1, participants responded faster to positive than to negative targets, t(47) = 4.12, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.59, with a mean difference of 39 ms (95% CI of difference: 21 ms to 58 ms). In Week 2, the positive advantage persisted, t(47) = 3.83, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.55, with a mean difference of 34 ms (95% CI: 15 ms to 52 ms). In Week 3, the positive advantage remained statistically reliable though smaller in magnitude: t(47) = 2.50, p = .016, Cohen’s d = 0.36, with a mean difference of 26 ms (95% CI: 5 ms to 46 ms). These tests demonstrate that the positive-face advantage was present in each weekly session.

Planned comparisons also confirmed significant improvements across sessions for both valence conditions. For positive targets, RTs decreased significantly from Week 1 to Week 2, t(47) = 5.02, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.72, with a mean reduction of 41 ms (95% CI: 28 ms to 54 ms). RTs also decreased from Week 2 to Week 3 for positive targets, t(47) = 2.86, p = .006, Cohen’s d = 0.41, with a mean reduction of 23 ms (95% CI: 7 ms to 38 ms). For negative targets, the reduction from Week 1 to Week 2 was significant, t(47) = 4.38, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.63, mean reduction = 46 ms (95% CI: 30 ms to 62 ms), and the reduction from Week 2 to Week 3 was also significant, t(47) = 3.11, p = .003, Cohen’s d = 0.45, mean reduction = 31 ms (95% CI: 10 ms to 51 ms). These results indicate robust practice-related improvements for both valence categories.

Accuracy analyses
A 2 x 3 repeated-measures ANOVA on accuracy rates revealed a small but significant main effect of Session, F(2,94) = 6.78, p = .002, ηp^2 = .126, indicating that accuracy improved slightly across sessions. There was no significant main effect of Valence on accuracy, F(1,47) = 2.01, p = .16, ηp^2 = .041, and no significant Valence x Session interaction, F(2,94) = 0.86, p = .43, ηp^2 = .018. Thus, although RTs differed by valence, accuracy remained high and did not differ substantially between positive and negative targets.

Mood (PANAS) analyses
To assess whether mood changed across the three-week testing interval, repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted separately for the PANAS Positive Affect (PA) and Negative Affect (NA) scales. For PA, the means were: Week 1: M = 31.2, SD = 7.1; Week 2: M = 31.0, SD = 6.8; Week 3: M = 30.8, SD = 7.0. The Session effect for PA was not significant, F(2,94) = 0.14, p = .87, ηp^2 = .003. For NA, the means were: Week 1: M = 12.5, SD = 3.9; Week 2: M = 12.6, SD = 4.1; Week 3: M = 12.3, SD = 3.8. The Session effect for NA was also not significant, F(2,94) = 0.28, p = .76, ηp^2 = .006. These analyses indicate that participants’ mood did not change appreciably over the three weekly sessions.

ANCOVA controlling for PANAS scores
To determine whether individual differences in PA or NA might account for RT effects, we conducted ANCOVAs with PA and NA scores included as covariates in the Valence x Session ANOVA on RT. Neither PA nor NA covariates accounted for a significant amount of variance in RT, and the main effects of Valence and Session remained significant when controlling for mood. The Valence main effect controlling for PA and NA was F(1,45) = 15.92, p < .001, ηp^2 = .261, and the Session main effect controlling for PA and NA was F(2,90) = 41.23, p < .001, ηp^2 = .478. The Valence x Session interaction remained non-significant in the ANCOVA. These results show that mood did not explain the valence difference or the improvements across sessions.

Trial-level learning analysis
To characterize the slope of practice effects across the task, we fitted linear mixed-effects models to trial-level RTs with trial number (within-session) as a continuous predictor and random intercepts and slopes for participants. The model showed a significant negative effect of trial number within each session, indicating that RTs decreased as participants proceeded through the trials of each session, β = -0.15 ms per trial, SE = 0.02 ms, t = -7.5, p < .001. Including Valence and the interaction of Valence x Trial Number did not yield a significant interaction, indicating that the within-session learning slope was similar for positive and negative targets. A secondary model that included session number as a fixed effect provided converging evidence that learning accrued both within and across sessions, with the largest reductions occurring between the first and second sessions.

Additional exploratory analyses
We examined whether individual differences in trait-level measures available from the screening questionnaire (e.g., self-reported trait anxiety) correlated with the magnitude of the valence effect (difference between negative and positive RTs in Week 1). No reliable correlations were observed in this sample; trait anxiety scores did not predict the size of the valence advantage, r = .09, p = .52. We also inspected possible speed-accuracy trade-offs by correlating accuracy with RT within participants and found small negative correlations (faster RTs associated with slightly lower accuracy) that did not reach significance in most comparisons, indicating that the improvement in RTs across sessions did not occur at the expense of accuracy.

Summary of results
In summary, the data revealed a persistent positive-face advantage: participants detected happy faces faster than angry faces across all three weekly sessions. Reaction times decreased substantially between Week 1 and Week 2 and decreased further, though to a lesser extent, between Week 2 and Week 3 for both valence conditions. The Valence x Session interaction was not significant, suggesting comparable practice-related improvements for positive and negative targets rather than a selective habituation of negative-target advantage. Mood, as indexed by PANAS PA and NA scales, remained stable across sessions and did not account for RT differences. Accuracy improved slightly across sessions but did not differ by valence. Together, these findings support the conclusion that practice effects best explain the reductions in search times across sessions while the valence-driven advantage observed in the initial session persisted rather than being eliminated by repeated exposure.

Discussion

This study set out to investigate whether a negative-face advantage in a visual-search task would present initially and then attenuate with repeated exposure across weekly sessions. Contrary to the a priori hypothesis derived from a threat-detection perspective, the principal outcome was that positive faces (happy expressions) were detected more rapidly than negative faces (angry expressions) across all three weekly sessions. At the same time, reaction times for both positive and negative targets declined substantially with repetition, with the most pronounced improvements occurring between the first and second sessions and smaller improvements between the second and third sessions. Mood, as measured by the PANAS, did not change over time and did not account for observed differences in RTs. These results yield several noteworthy implications for theories of emotional attention and for methodological considerations in repeated-measures designs that probe valence effects.

First, the presence of a positivity advantage across all sessions is an important empirical observation. While the literature contains many reports of a threat-related attentional bias favoring negative expressions, it is also the case that positive expressions can be detected rapidly under many conditions. Happy faces are highly distinctive visually, often involve exposed teeth and an open mouth, and are socially salient signals that may draw attention because of their affiliative value rather than their threat relevance. The consistent positive advantage observed here suggests that, at least with the stimuli, task parameters, and participant sample used in this study, positive emotional expressions are prioritized in visual search. This finding aligns with prior reports showing advantages for happy faces in some search contexts and underscores that valence effects are not universally threat-focused. The implication is that attentional prioritization for emotional faces is flexible and sensitive to task demands and stimulus properties.

Second, the absence of a selective habituation of the valence effect is theoretically informative. If negative faces were treated by the perceptual system primarily as threat signals that would lose salience with repeated non-threatening exposures, one would expect a declining negative-target advantage across sessions. Instead, we observed a stable positive advantage with no reliable Valence x Session interaction. Both positive and negative search times decreased with practice at similar rates, suggesting that general improvements in search efficiency rather than valence-specific reductions best characterize the longitudinal pattern. This outcome argues against a simple habituation account for valence-specific attentional biases in this context. At minimum, it suggests that habituation, if it occurs for emotional stimuli, may require different conditions—such as prolonged exposure within a single session, repeated exposure to identical exemplars over many more trials, or exposure to stimuli with stronger affective intensity—than those provided here.

Third, practice effects provide the most parsimonious explanation for the reliable reductions in RT across sessions. Participants became faster within sessions and across sessions, with the largest gains evident from Week 1 to Week 2. The pattern is consistent with procedural learning and refinement of search strategies, such as improved scanning patterns across array positions, faster mapping from stimulus detection to motor response, and enhanced criterion setting for discriminating targets from distractors. Our trial-level mixed-effects models also revealed significant within-session learning slopes that did not differ by valence. The combination of within-session and between-session improvements supports the interpretation that repeated performance led to more efficient task execution rather than changes in the intrinsic salience of particular emotional expressions.

Fourth, the stability of mood across sessions and its lack of explanatory power for RT changes strengthens the interpretation that neither valence effects nor practice-related improvements were driven by mood shifts. Given the well-documented influence of mood on attention, establishing the absence of systematic mood changes reinforces the internal validity of the findings. It is noteworthy that mood did not change across the three weekly sessions, likely due to the lack of mood induction procedures and the relatively short overall study duration.

Fifth, accuracy patterns complement the RT findings. High and slightly improving accuracy suggests that speed gains were not achieved through increasingly liberal response biases. Instead, participants improved both speed and maintaining high accuracy, which is consistent with genuine improvements in processing efficiency. The lack of valence-based differences in accuracy suggests that the RT differences reflect processing speed rather than a trade-off with errors.

The current results contribute to ongoing theoretical debates about the specificity and malleability of emotion-driven attentional biases. They indicate that positive expressions can be prioritized in visual search and that such prioritization can be robust to repeated exposures over at least a two-week span. From a theoretical perspective, this pattern encourages reconceptualizing attentional prioritization as sensitive to factors beyond valence alone. Perceptual distinctiveness, communicative value, and social relevance may all contribute to prioritization in ways that can produce positivity advantages under certain conditions. Moreover, the absence of valence-specific habituation suggests that the attentional system may encode emotional signals in a durable manner when they are task-relevant, resisting rapid declines in salience simply due to repeated exposure.

Several methodological considerations warrant discussion. The task employed target-present trials with emotional faces embedded among neutral distractors; equal numbers of positive-target and negative-target trials were randomly intermixed. This design maximizes ecological validity by creating an unpredictable target landscape and avoids confounding valence with block-level expectations. However, the mixture of valence trial types and the inclusion of different identities as targets on each trial might have contributed to the stability of the positive advantage by preventing participants from learning to suppress the salience of particular emotional expressions. Future studies might manipulate the predictability or prevalence of each valence to examine whether more systematic exposure to one valence produces differential habituation.

Stimulus properties are an additional consideration. Although faces were standardized and luminance- and contrast-matched, positive expressions may still have unique low-level features—such as raised cheeks and exposed teeth—that facilitate detection. While matching across valence in low-level features is a difficult task, future work could include more detailed image analyses or use synthetic faces to more tightly control for such visual cues. Additionally, the intensity of negative expressions in our stimulus set may not have been equivalent in perceived arousal to the positive expressions, a factor that could influence attentional capture. If negative expressions in the stimuli were somewhat milder in their threat signaling, they might not have been as attentionally potent as would be the case with very intense fearful or angry faces.

The temporal spacing of sessions also bears consideration. We chose a weekly spacing to balance the possibility of observing medium-term habituation without inducing participant fatigue or attrition. It remains possible that habituation of emotional salience would require more frequent exposure within a shorter timeframe or a greater number of exposures for its manifestation. Conversely, longer intervals might allow for consolidation processes that could maintain or even augment certain biases. Systematic manipulation of inter-session intervals could illuminate the temporal dynamics of any habituation or consolidation effects.

Individual differences were not a major focus of the present investigation, but they remain an important avenue for future research. In this sample, trait anxiety did not predict the size of valence effects, but larger and more diverse samples are needed to reliably detect such interactions. Clinical populations, or individuals with elevated baseline anxiety, might show different patterns of habituation or sensitivity to threat. Additionally, developmental factors could influence whether positive or negative faces capture attention; children and older adults might show different valence effects compared with young adults.

Several concrete empirical follow-ups would be informative. First, incorporating physiological measures of arousal such as skin conductance, heart rate variability, or pupil dilation could reveal whether negative faces elicit stronger autonomic responses that do not translate into faster overt search times. Second, eye-tracking would offer a direct measure of attentional allocation and search strategy, distinguishing faster motor responses due to improved scanning from early perceptual capture. Third, manipulating stimulus intensity or using dynamic facial expressions could test whether stronger or more ecologically valid negative signals produce a negative-face advantage that is more resistant to repetition. Fourth, varying the prevalence of target valence (making negative targets rarer) could assess how frequency and expectation interact with valence-driven attention and potential habituation.

Limitations of the study must be acknowledged. The sample, while adequate for within-subjects comparisons, consisted of university volunteers and thus may not generalize to broader populations. The stimuli, although standardized, cannot perfectly equate all low-level features across emotional categories, and our negative expressions may have been less arousing than negative signals used in some threat-detection studies. The weekly spacing of sessions represents only one temporal pattern of repetition, and different schedules might yield different outcomes. Finally, although the PANAS provided a useful check on mood stability, other affective constructs such as arousal or momentary anxiety were not measured and could potentially relate to attentional patterns in more subtle ways.

In conclusion, the present study provides evidence against a simple habituation account of threat-related attentional bias in face search over the time scale tested. Instead, we observed a robust positivity advantage that persisted across three weekly testing sessions alongside significant practice-related improvements in search speed for both positive and negative faces. Mood remained stable across the testing interval and did not account for these effects. These findings highlight the complexity of valence effects in attention and the importance of separating valence-specific processes from general practice effects in repeated-measures designs. Future work that integrates physiological measures, eye-tracking, manipulations of stimulus intensity and prevalence, and exploration of individual differences will further clarify the mechanisms that underlie the interplay of emotion, attention, and experience.
