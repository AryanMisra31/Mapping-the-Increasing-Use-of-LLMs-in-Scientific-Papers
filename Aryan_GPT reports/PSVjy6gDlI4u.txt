Introduction

Hemispheric lateralization is a foundational concept in cognitive neuroscience that describes the tendency for certain mental processes to be more strongly represented in one cerebral hemisphere than the other. Research on lateralization has identified robust examples, such as language processing bias to the left hemisphere in most individuals and spatial attention biases that tend toward the right hemisphere. The lateralization of emotion processing has been a particularly active area of investigation because emotion is central to interpersonal behavior and survival, and because lateralized processing may reveal fundamental organizational principles of the brain. Historically, two main frameworks have been advanced to account for emotional lateralization. One framework emphasizes valence, positing that positive emotions are preferentially processed in the left hemisphere while negative emotions are preferentially processed in the right hemisphere. An alternative framework emphasizes motivational direction, arguing that approach-related emotions preferentially engage the left hemisphere while withdrawal-related emotions engage the right hemisphere. Both frameworks have garnered empirical support in different contexts, and both have stimulated a large body of work aimed at identifying the boundary conditions of lateralized emotional processing. In parallel, studies that examine lateralization at the level of specific stimuli, such as facial expressions, have provided detailed insights into how the hemispheres contribute to the perception and interpretation of emotion-relevant cues. Face perception is especially relevant in this context because faces are a primary channel for emotional communication and because many components of face processing are known to be lateralized. For example, several studies using divided visual field paradigms, lesion studies, and neuroimaging have reported right hemisphere advantages for the perception of facial identity and facial emotion in right-handed individuals. The predominance of right-hemisphere processing for emotional faces has been interpreted as evidence for a specialized role of the right hemisphere in decoding social signals embedded in facial expressions. However, the literature is not wholly consistent, and considerable variability has been observed across tasks, stimuli, and individual differences. One methodological approach that has been widely used to estimate hemispheric biases for facial expressions is the chimeric faces task. In this task, pairs of faces are presented that combine different halves of emotional expressions, and observers indicate which composite face appears more expressive. Performance on this task is interpreted as an index of hemispheric dominance because visual input to the left or right visual field is preferentially processed by the contralateral hemisphere. Levy and colleagues employed such a chimeric faces task and reported that right-handed individuals tended to show a bias consistent with right hemisphere superiority for processing emotional facial expressions. This finding has been influential because it supports the notion that face-related emotional processing is lateralized at the group level in right-handed populations. At the same time as these lines of research were developing, other studies focused on individual-difference variables that might be linked to lateralization. For example, researchers have investigated whether dispositional traits such as optimism are associated with hemispheric asymmetries. Optimism, broadly construed as a general tendency to expect positive outcomes, has been related to approach-related motivational tendencies and to particular affective profiles. Hecht and colleagues proposed and provided evidence that optimism is predominantly processed in the left hemisphere, in line with theories linking positive affect and approach to left-lateralized neural systems. If optimism is associated with left-hemisphere processing and facial emotion processing is associated with the right hemisphere, then variability in optimism could conceivably modulate lateralization for facial expression processing in systematic ways. Specifically, individuals high in optimism might show a relative shift toward left-hemisphere involvement for some aspects of emotion processing, whereas individuals low in optimism might show stronger right-hemisphere indices. Investigating such relationships between dispositional traits and lateralization can inform whether lateralization is a fixed property of sensory-cognitive systems or whether it is modulated by enduring personal characteristics. Despite the plausibility of such interactions, few studies have directly examined the relationship between optimism and hemispheric lateralization for facial expression processing. This is an important gap because characterizing these associations can illuminate the degree to which affective personality traits are linked to the neural architecture of emotion perception. A study that explicitly examines the relationship between a validated measure of optimism and a well-established lateralization task would therefore contribute to both the lateralization and individual differences literatures. Several methodological considerations are relevant when designing such a study. First, handedness is a critical factor because hemispheric specializations often differ substantially between left- and right-handed individuals. Many classic lateralization effects, including language lateralization and face processing asymmetries, are more consistently observed in right-handed samples. As a consequence, studies seeking to detect group-level biases often restrict samples to right-handed participants to reduce heterogeneity. Second, gender can be relevant because sex differences have been reported for emotion processing in some studies, although those findings are not universally consistent. Restricting the sample to one sex can help control for potential sex-related variability, though it limits generalizability. Third, the reliability of the lateralization measure is essential, because low reliability reduces the power to detect associations with personality traits. The chimeric faces task has been widely used and can produce acceptable internal consistency with an adequate number of trials and carefully controlled stimuli. Fourth, the measurement of optimism must be psychometrically robust. The Life Orientation Test – Revised (LOT-R) is a commonly used self-report instrument with established reliability and validity for assessing dispositional optimism. Using a standard and validated optimism measure facilitates comparability across studies. Fifth, sample size considerations are important: correlations between dispositional traits and neural indices are often modest in magnitude, so adequate sample sizes are required to detect such effects reliably. Taken together, these considerations suggest a study that includes a reasonably large sample of right-handed participants, employs a reliable chimeric faces protocol, and uses the LOT-R to quantify optimism. The goal would be to test two primary hypotheses. First, consistent with Levy et al., right-handed individuals would, at the group level, show a bias indicating greater right hemisphere involvement in the processing of emotional facial expressions. Second, consistent with Hecht and colleagues, higher dispositional optimism would be associated with relative left-hemisphere processing, yielding a negative correlation between optimism and rightward lateralization indices or, equivalently, a positive correlation with leftward indices. If such relationships were observed, they would support the view that dispositional optimism modulates hemispheric contributions to facial emotion perception. Alternatively, failure to observe these relationships would suggest that facial expression lateralization and optimism are largely independent constructs, or that any relationship is too subtle to be detected with behavioral lateralization measures like the chimeric faces task. The current study was designed to test these hypotheses directly. The study recruited a large sample of right-handed female participants to match prior work indicating right-hemisphere bias in right-handers and to control for potential sex differences. Participants completed a chimeric faces task modeled on Levy et al.’s design, with a sufficient number of trials to obtain reliable lateralization estimates. Participants also completed the LOT-R to assess dispositional optimism. Statistical analyses included tests for group-level lateralization, correlations between optimism scores and lateralization indices, and supplementary analyses exploring potential moderating factors. The study also included reliability analyses to quantify the internal consistency of the chimeric faces measure in the present sample. The research plan sought to be sensitive to modest effects by including a relatively large sample and by pre-specifying analytic approaches that would provide converging evidence regarding the relationships of interest. By directly examining the interplay between dispositional optimism and lateralization of facial expression processing, the study aimed to clarify whether optimistic outlooks are reflected in the hemispheric organization of emotion perception. Given the mixed evidence in the literature and the theoretical importance of linking personality traits to brain lateralization, the study addressed a timely and substantive question in affective neuroscience and personality psychology. The following sections describe the methods used, the results obtained, and the implications of the findings for theories of emotional lateralization and the neural substrates of optimism.

Methods

Participants were 120 right-handed female volunteers recruited from a university participant pool and from community advertisements. All participants self-identified as right-handed on a standard handedness inventory administered during the screening process and reported having normal or corrected-to-normal vision. The sample size of 120 was selected to provide adequate statistical power to detect small-to-moderate correlations between dispositional measures and lateralization indices; an a priori calculation indicated that with 120 participants, the study had approximately 80% power to detect correlations of approximately r = 0.25 at alpha = 0.05. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 35 years with a mean age of 22.6 years and an SD of 3.4 years. Participants provided written informed consent prior to participation in accordance with the procedures approved by the institution’s research ethics committee. No monetary compensation beyond course credit and a small gift card was provided. Exclusion criteria included a history of neurological or psychiatric disorder, current psychoactive medication, or uncorrected visual impairments. All participants completed the full protocol in a single testing session that lasted approximately 45 minutes. The chimeric faces task was constructed to closely follow the procedures described by Levy and colleagues, with modifications to increase trial numbers and diversity of stimuli. Stimuli were grayscale photographs of adult faces selected from standardized facial expression databases; each face displayed either neutral, happy, or sad expressions. Chimeric faces were created by combining the left half of one expression with the right half of the same face displaying a different expression, resulting in two mirrored chimeric images for each original expression pair. For example, one chimeric image would present the left-half-happy/right-half-neutral composite while the mirror image would present the left-half-neutral/right-half-happy composite. Each pair of chimeric images was presented centrally on a monitor, and participants were asked to indicate which of the two images appeared more emotionally expressive. On each trial, participants pressed one of two keys to indicate their choice, and the side of the more expressive half was recorded. The task included 40 chimeric pairs, balanced across expression types and across which half contained the more expressive emotion. The trial order was randomized for each participant. Prior to the experimental trials, participants completed 6 practice trials with feedback to ensure task comprehension. The chimeric faces task was scored by counting the number of times participants selected the image in which the expressive half lay on the left or on the right. These counts were then converted into a lateralization index (LI) calculated as (Right choices − Left choices) / Total choices, yielding a value that ranged theoretically from −1 to +1, where positive values indicate a rightward bias and negative values indicate a leftward bias. Higher positive LIs were interpreted as greater reliance on right-hemisphere processing for emotional facial cues, consistent with standard interpretations of chimeric-face performance. Internal consistency of the chimeric faces task was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha on trial-level binary scores to ensure that the measure provided reliable estimates of individual differences in lateralization. Dispositional optimism was assessed using the Life Orientation Test – Revised (LOT-R). The LOT-R comprises 10 items, of which six are scored to generate the optimism score and four serve as fillers; each scored item is rated on a five-point Likert scale from 0 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree), yielding a composite optimism score that ranges from 0 to 24. Participants responded to the LOT-R after completing the chimeric faces task to avoid any priming effects from reflecting on personal dispositions prior to the perceptual task. The LOT-R was scored in the standard manner, with negatively worded items reverse-scored before summation. The internal consistency of the LOT-R in the present sample was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. In addition to the primary measures, participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire and a short state mood measure to capture transient affective states that could influence performance; the state mood measure consisted of three items assessing current positive affect, negative affect, and arousal on 7-point scales. The state mood variables were included as potential covariates in secondary analyses if they correlated with either the LI or the LOT-R score. Data quality checks included examination for missing responses, improbable response patterns, and outliers on key measures. No participants had missing data on the primary measures. One participant produced a response pattern on the chimeric faces task that was judged to be random (equal selection of left and right with extremely rapid response times across trials), and that participant’s data were examined; after secondary checks, the participant’s data did not meet exclusion criteria and therefore were retained. Statistical analyses were pre-specified and conducted using standard software. The primary analyses tested (a) whether the group exhibited a significant mean lateralization index differing from zero, and (b) whether LOT-R scores were associated with individual differences in lateralization. The group-level test was a one-sample t-test comparing the mean LI to zero. The correlation between LI and LOT-R scores used Pearson’s r. Effect sizes were computed (Cohen’s d for t-tests and r for correlations) and reported. Secondary analyses included a comparison of the number of participants showing a rightward bias versus a leftward bias using a binomial test, split-sample comparisons contrasting tertiles of optimism (highest vs. lowest third), and regression analyses controlling for age and state mood. Robustness checks included nonparametric tests (Wilcoxon signed-rank test for the LI, Spearman’s rho for correlations) and Bayesian analyses that provided Bayes factors to quantify evidence for the null versus alternative hypotheses. All statistical tests were two-tailed and used an alpha threshold of 0.05 for inferential decisions. Assumptions underlying parametric tests were evaluated by inspecting distributions, assessing skewness and kurtosis, and conducting formal tests where appropriate. Where assumptions were violated, nonparametric analogues were reported. The analysis plan also included exploratory assessments of potential moderating factors, such as the influence of expression type (happy vs. sad) on lateralization indices, by computing separate LI scores for positive and negative expression trials and testing correlations with optimism for each valence-specific LI. The decision to include these exploratory tests was made to probe whether optimism might interact with emotional valence in shaping lateralization. Finally, sensitivity analyses were planned to estimate the smallest effect size that the sample could reliably detect and to report Bayes factors to contextualize null findings. Ethical and data management procedures were followed to ensure confidentiality and secure storage of anonymized data. Participants were debriefed at the end of the session and given contact information for support services should questions or discomfort arise from participation.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The lateralization index (LI) derived from the chimeric faces task had a mean value of 0.01 and a standard deviation of 0.14. The range of LI values in the sample was from −0.45 to +0.50. The LOT-R optimism scores had a mean of 15.8 and a standard deviation of 3.9, with observed scores spanning from 4 to 23 on the 0–24 scale. Cronbach’s alpha for the chimeric faces task, computed on the trial-level binary response items across the 40 trials, was 0.72, indicating acceptable internal consistency. Cronbach’s alpha for the LOT-R in this sample was 0.84, indicating good internal consistency for the optimism measure. Inspection of the distributions for LI and LOT-R scores showed approximately symmetric distributions with no extreme skewness; LI had a skewness of 0.12 and kurtosis of −0.24, while LOT-R had a skewness of −0.45 and kurtosis of 0.11. Visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots did not reveal departures from normality that would invalidate parametric tests. The primary question of whether the right-handed female sample exhibited a group-level right-hemisphere bias for emotional facial expressions was tested using a one-sample t-test comparing the mean LI to zero. The one-sample t-test yielded t(119) = 0.87, p = 0.387, indicating that the mean LI did not significantly differ from zero. The corresponding Cohen’s d for this test was 0.08, representing a very small effect size. A nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirmed the lack of a systematic bias, with a two-tailed p-value of 0.412. To complement the null hypothesis test, a Bayesian one-sample t-test was conducted and produced a Bayes factor BF01 of 4.8, indicating that the observed data were approximately 4.8 times more likely under the null hypothesis of no group-level lateralization than under the alternative hypothesis. In terms of counts, 62 participants exhibited a net rightward bias (LI>0) and 58 exhibited a net leftward bias (LI<0), a distribution that did not differ significantly from chance according to a binomial test (p = 0.74). Thus, multiple analytic approaches converged on the conclusion that there was no group-level hemispheric dominance for emotional facial expression processing in this sample. The second primary question assessed whether dispositional optimism was associated with hemispheric lateralization for facial expressions. Pearson’s correlation between LOT-R scores and the lateralization index was r = 0.02 with 95% confidence interval [−0.14, 0.18] and p = 0.84. This result indicated essentially no linear association between optimism and LI. Spearman’s rho, computed as a robustness check, was 0.01, p = 0.91, yielding a concordant nonparametric outcome. A Bayesian correlation analysis yielded a BF01 of 6.5, providing moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis of no relationship between optimism and lateralization. To further explore potential non-linearities or threshold effects, participants were divided into tertiles based on LOT-R scores to create low, medium, and high optimism groups. The mean LI in the lowest tertile (n = 40) was −0.01 (SD = 0.13), in the middle tertile was 0.02 (SD = 0.15), and in the highest tertile was 0.02 (SD = 0.14). An ANOVA testing LI differences across the tertiles yielded F(2,117) = 0.32, p = 0.72, indicating no significant differences among the optimism groups. Planned contrast comparing the highest and lowest tertiles produced t(78) = 0.45, p = 0.65, again revealing no reliable effect. Regression analyses examined whether optimism predicted LI after controlling for age and state mood. Age did not correlate with LI (r = −0.03, p = 0.72) and state positive affect had a small non-significant correlation with LI (r = 0.10, p = 0.26). When LI was regressed on LOT-R, age, and state positive and negative affect, the overall model was not significant, R2 = 0.006, F(4,115) = 0.18, p = 0.95, and LOT-R had a standardized beta of 0.02, p = 0.83. This analysis indicated that controlling for potential covariates did not reveal a suppressed or masked relationship between optimism and lateralization. Exploratory analyses probed valence-specific lateralization by computing separate LI scores for trials involving positive (happy) facial expressions and for trials involving negative (sad) facial expressions. The mean LI for happy expression trials was 0.02 (SD = 0.16), and for sad expression trials was 0.00 (SD = 0.15). Correlations between LOT-R scores and the valence-specific LIs were r = −0.01 for happy trials (p = 0.89) and r = 0.05 for sad trials (p = 0.60). None of these correlations approached significance, and Bayesian analyses for these comparisons yielded BF01 values exceeding 5, indicating substantial evidence for null effects. A further exploratory analysis examined whether performance on the chimeric task itself exhibited systematic biases for particular stimulus exemplars. Item-level analyses showed some variability in how often the right or left half was chosen as more expressive, but no consistent pattern emerged across stimuli that would account for participant-level LI variability. The internal consistency estimate reported above (alpha = 0.72) suggested that the chimeric task provided a reliable individual-difference signal, making the lack of association with LOT-R less likely to be attributable to measurement unreliability. Sensitivity analyses examined the minimum detectable effect given the sample size and observed variability. Given the observed SD of 0.14 for LI and the sample size of 120, the present study had 80% power to detect a mean LI of approximately 0.05 (Cohen’s d ~ 0.36) versus zero. For correlations, the study had 80% power to detect r values of approximately 0.25. The observed effect sizes were well below these thresholds, suggesting that if any true association exists between optimism and LI, it is likely smaller than r = 0.25 and thus of limited practical magnitude. Finally, potential outliers were examined using standardized residuals in the correlation analysis. Two participants had LI values more than 3 standard deviations from the mean; analyses excluding these two participants yielded nearly identical results (r = 0.03, p = 0.78), indicating that outliers did not drive the null findings. In summary, the data provided no support for a group-level right-hemisphere dominance for processing of emotional facial expressions in this sample of right-handed females, and no evidence was found for an association between dispositional optimism and lateralization as measured by the chimeric faces task.

Discussion

The present study tested two interrelated hypotheses about hemispheric lateralization and dispositional optimism. First, we examined whether a sample of right-handed females would exhibit a group-level right-hemisphere bias for processing emotional facial expressions, replicating prior reports such as those by Levy and colleagues. Second, we explored whether individual differences in dispositional optimism, as measured by the LOT-R, were associated with hemispheric lateralization for facial emotion processing, consistent with proposals by Hecht and others linking optimism to left-hemisphere processing. Across multiple analytic approaches, the findings were clear and convergent: there was no reliable group-level lateralization in the chimeric faces task, and no association was observed between optimism and lateralization indices. These null results were robust to parametric and nonparametric tests, survived sensitivity analyses excluding outliers, and were supported by Bayesian analyses that provided moderate evidence favoring the null. The absence of a group-level right-hemisphere bias runs contrary to some earlier findings but is not unprecedented in the literature. Lateralization effects for face processing, including emotional faces, can be highly sensitive to methodological nuances such as stimulus characteristics, task demands, and sample composition. The chimeric faces task has been widely used as an indirect behavioral marker of hemispheric asymmetry, but its outcomes can vary with factors such as the number of trials, the particular set of facial stimuli, the emotional intensity of expressions, and the instruction set given to participants. In the present study, we used 40 chimeric trials and standardized stimuli to maximize reliability, and the task achieved acceptable internal consistency. Nonetheless, the lack of a mean bias suggests that, at least in this sample and with these procedures, lateralization for facial expressions did not manifest as a simple population-level tendency. One possible interpretation of the null group-level lateralization is that the lateralization of emotional face processing is not as robust at the behavioral level as sometimes assumed, or that it may manifest more reliably in neurophysiological or neuroimaging measures than in behavioral choice tasks. Neuroimaging work has revealed complex patterns of bilateral and interactive contributions from multiple cortical and subcortical regions during face perception, and behavioral tasks that aggregate across trials may not capture the temporal and spatial dynamics that produce lateralization at the neural level. Another possibility is that lateralization for facial emotion is more idiosyncratic across individuals than uniform across a right-handed population, producing a distribution centered near zero even though individuals show meaningful asymmetric processing. Indeed, individual differences in hemispheric organization are well documented for language and other domains, and face processing may be similarly heterogeneous. If lateralization is distributed across individuals in varying directions, group-level means will obscure important within-subject asymmetries. The second major finding — the lack of association between optimism and hemispheric lateralization — informs questions about whether dispositional traits such as optimism are reflected in the hemispheric organization of emotion perception. Hecht and colleagues proposed and reported evidence that optimism is predominantly linked with left-hemisphere processing, which raised the possibility that optimism could modulate lateralization for perceiving emotional signals from faces. The present null result suggests that, at least as assessed via the chimeric faces task, dispositional optimism does not exert a detectable influence on which hemisphere is preferentially engaged during facial emotion perception. There are several non-mutually-exclusive explanations for this lack of association. First, optimism may be more closely tied to higher-order cognitive appraisals and motivational processes that are not assessed by perceptual lateralization tasks. While optimism has been related to approach motivation and positive affect, these constructs may translate into differences in decision-making, interpretation biases, and anticipatory processing rather than into perceptual asymmetries for immediate facial cues. Second, the LOT-R assesses general expectancies about life outcomes, a trait-level construct that may not map directly onto the perceptual mechanisms that underlie chimeric face choices. The link between trait optimism and lateralized neural processes might therefore be indirect, mediated by cognitive or motivational processes that were not engaged in the present task. Third, it is possible that the neural substrates of optimism are more readily detected using direct neuroimaging or electrophysiological measures that can resolve lateralized activation patterns with higher spatial and temporal precision. Behavioral lateralization tasks might lack sensitivity to detect subtle hemispheric differences associated with a dispositional trait. For example, lateralized EEG alpha asymmetries have been linked to approach-avoidance tendencies and affective style in some studies, suggesting that scalp measures may reveal relations that behavioral tasks do not. Fourth, the relationship between optimism and hemispheric processing might be conditional on context or emotional valence in ways that were not captured here. We tested valence-specific LIs for happy and sad expressions and found no association with optimism for either valence. However, contextually rich or ecologically valid stimuli that evoke motivational relevance (e.g., stimuli linked to personally meaningful goals) might engage optimism-related processes more strongly and reveal lateralized patterns. Methodological considerations also bear on the interpretation of the null findings. Although the sample was reasonably large and included only right-handed females to reduce heterogeneity, this sample restriction limits generalizability. It remains possible that associations between optimism and lateralization are present in male participants or in mixed-sex samples. Moreover, restricting the sample to right-handers was intended to align with prior work showing more consistent lateralization in right-handed individuals, but individual differences in hemispheric organization persist even within right-handed samples. The chimeric faces task itself, while widely used, yields a coarse index of hemispheric bias. The LI collapses across trials and reduces rich perceptual dynamics to a single summary value. Even with acceptable internal consistency, the task may not capture transient lateralized processing that varies with trial-by-trial factors such as attentional focus, fatigue, or spontaneous mood fluctuations. Notably, the LOT-R was administered after the perceptual task to avoid priming, but state mood was measured and included as a covariate in secondary analyses; these covariates did not alter the null findings. The Bayesian analyses deserve special attention because they provide a way to quantify evidence for a null effect rather than simply failing to reject the null hypothesis. The BF01 values obtained consistently favored the null, some indicating moderate evidence. This suggests that the null effects reported here are not merely due to insufficient power or random fluctuation, but rather provide substantive evidence that, under these conditions, no meaningful relationship exists between optimism and chimeric-face lateralization. Still, Bayesian evidence is contingent on prior specifications and model choices, so these results should be interpreted alongside the full pattern of null findings rather than as definitive proof of absence. The theoretical implications of the results require careful consideration. If optimism is not associated with lateralization of facial emotion perception, this suggests that dispositional affective traits and perceptual lateralization are separable in some domains. It may be that optimism exerts its influence primarily on cognitive and motivational systems that operate downstream from early perceptual processes, while lateralized perceptual biases are governed more by structural and developmental factors. Alternatively, optimism could be related to lateralization in neural regions or networks that are not central to the processes tapped by the chimeric faces task. For example, optimism might relate to asymmetric activity in prefrontal regions involved in appraisal and expectation rather than in posterior occipito-temporal regions implicated in face perception. Future research could test this possibility directly using neuroimaging techniques that can localize valence-, motivation-, and expectancy-related asymmetries across cortical and subcortical regions. Several specific directions for future work emerge from the present study. First, studies employing neuroimaging (fMRI) or electrophysiological (EEG/MEG) methods could probe whether optimism relates to lateralized activation patterns during face perception at a finer-grained neural level. These methods can assess temporal dynamics and regional specificity that behavioral tasks cannot. Second, longitudinal or developmental studies could examine whether dispositional optimism and lateralization co-develop or influence one another over time; early-life experiences that shape optimism might also impact hemispheric organization. Third, experimental manipulations that temporarily alter optimism-related states (e.g., through mood inductions or expectancy manipulations) could test causal effects on lateralization, thereby distinguishing trait from state effects. Fourth, expanding samples to include males, left-handers, and diverse cultural groups would clarify the generality of the present null findings and explore potential moderators such as sex, handedness, and cultural norms regarding emotional expression. Fifth, employing more ecologically valid or motivationally salient facial stimuli, or tasks that require deeper appraisal rather than simple expressive judgments, might engage optimism-related processes and reveal subtle lateralized effects. Sixth, studies could investigate whether optimism relates to lateralized processing in other affective domains, such as reward processing, risk-taking, or decision-making under uncertainty, where approach-related tendencies might be more directly implicated. The present study has strengths that bolster confidence in its conclusions, including a relatively large sample, careful measurement of handedness and optimism, and the use of multiple complementary analytic approaches. Nonetheless, the study also has limitations that temper generalizations. The reliance on a behavioral lateralization measure constrains conclusions about underlying neural mechanisms. The single-session cross-sectional design prohibits causal inference. The sample’s restriction to right-handed females enhances internal control but limits external validity. Finally, while the chimeric faces task had acceptable internal consistency, it remains possible that it did not capture lateralized processing with sufficient sensitivity to detect modest trait associations. In conclusion, the study did not find evidence of a group-level right-hemisphere bias for the processing of emotional facial expressions in right-handed females, nor did it find an association between dispositional optimism and lateralization indices derived from the chimeric faces task. These findings suggest that optimism does not substantially modulate hemispheric contributions to facial emotion perception as measured behaviorally with chimeric face choices. The results invite a broader perspective on the relationships between personality traits and neural lateralization: such relations may be domain-specific, context-dependent, or more readily observable with direct neural measures rather than embedded in coarse behavioral indices. Future research that employs converging methodologies, diverse samples, and experimentally manipulable contexts will be essential to fully map the extent to which dispositional optimism is reflected in the lateralized organization of affective processing.
