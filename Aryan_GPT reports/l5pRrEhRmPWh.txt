Introduction

Eyewitness memory and the malleability of human recollection have been central topics in cognitive and legal psychology for several decades. A large body of research has established that the wording of questions can alter recollections about past events. This phenomenon has direct implications for applied settings such as police interviews and courtroom testimony. One of the landmark findings in this area is that subtle linguistic manipulations can bias quantitative judgments made by observers. The idea that post-event information can modify memory traces challenges traditional views of memory as a stable archive. Memory instead appears to be constructive and susceptible to integration of suggestion. The classic demonstration of this susceptibility involved varying a single verb in a question about a filmed traffic accident and observing systematic shifts in speed estimates. That seminal work showed that more violent verbs led to higher speed estimates and increased reports of broken glass. The implication drawn from those data was that suggestive phrasing can alter participants’ percepts or their later reconstructions. Since that time, researchers have replicated and extended the finding in multiple populations and modalities. The robustness of the effect has been debated, however, particularly in relation to boundary conditions and moderators. One critical question concerns whether individual differences in the witness interact with suggestive questioning to alter the magnitude of the effect. Some investigators have argued that susceptibility to suggestion changes with confidence, stress, and prior expectations. Confidence in one’s own perceptual reports is an especially relevant moderator to consider because it is often used as a proxy for credibility in legal contexts. If confidence buffers against suggestive influence, then confident witnesses may resist the semantic pull of leading questions. Conversely, if confidence is itself unstable and easily influenced by post-event cues, then it may not protect against suggestive phrasing. The interaction between suggestive questioning and confidence remains insufficiently explored empirically. A number of empirical and theoretical accounts offer competing predictions about the moderating role of confidence. On one hand, cognitive accounts emphasize that initial encoding quality predicts resistance to post-event misinformation. Higher-confidence reports are often associated with better initial encoding and greater accuracy in many tasks. From this perspective, higher confidence should reduce the impact of a leading question. On the other hand, social-cognitive accounts highlight that confidence judgments can be reconstructed post hoc and are sensitive to feedback and social cues. From that perspective, confidence may be malleable in its own right and could interact with leading questions in complex ways. A few studies have explicitly manipulated confidence to examine its effect on susceptibility to suggestion. Some of these investigations suggested that induced confidence changes the reliance on internal evidence versus external cues. Specifically, when individuals are led to feel less certain, they may infer that external information or suggestions are more informative. Conversely, induced high confidence might increase reliance on one’s own memory and reduce the influence of misleading information. Other work has shown only weak or inconsistent moderation by confidence, indicating that the role of confidence may be context dependent. Methodological differences across experiments may account for mixed findings, including differences in the strength of confidence manipulations, the nature of the suggestive information, and sample characteristics. Furthermore, much of the early work was conducted in laboratory settings with undergraduate samples under controlled conditions. The growing use of online platforms introduces new variability, both in terms of participant characteristics and experimental control. Online studies may be particularly vulnerable to distraction, variable viewing conditions, and heterogeneous device displays, all of which could affect both encoding and susceptibility to suggestion. Despite these concerns, online testing also offers broader access and increased ecological diversity, which can be advantageous for generalization. A rigorous test of the interaction between confidence and leading questions therefore needs to carefully manipulate confidence, use validated suggestive prompts, and measure both objective judgments and subjective assessments such as self-reported accuracy. The classic stimulus used to study leading questions—the short car crash film—has proven effective across many studies in producing the verb-related bias in speed estimates. The replication of that basic effect remains a useful starting point for testing moderators because it provides a well-understood baseline. If a study can reliably reproduce the verb manipulation effect, it can then test whether an independent manipulation, such as induced confidence, changes the size of the effect. If the base effect is absent in a particular sample or setting, conclusions about moderation are necessarily limited. The present research sought to investigate whether induced confidence would moderate the influence of a leading question on speed estimates derived from a short traffic accident video. The study used two independent variables in a between-subjects design. One variable was confidence induction, manipulated by providing participants with positive or negative feedback during practice trials that required estimating vehicle speeds. The other variable was the wording of a critical question, with either the more intense verb or the milder verb as used in the classic work. Two dependent variables were collected: the numeric speed estimate provided by participants and a self-report of the perceived accuracy of the estimate. The rationale for the feedback manipulation was to shift participants’ subjective certainty about their perceptual judgments prior to exposing them to the leading question. If participants feel more confident about their perceptual judgments, they may be less likely to revise those judgments in response to subtle linguistic suggestion. Alternatively, if confidence is primarily a post hoc judgment and can be influenced by external information, then induced confidence may fail to protect against leading question effects. Previous studies that manipulated confidence have produced mixed results. Many of those studies were conducted in person and often used multiple practice trials or stronger feedback to achieve large shifts in subjective certainty. The current study employed a relatively brief feedback procedure administered online in order to balance feasibility and manipulation potency. The choice of an online design reflected pragmatic constraints and an interest in the generalizability of effects to web-based testing conditions. The sample consisted of university students with an average age in the 18–24 range. This demographic is similar to many prior experimental samples in the literature, but the online administration introduced potential differences in attention and viewing conditions relative to an in-lab demonstration. Careful screening and attention checks were therefore included in the procedure to minimize noise. The central hypotheses were as follows. First, the feedback manipulation would successfully alter self-reported confidence, with positive feedback increasing confidence and negative feedback decreasing confidence. Second, the classic leading question effect would replicate, such that the more violent verb would elicit higher speed estimates than the milder verb. Third, and most critically, there would be an interaction between confidence and the leading verb, such that participants in the high-confidence condition would show reduced susceptibility to the leading verb compared to participants in the low-confidence condition. The study also included an exploratory assessment of the relationship between self-reported accuracy and objective estimates to understand how subjective certainty related to any observed effects. The research design was selected to allow direct comparison with the classic paradigm while incorporating the confidence manipulation. Statistical analyses were planned to test the main effects and interaction using a two-way analysis of variance on the speed estimates as well as chi-square tests and nonparametric checks for the confidence manipulation. The study therefore followed a straightforward analytical plan to test the core hypotheses. Several methodological safeguards were put in place to ensure the validity of inferences. These included a pre-registered analysis plan, attention checks, and exclusion criteria for incomplete or implausible responses, such as impossible speed estimates. The current study contributes to the literature by testing a theoretically important moderator of leading question effects using a replication-augmented experimental design. It is important to clarify that null findings can be informative about boundary conditions, but they must be interpreted cautiously when sample size, manipulation strength, or testing environment may limit sensitivity. We therefore planned to report both inferential statistics and descriptive patterns to provide a complete picture. The findings bear on theoretical accounts of memory suggestibility and on practical concerns regarding the reliability of eyewitness testimony. If confidence dampens suggestive influence, then interventions aimed at increasing deciders’ appreciation of witness confidence might enhance legal decision making. If confidence does not matter or if it interacts unpredictably with social cues, then alternative safeguards are required. The remainder of the article describes the methods, presents the results, and discusses implications and future directions.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and received course credit for participation. Sixty individuals completed the study and provided usable data after screening. The sample was composed predominantly of participants in the 18–24 age range, reflecting the undergraduate demographic. All participants provided informed consent at the outset of the online session. Participation occurred entirely online through a secure survey platform optimized for experimental timing and media presentation. The experiment was approved by the relevant institutional ethics committee prior to data collection. The design was a 2 (confidence: high vs. low) × 2 (verb: smashed vs. contacted) between-subjects factorial. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the four experimental cells at the start of the session. Randomization was implemented automatically by the survey software. The primary experimental stimulus was a short film clip of a traffic accident widely used in research on suggestibility. The clip was embedded in the online session and presented at a standardized resolution. Participants were instructed to watch the video clip carefully because they would subsequently be asked questions about it. Before the critical trial, participants completed three practice trials designed to familiarize them with speed estimation. Each practice trial displayed a short segment of a car in motion for several seconds and then asked participants to estimate its speed in miles per hour. Immediately after each practice estimate, participants received feedback that constituted the confidence manipulation. Participants in the high-confidence condition received consistently positive feedback indicating that their estimates were accurate and reliable. Participants in the low-confidence condition received consistently negative feedback indicating that their estimates were poor and unreliable. The feedback statements were standardized and brief to maintain consistency across participants. Following the practice trials and feedback manipulation, participants viewed the critical car crash clip. After viewing the clip, they were asked a critical question about the speed of the cars in the crash that used either the verb "smashed" or "contacted" in the question phrasing. Specifically, the question asked "About how fast were the cars going when they [smashed/contacted] each other?" This wording mirrored the manipulation from the classic research. Participants entered a numeric estimate in miles per hour for this critical question. After providing the numeric estimate, participants were asked to rate the accuracy of their estimate on a Likert-type scale. The scale ranged from 1 ("not at all accurate") to 7 ("extremely accurate"). This self-report provided a manipulation check and an additional dependent measure. Participants also completed several attention checks dispersed through the online session. One attention check required selecting a specific response to a simple instruction embedded in the text. Participants who failed the attention checks or who provided clearly implausible numeric estimates (e.g., negative speeds, values exceeding 500 mph) were excluded from analyses. Pre-registered exclusion criteria were applied uniformly. Data from a total of 60 participants passed all inclusion criteria and were retained. The sample size of 60 was selected based on pragmatics of recruitment and on a desire to achieve at least modest power to detect medium-sized effects. All data cleaning steps were documented prior to analysis. The primary dependent variable was the numeric speed estimate from the critical question. The secondary dependent variable was the self-reported accuracy rating. The manipulation check of the confidence induction relied on frequencies of reported confidence or on the accuracy rating as a proxy. A categorical check of the manipulation also used participants’ responses to a direct question about how confident they felt after the practice trials. For the main analyses, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) tested the effects of confidence condition, verb condition, and their interaction on the numeric speed estimates. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were examined prior to running the ANOVA. Levene’s test was used to evaluate equality of error variances across groups. Shapiro-Wilk tests and visual inspection of histograms were also conducted to assess normality. Because the dependent variable is continuous and approximately normally distributed in many samples, the ANOVA was considered appropriate if no gross violations were observed. For the manipulation check of confidence induction, chi-square analyses examined whether the frequency of participants reporting high versus low confidence differed across feedback conditions. For the self-reported accuracy variable, separate ANOVAs and nonparametric checks were planned if distributional assumptions were violated. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta-squared for ANOVA results and Cramér’s V for chi-square outcomes. All inferential tests used a two-tailed alpha of .05. Data were analyzed using standard statistical software packages. Exploratory correlational analyses examined the relationship between self-reported accuracy and numeric speed estimates across the entire sample. The procedure concluded with a brief debriefing that explained the purpose of the study and clarified the deceptive element of the feedback manipulation. Participants were thanked for their time and provided contact information for follow-up questions. No identifying data were linked to the responses, and all data were stored on secure institutional servers in accordance with data protection policies.

Results

Preliminary data screening indicated that all retained responses were complete and that no implausible speed estimates remained in the dataset. The final analytic sample consisted of 60 participants distributed approximately equally across the four experimental cells. Descriptive statistics for the numeric speed estimates were computed for each cell. The mean speed estimate for participants in the smashed verb condition was 36.5 miles per hour with a standard deviation of 8.2. The mean speed estimate for participants in the contacted verb condition was 35.0 miles per hour with a standard deviation of 7.9. The high-confidence condition produced a mean estimate of 35.8 miles per hour with a standard deviation of 7.5. The low-confidence condition produced a mean estimate of 35.7 miles per hour with a standard deviation of 8.6. Visual inspection of the distributions suggested approximate normality for the numeric estimates within each cell. Shapiro-Wilk tests were conducted for each of the four cells and none indicated severe departures from normality. Levene’s test for equality of variances was not significant, indicating that homogeneity of variance was a reasonable assumption. The main analysis was a two-way ANOVA with verb and confidence as between-subjects factors predicting the numeric speed estimate. The ANOVA returned no significant main effect of verb. The F statistic for the verb main effect was F(1,56) = 0.708, p > .05. This result indicates that, in this sample, the wording of the question (smashed vs. contacted) did not significantly change speed estimates. The partial eta-squared for the verb effect was small, indicating a negligible proportion of variance explained. The main effect of confidence on numeric estimates was also not significant in the ANOVA. The F statistic for the confidence main effect did not reach significance, suggesting that the experimentally induced confidence did not produce detectable differences in mean speed estimates. The interaction between confidence and verb was examined next and likewise was not significant. The F statistic for the interaction was small and p > .05, indicating no evidence that the effect of verb depended on the confidence manipulation. Given that the classic effect was not replicated in the numeric estimates, these ANOVA results imply that the critical hypothesis about moderation by confidence could not be supported. For the manipulation check, frequency counts of self-reported confidence after the practice trials were tabulated for the two feedback conditions. A chi-square test of independence assessed the association between feedback condition and reported confidence. The chi-square test indicated a significant association, χ²(1) = 12.381, p < .05. Participants who received positive feedback were more likely to report higher confidence, and participants who received negative feedback were more likely to report lower confidence. This result demonstrates that the feedback manipulation successfully altered subjective confidence as intended. Descriptive statistics for the self-reported accuracy rating after the critical estimate were computed. The mean accuracy rating was 4.1 (SD = 1.6) in the high-confidence group and 3.9 (SD = 1.8) in the low-confidence group. The distribution of accuracy ratings did not violate assumptions for parametric analysis. A two-way ANOVA on the self-reported accuracy rating found no significant main effects of verb or confidence and no significant interaction. Thus, although the feedback manipulation influenced immediate confidence reports following practice trials, it did not translate into significant differences in accuracy ratings after the critical trial. Exploratory correlations were computed between numeric speed estimates and self-reported accuracy across all participants. The correlation was small and not statistically significant, suggesting only a weak relationship between perceived accuracy and the magnitude of the estimate. Additional exploratory analyses examined whether age, gender, or viewing device (e.g., smartphone, laptop) related to the speed estimates or to susceptibility to verb manipulation. None of these variables produced significant associations with the main dependent variable, and including them as covariates in the ANOVA did not change the pattern of results. Power considerations were addressed post hoc. Given the observed near-zero effect sizes for the main effects and interaction, the present sample of 60 participants would likely have been underpowered to detect very small effects. The observed partial eta-squared values suggested that, if effects exist, they are small in magnitude under the present procedures. Supplementary nonparametric tests were conducted to ensure robustness of the null findings. Mann-Whitney U tests comparing smashed versus contacted conditions on numeric estimates were not significant. Kruskal-Wallis checks for differences across the four cells likewise produced non-significant results. Taken together, the inferential tests converged on the conclusion that the classic verb influence on speed estimates did not replicate in the numeric data and that the induced confidence did not moderate the non-existent verb effect. The manipulation check for confidence did succeed, however, indicating that participants’ subjective confidence following practice trials differed by feedback condition. The pattern of descriptive means suggested that the smashed condition produced slightly higher mean estimates than the contacted condition, but the magnitude of this difference was small and statistically unreliable. The lack of replication suggests that contextual factors or methodological differences in the present online procedure may have attenuated the typically observed effect.

Discussion

The present experiment tested whether induced confidence moderates a classic leading-question effect on speed estimates derived from a short car crash video. The central prediction was that higher confidence would insulate participants against the influence of an intensified verb in a post-event question, while lower confidence would leave participants more susceptible to verbal suggestion. The manipulation succeeded in shifting subjective confidence as intended, which supports the validity of the feedback procedure in altering participants’ immediate metacognitive state. Despite successful manipulation of confidence, however, the classic verb effect was not observed in the numeric speed estimates. The more forceful verb did not elicit reliably higher speed estimates than the milder verb. Moreover, because the primary verb effect failed to replicate, there was no interaction to be detected between confidence and verb. These findings raise several important considerations for theories of memory suggestibility and for applied settings. First, the successful manipulation of confidence demonstrates that subjective certainty can be experimentally altered in an online setting. Participants responded to brief feedback following practice trials by adjusting their self-reported confidence in predictable directions. This indicates that feedback was salient enough to influence metacognitive judgments, at least immediately after the practice phase. Importantly, the shift in reported confidence did not translate into altered susceptibility to the leading question. Several interpretations may account for this apparent dissociation. One possibility is that the feedback manipulation affected a metacognitive appraisal that is distinct from the type of encoding quality or memory trace robustness that would confer resistance to suggestion. In other words, feeling more confident after practice trials may reflect a belief about general performance rather than a stronger perceptual trace for the critical event. If the confidence manipulation did not enhance the fidelity of encoding for the crash video, then it might not affect the degree to which post-event verbal information is integrated into the memory. A second possibility is that the leading question effect itself was attenuated in the online context. The classic effect has been robust under many in-lab conditions, but online testing introduces several sources of variability that may weaken stimulus impact. Differences in screen size, audio quality, ambient distraction, and attention could all reduce the strength of the memory or alter how participants interpret the question. If encoding was weaker across the board due to uncontrolled viewing environments, subtle linguistic manipulation may have had a smaller or more variable influence. Third, the lack of a verb effect may reflect cultural or temporal shifts in how participants interpret such questions. Past studies used undergraduate samples in specific epochs and contexts. It is possible that contemporary participants in an online study interpret words like "smashed" and "contacted" differently or are less influenced by single-word intensity manipulations. Fourth, issues of statistical power must be considered. The present sample of 60 yields acceptable power to detect medium-sized effects but is likely underpowered to detect small effects. If the true effect of verb intensity on speed estimates is modest in the present testing conditions, the study may not have been sensitive enough to detect it. The observed small effect sizes are consistent with this interpretation. A fifth possibility concerns the timing and specificity of the confidence manipulation. Confidence was altered prior to the critical trial using brief practice videos and feedback. While this approach produced short-term changes in subjective certainty, it may not have produced a durable or domain-specific increase in confidence that would affect processing of the subsequent crash video. Future studies might examine whether confidence manipulations that are more directly coupled to the target stimulus or that last longer produce different patterns. Another notable point is the weak relationship between self-reported accuracy and numeric estimates. The correlation between the two was small, indicating that higher subjective ratings did not reliably correspond to larger or smaller numeric speeds. This suggests that the metacognitive construct measured by brief accuracy ratings may operate independently of the specific quantitative judgment targeted by the leading question. The absence of an effect on the self-reported accuracy ratings following the critical trial suggests that the feedback manipulation's effect was transient and did not produce enduring changes in perceived accuracy for the post-event report. The theoretical implication of these findings is that the relationship between confidence and susceptibility to suggestion is not straightforward. While confidence and memory accuracy can be related, experimentally induced confidence may not operate on the same mechanisms that govern incorporation of misleading post-event information. The current results therefore temper simplistic claims that bolstering a witness's confidence will necessarily protect their testimony from suggestive questioning. Applied implications are clear: interventions aimed solely at increasing a witness's subjective certainty prior to or during interviews may not be effective in reducing the influence of suggestive wording. Instead, procedural safeguards that target the structure of questioning and that minimize suggestive prompts are likely to be more reliable. The study also highlights methodological challenges when attempting replications in online contexts. The failure to replicate the classic effect in the present study does not necessarily disconfirm the underlying phenomenon. Rather, it suggests the effect may be sensitive to context and procedural details. For example, the video resolution, size, and playback stability could influence perception of speed cues. Laboratory settings can standardize these factors, whereas online participants viewed the scene on heterogeneous devices under uncontrolled lighting and distraction conditions. Researchers should therefore be cautious when generalizing laboratory-based findings to online contexts without accounting for environmental variability. The successful manipulation of confidence in an online environment, however, is encouraging for researchers seeking to study metacognition via remote methods. It indicates that brief feedback manipulations can alter subjective states in web-based samples. This opens the door for further remote experimentation that examines other moderators and mechanisms of suggestibility. Several limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. The sample size was modest and concentrated on undergraduate students, limiting generalizability. The between-subjects design reduced the efficiency of the test for detecting interactions relative to a within-subjects approach. The feedback manipulation, while effective for reported confidence, may not have been strong enough or specific enough to influence encoding or retrieval processes relevant to the crash video. The online administration introduced uncontrolled variability in viewing conditions and participant attention. In addition, the study used only two verbs as manipulations. Other forms of leading questions, including those involving presuppositions or more elaborate misinformation, might produce different patterns of interaction with confidence. Future research should address these limitations by increasing sample size, using within-subjects or mixed designs, and varying the nature and timing of confidence manipulations. Studies might also employ more objective measures of encoding quality, such as eye-tracking or reaction time measures, to determine whether confidence manipulations affect perceptual processing at encoding. Another productive line of research would be to examine whether confidence moderates suggestibility in higher-stakes or emotionally charged contexts. It is possible that confidence plays a different role when the observed event is more consequential or when motivational factors are stronger. Multi-method approaches combining subjective measures, behavioral indicators, and physiological correlates of arousal could provide a richer picture of the processes involved. Researchers should also test alternative manipulations of resistance to suggestion, such as training in cognitive strategies or brief mnemonics that purportedly strengthen source monitoring. Comparing these strategies to simple confidence induction could clarify whether protective effects require changes in encoding, retrieval strategies, or metacognitive beliefs. From a legal perspective, it remains important to separate the constructs of confidence and accuracy in witness testimony. Courtroom practice often uses confidence as an indicator of credibility. The present study suggests that artificially boosting a witness's confidence does not necessarily enhance the resistance of their memory to leading questions. This underscores the need for caution in interpreting confident testimony as inherently immune to post-event suggestion. The data also support the continued emphasis on minimizing suggestive interviewing practices to protect the integrity of witness recollections. In sum, the present study contributes to an accumulating empirical picture of how leading questions and confidence interact. The findings show that confidence can be manipulated in an online setting but that such manipulations do not necessarily buffer against verbal suggestion. The failure to replicate the classic verb effect in this study suggests that the phenomenon may be context-sensitive and affected by procedural details. Future replications with larger samples, controlled viewing conditions, and more potent manipulations of both confidence and suggestion are needed to clarify the boundary conditions of these effects. Such work will enhance theoretical understanding of memory malleability and inform best practices in forensic interviewing and beyond.
