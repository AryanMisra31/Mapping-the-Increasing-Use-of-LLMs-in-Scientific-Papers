Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental biological process that supports multiple aspects of physical and mental functioning, and disturbances in sleep are associated with adverse outcomes across cognitive, emotional, and physiological domains. Across populations, the prevalence of self-reported poor sleep quality is substantial, and identifying modifiable behavioral correlates of sleep quality remains a public health priority. One behavioral correlate that has attracted sustained attention is physical exercise, because of its potential to influence sleep via physiological, circadian, and psychological pathways. Exercise may influence sleep by increasing homeostatic sleep pressure through energy expenditure and metabolic changes, by shifting circadian rhythms through light exposure and timing of activity, and by reducing arousal and stress via psychological benefits. Because these mechanisms are plausibly sensitive to aspects of exercise such as intensity, duration, timing, and modality, understanding how exercise intensity relates to subjective sleep quality is important for translating exercise recommendations into sleep health interventions.

A substantial body of research has examined the relationship between exercise and sleep, and while many studies report beneficial effects of exercise participation on various sleep outcomes, findings are not uniform. Meta-analyses and narrative reviews have generally concluded that regular physical activity is associated with improved total sleep time and sleep efficiency in many populations, but effect sizes vary with study design and participant characteristics. Experimental studies that have manipulated exercise regimens over several weeks sometimes report improvements in sleep continuity and subjective sleep quality, whereas acute exercise studies have yielded mixed results depending on the timing and intensity of exercise relative to bedtime. Observational studies that rely on self-report measures often report positive associations between higher habitual activity levels and better subjective sleep, but the direction and size of these associations are influenced by confounding variables such as age, health status, and psychosocial stress.

A specific and theoretically important dimension of physical activity is intensity. Intensity may shape physiological responses such as core body temperature changes, hormonal secretion (including cortisol and growth hormone), sympathetic nervous system activity, and metabolic byproducts that can influence the depth and continuity of sleep. High-intensity exercise might lead to greater homeostatic sleep drive and therefore deeper or longer sleep, but conversely, very intense exercise close to bedtime could increase physiological arousal and delay sleep onset. Medium-intensity exercise could represent an optimal compromise, producing sufficient sleep-promoting physiological changes without excessive arousal. These competing mechanisms have led to inconsistent findings across studies, and the intensity–sleep relationship remains an open empirical question.

Another dimension that could moderate the exercise–sleep relationship is the specific population under study, particularly when considering groups for whom exercise is structured, frequent, and integral to identity and daily routine, such as competitive athletes and dancers. Both athletes and dancers engage in regular, often intense training, but there are notable differences in the nature of physical demands, scheduling, psychosocial stressors, and cultural norms around rest and recovery. Athletes in many sports may focus on strength, power, and endurance training with scheduled recovery periods, whereas dancers often combine athletic demands with aesthetic and performance-related pressures that can influence sleep through distinct pathways. Because sleep is sensitive to both physical and psychological factors, comparing the intensity–sleep association across dancers and athletes provides an opportunity to examine whether modality-specific characteristics moderate this relationship.

Previous studies that have sampled athletes have found variable associations between training intensity and sleep outcomes. Some investigations of endurance athletes documented longer sleep duration following high training loads, whereas other studies reported greater sleep fragmentation and poorer subjective recovery during periods of heavy training. Research on dancers has tended to emphasize injury risk, menstrual disturbances, and psychological stress, with sleep sometimes reported as compromised, particularly in contexts of overtraining or when dance practice is scheduled late in the evening. There is reason to believe that dancers may experience unique stressors that influence sleep independently of training intensity, including performance anxiety, body-image concerns, irregular schedules tied to performances and rehearsals, and the coupling of training with choreographic or artistic demands.

The existing literature also highlights methodological challenges that complicate interpretation of intensity–sleep findings. Many studies rely on self-report for both exercise and sleep, and while validated instruments are available for both domains, measures vary considerably in how intensity, duration, and sleep quality are operationalized. Objective measurement of activity using accelerometry or heart rate monitors offers another avenue, but it captures different constructs (e.g., movement counts, heart-rate zones) that may not correspond neatly to subjective perceptions of intensity. Likewise, objective sleep measurement via polysomnography provides granular physiological indicators but is expensive and often reserved for clinical samples; actigraphy yields objective estimates of sleep/wake patterns in naturalistic settings but does not capture subjective sleep satisfaction. Because subjective sleep quality is an important outcome in its own right and often drives help-seeking and quality-of-life concerns, research using self-rated sleep remains valuable.

Driver and Taylor’s meta-analytic work is often cited as evidence that exercise increases total sleep time, and it provides a useful generalization that physical activity can benefit sleep in aggregated samples. However, the meta-analysis aggregates studies across different populations, types of exercise, and methodological designs, which may obscure important heterogeneity. This heterogeneity could arise from differences in the intensity of exercise used in primary studies, the timing of exercise, participant-level moderators (such as age, sex, and baseline fitness), and psychological factors like stress or mood. Thus, while meta-analytic findings are encouraging for the broad exercise–sleep hypothesis, they leave open questions about how intensity operates within specific populations that habitually engage in intense training, such as dancers and athletes.

The aim of the present study was to investigate the relationship between self-reported exercise intensity and self-rated sleep quality in a sample comprised of dancers and athletes. We focused on self-report measures because subjective sleep quality is psychologically and clinically meaningful, and because self-perceived exercise intensity is central to individuals’ lay understanding of their training load. Our approach allowed us to examine whether self-rated intensity is associated with subjective sleep quality in everyday settings among individuals with varying training modalities. The rationale for sampling both dancers and athletes was twofold: first, both groups are engaged in high levels of physical activity and thus offer an opportunity to observe intensity-related variability within relatively active populations; second, comparing these groups permits an exploration of whether modality-specific factors moderate the intensity–sleep relationship.

We formulated hypotheses guided by prior literature and the competing mechanisms reviewed above. First, we hypothesized that higher self-reported exercise intensity would be associated with better self-rated sleep quality across the combined sample, consistent with the notion that greater intensity increases homeostatic pressure and promotes sleep. Second, given potential differences in psychosocial stressors and scheduling between dancers and athletes, we posited that the strength or direction of the intensity–sleep association might differ by group. Specifically, we considered two competing sub-hypotheses: that athletes might show a stronger positive association due to structured recovery practices, or alternatively, that dancers might show a weaker or null association due to performance-related stressors that could counteract intensity-related sleep benefits.

We anticipated that the investigation would yield insights relevant to both basic research on physiological and psychological mechanisms linking exercise to sleep and to applied contexts such as training planning and sleep hygiene for performers and athletes. By focusing on self-rated measures, the study also speaks directly to subjective sleep complaints that are the primary concern in many real-world settings. At the same time, we recognized that self-report measures have limitations including possible reporting biases and shared-method variance; accordingly, we interpreted findings with caution and considered alternative explanations for observed patterns.

In order to address confounding variables known to influence sleep, our design included collection of demographic information and basic training characteristics, such as weekly training hours, typical time of day of training, and duration of engagement in the activity. These variables allowed us to examine whether any observed association between intensity and sleep was robust to statistical adjustment for plausible covariates. We also explored whether timing of exercise (e.g., predominantly evening versus daytime training) moderated the intensity–sleep relationship, because the temporal proximity of exercise to bedtime is a theoretically important factor.

Our study used a cross-sectional design with volunteer participants drawn from dance and athletic communities. We obtained self-reported measures of exercise intensity and sleep quality through two brief questionnaires administered during the same study session. While longitudinal and experimental designs are preferable for inferring causality, cross-sectional data can provide an initial test of whether the hypothesized relationships are present in naturalistic settings and whether group differences merit further exploration in more intensive designs.

Given the conceptual background and empirical ambiguities, the specific contribution of the present study was to provide a focused comparison of dancers and athletes using parallel self-report measures, to examine the direct association between self-rated exercise intensity and self-rated sleep quality, and to assess whether group membership moderated any association. By reporting null findings as well as positive associations if observed, we aimed to contribute transparently to the literature and to help delineate boundary conditions for the exercise–sleep relationship. The present paper reports the full set of analyses we conducted, describes methodological considerations and limitations, and suggests avenues for future research, including designs that can better disentangle causal pathways.

Methods

Participants were recruited on a volunteer basis from local dance studios, university sports clubs, and community athletic organizations. Recruitment targeted adults aged 18 to 35 years in order to focus on a relatively homogeneous age range and to limit age-related differences in sleep architecture that could confound results. Respondents were screened via a brief online form to confirm that they currently engaged in either dance or athletic training at least twice per week and that they were not currently undergoing treatment for a diagnosed sleep disorder. Individuals who reported a current diagnosis of a sleep disorder or use of medication known to substantially alter sleep patterns were excluded from participation. The final sample comprised 100 participants who met inclusion criteria and provided complete data on the measures described below.

Of the 100 participants, 50 self-identified as dancers and 50 self-identified as athletes. Dancers were drawn from a range of dance genres including contemporary, ballet, jazz, and modern styles, and they ranged from recreationally serious to pre-professional in commitment. Athletes represented a variety of sports including soccer, track and field, swimming, and basketball, and similarly ranged from collegiate-level competitors to recreational club athletes. Demographic characteristics were collected for descriptive purposes and included age, sex, height, weight, weekly hours of training, typical time of day for training sessions (morning/afternoon/evening), and years engaged in the activity. Participants’ mean age across the sample was 22.4 years (SD = 3.6). The sample included 58 female and 42 male participants; sex distribution was similar across dancers and athletes. Mean weekly training hours were 9.4 (SD = 4.2) for the full sample, with dancers averaging 8.7 hours (SD = 3.9) and athletes averaging 10.1 hours (SD = 4.3).

Procedures involved an in-person or supervised online session during which participants completed two questionnaires, along with the demographic/training questionnaire. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation, and the study protocol was approved by an institutional review board. To limit order effects, the order of the two questionnaires (exercise intensity and sleep quality) was counterbalanced across participants. Each questionnaire consisted of items adapted from established instruments and was designed to be completed in approximately 10–15 minutes.

The exercise intensity questionnaire assessed participants’ typical perceived intensity of training over the past four weeks. The measure included items asking participants to rate their overall training intensity on a 1-to-10 scale, where 1 indicated "very low intensity" and 10 indicated "very high intensity." Participants were also asked to provide an estimate of the percentage of training sessions in which they engaged in "high-intensity" work (defined in the questionnaire as sessions that required maximal or near-maximal effort, heavy aerobic or anaerobic exertion, or intense technical/physical exertion lasting more than 20 minutes cumulatively). Additional items captured typical duration of sessions and perceived recovery adequacy. The intensity measure was designed to reflect subjective perceptions because perceived intensity can differ from objective physiological markers and may be more directly relevant to self-rated sleep quality.

The sleep quality questionnaire was modeled on common subjective sleep measures and asked participants to rate their overall sleep quality over the past four weeks on a 1-to-10 scale, where 1 indicated "very poor sleep" and 10 indicated "excellent sleep." The questionnaire also included items asking participants to report typical sleep onset latency, number of awakenings, sleep duration, and daytime sleepiness. These supplementary items were used for descriptive analyses and to contextualize the primary sleep quality rating. The sleep measure emphasized subjective evaluation because our research question focused on self-rated sleep quality rather than objective physiological sleep metrics.

Data screening procedures included checks for missing data and implausible responses. Participants with incomplete primary measures were excluded from analyses, resulting in the final N = 100. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables including means, standard deviations, ranges, and distributional properties. Normality of continuous variables was examined via skewness and kurtosis indices as well as visual inspection of histograms; none of the primary variables displayed extreme deviation from normality that would preclude use of parametric tests.

Primary analyses examined the association between self-reported exercise intensity and self-rated sleep quality. We computed Pearson correlation coefficients for the full sample and separately for dancers and athletes. The decision to use Pearson correlations was based on the continuous nature of the primary variables and the absence of severe non-normality. In addition to zero-order correlations, we conducted linear regression analyses to examine whether the association between intensity and sleep quality remained after statistically controlling for potential confounders, including age, sex, weekly training hours, and typical timing of training (categorized as predominantly morning/afternoon versus predominantly evening). Regression analyses were conducted for the combined sample and separately by group to probe for moderation by participant type.

We also ran exploratory analyses to examine whether the timing of training moderated the intensity–sleep relationship. To test moderation, we included an interaction term between intensity and training-time category in regression models. Because the sample size was moderate, these moderation analyses were treated as exploratory and interpreted cautiously. Additionally, we conducted independent samples t-tests to compare dancers and athletes on primary variables (exercise intensity and sleep quality) and on descriptive sleep items (sleep duration, sleep onset latency, and awakenings).

Statistical significance was evaluated at the conventional alpha level of .05, two-tailed. However, given the exploratory nature of subgroup analyses and the potential for Type II error with moderate sample sizes, we focused on effect sizes and confidence intervals as well as p-values in interpreting results. Correlation coefficients were interpreted using conventional guidelines for small (r ≈ .10), medium (r ≈ .30), and large (r ≈ .50) effects, recognizing that even small effects can be meaningful in real-world contexts.

All analyses were performed using standard statistical software. Data handling procedures ensured participant confidentiality; data were stored in de-identified form and accessible only to study team members. Prior to analysis, we pre-specified primary hypotheses (relationship between intensity and sleep quality in the combined sample and potential group differences) to guard against selective reporting. Secondary and exploratory analyses were clearly labeled as such in our analytic plan and are reported accordingly.

Results

Descriptive statistics for primary variables are presented first to characterize the sample. For the combined sample of 100 participants, the mean self-reported exercise intensity rating was 6.98 on the 1–10 scale (SD = 1.26). Self-rated sleep quality had a mean of 6.63 on the 1–10 scale (SD = 1.32) across the full sample. Inspection of distributions indicated modest variability around the means and no extreme skewness; skewness indices were within acceptable bounds and histograms displayed single peaks without pronounced tails. Dancers’ mean intensity rating was 6.80 (SD = 1.31), whereas athletes’ mean intensity rating was 7.16 (SD = 1.18). The mean sleep quality rating for dancers was 6.70 (SD = 1.29), and for athletes it was 6.56 (SD = 1.36). These group differences appeared small at the descriptive level.

Independent samples t-tests were conducted to compare dancers and athletes on exercise intensity and self-rated sleep quality. The t-test comparing intensity ratings across groups yielded t(98) = 1.50, p = .137, indicating no statistically significant difference between dancers and athletes in self-reported intensity. The observed effect size (Cohen’s d) for the intensity comparison was approximately 0.30, a small-to-medium effect, though it did not reach conventional significance. The t-test comparing sleep quality between dancers and athletes produced t(98) = 0.56, p = .576, which likewise indicated no significant group difference in self-rated sleep quality, and the effect size was small (d ≈ 0.11).

Next, Pearson correlation analyses examined associations between exercise intensity and self-rated sleep quality. For the combined sample, the correlation between intensity and sleep quality was r = .09, p = .365, indicating a small positive association that did not achieve statistical significance. Separate analyses for each group yielded correlations of r = .08 for dancers (p = .564) and r = .10 for athletes (p = .453). Both subgroup correlations were small in magnitude and non-significant. Inspection of scatterplots for each group suggested a lack of a clear linear relationship and no obvious outliers exerting undue influence on the correlation coefficients.

Given potential confounding by variables such as weekly training hours, age, sex, and training timing, we conducted linear regression analyses with self-rated sleep quality as the dependent variable and self-reported exercise intensity as the primary predictor. In the first regression for the combined sample, intensity was entered alone and explained a small and non-significant proportion of variance in sleep quality (R^2 = .008, F(1,98) = 0.83, p = .365). When covariates (age, sex, weekly training hours, and training-time category) were entered in a second model, the addition of intensity did not appreciably change explained variance; the full model accounted for R^2 = .042 of variance in sleep quality, and intensity remained a non-significant predictor (β = .07, p = .432). Among the covariates, weekly training hours showed a small negative association with sleep quality that approached but did not reach significance (β = -.16, p = .095), whereas age and sex were not significant predictors.

Regression analyses conducted separately for dancers and athletes likewise did not reveal significant associations. For dancers, intensity entered alone produced R^2 = .006 (F(1,48) = 0.29, p = .564). For athletes, intensity produced R^2 = .010 (F(1,48) = 0.57, p = .453). When covariates were included, intensity remained non-significant in both group-specific models. In dancers, the full model with covariates accounted for R^2 = .052, and intensity had β = .06 (p = .612). In athletes, the full model accounted for R^2 = .036, and intensity had β = .09 (p = .495). These results indicate that the small zero-order correlations were not attributable to simple confounding by measured demographic or training variables.

We explored whether the time of day of training moderated the intensity–sleep relationship, as late-evening training could theoretically amplify arousal and weaken any beneficial effects of intensity on sleep. Training time was categorized based on participants’ predominant training window (predominantly morning/afternoon versus predominantly evening). An interaction term between intensity and training-time category was included in regression models for the combined sample. The interaction term was not significant (β = -.03, p = .742), suggesting that the intensity–sleep relationship did not differ as a function of whether participants typically trained in the evening. Similar non-significant interactions were observed when analyses were stratified by group.

Because self-rated sleep quality is a global measure, we also examined associations between intensity and more specific sleep items to determine whether any component of subjective sleep was linked to intensity. Correlations between intensity and sleep duration were small and non-significant (r = .05, p = .612), as were correlations between intensity and sleep onset latency (r = -.06, p = .548) and between intensity and number of awakenings (r = .02, p = .843). These results were consistent across dancers and athletes, with no strong or significant associations detected for specific sleep parameters.

Given that effect sizes may be small in behavioral research, we also computed confidence intervals around correlation estimates to gauge the precision of our findings. For the combined sample correlation (r = .09), the 95% confidence interval ranged roughly from -.10 to .27, indicating that the data are compatible with a small positive association but also with negligible or no association. For subgroup correlations, confidence intervals were wider due to smaller sample sizes and encompassed both negative and positive values of small magnitude.

To assess whether particular subgroups might exhibit different patterns, we conducted exploratory analyses examining sex differences in the intensity–sleep association. Correlations between intensity and sleep quality were computed separately for males and females. For males (n = 42), the correlation was r = .12 (p = .440), and for females (n = 58), the correlation was r = .07 (p = .574). These sex-specific correlations were small and non-significant, and interaction tests in regression models did not indicate significant moderation by sex.

Finally, we examined whether self-reported adequacy of recovery moderated the relationship between intensity and sleep quality. Participants had rated their perceived recovery adequacy on a 1–5 scale, and an interaction term between intensity and perceived recovery was included in regression models. The interaction was non-significant (β = .04, p = .689), suggesting that perceived recovery did not condition the intensity–sleep association in our sample.

Across all analyses, the pattern of results was consistent: self-reported exercise intensity displayed small, positive point estimates with self-rated sleep quality but these associations did not reach statistical significance in the combined sample or within the dancer and athlete subgroups. Covariate adjustment and exploratory moderation tests did not reveal significant conditional effects. Descriptive group comparisons indicated minor differences between dancers and athletes in intensity and sleep quality, but these differences were small and not statistically reliable.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between self-reported exercise intensity and self-rated sleep quality in a sample of 100 participants drawn from dancer and athlete populations. Contrary to our initial hypothesis that greater perceived intensity would be associated with better subjective sleep, we observed only small, non-significant positive associations in the combined sample and within each subgroup. Group comparisons revealed no significant differences in mean exercise intensity or in mean sleep quality between dancers and athletes. Additional analyses adjusting for covariates such as age, sex, weekly training hours, and timing of training did not alter the results. Overall, our data do not provide evidence for a robust association between self-rated exercise intensity and self-rated sleep quality among dancers and athletes in this sample.

These findings can be interpreted in several ways, and they contribute to the larger literature by highlighting the potential limits of a simple intensity–sleep linkage in naturally occurring training contexts. One interpretation is that self-perceived intensity alone may be an insufficient predictor of subjective sleep quality when considered in isolation. Sleep is influenced by a constellation of factors including cumulative training load, variability in schedule, psychological stress, nutrition, recovery behaviors, and individual differences in sleep need and chronotype. In populations such as dancers and athletes, who frequently experience complex and interrelated demands, the specific role of intensity may be attenuated by these other factors. For instance, two individuals reporting similar perceived intensity could differ substantially in total weekly load, recovery practices, or stress levels, and these differences might be more consequential for sleep than intensity per se.

Another interpretation is that the relationship between intensity and sleep may be non-linear or contingent on timing. While moderate to high overall intensity might enhance sleep for some individuals by increasing sleep pressure, very high intensity, especially when coupled with inadequate recovery, may produce overreaching or overtraining syndromes that impair sleep. Our cross-sectional design and self-report measures limit our ability to evaluate non-linear dynamics or temporal fluctuations associated with training cycles. It is possible that during periods of unusual overload (e.g., intensive training blocks or competition periods), the intensity–sleep relationship takes on different characteristics that were not captured in our four-week retrospective window.

Methodological features of our study are important to consider in interpreting null findings. We relied on self-report measures for both intensity and sleep quality. While subjective measures are clinically and psychologically meaningful, they are subject to biases and may not align perfectly with objective physiological indicators. Perceived intensity is influenced not only by the absolute physiological load but also by psychological factors such as motivation and affect, whereas subjective sleep quality reflects global evaluations that incorporate daytime functioning and mood. These shared-method issues can produce either inflated associations (due to shared method variance) or attenuated associations (if measurement error is substantial). Our lack of significant associations suggests that measurement error or mismatch between subjective constructs might have reduced the ability to detect true underlying physiological relationships.

The sampling approach also shapes interpretation. Our participants were volunteers from a specific age range (18–35) and self-selected into the study, which may constrain generalizability. Within the sample, dancers and athletes represented heterogeneous subtypes of activity, and pooling across diverse sports and dance genres may have obscured modality-specific relationships. For example, endurance athletes and power athletes differ in training structure and physiological demands, and dancers vary widely in technical and aesthetic demands; these distinctions could yield different intensity–sleep dynamics that we could not fully explore given sample size constraints.

It is also possible that the intensity–sleep relationship is more apparent when intensity is operationalized using objective physiological markers such as heart rate zones, lactate thresholds, or accelerometer-derived physical activity counts. Objective measures capture aspects of exertion that subjective ratings may miss, particularly in trained individuals who adapt to high workloads and may rate intense activity as routine. Future studies that combine objective monitoring of physiological load with subjective reports would be better positioned to disentangle perceptual versus physiological contributors to sleep outcomes.

Despite the null primary findings, some aspects of the data warrant emphasis. The direction of the small point estimates—positive correlations between intensity and sleep quality—aligns with meta-analytic findings that exercise is often associated with increased total sleep time and better subjective sleep in aggregated samples. Thus, absence of significance in our study should not be taken as evidence that intensity has no relationship to sleep in all contexts, but rather that any such relationship may be modest and contingent on other factors in active populations like dancers and athletes.

From a practical perspective, our findings suggest caution in assuming that simply encouraging higher-intensity training will unambiguously improve subjective sleep among athletes and dancers. For coaches, choreographers, and practitioners, attention to broader load management and recovery practices may be more fruitful for supporting sleep than focusing narrowly on intensity. Recovery interventions, consistent scheduling, sleep hygiene education, and monitoring of cumulative load may be essential components of strategies aimed at optimizing sleep in these groups.

Several limitations of the present study highlight directions for future research. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences and limits the ability to detect dynamic relationships that unfold across training cycles. Longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to assess how changes in intensity affect sleep over time and whether there are delayed or cumulative effects. Second, the reliance on self-report measures limits precision; future work should incorporate objective measures of activity (e.g., heart rate, accelerometry) and sleep (e.g., actigraphy, polysomnography) to triangulate subjective reports. Third, our sample size, while adequate for detecting medium-sized effects, may have limited power to detect small but meaningful associations; larger samples and meta-analytic approaches could clarify the magnitude and consistency of intensity–sleep links.

Moreover, future studies would benefit from more fine-grained assessment of training variables, including specific intensity distributions within sessions, cumulative weekly and monthly loads, frequency of high-intensity interval sessions, and recovery modalities (e.g., sleep, nutrition, massage). Incorporating psychological variables such as perceived stress, anxiety, and mood would also be important because these factors could mediate or moderate the exercise–sleep relationship. For dancers in particular, assessment of performance-related stressors, body image concerns, and schedule irregularities associated with rehearsals and performances may be crucial for understanding sleep patterns.

Another productive direction is to examine individual differences that might buffer or amplify intensity effects on sleep. For instance, chronotype could moderate whether evening training interferes with sleep, and genetic or physiological traits could influence recovery capacity. Personalized approaches that consider individual recovery needs and scheduling preferences could inform tailored training plans that optimize both performance and sleep.

Experimental studies that manipulate intensity and timing within the same individuals, ideally using randomized crossover designs, would provide stronger evidence regarding causality and boundary conditions. For example, experiments that compare moderate-intensity versus high-intensity training sessions matched for duration and energy expenditure, with standardized recovery conditions and objective sleep monitoring, would clarify immediate and next-night effects of intensity. Similarly, interventions that manipulate training load over several weeks while monitoring sleep and biomarkers of stress and recovery could illuminate cumulative effects and potential maladaptive responses.

Finally, given that sleep and exercise interact reciprocally—poor sleep impairs subsequent training performance and increases injury risk—research that models bidirectional relationships using intensive longitudinal methods (e.g., daily diaries, ecological momentary assessment, wearable monitoring) would capture the feedback loops that may maintain or exacerbate sleep–training dynamics. Understanding these bidirectional processes is particularly relevant for practitioners seeking to balance training demands with recovery and for individuals aiming to maintain both performance and well-being.

In sum, the present study did not find significant associations between self-reported exercise intensity and self-rated sleep quality in a sample of dancers and athletes. The results suggest that, within these active populations, perceived intensity alone may not be a reliable predictor of subjective sleep. The findings point to the need for more comprehensive and temporally sensitive research designs that incorporate objective measurement and consider the broader constellation of factors impacting sleep. By advancing such research, the field can better inform evidence-based recommendations for optimizing sleep in populations that routinely engage in high levels of physical activity.
