Introduction

Human social life depends heavily on the capacity of individuals to behave in ways that benefit others or the collective group. Cooperation is a central topic in psychology because it underpins institutions, economic exchange, and interpersonal trust. A longstanding debate concerns the cognitive origins of cooperative behavior and whether such behavior is the product of deliberate calculation or more automatic processes. Dual-process models of cognition offer a useful framework for this debate by distinguishing between fast, intuitive processes and slower, deliberative processes. Within this framework, some scholars have argued that deliberation promotes cooperation because reason allows people to overcome short-term selfish impulses in favor of long-term collective benefits. Conversely, an alternative account proposes that intuition itself is cooperative, and that reflection can lead people to act more selfishly by overriding intuitive pro-social inclinations. A prominent statement of this latter view was advanced by Rand, Greene and Nowak, who reported that time pressure — which favors intuitive responses — increased cooperative behavior relative to time delay conditions that encourage reflection. This finding has been widely discussed because it suggests that humans may be predisposed toward cooperation at an intuitive level. Theoretical accounts built on this observation emphasize that cooperation may be a social heuristic acquired through daily life and that this heuristic is applied automatically in one-shot anonymous interactions. From this perspective, intuitive cooperation is an adaptive fit to environments where short-term interactions frequently require quick trust and reciprocity. However, the broader literature contains findings that complicate a simple interpretation of intuition as universally pro-social. Several studies indicate that intuition can also be self-interested, particularly when the decision-maker’s immediate environment or prior experiences encourage selfish responses. The interplay between contextual cues, personal history, and the mode of cognition can thus determine whether intuition yields pro-social or self-interested outcomes. A related issue concerns individual differences and group-level influences on cooperative intuition. For example, participation in organized group activities such as team sports has been linked to socialization into norms of fairness, coordination, and mutual support. By contrast, individuals engaged primarily in solitary activities might have different patterns of social preferences shaped by less frequent need for coordinated group action. If sport participation fosters cooperative norms, one might expect team-sport players to show stronger intuitive cooperation than individual-sport players or non-sport participants. Conversely, if intuition is shaped predominantly by domain-general social heuristics, participation in sport per se might not moderate the relationship between intuitive processing and cooperation. Previous research has examined the socializing effects of sport participation in relation to altruism, prosocial behavior, and moral decision-making. These lines of work often find that team-based athletic involvement is associated with greater endorsement of group-oriented values, but the evidence is mixed and may depend on the type of sport, level of competition, and the social context of training and competition. Moreover, such studies rarely manipulate cognitive processing mode directly, which complicates inferences about whether any observed differences are a consequence of intuitive versus deliberative processing. The present study was designed to address these gaps by testing two related questions. First, we sought to replicate the central claim that intuitive processing favors cooperation by using a time pressure versus time delay manipulation in a simple interactive economic game followed by a contribution decision. Second, we examined whether the type of sport participation — categorized as team sport, individual sport, or non-sport — moderated the effect of cognitive processing mode on cooperative behavior. We reasoned that if team-sport experience cultivates cooperative heuristics, then individuals who primarily play team sports should contribute more under time pressure relative to time delay, and perhaps more so than individuals in other groups. Alternatively, if intuitive cooperation reflects a more universal social heuristic that is not meaningfully shaped by the type of recreational activity, then sport category should not moderate the time pressure effect. A further rationale for this design derives from the contrast between intuitive and deliberative accounts of cooperation. Under an intuition-is-cooperative account, we expected that contributions would be higher under time pressure overall. Under a deliberation-promotes-cooperation account, the opposite pattern would be anticipated. Existing literature also suggests that contextual and procedural factors can influence the strength of the time pressure effect. For instance, the salience of one’s group membership, recent priming of self-interest, or the perceived anonymity of the decision can all modify intuitive tendencies. Therefore, it was important in the present study to control for task framing and to measure compliance with timing instructions to ensure that any observed effects could reasonably be attributed to differences in processing mode. We thus implemented a clear time constraint manipulation and included manipulation checks that recorded response latencies. We also collected self-report measures of perceived anonymity and intentions to ensure participants experienced the task as a one-shot, anonymous interaction. In addition, we measured basic demographic variables and experience level in sport to examine potential covariates and to ensure that any observed differences between sport categories were not due to confounding factors. The contribution decision was operationalized as a monetary donation to a common pool after an interactive game. This operationalization allowed us to capture a behavioral index of cooperative intent that has clear parallels with public goods and social dilemma tasks used in prior research. Importantly, the interactive element preceding the contribution decision was designed to be brief and standardized across participants so that differences in contributions could be plausibly linked to variations in cognitive processing and prior social experiences rather than to differences in the interaction itself. The study therefore provides a direct test of whether intuitive processing leads to greater cooperation and whether sport participation categories moderate this effect. Given the theoretical stakes and mixed findings in the literature, a replication and extension with attention to sport experience is warranted. Our primary hypotheses were specified as follows. Hypothesis 1 predicted a main effect of timing such that participants in the time pressure condition would make larger contributions to the common pool than participants in the time delay condition. Hypothesis 2 predicted a main effect of sport type such that team-sport participants would contribute more than individual-sport participants and non-sport participants. Hypothesis 3 predicted an interaction between time condition and sport type such that the time pressure advantage for contributions would be larger for team-sport participants than for the other groups. We also planned exploratory analyses to examine whether demographic variables, perceived anonymity, or response latency covaried with contributions. These secondary analyses were intended to help interpret the primary results and to identify potential boundary conditions for the time pressure effect. Overall, the study aims to clarify the cognitive basis of cooperative behavior and to explore whether recreational socialization through sport shapes the intuitive propensity to cooperate. By combining a time-based manipulation with an examination of sport participation, the project advances understanding of both domain-general and experience-dependent determinants of prosocial decision-making. The following sections describe the method, results, and interpretation of the study in detail.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and from community advertising aimed at local sports clubs. In total, 180 participants completed the study. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 35 years. The final sample included both male and female participants, with representation across undergraduate majors and local athletic clubs. Participants were pre-screened to ensure they could be categorized into one of three sport groups based on their primary recreational activity over the past twelve months. Participants were asked whether they primarily engaged in team sports, primarily engaged in individual sports, or did not engage in organized sport activities; these self-reports were used to assign sport-type conditions. Team sport participants included individuals who reported regular participation in sports such as football, basketball, rugby, or other sports requiring coordination with teammates. Individual sport participants included those who primarily engaged in sports such as running, swimming, tennis played mostly individually, or weightlifting. Non-sport participants reported no regular involvement in organized or club-based sports during the prior year. Participants received course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time and could earn additional money based on task performance. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee and participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The experimental design was a 3 (sport type: team, individual, non-sport) by 2 (time condition: time pressure, time delay) between-subjects factorial design. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the two time conditions within their sport category. Randomization was achieved using a computerized sequence. The primary dependent variable was the amount of money participants chose to contribute to a common pool following a brief interactive game. The contribution decision was framed as a one-shot, anonymous allocation to a group fund that would benefit anonymous others. The interactive game component was designed to be minimal and standardized across participants; it consisted of a computer-simulated partner choice task that mirrored choices used in previous economic games. The interactive element served primarily to operationalize the social context without creating extended strategic engagement. All instructions emphasized that the subsequent contribution decision would be anonymous and that partners in the interactive element would not be able to identify the participant. The time pressure manipulation was implemented during the contribution decision phase. Participants in the time pressure condition were instructed to make their contribution quickly and were given a 7-second countdown visible on the screen. They were told that if they did not respond within the allotted time the system would select a default contribution equal to half of their endowment. Participants in the time delay condition were instructed to think carefully and to wait at least 15 seconds before making their decision; a timer on the screen indicated the minimum wait time and the decision button was activated only after the delay elapsed. These timing parameters were chosen to create a clear contrast between intuitive (rapid) and deliberative (delayed) responding while remaining practical in a laboratory setting. Response latencies were recorded for all contribution decisions. Compliance with timing instructions was checked by examining response times and by recording instances of missed deadlines or premature responses. Participants who failed to comply with the basic timing instructions — for example, those in the time pressure condition who failed to respond within the allotted time and thereby triggered the default choice more than once in pilot trials — were noted and included in sensitivity analyses. Prior to the main experiment, a short pilot test with 20 participants confirmed that the timing manipulation produced substantially different average decision latencies. The endowment for the contribution decision was set at 10 monetary units, with participants free to contribute any integer amount between 0 and 10. Monetary units were translated into real currency at a fixed exchange rate and payments were made at the end of the session. The contribution decision was accompanied by standard comprehension questions to ensure that participants understood the payoff consequences. Participants also completed a post-task questionnaire that assessed perceived anonymity, perceived group identity with the simulated partner, and a brief measure of social value orientation. Demographic information and details of sport involvement, including average hours per week and years of participation, were collected for exploratory analyses. The study planned to analyze data using analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test the main effects of time condition and sport type and their interaction. Planned comparisons included pairwise contrasts between sport groups and simple effects of time condition within each sport group. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared for ANOVA and Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons. Prior to conducting inferential tests, data were screened for normality, outliers, and missing values. No participants were excluded for failing comprehension checks, but a small number of participants were excluded in sensitivity analyses for failing to comply with the timing manipulation as recorded by response latency. Statistical analyses were performed using standard statistical software and two-tailed alpha of 0.05 was used as the threshold for significance. Power considerations guided the sample size. Based on effect sizes reported in earlier studies of time pressure effects on cooperation and assuming a modest effect size for the sport-type moderator, a target sample of approximately 180 participants was selected to provide adequate power to detect medium-sized main effects and interactions. The consent form informed participants that the study concerned social decision-making and that some decisions would affect monetary payments. To preserve the clarity of the time manipulation, debriefing occurred after all measures were completed and participants were informed about the rationale for the timing conditions. The debriefing included an explanation of the anonymous nature of the task and an option for participants to withdraw their data if they were uncomfortable. Data handling procedures ensured confidentiality and data were stored on secure servers in accordance with institutional policies. The primary analysis plan was preregistered prior to data collection. The preregistration specified the main hypotheses, the primary dependent measure, the timing procedure, and the analytic approach including planned contrasts and the handling of timing noncompliance. Exploratory analyses examining relationships between demographic variables, sport experience metrics, and contributions were also described in the preregistration. This methodological approach was intended to provide a transparent and replicable test of the hypotheses concerning intuitive cooperation and the moderating role of sport participation.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the full sample are presented first. The final sample consisted of 180 participants with 60 participants in each sport category. Participants were approximately evenly distributed across time conditions within sport categories due to random assignment. The mean contribution across the entire sample was 5.94 monetary units with a standard deviation of 2.30. Response latencies differed markedly by condition as intended, with the mean decision time in the time pressure condition equal to 5.8 seconds and the mean decision time in the time delay condition equal to 18.6 seconds. This confirmed that the timing manipulation produced substantial differences in processing time. Compliance checks revealed that 6 participants failed to adhere to the timing instructions in a way that materially undermined the manipulation; these participants were retained in the primary intent-to-treat analyses but were removed in sensitivity analyses described below. The distribution of contributions within each cell was approximately normal with no extreme skew that would invalidate parametric procedures. The primary analysis was a two-way ANOVA with sport type and time condition as between-subject factors and contribution as the dependent variable. The ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of time condition. Participants in the time pressure condition contributed more on average than participants in the time delay condition. Specifically, the mean contribution in the time pressure condition was 6.47 units with a standard deviation of 2.13. The mean contribution in the time delay condition was 5.41 units with a standard deviation of 2.34. The main effect of time condition was statistically significant with F(1,174) = 10.27, p = .0016, and partial eta squared = .056, indicating a small-to-moderate effect size. Next, the ANOVA tested for a main effect of sport type. The main effect of sport type on contribution was not statistically significant. The means by sport type were as follows: team sport participants had a mean contribution of 6.09 units (SD = 2.28), individual sport participants had a mean of 5.82 units (SD = 2.27), and non-sport participants had a mean of 5.92 units (SD = 2.37). The F-test for sport type yielded F(2,174) = 0.42, p = .66, partial eta squared = .005. This indicates that there was no reliable evidence that sport category by itself predicted differences in contribution levels. The interaction between time condition and sport type was also non-significant. The interaction term produced F(2,174) = 0.33, p = .72, partial eta squared = .004. Examination of the cell means indicated that in each sport category the pattern of higher contributions under time pressure compared to time delay was present, but the magnitude of the difference varied slightly across the three groups. For team sport participants, the mean contributions were 6.39 (time pressure) versus 5.79 (time delay). For individual sport participants, the means were 6.57 (time pressure) versus 5.07 (time delay). For non-sport participants, the means were 6.45 (time pressure) versus 5.39 (time delay). Despite these directional differences, the interaction test indicated that these variations in magnitude did not meet conventional thresholds for statistical significance. Planned pairwise comparisons were conducted to further characterize the time condition effect. Collapsing across sport types, a two-sample t-test comparing time pressure and time delay confirmed the ANOVA result. The t-test produced t(178) = 3.21, p = .0016, Cohen’s d = 0.48, indicating a medium effect size favoring higher contributions under time pressure. Within-group comparisons were examined as exploratory tests. For team sport participants, the time pressure versus time delay comparison yielded t(58) = 1.12, p = .27, Cohen’s d = 0.29. For individual sport participants, the comparison yielded t(58) = 1.98, p = .053, Cohen’s d = 0.51, which approached conventional significance thresholds. For non-sport participants, the within-group comparison yielded t(58) = 1.59, p = .12, Cohen’s d = 0.41. These within-group analyses suggest the time pressure advantage was directionally consistent across sport groups but only reached marginal significance in the individual sport group. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the main effect of time condition. First, we removed the six participants who clearly violated timing instructions and reran the ANOVA. The pattern of results was unchanged and the main effect of time condition remained significant with F(1,168) = 9.84, p = .002, partial eta squared = .055. Second, we included decision latency as a covariate in an ANCOVA to examine whether response time itself explained variance in contributions beyond the categorical manipulation. Decision latency was inversely related to contribution, with longer latencies predicting lower contributions across the sample (beta = -.29, p < .001). When latency was entered as a covariate, the categorical time condition remained a significant predictor but with reduced magnitude, suggesting that the manipulation affected contributions partly through its effect on response speed. Third, demographic variables including age and gender were entered as covariates. Neither age nor gender significantly predicted contributions, and their inclusion did not alter the main effect of time condition. Exploratory analyses examined whether measures of perceived anonymity or social value orientation covaried with contributions. Perceived anonymity did not differ across time conditions and did not predict contributions significantly. A brief social value orientation index was modestly correlated with contributions (r = .21, p = .005), indicating that individuals with more pro-social orientations tended to contribute more. However, the time condition effect remained significant after controlling for social value orientation. We also examined the relationship between self-reported hours of sport engagement and contributions within the sport groups. Hours of participation did not predict contributions reliably in any group. We tested for a dose-response relationship between years of participation and contribution and found no linear association. These analyses provide no evidence that the intensity or duration of sport involvement across the prior year substantially influenced the time pressure effect. To provide additional context, frequency distributions of contributions by condition are described. In the time pressure condition, contributions were more likely to fall at the higher end of the scale, with 38% of participants contributing 8 or more units. In the time delay condition, only 22% contributed 8 or more units. Conversely, low contributions of 2 units or less were observed in 11% of the time pressure condition but in 20% of the time delay condition. These distributional differences mirror the mean differences and support the conclusion that time pressure encourages higher contributions overall. Finally, we conducted Bayesian analyses as a complementary approach to quantify evidence for the null interaction. The Bayes factor for the interaction term favored the null over the alternative, providing moderate support for the absence of an interaction between time condition and sport type. In sum, the results indicate a reliable main effect of time condition consistent with the intuition-is-cooperative hypothesis, while offering no evidence that sport type modifies this effect.

Discussion

The present study examined whether intuitive processing supports cooperative behavior and whether general patterns of recreational sport participation moderate this effect. We manipulated cognitive processing via a time pressure versus time delay paradigm and categorized participants by self-reported primary sport activity. The key finding was that participants under time pressure contributed more to a common pool than participants who were encouraged to deliberate. This pattern replicated central aspects of prior work proposing that intuition favors cooperation. At the same time, sport type did not exert a main effect on contributions and did not interact with the timing manipulation. Thus, the intuitive cooperation effect appeared robust across individuals who primarily play team sports, individual sports, or who do not play sports. These findings contribute to the literature in several important ways. First, they provide further empirical support for the dual-process account in which rapid, automatic processes can favor pro-social outcomes in anonymous social decisions. The main effect of time condition replicates earlier findings that time pressure can increase cooperative contributions in one-shot games. Our use of an interactive element followed by a contribution decision extends previous paradigms by embedding the contribution in a brief social context while preserving anonymity, showing that even minimal social cues do not eliminate the tendency toward intuitive cooperation. Second, the lack of an effect of sport type challenges the notion that participation in team sports produces readily observable differences in intuitive cooperative tendencies in this type of laboratory task. Although team sports are often thought to socialize individuals into group-oriented norms, these processes may not translate into measurable shifts in intuitive responses during anonymous one-shot allocations. Alternative explanations for the null sport effect are plausible and deserve careful consideration. One possibility is that the socialization effects of sport occur primarily in contexts where group identity and ongoing interaction are salient. In our experiment the contribution decision was explicitly framed as anonymous and one-shot, which may have attenuated any group-based tendencies. Team-sport participants may show heightened cooperation in contexts that cue team identity or involve repeated interaction with known teammates, but not in anonymous laboratory tasks. A second possibility is that the majority of participants were recreational rather than elite athletes, and the intensity or type of socialization experienced by recreational athletes may differ from that of highly committed or professional players. Our exploratory analyses did not find that hours per week or years of participation predicted contributions, but more granular measurement of the social dynamics within training environments may be necessary to capture the hypothesized socialization effects. Third, it is possible that any cooperative tendencies imparted by sport participation are domain-specific, applying to behaviors within the sport milieu rather than generalizing to abstract economic allocations. If so, sport-related socialization may shape situational cooperation but not cross-context intuitive heuristics. The observed effect of time pressure on contributions supports the interpretation that intuition often guides pro-social choices, but it does not identify the origin of the cooperative heuristic. The social heuristics hypothesis posits that intuitive cooperation arises from learned strategies that are beneficial in everyday social interactions. These strategies can become automatic and are applied in novel situations. Our data are consistent with this account in that time pressure increased contributions regardless of sport history, implying the presence of a general cooperative heuristic across the sample. However, the alternative possibility that intuitively pro-social responses are innate or culturally widespread rather than learned through specific experiences cannot be ruled out by the present design. Several methodological considerations temper strong causal claims. First, the timing manipulation, while effective, is an indirect proxy for cognitive mode. Although shorter latencies are typically associated with more automatic processing, it remains possible that some participants in the time pressure condition engaged in rapid deliberation or relied on simple rule-based reasoning. We mitigated this concern by recording response latencies and conducting sensitivity analyses that showed latency partially mediated the timing effect, but future studies employing process-tracing methods or neurocognitive measures would strengthen causal inference. Second, sport-type classification relied on self-report and a relatively coarse categorization. This approach facilitated sample recruitment and ecological validity but may have missed important within-category heterogeneity. For instance, certain team sports emphasize individual performance within a team structure, and some individual sports involve collaborative training settings. More fine-grained assessments of the social climate of sport participation, coaching styles, and the extent of cooperative practice would improve measurement sensitivity. Third, the experimental setting and population limit generalizability. The sample was drawn largely from university-affiliated participants and local recreational athletes, which may not reflect broader demographics or cultures where sport participation and cooperative norms differ. Cross-cultural replication would be valuable to determine whether societal norms moderate intuitive cooperation. Fourth, while the contribution decision was framed as anonymous and one-shot to mirror prior research, real-world cooperative situations often involve reputational stakes and repeated interactions. Future work should examine whether the time pressure effect persists when reputational consequences are salient or when interactions are iterative. The absence of a significant interaction between sport type and timing suggests that the basic cognitive mechanism linking intuition and cooperation is not strongly conditioned by generalized sport involvement in the way we measured it. This finding prompts several theoretical reflections. It suggests that domain-general social heuristics may be widespread in contemporary populations and that sport participation, at least at recreational levels, does not substantially alter the deployment of these heuristics in anonymous contexts. Alternatively, if sport participation does shape cooperative behavior, the boundary conditions for detecting such effects in laboratory tasks require clarification. For example, sport-related cooperative tendencies may be activated only when cues of shared identity or mutual dependence are present. The pattern of results also has implications for interventions aimed at promoting cooperation. If intuition often supports cooperative behavior, then designing environments that make pro-social norms salient and reduce excessive deliberation could enhance collective outcomes. This is not to suggest that reflection is uniformly detrimental; deliberation may promote cooperation in situations where immediate behavior would undermine long-term interests or where strategic thinking is required. Therefore, the practical implication is to recognize the context-dependence of both intuitive and deliberative processing in shaping social behavior. There are several concrete directions for future research. One priority is to examine the neural and physiological correlates of time pressure-induced cooperation to uncover the mechanisms of intuitive prosociality. Techniques such as EEG or fMRI could reveal whether time pressure engages distinct affective or rule-based systems associated with social valuation. Another avenue is to manipulate the social identity salience in conjunction with sport participation to test whether team-sport players exhibit stronger intuitive cooperation when their team identity is cued. Longitudinal designs that track individuals as they enter or leave sport participation could shed light on causal processes of socialization. Additionally, experimental manipulations that simulate repeated interaction or reputation-building would test the generalizability of the time pressure effect to more ecologically valid social dilemmas. It would also be informative to explore individual differences in cognitive style and how they interact with context. For instance, people high in trait impulsivity might be more influenced by time pressure, and those with strong deliberative tendencies might resist the intuitive pull. Our exploratory finding that social value orientation correlated with contributions suggests that dispositional factors shape cooperative decisions, and integrating such measures could clarify heterogeneity in responses. Finally, cross-cultural studies that examine populations with different social norms and sport cultures would extend understanding of the universality of intuitive cooperation. In conclusion, the present study supports the view that rapid, intuitive processing can favor cooperative monetary decisions in an anonymous, one-shot setting. This effect held across participants categorized by their dominant recreational activity, suggesting that a general cooperative heuristic may operate widely. However, the absence of sport-type effects highlights the need to refine conceptualizations of how specific social experiences shape intuitive tendencies. The findings underscore the complexity of the relationship between cognition and prosocial behavior and point to rich opportunities for future research integrating cognitive, social, and developmental perspectives.
