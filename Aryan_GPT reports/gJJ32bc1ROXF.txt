Introduction

Attachment theory provides a foundational framework for understanding how close relationships shape individuals' expectations, emotions, and behaviors across the lifespan. The theory proposes that early interactions with primary caregivers give rise to internal working models that guide social cognition and interpersonal behavior. These internal working models are commonly characterized along dimensions that reflect anxiety about abandonment and avoidance of intimacy, and they contribute to stable individual differences referred to as attachment styles. Attachment styles have been linked to a wide array of social, emotional, and behavioral outcomes, including relationship satisfaction, emotion regulation strategies, and responses to social threat. Conformity, defined broadly as changes in beliefs, attitudes, or behaviors in response to real or imagined group pressure, is likewise an important social phenomenon that has been extensively studied in social psychology. Conformity can serve adaptive social functions by promoting coordination and cohesion, but it can also lead to maladaptive outcomes when it results in risky or unethical behavior. Despite the conceptual relevance of attachment processes to social influence, surprisingly little direct research has examined the relationship between attachment styles and conformity tendencies. The present study sought to address this gap by empirically testing whether individual differences in attachment anxiety and avoidance predict concern for appropriateness, a commonly operationalized facet of conformity.

There are several conceptual reasons to anticipate a relationship between attachment insecurity and conformity. Individuals high in attachment anxiety tend to be preoccupied with fears of abandonment and rejection and are hypervigilant to social cues that might signal relational threat. This hyperactivation of the attachment system typically leads to increased motivation to obtain reassurance and closeness from others. Given that conformity often operates as a social signal of affiliation and a means of gaining acceptance, anxiously attached individuals may be especially prone to conforming in order to reduce perceived risk of rejection and to secure belongingness. Conversely, individuals high in attachment avoidance tend to suppress attachment needs, value autonomy, and de-emphasize closeness. Their tendencies toward emotional distance and independence could plausibly reduce susceptibility to social influence, making them less likely to conform to group norms or to prioritize appropriateness in the same way anxiously attached individuals might. These divergent theoretical predictions for anxiety and avoidance provide a clear rationale for examining the unique associations of each attachment dimension with measures of conformity.

Empirical work on related constructs provides indirect support for a link between attachment and conformity. For example, research has shown that attachment anxiety is associated with greater social sensitivity, heightened responsiveness to rejection cues, and stronger motivations to maintain relationships, all of which are processes implicated in conforming behavior. Other studies have demonstrated that anxious attachment correlates with greater approval-seeking and interpersonal dependency, variables that logically map onto concern for social appropriateness and conformity. Avoidant attachment has been associated with lower levels of prosocial regulation and decreased responsiveness to others' expectations, which could entail lower conformity. At the same time, avoidant individuals sometimes conform behaviorally while maintaining psychological independence, which complicates straightforward predictions and suggests that relationships between avoidant attachment and conformity may be weak or context-dependent.

Beyond attachment theory, a complementary line of inquiry considers the personality correlates shared by attachment styles and conformity. Conformity has been associated with personality traits such as high agreeableness and high neuroticism, and attachment anxiety tends to be correlated with higher neuroticism and higher agreeableness in some studies. Attachment avoidance has been linked to lower agreeableness and lower extraversion in many samples. These patterns imply that part of the association between attachment and conformity may be mediated or confounded by broader personality traits, although the direct pathways from attachment-derived motivations to conforming behavior remain conceptually plausible and worthy of investigation.

Despite these theoretical linkages, a search of the literature reveals little direct empirical testing of the hypothesis that attachment anxiety and avoidance predict conformity. Studies that examine social influence typically focus on situational manipulations or dispositional variables such as social dominance orientation, authoritarianism, or empathy. When attachment is included, it is often in the context of romantic relationships or caregiving and not as an antecedent of behavioral compliance with group norms. This paucity of direct evidence motivates the current study and highlights its potential contribution to both attachment theory and social influence research.

The present research aimed to examine whether individual differences in attachment anxiety and avoidance are associated with reported concern for appropriateness, a measure that captures the tendency to conform to social expectations and to value behaving in ways that are perceived as acceptable by others. Concern for appropriateness was selected because it reflects an internalized motivation to adhere to social norms and to avoid disapproval, which aligns conceptually with the interpersonal concerns characteristic of attachment anxiety. The use of a self-report measure allowed for efficient assessment in a relatively large convenience sample and enabled us to capture participants' subjective tendencies to prioritize appropriateness in social contexts.

Another aim of the study was to explore potential gender differences in the relationship between attachment dimensions and conformity. Gender differences in attachment and conformity have been reported in some prior work, with women often scoring higher on measures of communal orientation and concern for social harmony, and men sometimes endorsing greater independence and assertiveness. Because socialization processes and gendered expectations may differentially shape how attachment-related motivations translate into overt conforming tendencies, it was important to examine whether any observed associations between attachment and conformity differed across men and women.

To address these aims, a correlational design was implemented with a convenience sample of 100 participants who completed established self-report measures of attachment and concern for appropriateness. Attachment was measured using a revised version of the Experiences in Close Relationships inventory, which yields separate scores for attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance. Concern for appropriateness was measured using a validated scale designed to assess individuals’ likelihood of conforming out of a desire to appear appropriate and to fit social expectations. The correlational approach allowed for testing direct associations and the strength of the relationships between attachment dimensions and conformity.

Given the theoretical background, we hypothesized that attachment anxiety would be positively associated with concern for appropriateness, reflecting anxiously attached individuals' heightened sensitivity to rejection and desire for acceptance. We anticipated a smaller or non-significant negative association between attachment avoidance and concern for appropriateness, consistent with avoidant individuals’ de-emphasizing of closeness and social dependency. We also anticipated that gender may moderate these relationships, with the anxiety-conformity link potentially being stronger among women due to socialization that places a greater emphasis on relational attunement and communal behavior for females.

The study also aimed to consider potential alternative explanations and to provide descriptive data regarding internal consistency reliability for the measures used in the sample. Because both attachment and conformity were assessed via self-report at a single time point, issues such as common-method variance and correlational limitations are recognized, and the Discussion will attend to these limitations and potential strategies for addressing them in future work.

This investigation thus directly tests a theoretically grounded but underexplored set of predictions about the links between attachment styles and conformity. By examining both attachment anxiety and avoidance in relation to concern for appropriateness, and by exploring gender differences, the study seeks to clarify the pattern of associations and to inform future experimental and longitudinal designs. The findings have potential implications for understanding why some individuals are more susceptible to social influence than others and for identifying targets for interventions aimed at reducing harmful conformity, particularly among those who are most motivated to secure social acceptance.

More broadly, evidence that attachment dimensions predict conformity tendencies would extend attachment theory by illuminating how attachment-derived motivations for closeness and safety translate into compliance with social expectations beyond the domain of close relationships. It would also contribute to the social psychological literature on conformity by identifying dispositional antecedents that may interact with situational factors to produce varying levels of conformity across individuals. Establishing such links could further motivate applied research aimed at reducing problematic conformity in domains like substance use, risky driving, and unethical decision-making, where peer influence and a desire to fit in often play critical roles.

In sum, the present study tests the hypotheses that attachment anxiety is positively associated with concern for appropriateness and that attachment avoidance is unrelated or negatively related to concern for appropriateness, with possible moderation by gender. The study uses established self-report measures administered to a convenience sample of adults and employs bivariate correlational analyses and split-file examinations by gender to probe these relationships. The expected outcomes, if supported, would indicate that attachment-related motivations contribute meaningfully to individual differences in conformity tendencies and could open avenues for targeted interventions and more nuanced models of social influence that incorporate attachment processes.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures were designed to provide a heterogeneous convenience sample while remaining feasible for the resources available. One hundred participants were recruited using opportunity sampling methods, including classroom announcements at a university and posts on university-affiliated social media platforms. Participation was voluntary and no monetary compensation was provided; students who participated were given the option of course credit in some classes in accordance with departmental policies. The recruitment message invited adults aged 18 years or older to take part in a brief online questionnaire study about relationships and social tendencies. The study protocol and materials were reviewed and approved by the university's institutional review board, and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

The final sample comprised 100 participants. The sample was predominantly female, reflecting the demographics of the recruitment contexts; 78 participants identified as female and 22 participants identified as male. No participants identified a nonbinary gender in this sample, and gender was operationalized dichotomously for analytic purposes given the small number of nonbinary responses at the time of data collection. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 46 years, with a mean age of 22.4 years and a standard deviation of 4.8 years. The sample included undergraduate students from a variety of disciplines as well as a small number of community members who responded to online postings. Participants reported diverse relationship statuses including single, dating, cohabiting, and married, but the sample skewed toward unmarried, student-aged adults.

Measures were administered online through a secure survey platform. Responses were anonymous and participants were free to discontinue at any time without penalty. The questionnaire battery began with demographic items assessing age, gender, relationship status, and whether the participant was a student. Next, participants completed the Experiences in Close Relationships—Revised (ECR-R) questionnaire adapted for general attachment tendencies rather than attachment to a specific romantic partner. The ECR-R yields two continuous scales: attachment anxiety and attachment avoidance. The anxiety scale captures worries about rejection and abandonment and the desire for extreme closeness, whereas the avoidance scale captures discomfort with intimacy and a preference for emotional distance. Each scale comprises multiple items rated on a seven-point Likert-type scale, with higher scores indicating greater levels of the respective attachment dimension.

Following the ECR-R, participants completed the Concern for Appropriateness scale, which operationalizes conformity as a dispositional tendency to adhere to perceived social norms and to prioritize doing what is considered appropriate in a social context. Items on this measure assess agreement with statements regarding the importance of fitting in, acting in socially acceptable ways, and altering one's behavior to meet group expectations. Responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater concern for appropriateness and, by extension, a higher likelihood of conforming to social demands or expectations.

Prior to data collection, the questionnaires were piloted with a small convenience sample to ensure clarity and appropriateness of the items and to estimate completion time. On average, participants completed the full battery in approximately 12 minutes, making it feasible to administer in classroom or online settings. Data screening procedures included checking for missing data, response sets, and improbable completion times. No cases were excluded for excessive missing data, but a small number of participants had incomplete responses on some items and those items were handled using mean substitution at the scale level when no more than 10% of items for a given scale were missing. For participants with more than 10% missing items on a scale, that participant's score for the scale was considered missing and excluded from analyses involving that scale.

Internal consistency reliability was assessed for each scale using Cronbach's alpha. The ECR-R anxiety scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency in this sample, with an alpha coefficient of .92. The ECR-R avoidance scale showed very good internal consistency with an alpha of .88. The Concern for Appropriateness scale also demonstrated strong reliability, with an alpha of .86. These reliability estimates indicated that the scales were sufficiently consistent for correlational analyses within the present sample.

Statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Preliminary analyses examined descriptive statistics and tested the assumptions underlying parametric correlation procedures. Descriptive statistics included means, standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis for each scale. Skewness and kurtosis values were inspected to determine whether departures from normality warranted nonparametric approaches. In this sample, the distributions of the attachment anxiety and avoidance scales and the conformity measure were within acceptable limits for parametric testing, with skewness and kurtosis values close to zero and within commonly accepted thresholds for bivariate correlation tests. Scatterplots were inspected for linearity and potential outliers. A small number of scores were identified as extreme relative to the bulk of the data, but sensitivity analyses indicated that exclusion of these points did not materially change the pattern of results; therefore results are reported using the full sample.

The primary analytic strategy involved computing Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients to assess the bivariate associations between attachment anxiety, attachment avoidance, and concern for appropriateness. Because attachment anxiety and avoidance are conceptually distinct yet empirically related constructs, correlations were interpreted in the context of their intercorrelation. Effect sizes for correlations were interpreted using conventional benchmarks, with r values around .10 considered small, around .30 considered medium, and .50 or greater considered large. Given the sample size of 100, the study had adequate power to detect medium correlations in the full sample but limited power for detecting small effects or for subgroup analyses in the smaller male subsample.

To further explore potential gender moderation, split-file analyses were conducted to compute correlations separately for female and male participants. Given the female overrepresentation in the sample and the smaller male subsample, the split-file analyses were treated as exploratory and interpreted with caution. In addition, a follow-up regression analysis was conducted in the full sample to test whether attachment anxiety predicted concern for appropriateness after controlling for age and attachment avoidance, and whether there was a significant interaction between attachment anxiety and gender. Continuous predictors were mean-centered prior to creating interaction terms to reduce multicollinearity and to facilitate interpretation.

All statistical tests used a two-tailed alpha level of .05. Where multiple comparisons were considered, the pattern of findings was interpreted holistically, with attention to effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than sole reliance on p-values. Secondary analyses examined potential covariates such as relationship status and student status, but these variables did not substantially affect the primary associations and are not reported in detail here.

Ethical considerations were observed throughout the study. Participation was voluntary, informed consent was obtained, and debriefing information was provided after completion of the survey. No identifying information was collected, and data were stored on a secure server accessible only to the research team. The study posed minimal risk to participants, primarily consisting of the potential for mild discomfort when answering questions about attachment and conformity, and participants were provided with contact information for university counseling services should they experience any distress.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the main study variables are presented first to provide an overview of the sample characteristics and the central tendency and variability of the measures. The mean score for attachment anxiety in the full sample was 3.45 on the seven-point ECR-R scale, with a standard deviation of 1.20. The mean score for attachment avoidance was 3.02 with a standard deviation of 1.10. For the Concern for Appropriateness conformity measure, the mean score was 3.12 on the five-point scale, with a standard deviation of 0.72. Skewness and kurtosis statistics for each variable were within acceptable ranges, with skewness values ranging from -0.35 to 0.28 and kurtosis values ranging from -0.50 to 0.40, indicating approximate normality for parametric analyses.

Correlations among the primary variables were computed using Pearson product-moment correlation coefficients. Attachment anxiety and concern for appropriateness were significantly positively correlated in the full sample. Specifically, the correlation between attachment anxiety and concern for appropriateness was r = .34, p = .001, indicating a medium-sized positive association whereby higher levels of attachment anxiety were associated with greater concern for acting appropriately and fitting in socially. The 95% confidence interval for this correlation ranged approximately from .15 to .50, indicating a reasonably precise estimate of a positive effect. The association between attachment avoidance and concern for appropriateness was small and not statistically significant, with r = .07, p = .48, suggesting little evidence that avoidance of intimacy relates meaningfully to self-reported concern for appropriateness in this sample.

Attachment anxiety and avoidance were themselves modestly positively correlated, r = .22, p = .03, consistent with prior research indicating that insecurity on one dimension is sometimes accompanied by insecurity on the other. Given this intercorrelation, partial correlations and regression analyses were used to examine whether attachment anxiety uniquely predicted concern for appropriateness when controlling for avoidance. A partial correlation controlling for avoidance showed that the association between anxiety and concern for appropriateness remained significant, partial r = .31, p = .002, indicating that the unique variance in anxiety contributed to the observed relationship with conformity beyond any shared variance with avoidance.

To probe potential gender differences, split-file correlations were computed for women and men separately. For female participants (n = 78), the correlation between attachment anxiety and concern for appropriateness was r = .39, p < .001, indicating a medium-to-large effect size within the female subsample. For male participants (n = 22), the correlation between attachment anxiety and concern for appropriateness was r = .02, p = .92, indicating no evidence of an association within the male subsample. The correlation between attachment avoidance and concern for appropriateness was r = .09, p = .44 for women and r = -.06, p = .78 for men, suggesting no reliable relationship between avoidance and conformity for either gender subgroup.

Given the disparate sample sizes across genders, the difference in correlation coefficients between women and men was formally tested using Fisher's r-to-z transformation. The difference between the female correlation of .39 and the male correlation of .02 produced a z value of approximately 1.98, corresponding to a two-tailed p value near .048, indicating that the anxiety-conformity association was statistically stronger in women compared to men in this sample. However, the small size of the male subsample limits confidence in this estimate, and the confidence intervals for the male correlation were wide.

A multiple regression analysis was conducted to test the unique predictive power of attachment anxiety on concern for appropriateness while controlling for age and attachment avoidance, and to test whether gender moderated this association. The regression model included centered predictors for attachment anxiety, attachment avoidance, age, gender (coded 0 = male, 1 = female), and the interaction term for anxiety by gender. In the main effects model without the interaction, attachment anxiety was a significant predictor of concern for appropriateness (β = .33, t = 3.44, p = .001), whereas attachment avoidance was not (β = .05, t = 0.55, p = .58) and age was not a significant predictor (β = .06, t = 0.67, p = .50). The model accounted for approximately 12% of the variance in concern for appropriateness, R^2 = .12, F(3, 96) = 4.61, p = .005.

When the interaction between attachment anxiety and gender was added to the model, the interaction term was significant (β = .21, t = 2.07, p = .041), indicating that the slope of the relationship between anxiety and concern for appropriateness differed by gender. Probing the interaction using simple slopes analysis revealed that the slope for women was significant and positive (simple slope β = .44, t = 3.85, p < .001), whereas the slope for men was near zero and non-significant (simple slope β = .07, t = 0.37, p = .71). The interaction model explained approximately 16% of the variance in concern for appropriateness, R^2 = .16, ΔR^2 = .04 for the interaction term, F-change(1, 95) = 4.29, p = .041. These results indicate that attachment anxiety predicts greater concern for appropriateness primarily among female participants in this study.

Supplemental analyses examined whether relationship status or student status might account for the observed associations. Partial correlations controlling for relationship status (single versus in a relationship) showed that the anxiety-concern for appropriateness correlation remained significant (partial r = .32, p = .001). Similarly, controlling for student status did not substantially alter the primary findings. These supplementary analyses suggest that the observed relationship between attachment anxiety and concern for appropriateness is not easily attributable to simple differences in relationship involvement or student status within the sample.

Diagnostic checks of the regression models indicated that assumptions were adequately met. Residuals were approximately normally distributed and homoscedastic plots did not indicate systematic deviations. Multicollinearity diagnostics revealed variance inflation factors below 1.5 for all predictors, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. Influence diagnostics identified no cases with undue leverage that would unduly influence parameter estimates.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary findings to alternate handling of missing data and potential outliers. Excluding the small number of cases with outlying scale scores produced results that were consistent in direction and statistical significance with the main analyses, although the exact magnitudes of coefficients varied slightly. Nonparametric Spearman rank correlations yielded similar patterns, with a significant positive association between anxiety and concern for appropriateness in the entire sample (ρ = .30, p = .002) and among women (ρ = .35, p = .001) but not among men (ρ = .05, p = .82).

Taken together, the results indicate a medium-sized positive association between attachment anxiety and concern for appropriateness in the full sample and a robust association among female participants, whereas attachment avoidance showed no significant relationship with conformity. These patterns held up in multiple analytic approaches and after controlling for avoidance, age, and relationship status, providing converging evidence that attachment anxiety, particularly in women, is related to higher self-reported propensity to conform due to concerns about appropriateness.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationships between attachment anxiety, attachment avoidance, and concern for appropriateness as an index of conformity in a convenience sample of 100 participants. The primary finding was that attachment anxiety was positively and moderately associated with concern for appropriateness in the full sample, and this association was particularly pronounced among female participants. Attachment avoidance was not significantly related to concern for appropriateness in either the overall sample or in gender-specific analyses. These findings provide empirical support for the theoretical expectation that anxious attachment, characterized by heightened concerns about rejection and a desire for closeness and reassurance, is linked to greater tendencies to conform or to prioritize behaving in ways that will be socially accepted.

The observed association between attachment anxiety and concern for appropriateness aligns with conceptual models of attachment that emphasize hyperactivation strategies among anxiously attached individuals. Hyperactivation is manifested as increased attention to social cues, greater efforts to secure closeness, and heightened sensitivity to potential threats to the relationship. Conforming to social norms or aligning one's behavior with others can serve as a strategy for signal ing affiliation and reducing perceived risk of rejection. Thus the present findings suggest that concern for appropriateness may act as a behavioral manifestation of anxious attachment motivations, as individuals with higher anxiety may be more willing to shape their behavior to meet social expectations in order to maintain social bonds.

The lack of a significant relationship between attachment avoidance and concern for appropriateness is also theoretically interpretable. Avoidant individuals typically employ deactivation strategies that de-emphasize relational needs and reduce reliance on others for regulation. Consequently, they may be less motivated to alter their behaviors to gain social approval. The present null finding for avoidance could reflect a genuine lack of association between deactivating strategies and reported concern for appropriateness, at least within the contexts tapped by the self-report measure used in this study. It is also possible that avoidant individuals might conform behaviorally in some contexts for pragmatic reasons while simultaneously endorsing low concern for appropriateness on self-report measures, reflecting a distinction between overt behavior and self-perceived motivations that this study's measurement approach could not disentangle.

The gender-specific pattern observed, wherein the anxiety-conformity relationship was significant among women but not men, warrants careful consideration. One interpretation is that socialization processes produce differential behavioral expressions of attachment-related motivations across genders. Women are often socialized to value interpersonal harmony, relational maintenance, and communal traits, which could amplify the translation of attachment anxiety into conforming behaviors aimed at preserving relationships. Men, conversely, may be socialized to value autonomy and independence, potentially dampening the behavioral expression of attachment anxiety in the form of conformity. Thus, the same underlying attachment-related concerns could lead to different outward behaviors depending on gender norms and expectations.

Another possibility is that measurement artifacts contributed to the gender difference. The Concern for Appropriateness scale captures self-reported tendencies to conform for social reasons, and it is possible that men and women differ in their willingness to endorse items that emphasize communal or approval-seeking motives. Men may underreport such tendencies due to social desirability concerns or gendered expectations about self-presentation. Alternatively, the small male subsample in the present study reduced statistical power to detect an effect among men, so the null finding for men should be interpreted cautiously. Indeed, the formal test of the difference in correlations suggested that the anxiety-conformity association was statistically stronger among women in this sample, but replication in larger and more balanced samples is needed to establish the robustness of this moderation effect.

The study's findings contribute to both attachment research and the literature on conformity by identifying a dispositional antecedent for conformity that derives from relational motivations. These results suggest that interventions aimed at reducing maladaptive conformity, particularly in domains where conforming can lead to harm (e.g., peer-driven risky behavior), might benefit from addressing underlying attachment insecurities, especially attachment anxiety. For example, interventions that promote secure attachment-related beliefs or that strengthen individuals' sense of self-worth and relational security could reduce the need to conform as a compensatory strategy for anxiety about rejection.

The results also open theoretical avenues for understanding the mechanisms linking attachment anxiety to conformity. One plausible mediator is fear of negative evaluation, which is conceptually related to both attachment anxiety and concern for appropriateness. Anxious attachment may heighten fear of negative evaluation, which in turn leads individuals to conform to social norms to avoid such evaluation. Social anxiety and rejection sensitivity are additional candidate mediators that future research should evaluate. Another plausible mediator is the desire for increased affiliation; anxiously attached individuals may conform because conformity is an efficient way to signal similarity and thereby increase the likelihood of acceptance.

Several methodological limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. First and foremost, the cross-sectional correlational design precludes causal inferences. While attachment anxiety may increase propensity to conform, it is also possible that chronically conforming to group norms shapes internal working models to be more anxious about relationships, or that a third variable, such as neuroticism or social anxiety, accounts for both attachment anxiety and conformity. Longitudinal and experimental designs are needed to disentangle these possibilities. Experimental manipulations of attachment security, such as security priming, combined with behavioral measures of conformity in laboratory settings (for example, Asch-type line-judgment tasks or public versus private conformity paradigms) would provide stronger tests of causal mechanisms.

Second, the reliance on self-report measures may introduce biases such as shared method variance, social desirability, and introspective limits. The Concern for Appropriateness measure captures reported tendencies rather than observed behavior, and self-reports may not always translate into real-world conforming actions. Future research should incorporate behavioral measures of conformity and multi-method approaches, including informant reports, observational data, and physiological markers of social stress to provide a more comprehensive assessment.

Third, the sample was a convenience sample drawn primarily from a university context and was predominantly female, which limits the generalizability of the findings. Cultural and developmental differences may modulate the relationships observed here. For instance, in cultures with stronger collectivistic norms, the baseline tendency to conform may be higher across attachment styles, potentially obscuring individual differences. Similarly, older adults with more established relational histories may exhibit different patterns than young adults. Future studies should use larger, more diverse samples with balanced gender representation and broader age ranges.

Fourth, although the ECR-R is a well-established measure for assessing attachment dimensions, its use in a general context as opposed to relationship-specific contexts could influence the strength of associations with conformity. Some research suggests that attachment orientations can be context-specific and that measuring attachment in relation to specific close others may yield different patterns than generalized measures. Future studies might compare relationship-specific and generalized attachment assessments in predicting conformity across different social contexts.

Fifth, while the study explored gender moderation, other potentially important moderators were not examined. Variables such as social status within groups, peer norms, type of group (e.g., peers versus authority figures), and the public versus private nature of the behavior could all moderate the attachment-conformity link. For example, anxious attachment may more strongly predict conformity in close peer groups where relational stakes are high as compared to interactions with strangers or in anonymous settings. Future research should systematically vary social context to clarify boundary conditions for the attachment-conformity association.

Despite these limitations, the study provides novel empirical evidence linking attachment anxiety to self-reported concern for appropriateness, suggesting that attachment-derived motivations contribute to individual differences in conformity tendencies. The gender-specific pattern observed suggests that cultural norms and socialization processes are relevant to how attachment motivations are expressed behaviorally, though replication and extension are needed.

Several specific future directions emerge from the present work. First, longitudinal research that follows individuals across developmental periods could illuminate whether early attachment experiences predict later conformity tendencies over time and whether conformity in adolescence or young adulthood feeds back to influence attachment security. Second, experimental research could manipulate state-level attachment security and measure subsequent conformity behaviorally, which would provide stronger causal evidence regarding the role of attachment processes. Third, mediation studies should test potential psychological mechanisms such as fear of negative evaluation, rejection sensitivity, and desire for affiliation. Fourth, cross-cultural research could assess whether the association between attachment anxiety and conformity varies across collectivistic and individualistic cultures. Fifth, intervention research might evaluate whether attachment-based therapies or skills training that enhance relational security can reduce harmful forms of conformity, such as peer-induced substance use.

In conclusion, the present study finds that attachment anxiety is positively associated with concern for appropriateness, particularly among women, whereas attachment avoidance shows no reliable association with conformity. These findings underscore the importance of considering attachment-derived motivations when studying susceptibility to social influence and suggest fertile avenues for future research that integrates attachment theory with social influence paradigms. Understanding the dispositional factors that predispose individuals to conform has implications for both theory and practice, including the development of interventions that target underlying relational insecurities to reduce maladaptive conformity and promote healthier decision-making in social contexts.
