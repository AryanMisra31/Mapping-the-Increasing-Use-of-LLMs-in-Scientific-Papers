Introduction

Stress is a pervasive feature of modern life and has important consequences for physical and mental health. Exposure to stressors triggers a cascade of physiological responses that engage neuroendocrine systems, autonomic arousal, and behavioral changes. One behavioral domain that appears particularly sensitive to stress is appetite regulation. Appetite and food intake can be altered markedly by acute and chronic stress, and these alterations have been implicated in weight change and in the development or exacerbation of eating disorders. The regulation of appetite under stress is therefore a subject of substantial clinical and theoretical importance. The hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and associated cortisol release have been proposed as key mechanisms linking stress and appetite, but other hormonal pathways including ghrelin and leptin, as well as neural circuits governing reward and homeostatic eating, also contribute to how stress affects hunger and food-seeking behavior. Acute stress can increase arousal and engage reward circuits that bias individuals toward energy-dense comfort foods, while chronic stress may shift homeostatic set points and metabolic responses. Empirical studies have reported mixed outcomes, with some showing stress-induced hyperphagia and others demonstrating stress-related reductions in food intake, indicating that the relationship between stress and appetite is nuanced and moderated by contextual and individual difference factors. Individual differences such as emotional eating propensity, dietary restraint, gender, and baseline body mass index appear to moderate whether an individual is more likely to eat in response to stress or to experience appetite suppression. The type of stressor also appears to matter, with social-evaluative and uncontrollable stressors often producing robust physiological responses that can influence appetite differently than mild cognitive stressors. Laboratory experimental paradigms have used tasks such as the Trier Social Stress Test, public speaking, and timed mental arithmetic to induce acute stress, and these tasks reliably elevate subjective stress and physiological markers in many participants. In contrast, interventions aimed at stress reduction, including mindfulness, guided relaxation, and simple structured activities such as coloring, have been shown to reduce subjective stress and physiological markers in some contexts. Because of these diverse findings, there remains a need for research that directly compares the effects of a standardized acute stressor and a simple stress-reduction task on hunger and appetite in a defined population. University students constitute a population of particular interest because they commonly experience fluctuations in academic stress, have variable sleep and eating habits, and are at an age where maladaptive eating patterns may develop or consolidate. Epidemiological work has documented elevated stress and anxiety among university students relative to the general population, and survey studies often report that students endorse changes in appetite and weight during peak stress periods such as examinations. Given the vulnerability of this group, investigating how acute stressors and stress-relief activities alter appetite and subjective hunger has both theoretical and practical relevance for health promotion in university settings. Moreover, understanding whether simple, low-cost activities like structured coloring can attenuate stress-related increases in hunger would have implications for developing scalable interventions to reduce stress-driven overeating. Prior experimental investigations with student samples have sometimes focused exclusively on stress induction or on stress reduction but have rarely included both manipulations within the same protocol to enable direct comparisons of the effects of stress and stress relief on hunger. Including both a stress induction and a stress-relieving task in the same within-subjects design permits examination of directionality and reversibility of appetite changes in response to short-term fluctuations in subjective stress. Additionally, measuring hunger and stress using repeated self-report scales surrounding each task allows for within-subjects estimation of the magnitude of change and the covariance between stress change and hunger change. The present study was designed to address these gaps by examining how exposure to an acute mental arithmetic stressor influences subjective hunger and how a brief, structured non free-form coloring task affects both stress and reported hunger within a university student sample. We operationalized the acute stressor as a timed mental mathematics task performed under mild evaluative pressure, a commonly used and ecologically relevant laboratory stressor that reliably elevates subjective stress in student samples. We operationalized stress relief using a non free-form coloring task because structured coloring has been promoted as a calming activity that requires attention but minimizes demands for originality, thus reducing performance anxiety while providing a focused, rhythmic activity. These two tasks were chosen because they represent conditions that many students can readily relate to: performance under time pressure and a simple, low-effort leisure activity. We collected hunger and stress ratings at baseline and immediately following each task to capture immediate, short-term shifts in subjective experience. We also recorded demographic and control variables including time since last meal to account for basic drivers of hunger and to reduce confounding from recent food intake. Guided by prior literature suggesting that acute stress often increases subjective desire for palatable foods and may increase reported hunger in some individuals, we hypothesized that the mental arithmetic stressor would produce a significant increase in subjective stress ratings and a concomitant increase in reported hunger compared with baseline. We further hypothesized that the coloring task would reduce subjective stress relative to the stressed state and would be associated with a decrease in reported hunger relative to the stressed state. Finally, we predicted that individual differences in baseline hunger and in time since last meal would account for some variance in reported hunger, but that change in subjective stress would explain unique variance in change in hunger. The study therefore aimed not only to describe mean changes in stress and hunger across tasks but also to test whether within-subject variation in stress predicted variation in hunger after accounting for basic physiological predictors. Such findings would shed light on the short-term dynamics between cognition-related stress and appetite in a young adult population and could inform brief behavioral strategies to mitigate stress-induced appetite changes. The study additionally sought to evaluate the directionality of these effects within individuals by using a repeated-measures design in which participants served as their own controls, thereby increasing statistical sensitivity to detect within-person shifts that might be obscured in between-subjects designs. Given the high prevalence of self-reported stress-related changes in appetite among university students, documenting experimentally the effects of a common cognitive stressor and a brief stress-relief task on hunger has potential translational value for campus wellness programs. The study’s design and measures were selected to balance experimental control with ecological relevance, allowing conclusions about immediate subjective effects while acknowledging limitations related to laboratory settings and self-report measures. Together, these considerations motivated the present investigation into how a standardized mental arithmetic stressor and a structured coloring task influence hunger and appetite among university students, with specific predictions about increases in hunger following stress and reductions following a stress-relief activity.

Methods

Participants were 120 undergraduate and graduate students recruited from a mid-sized university through a combination of course participation pools and campus volunteer advertisements. The sample included individuals across a range of academic disciplines and years of study and was intentionally recruited to be broadly representative of the student body in terms of gender composition and age range. The mean age of participants was 20.7 years with a standard deviation of 2.8 years. Gender was self-reported and included categories of female, male, and other, with the majority identifying as female in this sample. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years of age, enrolled as a student at the university, and fluent in English. Exclusion criteria included a current diagnosis of an eating disorder, current use of medications known to markedly affect appetite (such as stimulants or appetite suppressants), and any reported medical condition that would preclude safe participation in a brief cognitive stress task. These exclusion criteria were assessed via an initial screening questionnaire prior to scheduling laboratory sessions. The study received approval from the university institutional review board and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Participants were asked to refrain from eating for at least two hours before their scheduled session in order to reduce variability in hunger due to recent meals while still maintaining participant comfort. Time since last meal was recorded for each participant as a covariate in subsequent analyses. Sessions were scheduled between midmorning and early afternoon to minimize circadian variation in hunger and cortisol across participants. The experiment used a within-subjects repeated-measures design in which each participant completed three measurement epochs: baseline, post-stressor, and post-coloring. Each epoch included brief self-report measures of subjective stress and hunger. The order of the stress-induction and coloring tasks was fixed to match the study’s primary aim of examining the immediate effect of an acute stressor and the potential mitigating effect of a subsequent stress-relief activity. The protocol always included the baseline assessment, followed by the mental arithmetic stressor, the post-stressor assessment, the coloring task, and then the post-coloring assessment. This ordering was chosen so that the coloring task could be evaluated as a recovery-oriented intervention following a standardized stress induction. All experimental sessions were conducted individually in a quiet laboratory room with minimal external distractions and with the participant seated comfortably at a table. At the start of each session participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire and reported the time since their last meal and any caffeine or nicotine consumption in the preceding two hours. They then completed baseline subjective ratings of stress and hunger using single-item 7-point Likert scales. The stress scale asked participants to indicate “How stressed do you feel right now?” with response options ranging from 1 (Not at all) to 7 (Extremely). The hunger scale asked participants to indicate “How hungry do you feel right now?” on a similar 7-point scale. In addition to these core items, participants completed a brief trait measure of emotional eating composed of five items adapted from standard scales, which was included to capture stable individual differences in propensity to eat in response to negative affect. The emotional eating score was calculated as the mean of the five items and was used in exploratory analyses as a moderator. After completing baseline measures participants were given standardized instructions for the mental arithmetic stressor. The stressor consisted of a 5-minute timed serial subtraction task in which participants were instructed to subtract 17 repeatedly from a four-digit number displayed on a laptop screen as quickly and as accurately as possible. The task was presented with a visible countdown timer and intermittent evaluative prompts delivered via pre-scripted audio reminding the participant to respond quickly and to avoid errors. Participants were told that their performance would be recorded and evaluated for accuracy and speed to increase evaluative pressure. This mental arithmetic procedure has been used in prior laboratory work to induce short-term cognitive stress and is straightforward to administer in a student sample. Immediately after the 5-minute stressor the experimenter escorted the participant to the assessment terminal and participants completed the post-stressor subjective ratings of stress and hunger on the same 7-point scales as at baseline. Following the post-stressor assessment participants were provided a short break of 30 seconds and were then given the coloring task instructions. The coloring task was a structured non free-form coloring exercise in which participants were given a pre-printed intricate design and a set of colored pencils and were instructed to color within the lines for 8 minutes. The design selected for all participants required attention but did not necessitate creative decision-making beyond color choice, and participants were reminded that the task was not being evaluated for quality to minimize performance anxiety. The 8-minute duration was chosen to be long enough to allow for engagement and potential stress reduction while keeping the overall session time manageable. After completion of the coloring task participants again completed the subjective stress and hunger ratings. To minimize demand characteristics participants were given neutral instructions about the purpose of the study, described generally as investigating “mood and cognitive activity,” and were debriefed at the end of the session regarding the specific study aims and hypotheses. All subjective ratings were administered electronically and timestamped to ensure immediacy of responses following each task. The primary outcome variables were the self-reported stress and hunger ratings at the three epochs: baseline, post-stressor, and post-coloring. Additional variables included time since last meal, emotional eating trait score, age, and gender. The main statistical approach involved repeated-measures analyses to test for changes across the three time points for stress and for hunger. Specifically, separate one-way repeated-measures ANOVAs were conducted for stress and hunger with time (baseline, post-stressor, post-coloring) as the within-subjects factor. Significant omnibus effects were followed up with paired-sample t-tests comparing baseline to post-stressor, post-stressor to post-coloring, and baseline to post-coloring with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared for ANOVAs and Cohen’s d for paired t-tests. Pearson correlations were computed to examine associations between changes in stress and changes in hunger, operationalized as difference scores (post-stressor minus baseline and post-coloring minus post-stressor). To examine whether change in stress predicted change in hunger above and beyond basic physiological predictors, hierarchical linear regression models were performed with hunger change as the dependent variable. In the regression analyses baseline hunger and time since last meal were entered in the first step, and stress change was entered in the second step. Emotional eating trait score was included as an exploratory moderator in interaction models to test whether the stress-hunger link varied as a function of trait emotional eating. Assumptions of normality and sphericity were assessed and where the sphericity assumption was violated Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. All statistical tests were two-tailed and the alpha level was set at .05. Statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Data quality checks included inspection for missing values and outliers. Two participants were excluded from the final analyses due to incomplete data, yielding a final sample size of 118 for the reported analyses.

Results

Participant characteristics indicated a final analyzed sample of 118 students after the exclusion of two participants with incomplete data. The mean age of participants was 20.7 years with a standard deviation of 2.8 years. The sample included 74 participants identifying as female, 41 identifying as male, and 3 identifying as other. The mean time since last meal reported by participants was 3.1 hours with a standard deviation of 1.2 hours. The mean baseline emotional eating trait score was 2.8 on a 5-point scale with a standard deviation of 0.9, indicating moderate variation in emotional eating tendencies across the sample. Inspection of the outcome variables at baseline indicated mean baseline subjective stress of 3.12 on the 7-point scale with a standard deviation of 1.18. Baseline hunger mean was 3.41 with a standard deviation of 1.29. These baseline values provided a reference point against which to evaluate changes after the experimental manipulations. The one-way repeated-measures ANOVA for subjective stress with time as the within-subjects factor yielded a significant omnibus effect, indicating that stress ratings differed across the three measurement epochs. The ANOVA produced an F(2,236) = 214.36, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .65, indicating a large effect of task epoch on reported stress. Paired-sample comparisons showed that stress increased significantly from baseline to post-stressor; the mean stress rating rose from 3.12 (SD = 1.18) at baseline to 4.98 (SD = 1.11) post-stressor. The paired t-test for baseline to post-stressor yielded t(117) = 16.53, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.52, indicating a large effect size. The transition from post-stressor to post-coloring showed a significant decrease in stress, with mean stress falling from 4.98 (SD = 1.11) to 2.62 (SD = 1.02); the paired t-test for this comparison produced t(117) = -20.04, p < .001, Cohen’s d = -1.84. The comparison between baseline and post-coloring also indicated a small but significant reduction; mean post-coloring stress of 2.62 was lower than baseline stress of 3.12, with t(117) = -4.13, p < .001, Cohen’s d = -0.38. These findings confirmed that the mental arithmetic task effectively elevated subjective stress and that the coloring task produced a pronounced reduction in stress, bringing mean stress below baseline levels for the sample as a whole. The one-way repeated-measures ANOVA for hunger across time similarly yielded a significant omnibus effect, indicating that hunger ratings varied across the three epochs. The ANOVA produced an F(2,236) = 31.29, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .21, reflecting a moderate effect of task epoch on reported hunger. Paired comparisons revealed that hunger increased from baseline to post-stressor; mean hunger rose from 3.41 (SD = 1.29) to 4.19 (SD = 1.35). The paired t-test for baseline to post-stressor produced t(117) = 6.02, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.55, indicating a medium effect. From post-stressor to post-coloring the mean hunger rating decreased from 4.19 (SD = 1.35) to 3.05 (SD = 1.20). The paired t-test for the post-stressor to post-coloring comparison yielded t(117) = -9.12, p < .001, Cohen’s d = -0.84. The comparison of baseline to post-coloring showed that hunger at the end of the session was modestly but significantly lower than baseline hunger (3.05 vs. 3.41), with t(117) = -3.39, p = .001, Cohen’s d = -0.31. These paired comparisons indicated that the acute cognitive stressor increased subjective hunger appreciably and that the subsequent coloring task decreased hunger to levels below those observed immediately after the stressor and modestly below baseline. To examine the association between stress change and hunger change, Pearson correlations were computed using difference scores. The correlation between change in stress from baseline to post-stressor and change in hunger from baseline to post-stressor was r = .46, p < .001, indicating a moderate positive relationship such that individuals who experienced larger stress increases tended to report larger increases in hunger. The correlation between change in stress from post-stressor to post-coloring and change in hunger over the same interval was r = .39, p < .001, again indicating a moderate positive association between reductions in stress and reductions in hunger. These correlations supported the notion that stress and hunger co-vary dynamically within individuals. Hierarchical linear regressions were conducted to test whether change in subjective stress predicted change in hunger above and beyond baseline hunger and time since last meal. In the first step of the model, baseline hunger and time since last meal were entered and together accounted for 18% of the variance in hunger change (R^2 = .18, p < .001). Adding stress change (post-stressor minus baseline) in the second step significantly increased explained variance by 12% (ΔR^2 = .12, p < .001), resulting in a total R^2 of .30. In the final model, stress change was a significant predictor of hunger change (β = .42, p < .001) after controlling for baseline hunger (β = .28, p < .001) and time since last meal (β = .19, p = .01). These results indicated that the increase in subjective stress made an independent contribution to the increase in hunger beyond the effects of prior hunger and time since eating. An exploratory moderation analysis tested whether trait emotional eating moderated the relationship between stress change and hunger change. The interaction term between stress change and emotional eating trait score accounted for an additional 3% of variance in hunger change (ΔR^2 = .03, p = .02). Simple slopes analysis indicated that the association between stress change and hunger change was stronger for individuals with higher emotional eating scores, with a β of .56 for participants one standard deviation above the mean on the trait measure, compared with a β of .28 for participants one standard deviation below the mean. Gender differences were examined descriptively and via interaction tests. The main effects of time on stress and hunger were significant for both men and women, and there was no significant interaction between gender and time for either outcome, indicating that the general pattern of stress increase with the arithmetic task and stress reduction with coloring was consistent across genders. However, when stratified, the magnitude of the stress-related hunger increase was somewhat larger in participants identifying as female, although this difference did not reach statistical significance in interaction testing. Checks of statistical assumptions indicated that the distribution of residuals for the ANOVA and regression models was approximately normal and that the sphericity assumption for repeated measures on stress and hunger was supported; where violations occurred they were corrected with Greenhouse-Geisser adjustments. There were no influential outliers detected after inspection of leverage and Cook’s distance statistics. Finally, subjective debriefing responses indicated that participants generally found the mental arithmetic task challenging and somewhat stressful and reported that the coloring task felt relaxing and distracting. No adverse events occurred during the study and compliance with task instructions was high, with the majority of participants engaging with both tasks throughout the prescribed durations. Taken together, these results supported the study hypotheses by demonstrating that an acute mental arithmetic stressor produced robust increases in subjective stress and a concomitant increase in reported hunger, and that a subsequent structured coloring task reduced stress and was associated with decreases in hunger. Regression and moderation analyses further indicated that stress change contributes unique variance to changes in hunger and that individuals with higher emotional eating tendencies show stronger coupling between stress and appetite changes.

Discussion

The current study examined how an acute cognitive stressor and a brief, structured stress-relief activity influenced subjective stress and hunger in a university student sample. The findings demonstrated a clear pattern in which a timed mental arithmetic task elicited a substantial increase in self-reported stress and a simultaneous increase in reported hunger. A subsequent non free-form coloring task produced marked reductions in stress and was associated with reductions in hunger that brought average hunger levels below those observed at baseline. The strong within-subject associations and the demonstration that stress change accounted for unique variance in hunger change after controlling for baseline hunger and time since last meal provide evidence that short-term fluctuations in subjective stress and appetite are tightly linked in this population. The present results have several theoretical and practical implications and align with a body of literature suggesting that acute stress can prompt increases in appetite for at least some individuals. One key theoretical implication concerns the mechanisms by which acute psychological stress can influence subjective hunger. Although the present study relied on self-report measures and did not include physiological biomarkers, the observed association between stress and hunger is consistent with models implicating stress-responsive neuroendocrine systems and reward circuitry in driving appetite changes. Acute cognitive stress has been shown in prior work to elevate circulating cortisol in some individuals, and cortisol in turn can modulate appetite-related hormones and neural sensitivity to food cues. Parallel pathways involving ghrelin, a hunger-promoting peptide, may also be responsive to stress and could drive increased subjective hunger and desire for palatable food. Engagement of reward-related neural circuits in response to stress may bias behavior toward seeking rewarding, energy-dense foods as a form of affect regulation. These mechanistic interpretations are consistent with the current behavioral data and provide plausible biological substrates for the observed coupling of stress and hunger. The finding that the coloring task not only reduced stress but also reduced hunger suggests that interventions which shift subjective arousal and affective state can have rapid downstream effects on appetite. Structured coloring likely acted as a form of attentional engagement combined with low cognitive demand and predictable sensory feedback, fostering relaxation and interrupting ruminative or performance-oriented thought patterns triggered by the stressor. The reductions in hunger after coloring could therefore reflect both diminished physiological arousal and reduced motivational drive to seek food as a coping mechanism. Importantly, the decrease in hunger to somewhat below baseline levels after coloring suggests that simple, low-cost activities that reduce stress may be effective adjuncts for regulating appetite in acute contexts. The moderation by trait emotional eating indicates that the coupling between stress and hunger is not uniform across individuals. Participants with higher emotional eating scores showed stronger associations between stress increases and hunger increases, suggesting that stable predispositions to use food to regulate affect amplify the appetite response to acute stress. This finding is in line with prior work emphasizing individual differences in stress-eating tendencies and highlights the importance of considering trait-level moderators when predicting who is most likely to increase food intake under stress. From a practical standpoint, this suggests that stress-reduction techniques may be particularly beneficial for individuals prone to emotional eating. The study design has several strengths that support the internal validity of the conclusions. The within-subject repeated-measures approach allowed each participant to serve as their own control, enhancing sensitivity to detect within-individual shifts in stress and hunger and reducing variability due to between-subject differences. The standardized mental arithmetic stressor is a well-established cognitive challenge that is ecologically relevant to student experiences and was successful in producing robust stress elevations in this sample. The coloring task provided a simple, replicable stress-relief activity that can be implemented easily in real-world settings such as study halls and counseling centers. The use of immediate post-task assessments minimized retrospective bias and captured short-term subjective dynamics. Notwithstanding these strengths, several limitations temper the conclusions and point to directions for future research. First, the reliance on self-report measures of stress and hunger does not provide direct evidence of physiological changes. Future studies should incorporate biological measures such as salivary cortisol, ghrelin, and autonomic indicators (heart rate variability, skin conductance) to elucidate the physiological mechanisms linking stress and appetite. Second, the laboratory tasks, while standardized, may not fully capture the complexity of stressors encountered in daily life, such as prolonged academic pressures, interpersonal conflicts, or sleep deprivation, and thus ecological validity is limited. Ecological momentary assessment methods in naturalistic settings could complement laboratory findings and help determine whether the same patterns of stress-related appetite change occur in real-world contexts. Third, although the within-subjects design controlled for many confounds, the fixed task order (stressor followed by coloring) precluded examination of whether coloring prior to a stressor might produce protective effects or whether the order interacts with individual differences. Future work could adopt counterbalanced designs to examine order effects and to test whether preemptive engagement in stress-relief activities attenuates the impact of subsequent stressors on appetite. Fourth, the sample consisted of university students who are a specific demographic group; generalization to older adults, individuals with metabolic disorders, or clinical populations with eating disorders requires caution. Studies in more diverse populations and in clinical samples could clarify the boundaries of the effect and its clinical relevance. Fifth, time since last meal was controlled statistically and participants were instructed to refrain from eating for at least two hours before the session, but absolute control over pre-session dietary intake was not feasible and residual variability in energy status may have contributed to hunger ratings. Future studies could standardize pre-session meals more tightly or manipulate energy status experimentally to examine interactions between metabolic state and stress-induced appetite changes. Sixth, although the emotional eating trait provided useful moderation information, the study did not include measures of dietary restraint or detailed assessments of habitual dietary patterns, which could further clarify how different facets of trait eating behavior influence stress-appetite coupling. Seventh, the study did not include an objective behavioral measure of food intake following the tasks, and so it remains an open question whether the subjective hunger changes observed translated into proportional changes in actual consumption. Incorporating ad libitum snack intake paradigms or follow-up meal measures would enable linkage between subjective appetite and behavior. Eighth, while the coloring task reduced stress in the present design, the specific active ingredients of coloring that produce relaxation remain uncertain; future dismantling studies could compare coloring to other low-demand activities such as listening to music or brief guided breathing to parse common and unique mechanisms. Ninth, the study’s cross-sectional experimental design does not speak to long-term patterns of stress-related eating or to causal chains linking chronic stress exposure to sustained appetite changes; longitudinal designs could examine how repeated acute stress episodes and repeated use of stress-relief strategies influence weight trajectories and eating pathology over time. Tenth, the study did not assess the types of foods participants would prefer under stress, and it is possible that stress-induced hunger preferentially motivates consumption of high-fat, high-sugar foods; including measures of food preference and reward responsiveness would refine understanding of dietary risk. Despite these limitations, the present findings contribute to an integrative understanding of stress and appetite by demonstrating that a brief cognitive stressor elevates subjective hunger and that a subsequent simple relaxation-oriented activity can reverse this effect within minutes in a student population. The results have implications for health promotion efforts on university campuses, suggesting that encouraging short, accessible stress-relief activities during high-pressure periods might mitigate stress-driven increases in appetite and potentially reduce unhealthy snacking. For individuals prone to emotional eating, targeted interventions that combine stress management training with strategies to reduce impulsive eating may be particularly beneficial. Future research should build on the current findings by incorporating physiological measures, assessing actual food intake, extending the work to diverse populations, testing alternative task orders, and examining long-term outcomes. Integrating laboratory and ecological approaches will be important for translating these findings into practical recommendations and interventions. Overall, the study provides evidence that stress and hunger are dynamically linked within individuals and that brief behavioral activities capable of reducing subjective stress can concurrently attenuate appetite, supporting the potential for simple, scalable strategies to address stress-related eating in vulnerable populations such as university students.
