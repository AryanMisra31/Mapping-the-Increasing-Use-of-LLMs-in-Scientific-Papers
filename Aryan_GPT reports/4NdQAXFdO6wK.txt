Introduction

Human faces are a primary source of social information, and the ability to perceive and interpret facial expressions is central to social cognition. Facial expressions convey a range of emotional states that guide interpersonal interaction, inform decisions, and shape social learning. The neural underpinnings of facial expression processing have been a major focus of cognitive neuroscience and neuropsychology for decades. A prominent finding in this literature is that the two cerebral hemispheres are not functionally identical, and that many aspects of face processing show hemispheric asymmetries. Hemispheric lateralisation has been reported across a range of perceptual and cognitive functions, and it is widely accepted that lateralisation serves to increase processing efficiency by reducing redundancy. One domain in which lateralisation has been particularly studied is emotion perception, including the perception of facial expressions. Early lesion and split-brain studies suggested that the right hemisphere plays a privileged role in processing facial expressions of emotion, and subsequent neuroimaging and behavioural work has supported a right-hemisphere advantage for a range of emotional tasks.

Within the facial expression literature, a specific set of behavioural paradigms has been used to probe hemispheric differences. One such method is the chimeric face task, in which stimuli are created by combining the left half of one face with the right half of another. When participants are shown pairs of chimeric faces that are mirror images of each other and asked to judge which appears more emotional, biases in selection can reveal lateralised perceptual dominance. The logic of the chimeric face task rests on the contralateral organisation of the visual system, such that information presented in the left visual field is predominantly processed by the right hemisphere and vice versa. If the right hemisphere is more sensitive to emotional expression, an asymmetry should be revealed in choices favouring the half-face in the left visual field when that half contains an emotional expression. Many studies using chimeric faces have reported a left visual field / right hemisphere advantage for the perception of expressed emotion, particularly for smiling or happy faces. This line of work has been influential in shaping the idea that positive affect, at least as conveyed by smiles, is processed preferentially by the right hemisphere.

However, the literature is not monolithic, and there are well-documented inconsistencies. While some studies find robust right-hemisphere advantages for emotional faces, others report null effects or even left-hemisphere biases under certain conditions. Methodological differences across studies are substantial and include variations in stimulus construction, the emotional valence under investigation, the identity and sex of faces and participants, the task instructions, and the statistical power of samples. For example, the strength of a lateralisation effect can depend on whether the expression is subtle or exaggerated, whether the task requires explicit emotional labelling or relative judgement, and whether participants are static or dynamic. The degree to which chimeric face tasks tap early perceptual asymmetries versus later interpretive processes is also debated. Taken together, the empirical picture suggests that hemispheric asymmetries for facial expressions are real in some contexts but not inevitable across all paradigms.

A further set of findings touches on individual differences in lateralisation and how they relate to mood and personality. Mood disorders such as depression have been linked to altered patterns of hemispheric organisation, but the direction of effects has been inconsistent. Some clinical and subclinical studies report a relative left-hemisphere hypoactivity in depression that manifests as reduced processing of positive affect and diminished reactivity to pleasant stimuli. Other studies have not replicated these findings or have reported more complex interactions with comorbid anxiety, medication status, and task demands. Parallel to clinical work, studies of trait psychological characteristics such as optimism and pessimism have explored whether these dispositions map onto lateralised neural processing. Optimism, defined as a generalized expectancy for positive outcomes, is associated with a cluster of behavioural and physiological correlates that include positive affect, approach motivation, and certain patterns of attention. If optimism is linked to enhanced sensitivity to positive facial expressions, and if positive expressions are processed preferentially in one hemisphere, a relationship between optimism and lateralisation might be expected.

Despite the theoretical plausibility of a link between optimism and hemispheric asymmetry in facial expression processing, empirical evidence has been sparse and mixed. Some studies have suggested that individuals who report higher levels of negative affectivity or depressive symptoms show altered chimeric face biases, whereas others find no relationship between affective traits and lateralised perception. There is reason to hypothesise that optimism could show a negative relationship with a right-hemisphere bias: individuals who are more optimistic might be less reliant on a right-hemisphere mechanism for detecting positive cues because they have generally heightened approach motivation or more balanced processing across hemispheres. Conversely, optimistic individuals might show an enhanced right-hemisphere bias if that hemisphere supports rapid detection of positive social signals. Without consistent prior results, the sign of any association remains theoretically open.

The complexities in the literature also extend to the influence of handedness and sex. Handedness is a rough proxy for the organisation of language lateralisation and can be associated with broader patterns of cerebral asymmetry. Many studies of lateralised emotion perception restrict samples to right-handed individuals to reduce variance attributable to atypical hemispheric organisation. Sex differences have been reported in emotional processing and lateralisation, although findings are again mixed. Some research has suggested that women may show stronger sensitivity to emotional expressions or different patterns of hemispheric specialisation compared to men. These complexities motivate careful sample selection and reporting when assessing lateralisation and its correlates.

In this study, we sought to address two primary questions. First, we sought to replicate the commonly reported right-hemisphere bias for processing smiling faces using a chimeric face task in a sample of right-handed, female undergraduate participants. Replication is important given the variability across prior studies and the reliance on relatively small samples in some work. Second, we sought to test whether dispositional optimism, measured via a validated self-report instrument, is associated with the magnitude of a lateralisation effect in the perception of smiling chimeric faces. Based on prior reports of a right-hemisphere advantage for smiling faces, we hypothesised that participants would show a right-hemisphere dominance in the chimeric face task, operationalised as a selection bias favouring faces with smiles located on the left half (left visual field). We further hypothesised that there would be a negative correlation between optimism scores and the magnitude of the laterality effect. The negative direction of this prediction was informed by theoretical considerations that more optimistic individuals might demonstrate a reduced dependence on a right-hemisphere mechanism for detecting positive expressions, possibly reflecting more distributed or bilateral processing of positive social cues.

Our sample was composed of right-handed female undergraduates to limit heterogeneity due to handedness and sex-related factors that could influence lateralisation and emotional processing. Handedness was assessed with the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory to ensure robust right-handedness. Dispositional optimism was assessed with a revised version of a widely used life orientation measure. Stimuli consisted of 36 pairs of chimeric faces constructed to present a smile on either the left or the right side of the face in mirror-image pairs. Participants were asked to choose which face in each pair appeared happier, a forced-choice relative judgement that places minimal demands on explicit labelling and maximises sensitivity to subtle perceptual biases.

The chimeric face task was chosen for its direct behavioural link to lateralised visual processing, and because it has been widely used in prior lateralisation research. The use of mirrored chimeric face pairs provides a straightforward index of hemisphere-dominant perception when choices systematically favour one presentation of the emotional half over the other. By examining choices across 36 trials we sought to obtain a reliable measure of individual laterality effect magnitude, which could then be related to optimism scores. Statistical analyses focused on group-level tests of whether the laterality effect differed from chance, and on correlational tests between laterality indices and optimism scores. Secondary analyses explored the distributional properties of both measures and examined whether any observed effects might be explained by outliers or restricted range.

Given the prior literature, several alternative outcomes were plausible. A robust replication of a right-hemisphere bias would provide further support for the generality of a right-hemisphere role in processing positive facial expressions. A null result at the group level would suggest that any lateralisation for smiling faces is sensitive to sample characteristics or task parameters and is not inevitable. Regarding the association with optimism, a significant negative correlation would support the notion that dispositional positivity is linked to reduced right-hemisphere dominance in positive expression perception, whereas a non-significant or positive correlation would require rethinking of how personality traits relate to lateralised perceptual processes. Regardless of outcome, the study offers an opportunity to clarify the relationship between a well-studied behavioural lateralisation paradigm and an important dispositional trait.

Method

Participants were 94 right-handed female undergraduate students recruited from an introductory psychology participant pool. The sample size of 94 reflected the total number of eligible volunteers who met handedness criteria and completed the study during the data collection period. All participants were English-speaking undergraduates aged between 18 and 23 years. Participants were screened for neurological or psychiatric conditions via a brief self-report health questionnaire; those reporting a history of neurological disorder or current psychiatric treatment were excluded. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were compensated with course credit. The study received institutional ethical approval from the university's human subjects review board.

Handedness was assessed using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory. Participants completed the standard items assessing hand preference for a variety of everyday activities. A laterality quotient was computed for each participant using the conventional scoring procedure, yielding values ranging from -100 to +100; only participants with scores indicating clear right-handedness (positive scores above a conservative cutoff) were included in the sample. The handedness assessment served both to screen participants and to provide a continuous record of hand preference for descriptive purposes.

Dispositional optimism was assessed using the Revised Life Orientation Test. The measure consists of items that probe generalized expectations for positive versus negative outcomes. Participants responded on a Likert-type scale, and scores were summed to produce a total optimism score, with higher scores indicating greater optimism. The instrument has been widely used in prior research and was chosen for its brevity and theoretical relevance to the study aims. Instructions emphasised that responses should reflect general tendencies rather than situational feelings.

Stimuli for the chimeric face task were created from standardized photographs of neutral and smiling faces, drawn from a library of carefully controlled facial images. For each identity, a smiling facial image was combined with a neutral image to create two chimeric faces: one in which the smile was present on the left half of the face and neutral on the right, and its mirror image in which the smile was on the right half and neutral on the left. Edge blending procedures were used to minimise abrupt transitions at the midline and to maintain a naturalistic appearance. A total of 36 chimeric pairs were constructed using a range of facial identities to reduce potential effects of idiosyncratic features. Stimuli were presented in pairs such that participants saw the two mirror-image chimeras side by side.

The behavioural task required participants to view each pair of chimeric faces on a computer screen in a quiet experimental room and to indicate which face appeared happier. Participants were instructed to make a forced-choice selection and were encouraged to rely on their immediate perceptual impression rather than analytic deliberation. Pair presentation order and the side of presentation (left versus right on the screen) were counterbalanced across trials and participants to prevent systematic spatial biases from confounding the lateralisation index. Each trial began with a fixation cross presented centrally for 500 ms, followed by the chimeric pair displayed until response or for a maximum of 4 seconds. Participants responded by pressing one of two keys to indicate their choice; key mappings were explained prior to the task and practised in a brief training block.

Responses were coded to create a laterality score for each participant. For each trial, a response that selected the face with the smile on the left half (i.e., left-localised smile) was coded as a leftward choice, and a response selecting the face with the smile on the right half was coded as a rightward choice. A laterality index was computed as the proportion of left-localised choices minus the proportion of right-localised choices to create a score ranging from -1 to +1, where positive values indicate a bias favouring left-localised smiles and negative values indicate the opposite. For group-level analyses, a one-sample test against zero was used to assess whether the mean laterality index significantly differed from chance.

Data preprocessing included checks for missing responses and extreme response patterns. Trials with no response were rare and excluded from that participant's proportion calculation; participants with more than 10% missing trials would have been excluded, though none met this criterion. The distribution of laterality indices was examined for normality using skewness and kurtosis statistics and visual inspection of histograms. Optimism scores were similarly examined for distributional properties and for potential floor or ceiling effects.

Statistical analyses were conducted using standard parametric techniques. The primary test for the lateralisation hypothesis was a one-sample t-test comparing the group mean laterality index to zero. Effect size was reported as Cohen's d to provide an estimate of the magnitude of any observed bias. The relationship between optimism and lateralisation was examined using Pearson correlation coefficients between the life orientation score and the laterality index. To complement the primary correlational test, nonparametric Spearman rank correlations were also computed to assess robustness to deviations from normality. Statistical significance was evaluated using an alpha level of .05.

Secondary analyses explored potential moderating factors and robustness checks. These included splitting the sample by median optimism to examine group differences in laterality indices, examining the consistency of lateralisation across subsets of trials (e.g., odd versus even trials) to assess reliability, and conducting a binomial test at the trial level to inspect whether left-localised selections exceeded chance across the entire sample. Outlier analysis used standard leverage and studentised residual checks for the correlation to determine whether any single participant exerted undue influence on the relationship between optimism and laterality. Finally, a post hoc power analysis estimated the detectable effect size given the available sample size to contextualise non-significant findings.

Results

Descriptive statistics indicated that all 94 participants met the handedness inclusion criterion and completed the experimental tasks. The mean age of participants was 19.4 years with a standard deviation of 1.2 years. Edinburgh Handedness Inventory scores confirmed strong right-handedness in the sample, with a mean laterality quotient of 82.3 and a standard deviation of 11.7; no participant's score fell below the predefined right-handed cutoff. The Revised Life Orientation Test scores ranged from 6 to 24 in the sample, with a mean optimism score of 14.8 and a standard deviation of 3.9. Inspection of the optimism score distribution showed a near-normal distribution with no evidence of floor or ceiling effects, although the range reflects the undergraduate sample and is therefore truncated relative to a broader population.

Participants completed a total of 36 chimeric face trials. The mean number of responses per participant was 35.9, with missing responses extremely infrequent and no participant excluded due to missing data. The laterality index, computed as the proportion of left-localised choices minus the proportion of right-localised choices, had a group mean of -0.021 and a standard deviation of 0.198. The distribution of laterality indices was approximately symmetric around zero with a slight leftward skew, but measures of skewness and kurtosis did not indicate severe departure from normality. Visual inspection of histogram and Q-Q plots suggested that parametric tests were appropriate.

The primary test of lateralisation used a one-sample t-test comparing the mean laterality index to zero. The result indicated that the mean laterality index did not differ from zero, t(93) = -0.96, p = .339, Cohen's d = -0.10. The non-significant result suggests that, at the group level, there was no reliable bias favouring left-localised smiles that would indicate a right-hemisphere dominance in perception of happy chimeric faces. The effect size was small and in the opposite direction to the hypothesised right-hemisphere advantage, but it was well within sampling variability.

A complementary binomial analysis considered choices across all trials in the sample. Across the 94 participants and 36 trials per participant, there were 3,384 trials in total. Left-localised choices accounted for 1,648 selections (48.7% of trials) and right-localised choices accounted for 1,736 selections (51.3% of trials). A binomial test comparing the frequency of left- versus right-localised selections across trials confirmed no significant deviation from chance, p = .33. Thus, the aggregate pattern of trial-level choices did not reveal a left visual field advantage for smiling faces.

The relationship between optimism and laterality was examined with Pearson correlation. The correlation between Revised Life Orientation Test scores and laterality indices was r = -0.12, p = .256. This negative correlation indicates that higher optimism scores were modestly associated with more rightward choices (i.e., a tendency opposite to the hypothesised direction), but the association did not reach statistical significance. The 95% confidence interval for the correlation spanned from approximately -0.33 to +0.10, indicating considerable uncertainty and compatibility with both small negative and small positive true correlations. A nonparametric Spearman correlation yielded a similar pattern, rho = -0.10, p = .31, which suggests that the result is robust to minor deviations from normality.

To probe whether any subgroups showed different patterns, an exploratory median split on optimism was performed. Participants in the high optimism group (n = 47) had a mean laterality index of -0.034 (SD = 0.201), whereas those in the low optimism group (n = 47) had a mean laterality index of -0.008 (SD = 0.195). An independent-samples t-test comparing these two group means was non-significant, t(92) = -0.62, p = .54, indicating no reliable difference in lateralisation between higher and lower optimism participants. Effect sizes for the group comparison were small (Cohen's d = -0.13).

Reliability of the laterality measure was assessed by correlating laterality indices computed from odd and even trials. The split-half correlation was r = .49, and after applying the Spearman-Brown correction the internal consistency estimate was .66. This suggests moderate reliability for the 36-item chimeric face measure in this sample and indicates that individual laterality estimates are of sufficient stability to be meaningfully correlated with dispositional measures, albeit not highly reliable.

Outlier diagnostics for the correlation between optimism and laterality indicated one participant with a particularly large studentised residual and high leverage relative to others. Recomputing the Pearson correlation with this participant excluded produced r = -0.10, p = .33, which did not appreciably change the conclusion of a non-significant association. Thus, the negative correlation observed in the full sample does not appear to be driven by a single influential case.

Given the non-significant primary findings, a post hoc power analysis was conducted to estimate the smallest effect size that could be reliably detected with the present sample. With 94 participants and alpha set at .05, two-tailed, the study had 80% power to detect correlations of approximately r = .28 or larger. For the one-sample t-test of laterality index against zero, the detectable standardized mean difference (Cohen's d) with 80% power was approximately .29. The observed correlation of r = -0.12 and the observed mean difference corresponding to Cohen's d = -0.10 lie well below these thresholds, suggesting that the present study was not powered to detect small effects but was adequate for moderate-sized effects.

Exploratory analyses examined whether the laterality effect varied as a function of response time. Mean reaction times for choices did not differ significantly when participants selected left-localised versus right-localised smiles (mean RT left choices = 1,012 ms, mean RT right choices = 1,027 ms), t(93) = -0.69, p = .49. This suggests that choice patterns were not confounded by speed-accuracy trade-offs or differential difficulty across the two spatial options. Additional exploratory checks evaluated whether the side of presentation (left side of screen vs right side) had any systematic effects; logistic regression predicting left-localised choice from screen side indicated no significant main effect of side once stimulus configuration was taken into account, p = .44.

In summary, group-level tests revealed no evidence of a right-hemisphere advantage for perceiving smiles in the chimeric face task in this sample of right-handed female undergraduates. The hypothesised negative correlation between optimism and the laterality index was observed in direction but not magnitude or statistical significance. Reliability checks and sensitivity analyses suggest that the measures were adequate but that the study would have limited sensitivity to small effects. Overall, the data do not support the presence of a robust lateralisation effect for smiling faces in this specific methodological context, nor do they provide evidence for a meaningful relationship between dispositional optimism and lateralised perception of positive facial expressions.

Discussion

The present study examined hemispheric lateralisation in the perception of smiling chimeric faces and tested whether dispositional optimism was related to the magnitude of any lateralisation effect. At the group level, participants did not display a systematic bias favouring left-localised smiles, and the laterality index did not differ significantly from chance. Additionally, although the correlation between optimism and laterality was negative as predicted, this association was small and did not reach statistical significance. These results indicate that, in this sample of right-handed female undergraduates using the described chimeric face procedure, there is no clear evidence for right-hemisphere dominance in processing smiling faces, and optimism does not reliably predict individual differences in lateralised perception of positive facial expressions.

The failure to replicate a robust right-hemisphere advantage for smiling faces invites careful consideration relative to prior literature. A sizable body of work has reported left visual field advantages for emotional face perception, particularly for expressions conveying happiness or other emotions. However, not all studies converge on this finding, and research has documented considerable variability attributable to multiple methodological and sample-related factors. The present null result contributes to this literature by demonstrating that the presence of a right-hemisphere bias is not a universal property of human perceptual processing of smiles. It highlights the contingent nature of lateralisation effects and underscores the importance of considering task parameters, stimulus characteristics, and sample composition when interpreting lateralisation findings.

Several methodological distinctions could account for differences between the present findings and studies reporting right-hemisphere advantages. First, the construction of chimeric stimuli involves choices about how to blend face halves, the intensity of expressions, and the identities used. Subtle differences in stimulus realism or midline blending could influence the salience of the half-face expression and thus the strength of any lateralised response. Second, task instructions and demands matter: forced-choice relative judgments like the present task tap perceptual salience in a different way than tasks requiring explicit identification or categorisation of emotions. Third, the participant population in the present study was restricted to right-handed women in a narrow age range, which reduces heterogeneity but also limits generalisability; effects that are robust across mixed samples may attenuate in highly selected groups. Fourth, the reliability of the laterality measure, while moderate, was not high, and measurement error can attenuate observed effects and correlations.

The observed small negative correlation between optimism and laterality, although not statistically significant, raises interpretive possibilities. If real, a negative association might suggest that more optimistic individuals show relatively less left visual field dominance for positive faces, consistent with our directional hypothesis that optimism could be associated with more bilateral or left-leaning processing of positive social cues. However, the correlation observed was small and compatible with a wide range of true effects including zero, so firm conclusions cannot be drawn. The pattern might reflect sampling variability or range restriction in optimism scores in the undergraduate sample. Alternatively, the absence of a robust association could indicate that optimism is not meaningfully related to early perceptual lateralisation of facial expressions, though it might nonetheless be related to other aspects of emotional processing such as attentional bias, memory for positive stimuli, or neural reactivity that are not captured by the chimeric face task.

The present study's design also illuminated practical considerations for research on lateralisation and personality. The chimeric face measure derived from 36 trials showed only moderate reliability, suggesting that longer or multiple sessions might be necessary to obtain highly reliable individual difference measures suitable for correlational work. Given that correlations between personality traits and behavioural indices are commonly modest, low reliability in the behavioural index can greatly reduce statistical power to detect true associations. Future studies aiming to examine trait-behaviour relationships in lateralisation would benefit from designing tasks with enhanced reliability, perhaps by increasing the number of trials, using more homogeneous and salient stimuli, or incorporating multiple complementary measures of lateralised processing such as divided visual field tasks or neurophysiological indices.

The sample characteristics limit the extent to which the present findings generalise. Restricting participants to right-handed females was deliberately chosen to minimise heterogeneity in hemispheric organisation and to test a relatively controlled sample, but it also constrains the ability to detect effects that might be present in mixed-sex or left-handed populations. Sex differences in emotion perception and hemispheric specialisation have been reported, and it is possible that males or mixed samples could show different patterns. Additionally, the young undergraduate sample may differ in optimism distribution and neural plasticity relative to older adults or clinical groups. Therefore, replications in more diverse samples, including mixed sex and varying handedness, are needed to determine the generality of the current null findings.

Another set of limitations pertains to measurement of optimism. The Revised Life Orientation Test is a brief self-report measure that captures generalized expectancies for positive outcomes but does not dissect components of dispositional positivity such as approach motivation, positive affectivity, or regulatory focus. It is possible that facets of positive disposition more closely tied to social reward sensitivity, rather than global optimism, would exhibit stronger associations with lateralised perception of positive faces. Moreover, self-report measures are susceptible to biases and may not map directly onto perceptual processing styles. Future research might complement self-report with behavioural or physiological markers of optimism-related constructs, such as effort expenditure for reward, subjective valuation of positive social cues, or neuroendocrine indicators.

Methodological extensions could strengthen future investigations. Neuroimaging techniques like functional MRI could assess whether neural responsiveness in putative face or emotion processing regions demonstrates lateralised patterns associated with optimism. Electroencephalography or event-related potentials could provide temporal precision to determine whether early perceptual components are lateralised and whether those patterns relate to dispositional traits. Combining behavioural chimeric tasks with direct neural measures would clarify whether null behavioural effects reflect lack of neural lateralisation or compensatory mechanisms that mask neural asymmetries in overt choices.

Another useful avenue is to examine clinical and developmental populations. Depression, anxiety, and other mood-related conditions have been linked to anomalies in lateralised emotional processing, but the direction and mechanisms remain debated. Longitudinal designs tracking changes in mood and lateralisation over time, or intervention studies that modify mood or optimism, could reveal causal relations. Developmental studies could elucidate when lateralisation for emotional expressions emerges and whether personality traits shape developmental trajectories.

The present work also raises theoretical questions about the nature of hemispheric specialisation for emotional perception. Right-hemisphere dominance for emotion has been framed in several ways: as a general advantage for processing global configural information, as specialised processing of affective content, or as dominance for attentional or arousal-related aspects of emotion. The inconsistent pattern of results across studies suggests that lateralisation is not monolithic but contingent on the interaction of stimulus features, task demands, individual differences, and possibly transient state factors. Embracing this complexity will require theoretical models that specify how these factors modulate lateralisation and predict when right-hemisphere advantages should be observed.

Practically, the null findings caution against assuming that lateralisation for emotional faces is a stable individual trait that can be routinely leveraged as an index of affective disposition. While lateralised processing may be detectable in some experimental contexts, its absence in other well-controlled contexts suggests limits on its utility as a straightforward biomarker. Researchers interested in individual differences should combine multiple convergent measures and ensure high reliability, and they should be cautious in interpreting null associations in small or moderately sized samples.

In conclusion, the present study found no evidence of right-hemisphere dominance in the perception of smiling chimeric faces among right-handed female undergraduates and no significant association between dispositional optimism and lateralisation magnitude. These results underscore the variability of lateralisation effects in the literature and the need for careful methodological design when testing individual differences. Future research should prioritise replication in diverse samples, the use of highly reliable behavioural and neural measures, and the integration of richer assessments of positive disposition. By refining methods and clarifying theoretical mechanisms, subsequent work can better determine under what circumstances hemispheric asymmetries for emotional faces emerge and how they relate to enduring personality traits.
