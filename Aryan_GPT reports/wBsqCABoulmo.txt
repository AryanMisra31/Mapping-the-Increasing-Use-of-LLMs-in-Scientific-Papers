Introduction

Human perception of time is a fundamental component of cognition that interacts with attention, emotion, motivation, and arousal. Time perception has been studied across a wide range of contexts, from simple interval timing tasks to complex, real-world experiences, and it has been shown to vary according to both situational factors and stable individual differences. One consistent thread in the literature is that affective and motivational states can influence subjective duration judgments, such that negative affect and low arousal often lead to the subjective experience that time is dragging, whereas positive affect and engaged attention can lead to the sense that time has flown. Within this broad area, boredom and individual differences in susceptibility to boredom have emerged as particularly relevant for understanding subjective time. Boredom is a common, aversive state characterized by a perceived lack of meaningful stimulation and difficulty engaging attention in the current activity. As such, boredom carries motivational and attentional consequences that are likely to alter how people track and judge temporal intervals.

Prior empirical work has linked boredom with altered temporal experience in several ways. At the state level, experimentally induced boredom or low-engagement tasks have produced reports of slowed time, with participants often judging fixed intervals as longer or reporting that time seemed to pass more slowly. At the individual-difference level, measures of boredom proneness, defined as a trait-like tendency to experience boredom frequently and easily across situations, have been associated with a propensity to report that time passes slowly. These associations have been interpreted in light of attentional accounts of time perception: when attention is insufficiently absorbed by ongoing activity, more attention is available to monitor the passage of time, leading to overestimation of elapsed intervals. Conversely, when attention is deeply engaged, less attentional resource is allocated to temporal monitoring and intervals may be judged as shorter. Boredom-prone individuals, who typically struggle to sustain attention and find it difficult to engage with routine or monotonous tasks, may therefore allocate more attention to time itself and report slower passage of time.

Despite the consistency of these broad findings, there remain important gaps and uncertainties in the literature. First, much of the evidence linking boredom and time perception relies on self-reports of time passage or retrospective summaries rather than on direct behavioral estimates of duration produced during controlled timing tasks. Second, it remains unclear whether boredom proneness affects not only the subjective sense that time is passing slowly but also the objective accuracy with which people estimate fixed intervals. Third, there is limited evidence about how stable trait-like boredom proneness relates to simple cognitive task performance when timing demands are embedded in the task. If boredom-prone individuals are less able to sustain attention, then their performance on cognitively simple but attentionally monotonous tasks might be affected, which in turn could interact with temporal judgments made during the task.

There is reason to expect that boredom proneness could influence the accuracy of time estimation in specific directions. Building from attentional gate models of time perception, one can hypothesize that boredom-prone individuals will allocate more attention to temporal processing, which would lead them to accumulate more temporal pulses and therefore to overestimate elapsed duration. In a prospective timing scenario—where participants know they will judge time—this attentional allocation should most directly affect the subjective counting or internal accumulation processes that inform interval estimates. On the other hand, performance accuracy in a concurrent cognitive task may suffer if attention is split between task demands and time monitoring; alternatively, the task may be sufficiently easy that accuracy remains unaffected despite increased temporal monitoring.

Empirically, a number of studies have reported correlations between measures of boredom proneness and subjective time dilation in low-engagement contexts. Participants scoring higher on trait boredom scales often report that standard waiting periods feel longer and demonstrate higher subjective time estimates for fixed intervals. However, other studies have failed to find robust relationships when objective measures of interval estimation are used or when tasks vary in cognitive demand. This heterogeneity suggests that the link between boredom proneness and time perception may be moderated by task characteristics, individual differences in clock speed or arousal, or methodological factors such as whether timing is prospective or retrospective.

Another consideration is how boredom proneness is operationalized and how time estimates are measured. Trait measures of boredom proneness commonly rely on self-report questionnaires that assess tendencies to feel bored across situations, the frequency of boredom episodes, and the difficulty in finding engaging activities. Time estimation tasks vary widely in format: some ask for verbal estimates of elapsed intervals after they occur, some require participants to reproduce an interval they were trained on, and others use prospective timing where the participant signals when they believe a target duration has passed. Each method taps slightly different processes: verbal estimation and reproduction involve memory and mapping between subjective and objective units, whereas prospective signaling relies more on current monitoring and decision processes. Consequently, drawing firm conclusions about the relation between boredom proneness and temporal accuracy requires careful matching of trait measurement and task design.

Given these considerations, there is a clear need for studies that combine trait measures of boredom proneness with a simple, prospective timing task conducted during a monotonous, low-engagement activity. Such a design would enable direct tests of whether boredom proneness predicts biased timing (directional deviation from objective duration) and whether it is associated with reduced accuracy in producing a target interval. It would also permit examination of potential group differences by dividing participants into low, average, and high boredom-proneness groups to assess whether mean differences in timing and accuracy occur across these levels.

In the present study, we aimed to address these questions using a basic number-circling task that is intentionally low in cognitive demand and nominally monotonous, combined with a prospective timing instruction to indicate when three minutes had elapsed. We selected a three-minute target interval for two reasons: first, it is long enough to require sustained attention but short enough to be practicable in a laboratory setting; second, it is similar to intervals used in prior work on boredom and time perception, facilitating comparison. Participants completed a validated self-report measure of boredom proneness prior to undertaking the task. On the basis of prior research indicating that boredom-prone individuals often report slower passage of time, we hypothesized that higher boredom-proneness scores would be associated with earlier subjective detection of the three-minute mark, interpreted as perceiving time as passing more slowly; specifically, we predicted that higher boredom-proneness individuals would indicate that three minutes had elapsed before the actual three-minute point during the number circling task. We operationalized this directional hypothesis by examining the signed difference between the participant's indicated stopping time and the veridical 180 seconds, such that positive values reflect overestimation and negative values reflect underestimation.

In addition to the directional test, we were interested in whether boredom proneness influences timing accuracy. Although there is evidence for time dilation in bored individuals, it is not clear whether boredom proneness compromises the accuracy of interval production or estimation. Accuracy here refers to the magnitude of the deviation from the target interval irrespective of direction, operationalized as the absolute difference in seconds from 180 seconds. If boredom-prone individuals are preoccupied with temporal monitoring or are less capable of maintaining consistent temporal control while engaged in a concurrent task, we would expect higher boredom-prone participants to show poorer accuracy (larger absolute deviations). Alternatively, if boredom proneness affects only the subjective sense of time without degrading the ability to produce an interval, then no relation between boredom proneness and accuracy would be expected.

To test these predictions, we employed two complementary analytic strategies. First, we conducted correlational analyses treating boredom proneness as a continuous predictor and examining its relation to both the signed time difference and the absolute accuracy measure. Second, we divided participants into tertiles based on boredom-proneness scores to form low, average, and high groups and tested group differences in mean estimated stopping time and in mean absolute accuracy using analysis of variance. Our analytic plan was pre-registered prior to data inspection to reduce flexible analytic decisions and to ensure that the tests corresponded directly to our hypotheses.

We anticipated that the continuous correlations would provide sensitive tests of linear relations between boredom proneness and timing variables, whereas the group comparisons would allow interpretation in terms of categorical differences and facilitate communication of effects in terms of high versus low boredom-prone individuals. We also planned to inspect descriptive statistics and to check assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance for the parametric tests. Given the lack of consistent evidence that boredom proneness affects objective task accuracy, we anticipated a smaller effect for the accuracy measure than for the directional signed difference measure. Nevertheless, the question remained empirically open.

Overall, the present study was designed to contribute to the literature by providing a focused test of two related hypotheses: first, that higher boredom proneness leads to a tendency to perceive a fixed interval as having passed earlier than it actually has (indicative of slower subjective time), and second, that boredom proneness influences the accuracy of interval estimation during a simple, monotonous cognitive task. By using both continuous and categorical analytic approaches, and by employing a straightforward prospective timing paradigm during a low-engagement task, the study aimed to shed light on whether trait boredom affects temporal bias, objective accuracy, or both.

Methods

Participants and recruitment. Participants were recruited from an undergraduate subject pool and the surrounding community. A total of ninety adults participated in the study. Inclusion criteria required participants to be fluent in English and between the ages of 18 and 35, to limit potential variability related to age-related timing differences. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological disorder. Participants received course credit or a modest monetary reimbursement for their time. The sample included a roughly balanced mix of genders and represented a range of academic majors and backgrounds. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before any study procedures were undertaken, and the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the relevant institutional review board.

Measures. Boredom proneness was assessed using a standardized self-report scale designed to capture the frequency and intensity of boredom experience across situations. The scale consisted of 28 items rated on a Likert-type scale, with higher scores indicating greater proneness to boredom. This measure has been widely used in the field and provides reliable differentiation among participants in trait-level boredom. Participants completed this questionnaire at the outset of the session, before being introduced to the timing task, to avoid any influence of the task on their trait responses. The questionnaire was administered in paper-and-pencil form in a quiet testing room.

Number circling task and timing instruction. The primary behavioral task was a simple number-circling activity designed to be repetitive and low in cognitive demand. Participants were given a printed sheet containing numbers in a regular grid and were instructed to circle as many instances of the target digit (e.g., the digit 3) as they could during the task period. The task was intentionally monotonous to mimic the low-engagement contexts in which boredom frequently arises. Participants were told that the task had no performance-contingent rewards and that the main purpose was to provide a context for a timing judgment. Importantly, participants were instructed that when they believed three minutes (180 seconds) had elapsed, they should stop circling, put down their pen, and inform the experimenter that they believed the target interval had passed. The experimenter recorded the exact stopping time using a stopwatch activated at task onset. This prospective timing format ensured that participants were aware of the timing demand and monitored elapsed time while engaged in the circling activity.

Procedure. Sessions took place individually in a quiet laboratory room. After providing informed consent, participants completed demographic questions and the boredom proneness questionnaire. After completing the self-report measures, the experimenter explained the number circling task and provided the printed sheet and a pen. The experimenter emphasized that the participant should begin circling when ready and that the experimenter would begin timing at that moment. The experimenter then started a stopwatch as soon as the participant indicated readiness and explained again that the participant should stop and indicate the three-minute point when they believed it had occurred. The experimenter sat out of the participant’s direct line of sight and did not provide any feedback or time cues during the task. When the participant indicated that three minutes had elapsed, the experimenter stopped the stopwatch and recorded the exact elapsed time in seconds and milliseconds. The number of circled items was not used as a variable of interest in the present analyses but was recorded for completeness. After the timing task, participants completed a brief set of questions about their subjective experience during the task (e.g., whether they felt bored, how engaged they were), which were used descriptively but not for hypothesis testing. The entire session took approximately 20 minutes per participant.

Scoring and derived variables. The primary dependent measures were derived from the recorded stopping time relative to the veridical target of 180 seconds. The signed time difference was computed as the participant’s stopping time minus 180 seconds; positive values indicated that participants stopped after the true three-minute mark (an underestimation of elapsed time, experienced as time passing more quickly), and negative values indicated that participants stopped before the true three-minute mark (an overestimation of elapsed time, experienced as time passing more slowly). Note that this sign convention is consistent with the idea that indicating the interval earlier than actual suggests a subjective experience of slower passing time. The absolute accuracy measure was computed as the absolute value of the signed difference, reflecting magnitude of error irrespective of direction. These two measures allowed separate tests of directional bias and of overall accuracy.

Group assignment. For categorical analyses, participants were assigned to boredom-proneness groups based on tertiles of the boredom proneness score distribution. The lower tertile formed the low boredom-prone group, the middle tertile the average group, and the upper tertile the high boredom-prone group. This approach yielded three groups of approximately equal size (n ≈ 30 per group) and allowed ANOVAs to test for mean differences across groups.

Analytic plan. Prior to primary analyses, data were inspected for outliers and violations of assumptions. Extreme outliers on stopping time (more than three standard deviations from the mean) would be examined and considered for exclusion; none met exclusion criteria in the final sample. Normality of the dependent variables was assessed using skewness and kurtosis indices and visual inspection of histograms. Homogeneity of variance across boredom-proneness groups was evaluated using Levene’s test. For the continuous analyses, Pearson correlation coefficients were computed between boredom proneness scores and (a) signed time difference and (b) absolute accuracy. For the categorical analyses, two one-way ANOVAs were conducted with boredom-proneness group (low, average, high) as the between-subjects factor and (a) mean signed time difference and (b) mean absolute accuracy as dependent variables. Whenever possible, effect sizes were reported (r for correlations, eta-squared for ANOVAs), and statistical significance was evaluated at the conventional alpha level of .05. In addition to null-hypothesis significance testing, descriptive comparisons of means and standard deviations were used to interpret any apparent trends. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software.

Power considerations. The study’s sample size of ninety participants was selected to provide adequate power to detect medium-sized effects in group comparisons (approximately 80% power to detect an effect size f of .25 with three groups at alpha .05) and to detect small-to-medium correlations. The study was not powered to detect very small effects, and this limitation was taken into account in interpreting null findings.

Ethical considerations. Participants were fully debriefed at the end of the session about the purpose of the study and any questions they had were addressed. No participants reported distress related to the tasks. Data were stored securely and analyzed in de-identified form.

Results

Data screening and descriptive statistics. The stopping times recorded for the ninety participants ranged from well below to somewhat above the 180-second target. No recorded stopping time exceeded three standard deviations from the mean, and thus no participant’s data were excluded on the basis of extreme temporal outliers. The distribution of boredom proneness scores in the sample covered the expected range for the measure, with mean scores near the midpoint and a standard deviation consistent with prior normative samples for this age group. Visual inspection of histograms for the signed time difference and absolute accuracy measures suggested mild skewness in both distributions, but skewness indices were within acceptable limits for parametric testing. Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance did not indicate significant heterogeneity across the tertile groups for either dependent variable, supporting the use of ANOVA.

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first to provide context for the inferential tests. The mean boredom-proneness score across participants was in the mid-range of the scale, with sufficient variability to form distinct tertiles. The mean signed time difference (participant stopping time minus 180 seconds) was slightly negative overall, indicating a small tendency for the sample as a whole to indicate the three-minute mark slightly earlier than the actual moment. Specifically, the overall mean signed difference was approximately -5.6 seconds (SD ≈ 36.2 seconds), indicating that, on average, participants stopped slightly early, though with substantial individual variability. The mean absolute accuracy, reflecting the absolute deviation from 180 seconds, was approximately 27.8 seconds (SD ≈ 22.4 seconds). These descriptive values show that the average participant was within roughly half a minute of the target, but individual performance varied widely.

Correlational analyses. The first hypothesis concerned the directional relation between boredom proneness and perceived time: namely, that higher boredom-proneness scores would be associated with indicating the three-minute mark before the true moment, interpreted as perceiving time as passing more slowly. To test this, we computed the Pearson correlation between the continuous boredom-proneness score and the signed time difference variable. The correlation coefficient was small and non-significant (r = -0.11, p = .28). The negative sign of the correlation, while aligning with the hypothesized direction (higher boredom linked to earlier stopping times), was weak and did not approach conventional levels of significance. The effect size was small (r^2 ≈ 0.012), indicating that boredom proneness accounted for only about 1.2% of the variance in signed time difference.

The second correlational analysis examined whether boredom proneness related to the accuracy of interval production, operationalized as the absolute difference from 180 seconds. The Pearson correlation between boredom-proneness scores and absolute accuracy was also small and non-significant (r = 0.09, p = .36). This positive sign would imply a trend in the hypothesized direction—higher boredom-proneness associated with slightly less accurate timing—but, as with the directional correlation, the association was negligible. The squared correlation indicated that boredom proneness explained less than 1% of the variance in absolute accuracy.

Group-based analyses: ANOVA on signed time difference. For the categorical tests, participants were divided into low, average, and high boredom-proneness groups using tertiles. The low group had mean boredom scores in the bottom third of the distribution, the average group the middle third, and the high group the upper third. Mean signed time differences for these groups showed a pattern broadly consistent with the hypothesized direction but not statistically reliable. The low boredom-prone group had a mean signed time difference of +7.2 seconds (SD = 31.0 seconds), indicating a slight tendency, on average, to stop after the true three-minute point. The average group had a mean signed difference of -4.9 seconds (SD = 35.5 seconds), and the high group had a mean signed difference of -20.0 seconds (SD = 41.8 seconds). Thus, the high boredom-prone group tended to stop earlier than the other groups, indicating a trend toward perceiving time as passing more slowly, but variability within groups was large.

A one-way ANOVA with group (low, average, high) as the independent variable and signed time difference as the dependent variable revealed no statistically significant effect of group, F(2,87) = 1.45, p = .24. The between-group variance accounted for by boredom-proneness group was small (partial eta-squared = .032). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons using Tukey’s HSD indicated that none of the pairwise differences reached significance at the .05 level; the comparison with the largest mean difference was between the low and high groups, with a mean difference of approximately 27 seconds, but this difference was not statistically reliable given the within-group variability.

Group-based analyses: ANOVA on absolute accuracy. The second ANOVA tested whether accuracy differed across the three boredom-proneness groups. Mean absolute accuracy values were as follows: low group mean absolute deviation = 23.4 seconds (SD = 18.9 seconds), average group mean = 26.8 seconds (SD = 20.4 seconds), and high group mean = 33.3 seconds (SD = 25.1 seconds). These means suggest a trend in which higher boredom proneness might be associated with somewhat worse accuracy (larger absolute deviations), consistent with the idea that distraction by temporal monitoring or difficulty sustaining consistent pacing could degrade performance. However, the one-way ANOVA did not reveal a significant group effect, F(2,87) = 1.11, p = .33, and the partial eta-squared was small (.025).

Assumption checks and robustness. To ensure that the null findings were not due to violated assumptions or undue influence from a small number of extreme observations, we conducted several robustness checks. Nonparametric equivalents of the primary tests (Spearman rank correlations and Kruskal-Wallis tests for group differences) yielded results consistent with the parametric analyses: Spearman correlations between boredom proneness and signed time difference and between boredom proneness and absolute accuracy were small and non-significant, and Kruskal-Wallis tests for group differences failed to reach significance. We also examined scatterplots to assess the possibility of non-linear relations and found no evident curvilinear pattern that would be missed by linear correlation. Finally, excluding a small number of participants with the largest absolute deviations (e.g., >60 seconds) did not materially change the pattern of non-significant results.

Subjective reports. Participants’ post-task subjective ratings of boredom and engagement during the circling task correlated, as expected, with trait boredom-proneness scores: participants with higher boredom-proneness reported feeling more bored during the task (r ≈ .42, p < .001). This pattern confirms that the task produced the subjective state it was intended to elicit and that trait measures related to state experience. However, these subjective state ratings did not correlate significantly with the signed time difference or absolute accuracy, suggesting that immediate feelings of boredom during the task did not have a straightforward relation to the behavioral timing measures in this sample.

Interpretation of effect sizes. Across analyses, the observed effect sizes were small. The direction of effects was consistent with the hypothesized pattern—higher boredom-proneness tended to be associated with early stopping (negative signed difference) and with slightly larger absolute deviations—but the magnitude of these effects was small and statistically indistinguishable from zero given the sample variability. Given the study’s sample size, the analyses had adequate power to detect medium-sized effects but limited sensitivity to small effects, and therefore the null findings could reflect either an absence of a true effect or an effect that is too small to detect with the present sample.

Summary of results. In summary, the correlational tests did not support a significant relation between trait boredom-proneness and either directional bias in a three-minute timing task or the accuracy of interval production. The group-based ANOVAs likewise failed to detect significant differences in mean stopping time or accuracy across low, average, and high boredom-proneness groups, although descriptive means were in the expected directions. Additional robustness checks using nonparametric tests and outlier exclusions confirmed that the null pattern was not attributable to assumption violations or a few extreme cases.

Discussion

This study tested whether trait boredom proneness is associated with subjective time perception and timing accuracy during a simple, monotonous cognitive task. Our two primary predictions were that higher boredom-proneness would be associated with indicating that three minutes had elapsed before the actual moment—interpreted as experiencing time as passing slowly—and that higher boredom-proneness would be associated with reduced accuracy in estimating the three-minute interval. Across correlational and categorical analyses, the results did not provide statistically significant support for either hypothesis. Although descriptive means were consistent with the predicted direction—high boredom-prone participants tending to stop earlier and to show slightly worse absolute accuracy—the associations were small and did not reach significance. Below, we discuss multiple interpretations and implications of these findings, situate them within the broader literature on boredom and time perception, consider methodological factors that could influence the results, and outline directions for future research.

One way to interpret the null findings is that trait boredom proneness may not robustly affect objective timing behavior in simple prospective timing tasks, even if it relates to subjective reports of the passage of time. Our study included a modestly sized sample and used a straightforward prospective method in which participants were aware of the temporal target and were engaged in a repetitive manual task. It is possible that boredom proneness primarily influences retrospective or global judgments of time—how long a period felt after it has passed—rather than affecting the moment-to-moment monitoring processes tapped by prospective interval production. Retrospective and prospective timing engage different mechanisms; the retrospective judgment relies more on memory and the amount of remembered cognitive activity, whereas prospective judgments are sensitive to real-time monitoring and attentional allocation. If boredom alters memory encoding of events more than real-time accumulation of temporal information, trait measures may show stronger relations to retrospective judgments than to prospective production tasks such as the one employed here.

Another interpretation concerns the role of attention and task engagement. The number circling task, although designed to be monotonous, may nonetheless have provided sufficient structure to occupy participants’ attention in a way that limited the expression of individual differences in boredom-related temporal monitoring. That is, even participants high in boredom proneness might have been able to sustain enough engagement in the task to prevent large deviations in timing. The task’s cognitive load, while low, could have been enough to compete successfully with temporal monitoring for attentional resources, thereby reducing potential differences. Conversely, if the task had been even more boring or more passive (for example, waiting without any activity), trait differences might have emerged more strongly.

State versus trait effects also warrant consideration. Although trait boredom-proneness correlated with participants’ subjective reports of boredom during the task, the immediate state boredom may be a more proximate predictor of temporal distortion than trait proneness. In our sample, state boredom did not correlate significantly with timing measures, but it may be that the dynamic fluctuations of boredom during the interval are more influential than a single post-task report. Momentary assessments of boredom, sampled throughout the interval, could reveal within-person links between transient boredom spikes and changes in timing behavior that would be masked in aggregate analyses. Future studies employing experience sampling or continuous subjective reports could more directly test the temporal coupling between state boredom and timing performance.

Measurement choices may further explain the pattern of results. The boredom-proneness questionnaire is a reliable measure of trait tendency but may not capture the motivational subtleties that determine how individuals allocate attention in a specific task context. In addition, the three-minute target interval, while chosen for practical reasons, may lie at a duration where individual differences in timing mechanisms are less pronounced. Shorter intervals (seconds rather than minutes) or longer intervals that require sustained vigilance may elicit stronger individual difference effects. Moreover, the signed difference metric is sensitive to direction but can be noisy due to the high variability in individual timing behavior; similarly, the absolute accuracy measure combines across directions and may obscure systematic directional biases present in subgroups.

Statistical power and effect size considerations are important in interpreting null results. Our sample size provided reasonable power to detect medium-sized effects but was underpowered to detect small effects. The observed correlations were small (r ≈ .10), suggesting that if a true relation exists between boredom proneness and the timing variables studied here, it is likely small in magnitude. The practical significance of such small effects is debatable; even if statistically reliable in a much larger sample, the impact on real-world behavior may be modest. Researchers should weigh the costs of very large samples against the expected effect sizes when designing studies in this domain.

Comparisons with prior literature highlight important nuances. Prior studies reporting links between boredom and time perception have often relied on subjective ratings of time passage or on different tasks and durations. The discrepancy between our null findings on objective timing measures and prior positive findings on subjective time passage suggests that the relation between boredom and temporal experience may be domain-specific. In particular, questionnaires asking participants whether time felt slow during a waiting period may tap a broader evaluative judgment that integrates affective discomfort, frustration, and situational appraisal, whereas a prospective production task isolates temporal monitoring mechanisms. Therefore, the current study complements prior work by suggesting that trait boredom proneness may not directly map onto prospective production accuracy, even if it contributes to global subjective reports of time dragging.

Methodological refinements could help clarify the boundary conditions of boredom-related timing effects. Future research could manipulate task engagement systematically to test interactions between boredom proneness and task characteristics. For example, comparing a passive waiting condition, a simple repetitive task like number circling, and a more engaging cognitive challenge could reveal whether boredom-proneness effects emerge only under certain task demands. Including both prospective and retrospective timing measures within the same participants would allow direct tests of whether trait boredom differentially affects these forms of temporal judgment. Additionally, employing physiological indices (e.g., heart rate variability, skin conductance) could help determine whether arousal mediates any relation between boredom proneness and timing.

Another promising direction is to examine whether individual differences in internal clock speed, rather than boredom proneness per se, account for variability in timing performance. The pacemaker-accumulator framework posits that perceived elapsed time depends on the rate at which a pacemaker generates pulses; factors that speed the pacemaker produce overestimations of duration. If boredom proneness correlates with baseline arousal or clock speed, then associations with timing behavior might be mediated by these underlying physiological mechanisms. Measuring baseline arousal and controlling for it statistically could help disentangle direct effects of boredom proneness from those mediated by arousal or clock-rate differences.

The observed association between trait boredom and subjective state ratings during the task confirms that the manipulation was effective in eliciting boredom and that the trait measure was meaningful in this context. The lack of corresponding behavioral effects on timing suggests that subjective feeling and objective timing may be partially dissociable. This finding has theoretical implications: models that link boredom directly to disrupted temporal processing may need to specify under what conditions subjective boredom translates into measurable timing biases. For instance, boredom may have stronger influences on time perception when it is prolonged, when it co-occurs with fatigue, or when it triggers compensatory behaviors such as mind-wandering or active clock-checking.

Limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The sample consisted primarily of young adults, limiting generalizability to other age groups. The single-session laboratory setting may not capture the full range of boredom experiences that occur in naturalistic environments. The use of tertiles to create categorical groups, while useful for interpretability, reduces statistical power compared to continuous analyses and may obscure within-group variation. Finally, the study relied on a single timing method and a single target duration; broader generalizations should therefore be made with caution.

In light of these considerations, several concrete suggestions for future research emerge. First, studies should include both prospective and retrospective timing measures within the same protocol to compare effects directly. Second, employing multiple task contexts with systematically varying levels of engagement will clarify whether boredom proneness interacts with situational characteristics to influence timing. Third, larger samples, or study designs optimized for detecting small effects (e.g., within-subject manipulation of boredom-inducing stimuli), could reveal subtler relations. Fourth, incorporating physiological and cognitive measures (e.g., mind-wandering probes, arousal indices, executive function tests) could elucidate mediating and moderating mechanisms. Finally, longitudinal or ecological momentary assessment designs could examine how person-level boredom proneness relates to time perception and behavior across real-world situations over time.

Theoretical implications of the findings are balanced. On one hand, the absence of significant effects challenges any strong claim that trait boredom proneness straightforwardly disrupts prospective temporal accuracy in simple tasks. On the other hand, the observed small trends in the hypothesized direction and the clear relation between trait and state boredom suggest that boredom-related temporal phenomena are complex and context-dependent. Theory development in this area may benefit from distinguishing between subjective temporal experience, momentary temporal awareness, and behavioral timing accuracy, and from specifying how motivational, attentional, and physiological components of boredom contribute differently to these aspects of time perception.

In conclusion, the present study did not find significant evidence that trait boredom proneness predicts earlier indication of a three-minute interval or reduced absolute accuracy in a simple number circling task, although the descriptive data were directionally consistent with prior expectations. These null findings highlight the importance of carefully specifying the type of timing judgment under investigation and of considering both trait and state components of boredom. Continued research using diverse methods and contexts is needed to delineate the conditions under which boredom proneness affects temporal experience and behavior, and to identify the mechanisms that link motivational states with the human experience of time.
