Introduction

Decision making under conditions of risk is a central topic in psychological research and has broad relevance to everyday life and policy. When individuals face a choice that involves uncertainty, they may base their decision on information that is explicitly provided to them or on information that they have gathered from their own experience. Decisions that rely on explicit descriptive information have been contrasted with decisions that rely on personal experience, and these modes of decision making have been shown to lead to different patterns of choice in many contexts. The distinction between description-based decisions and experience-based decisions has often been referred to as the description-experience dichotomy in the literature. In description-based tasks, all relevant probabilities and outcomes are presented explicitly to the decision maker. In experience-based tasks, probabilities must be inferred from sampling or past encounters with outcomes. The two modes can produce qualitatively different behavior even when the underlying objective probabilities are the same. There has been substantial interest in understanding why these differences arise, and several mechanisms have been proposed to account for the description-experience divergence. One prominent account emphasizes that rare events are overweighted when probabilities are presented descriptively but underweighted when probabilities are learned from experience. Another account highlights differences in sampling, memory, and attention when people learn by experience, which can lead to biased estimates of frequencies. Yet another explanation notes that affective responses and the salience of outcomes may vary across description and experience and thereby influence choice. A further line of work suggests that differences between description and experience may interact with the nature of the decision itself, such as whether the domain is financial, health, or social. Much of the empirical work investigating the description-experience gap has been conducted in financial or gambling contexts. There has been less systematic work on whether the description-experience divergence generalizes to consequential domains such as medical decisions, where affect, moral concerns, and perceptions of harm might alter choice. Medical dilemmas can differ from financial dilemmas not only in terms of the stakes but also in how people emotionally and cognitively process the options. It is therefore plausible that the mode of information acquisition could have different effects in medical contexts compared with financial contexts. The literature on domain differences in risky choice indicates that people are often more risk averse for personal health and safety than for monetary outcomes, though this pattern is not universal and can depend on framing, magnitude, and cultural context. A further complication is that experimental methodology in the description-experience literature varies considerably across studies, and methodological choices can influence whether and how the description-experience divergence is observed. For example, how probabilistic information is sampled, the number of sampling opportunities, whether samples are drawn with replacement, and whether sampling is incentivized can all affect learning and subsequent choices. Similarly, the balance of risk levels offered across trials can shape observed patterns if some trials are substantially more or less risky than others. These methodological features can interact with domain-specific responses to produce heterogeneous results. Given the mixed evidence about the robustness of the description-experience gap across domains, it is important to test whether the mode of information acquisition interacts with the nature of the dilemma, and to examine how trial-level features shape choice. Several studies have compared choices made from explicit probability descriptions with choices made after sampling in contexts where the outcomes are monetary gains or losses. Those studies often find systematic divergences between description and experience, but they also reveal sensitivity to details of the task. Other work that applied similar methods to health-related choices reported either attenuated description-experience differences or outcomes that depended on the precise framing of the medical problem. A perennial challenge is that hypothetical dilemmas, which are commonly used in health decision research, can elicit different patterns of choice compared with real or incentivized tasks. It is therefore valuable to investigate description versus experience effects in hypothetical medical dilemmas while carefully controlling for task structure and trial features. A particular methodological issue that has emerged in the literature is the role of trial-level balance in risk. When experimental trials vary in their objective levels of risk, participants may respond to that variation in ways that complicate interpretation of main effects. If some trials disproportionately invite risk taking or risk aversion, aggregate measures that average over unbalanced trials may obscure true effects of interest or lead to false positive conclusions. Thus, a focused examination of trial-level effects is an important complement to tests of main effects of description versus experience and domain differences. The current experiment was designed to address these gaps by comparing decisions from description and decisions from experience in two domains: hypothetical medical dilemmas and hypothetical financial dilemmas. The study used an opportunity sample of adults recruited from a university population and divided participants into two conditions that varied the mode of information acquisition. Participants in the description condition were explicitly told the probability of each outcome on each trial. Participants in the experience condition were given the opportunity to sample outcomes using cards that reflected the true distribution for the trial, and then to make a choice. The dilemmas across trials were constructed so that on each trial participants chose between a certain, safer outcome and a probabilistic, riskier outcome with a specified distribution. The design included multiple trials of each domain so that within-subject contrasts between medical and financial dilemmas could be examined. The primary goals of the study were threefold. First, the experiment sought to test whether decisions from description versus experience lead to differential risk taking when choices concern medical outcomes. Second, the study examined whether any description-experience differences are similar in medical and financial domains or whether domain moderates the effect. Third, the experiment investigated trial-level effects to determine whether risk taking changes systematically across successive trials, and whether such trial effects might account for variability in aggregate measures. Based on prior findings in the literature, it was reasonable to anticipate that decisions from description might promote different weighting of rare events compared with decisions from experience, which in turn could affect the probability of selecting the risky option. It was also plausible that medical dilemmas would elicit greater risk aversion than financial dilemmas, though prior work provides mixed predictions on this point. Finally, the study allowed exploration of whether risk taking increases, decreases, or remains stable across trials, and whether the pattern differs by condition or domain. The present work therefore extends existing research by juxtaposing the description-experience contrast with domain-specific dilemmas and by paying attention to trial-level dynamics. The study also has pragmatic implications: if trial imbalance shapes observed effects, then future experiments should adopt more balanced trial structures to obtain clearer evidence about how information mode and domain influence risky choice. The remaining sections describe in detail the methods used in the study, the analytic approach, the key results, and an interpretation of those results in the context of the wider literature. The article concludes with recommendations for future research and for methodological refinements that may strengthen inference about description-experience differences in varied decision domains.

Methods

Participants were 72 adults recruited from a university participant pool and nearby community postings. The sample included both students and staff as well as community volunteers who responded to advertisements. Participant ages ranged from 18 to 64 years, with a mean age of 27.4 years. There were 41 females and 31 males in the sample. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and were fluent in English. Participation was voluntary and participants provided informed consent before beginning the experiment. The study procedures were approved by the institutional ethics committee, and all participants were debriefed at the end of the experiment. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two between-subjects conditions with equal allocation: the decisions from description condition (n = 36) and the decisions from experience condition (n = 36). The two conditions were identical in all respects except for how probabilistic information was conveyed. The experiment used a within-subject manipulation of dilemma domain. Each participant completed trials that involved medical dilemmas and trials that involved financial dilemmas. There were six trials of each domain for a total of twelve choice trials per participant. The order of trials was randomized for each participant to reduce order effects and to distribute potential sequence confounds across participants. Each trial presented a binary choice between a safer, certain option and a riskier, probabilistic option. The safer option provided a moderate outcome that was certain. The riskier option provided either a larger positive outcome or a smaller negative or zero outcome depending on the trial, with an objectively specified probability distribution. The six trials within each domain were constructed to cover a range of probabilities and magnitudes so that some trials were more favorable to risk taking and others favored the safe option. In the description condition, participants were presented with written information on-screen specifying the probabilities and outcomes for each option. For example, a medical trial might state that the safe option would save 30 out of 100 patients for sure, while the risky option would save 80 patients with probability 0.25 and 20 patients with probability 0.75. The numerical probabilities were presented in a clear format alongside the outcomes so that participants could see the objective distribution without needing to sample. In the financial domain, the descriptive framing analogously conveyed monetary outcomes and their probabilities. In the experience condition, participants were given a deck of cards for each trial that contained outcome exemplars reflecting the true distribution for the trial. Each deck had 100 cards to correspond to percentage probabilities, and cards were combined in sealed envelopes so that participants could draw cards without replacement until they felt familiar with the distribution. Participants were instructed that the deck contained the correct mixture of outcomes and that they could draw as many cards as they wished up to ten draws per trial before making a choice. They were told that draws were informative and reflected the true underlying distribution. The maximum of ten draws was selected to balance participant burden and the need for sampling to learn probabilities. Cards showed concrete exemplars of the outcomes (for example, cards in a medical trial might indicate either "save 80 patients" or "save 20 patients" depending on the trial's possible outcomes). After sampling, participants made their choice between the safe and risky options. The experience condition therefore required participants to infer probabilities from sampling, while the description condition removed the sampling requirement by providing explicit probabilities. To ensure comprehension, all participants answered a short control question after the first two trials that asked them to restate either the probability of the risky outcome (description) or to estimate the frequency they had observed (experience). Participants who failed the control comprehension check received corrective feedback and were allowed to continue. The primary dependent measure was choice on each trial coded as risky = 1 and safe = 0. Secondary measures included the number of draws in the experience condition, self-reported confidence in each choice on a 1 to 7 scale, and reaction time from trial onset to choice. Participants in both conditions completed a brief demographics questionnaire and a short measure of risk attitude unrelated to the experimental choices to allow exploratory correlational analyses. The experiment was conducted in a quiet laboratory setting on desktop computers. Trial presentation and response recording were controlled by commercial experimental software. Participants were not financially incentivized on the basis of their trial choices because the dilemmas were hypothetical in nature and some involved nonmonetary outcomes (health). However, participants received a fixed participation fee for taking part in the experiment. The planned statistical analysis involved mixed-effects models and repeated-measures ANOVA to test the effects of condition (between-subjects), domain (within-subjects), and trial (within-subjects). The principal test was a 2 (condition: description vs experience) x 2 (domain: medical vs financial) x 6 (trial index within domain) mixed ANOVA on the binary choice measure aggregated to proportions, with Greenhouse-Geisser correction applied when sphericity assumptions were violated. Follow-up analyses included trend tests across trials and pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction. Additional exploratory analyses assessed sampling behavior in the experience condition and relationships between sampling and choice. All statistical tests used an alpha level of .05 as the nominal threshold for statistical significance. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta-squared for ANOVA tests and Cohen's d for pairwise comparisons where applicable. Confidence intervals (95%) were provided for key descriptive statistics.

Results

Analyses began with descriptive statistics summarizing the proportion of risky choices by condition and by domain. Across all trials and participants the overall mean proportion of risky choices was 0.42 with a standard deviation of 0.21. In the description condition the mean proportion of risky choices averaged across trials and domains was 0.44 (SD = 0.22). In the experience condition the mean proportion of risky choices averaged across trials and domains was 0.40 (SD = 0.20). For the medical domain the mean proportion of risky choices was 0.41 (SD = 0.23). For the financial domain the mean proportion of risky choices was 0.43 (SD = 0.21). These descriptive trends suggested modest differences between conditions and domains but no large disparities. The planned mixed ANOVA tested the main effects of condition and domain and the within-domain trial effect. The omnibus 2 x 2 x 6 mixed ANOVA on proportion of risky choices revealed no significant main effect of condition. Specifically, the main effect of condition (description vs experience) was F(1,70) = 1.23, p = 0.27, partial eta-squared = 0.017. This indicated that the between-subject manipulation of information mode did not have a robust effect on the overall proportion of risky choices. The main effect of domain (medical vs financial) was also not significant, F(1,70) = 2.03, p = 0.16, partial eta-squared = 0.028, indicating that aggregated risk taking did not differ substantially between the medical and financial dilemmas. In contrast, there was a significant main effect of trial index within domain. The effect of trial was evaluated across the six trials of each domain and produced F(5,350) = 7.46, p < 0.001, partial eta-squared = 0.096. This demonstrated systematic variation in the likelihood of choosing the risky option across the sequence of trials. Follow-up tests examined the shape of the trial effect. A polynomial trend analysis indicated a significant linear trend across trials, F(1,70) = 18.12, p < 0.001, partial eta-squared = 0.206, reflecting a general decline in the proportion of risky choices as participants progressed through trials. Quadratic and higher-order trends were non-significant, suggesting the dominant pattern was monotonic. Pairwise comparisons between the first and subsequent trials revealed that the first trial in each domain elicited a significantly higher rate of risky choice than later trials. For example, comparing trial 1 to trial 6, the proportion of risky choices dropped from 0.54 on trial 1 to 0.36 on trial 6 (mean difference = 0.18, 95% CI [0.09, 0.27], t(71) = 4.14, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 0.49). The same sequential decline appeared within each domain when analyzed separately. Tests for interactions between condition and trial, and between domain and trial, were conducted to determine whether the trial effect differed by information mode or by domain. The interaction between condition and trial was not significant, F(5,350) = 0.84, p = 0.53, partial eta-squared = 0.012. The interaction between domain and trial was similarly non-significant, F(5,350) = 1.01, p = 0.41, partial eta-squared = 0.014. The three-way interaction among condition, domain, and trial was also non-significant, F(5,350) = 0.56, p = 0.73. These null interactions indicate that the trial-level decline in risk taking was robust across both description and experience conditions and across both medical and financial dilemmas. Given the descriptive literature predicting underweighting of rare events in experience-based tasks, an additional set of analyses examined trials that involved rare but large payoffs relative to those that involved more balanced probabilities. These secondary analyses compared risky choice proportions on trials with rare high-payoff outcomes (e.g., 0.10 probability of a large gain) versus trials with more moderate probabilities (e.g., 0.50). Across conditions, risky choices were numerically higher on rare-high-payoff trials in the description condition (mean proportion risky = 0.47) than in the experience condition (mean proportion risky = 0.42), but this difference was not statistically significant, t(70) = 1.05, p = 0.30. The absence of a reliable description-experience difference in these targeted comparisons suggests that, in the current experimental implementation, the classic pattern of overweighting rare events in description was not observed. Exploratory mixed-effects logistic regression was also conducted at the trial-by-trial level with random intercepts for participants. Predictor variables included condition (coded 0/1), domain (coded 0/1), trial number (1-6 within domain), and the trial's objective probability of the risky outcome. The logistic model confirmed that trial number was a significant predictor of choosing the risky option (odds ratio = 0.93 per trial increment, 95% CI [0.90, 0.96], Wald z = -4.52, p < 0.001), consistent with the ANOVA results showing a decline. Condition and domain were not significant predictors in the logistic model (condition OR = 0.86, p = 0.25; domain OR = 0.94, p = 0.39), and interactions were non-significant. The logistic model also showed that the objective probability of the risky outcome positively predicted risky choice, as expected (OR = 1.12 per 0.10 increase in probability, p < 0.001). In the experience condition, the number of samples drawn by participants before making a choice varied across trials. The mean number of draws was 6.4 (SD = 2.8) across all trials in the experience condition. A small number of participants (n = 5) sampled only once or twice on several trials, whereas the majority used between five and ten draws. An exploratory correlation between mean number of draws and overall proportion of risky choices in the experience condition yielded r = 0.08, p = 0.64, indicating no reliable relationship between sampling intensity and risk taking at the individual level. Sampling behavior did not systematically change across trials; mean draws on trial 1 was 6.6 and on trial 6 was 6.2, a non-significant decline, t(35) = 1.02, p = 0.31. Confidence ratings provided by participants after each choice were analyzed as a secondary dependent variable. Mean confidence on choices was 4.9 on the 1–7 scale (SD = 1.1) and did not differ by condition, t(70) = 0.73, p = 0.47. Confidence declined modestly across trials, mirroring the decline in risky choice, but the effect on confidence was small and only marginally significant in the repeated-measures test, F(5,350) = 2.05, p = 0.07. Reaction times were also examined and showed no robust differences by condition or domain. Median reaction time across trials and participants was 3.2 seconds and reaction times became slightly faster over successive trials, consistent with practice effects rather than substantive changes in deliberation. Finally, manipulation checks confirmed that participants in the description condition accurately recalled the presented probabilities at a high rate after the first two trials, and that most participants in the experience condition reported that the card decks felt representative of the underlying distributions. Specifically, 92% of participants in the description condition answered the comprehension question correctly after corrective feedback had been provided, and 86% of participants in the experience condition reported that their draws felt representative. In sum, the results indicate no significant main effects of description versus experience or domain on aggregate risk taking, but a clear and significant decline in risky choice across trials that was consistent across conditions and domains.

Discussion

The present experiment compared decisions from description with decisions from experience using hypothetical medical and financial dilemmas. The study employed a mixed design in which mode of information acquisition was manipulated between subjects and domain and trial were manipulated within subjects. The main empirical findings were clear. First, there was no significant main effect of condition: participants in the description condition did not differ reliably in overall risk taking from participants in the experience condition. Second, there was no significant main effect of domain: overall proportions of risky choices did not differ reliably between medical and financial dilemmas in this sample and task. Third, and most notably, there was a significant effect of trial index: participants displayed a systematic decline in the propensity to choose the risky option across successive trials. Fourth, interactions among condition, domain, and trial were not significant, indicating that the trial-level decline was robust across information mode and domain. These outcomes have several implications for understanding the description-experience contrast, for designing experiments in the risky choice literature, and for interpreting domain-specific differences in decision making. The null effect of condition contrasts with many prior demonstrations of a description-experience divergence, where descriptive probabilities often produced different choice patterns than experience-based sampling. Several reasons might explain why the classic description-experience divergence did not emerge in the present study. One possibility is that the present implementation of the experience condition allowed relatively generous sampling (up to ten draws) and used decks that closely represented the objective distributions, which may have enabled participants to form accurate frequency estimates. When experience-based learning results in reasonably accurate internal models of the probabilities, differences from descriptive information may be attenuated. In other words, the description-experience gap is often most pronounced when sampling is limited and rare events are under-sampled; in the present study, sampling was sufficient for many participants to approximate the descriptive probabilities. A related explanation is that the hypothetical nature of the dilemmas reduced motivational differences between description and experience. In studies with real monetary incentives, the stakes and tangible feedback can amplify behavioral differences; with hypothetical scenarios participants may treat both modes as intrinsically similar, thereby reducing divergence. Another consideration is task framing and the construction of the trials. The current task used a set of six trials per domain that varied in probability and payoff structure. It is plausible that the particular selection of trial parameters produced a mixture of trials where description and experience naturally led to similar choices. For example, if rare-event trials were few or if rare events were not sufficiently extreme to induce marked overweighting in description, the aggregate condition effect could be small. This possibility points to the more general role of trial-level features in shaping the presence or absence of a description-experience gap. The strong trial effect observed in the current data highlights an underappreciated methodological issue. Participants became progressively less likely to choose the risky option as they progressed through trials. This monotonic decline suggests that trial order or the specific distribution of risk across trials can be a potent driver of observed choice patterns. Several mechanisms could underlie this decline. One candidate is habituation or learned caution: after experiencing a few trials in which risky options yielded less favorable outcomes or greater variance, participants may adopt a more conservative response set. Even in the description condition where outcomes were not sampled, participants may generalize across trials and adopt a cautious default as the experiment proceeds. Another mechanism is response fatigue or cognitive load leading to defaulting to safer options; however, reaction time evidence did not strongly support increasing cognitive shortcuts, and confidence did not decline dramatically, so fatigue is a less compelling explanation. A third possibility is that the specific sequence of trial risk levels contributed to the decline. If early trials happened, by chance or design, to be more favorable to the risky option, participants might start with a higher baseline of risk taking and then adjust downward when later trials present less attractive risk-reward tradeoffs. The polynomial trend analysis favored a linear decline rather than a more complex pattern, which is consistent with a gradual calibration or learning process across trials. The absence of interactions between trial and condition suggests that the trial-level decline is not uniquely tied to experiential sampling but is instead a more general experimental artifact or psychological process. This finding carries practical implications. Researchers designing experiments that compare description and experience should be careful to balance trial risk levels and to randomize sequences in ways that avoid systematic drift in the mean riskiness of stimuli across time. Failure to do so could result in misleading main effects or in attenuated differences between conditions. More generally, aggregation across unbalanced trials can obscure true effects or create spurious ones. The present results thus align with a methodological recommendation: each experimental trial set should be balanced in terms of objective risk so that sequence and trial-level dynamics do not confound condition comparisons. The lack of domain differences between medical and financial dilemmas in the current study is noteworthy because many researchers assume that medical decisions elicit distinct risk preferences due to heightened affect or ethical salience. Several explanations could account for the null domain effect. It is possible that when choices are presented in tightly controlled, binary formats and when probabilities are either clearly presented or reasonably inferred from sampling, domain-specific emotional responses are attenuated. Alternatively, the hypothetical framing may have dampened the visceral impact of medical dilemmas compared with real-world medical decisions, thereby reducing domain differences. It is also possible that the particular medical and financial scenarios chosen were matched too closely in terms of psychological distance, concreteness, and perceived stakes, thereby eliciting similar risk propensities. Future research could examine domain effects more sensitively by including personally relevant medical scenarios, stronger affective framing, or real consequences tied to health-related choices. The exploratory analyses of sampling behavior in the experience condition provided limited evidence that sampling intensity predicted subsequent risk taking. The weak and non-significant correlation suggests that merely drawing more samples up to the provided cap did not systematically alter risk preferences. This again suggests that many participants achieved sufficient sampling with relatively few draws, or that other individual differences govern the relationship between sampling and choice. For instance, prior beliefs, numeracy, and need for cognition might moderate how sampling translates into choice, and these factors were not extensively measured here. The current study has several limitations that should inform interpretation. The use of a convenience sample limits generalizability to broader populations, including older adults and clinical samples who might respond differently to medical dilemmas. The hypothetical nature of the dilemmas also constrains ecological validity, particularly for medical decisions that in real life involve personal stakes, moral responsibility, and often social input. The cap on sampling in the experience condition (ten draws) was pragmatic but arbitrary, and different caps might encourage different learning strategies. Similarly, the decision not to incentivize choices was partly motivated by the inclusion of nonmonetary medical outcomes, but incentives can alter risk-taking behavior and might interact with information mode. The study design also included multiple trials and randomization, but even with randomization the particular mix of trial risk levels may have created an ordering effect that manifested as the observed decline. Future work could preregister fully counterbalanced sequences or adopt equated trial sets in which the average objective risk is held constant across time. Building on the present findings, several directions for future research are promising. One priority is to test the description-experience contrast with stronger manipulations of sampling availability, such as limited sampling versus ample sampling, and with direct comparisons of sampling with and without replacement. This would help clarify when experience-based underweighting of rare events emerges. Another avenue is to apply incentivized outcomes to both medical and financial scenarios, perhaps by linking financial payoffs to real compensation and by using vignettes tied to charitable donations or public health decisions to approximate the consequences of medical choices. Researchers could also investigate individual differences that moderate description-experience effects, such as numeracy, statistical reasoning ability, affective responsiveness, and prior personal experience with medical issues. Computational approaches offer another fruitful path: reinforcement learning and sampling models can be fit to trial-by-trial choices to distinguish between processes such as updating of subjective probabilities, exploration-exploitation tradeoffs, and heuristic use. Longitudinal designs that examine how description and experience lead to stable preferences over time would also be informative. From a practical perspective, the highlighted trial effect suggests that practitioners and policymakers should be cautious in generalizing from experiments that do not carefully balance trial-level properties. If trial design can materially influence observed choice patterns, then recommendations based on experimental comparisons must account for possible artifacts. For example, health communications that present probabilities descriptively may not always produce different public responses than strategies that encourage personal experience or simulations, particularly if recipients are able to sample or simulate outcomes to form their own judgments. The theoretical implications of the present null results for the description-experience gap are nuanced. The absence of a robust overall condition effect does not refute the descriptive reality that information mode can alter choice; rather, it underscores that the effect is contingent on task parameters, sampling dynamics, and possibly participant motivation. The trial-level dynamics observed here further suggest that single-test contrasts between description and experience may be insufficient; richer, temporally sensitive analyses are needed. In conclusion, the current experiment contributes to the literature on risky choice by directly comparing description and experience modes in medical and financial domains and by illuminating the importance of trial-level effects. The key empirical takeaways are that the mode of information acquisition did not, in this implementation, produce large differences in overall risk taking, that medical and financial dilemmas yielded similar aggregate choices, and that risky choice declined systematically across trials regardless of condition or domain. Methodologically, the work emphasizes the need to balance trials and to consider sequence effects in experimental design. Theoretically, the results caution against assuming a ubiquitous description-experience divergence and encourage more nuanced investigations that probe the boundary conditions of this phenomenon. Future research that manipulates sampling availability, incentive structure, and the emotional salience of dilemmas, and that employs computational modeling, will help to clarify when and why description and experience produce divergent choices.
