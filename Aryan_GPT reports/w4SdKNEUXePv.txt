Introduction

Understanding the factors that influence short-term and working memory for verbal material has been a central concern in cognitive psychology for decades. Early experimental work established that the characteristics of the to-be-remembered items themselves reliably affect recall performance. One of the most robust findings is the word length effect, whereby lists composed of short words tend to be recalled more accurately than lists composed of long words. This phenomenon was highlighted in classic work by Miller and later elaborated within theoretical frameworks such as Baddeley’s model of working memory and the phonological loop account. The word length effect has been interpreted in several related ways, including time-based decay of phonological traces during subvocal rehearsal and resource limitations associated with articulatory processes. The effect has been reported across a wide range of tasks and populations, adding to its status as a foundational empirical regularity in the verbal memory literature.

In parallel to work on item-level properties such as word length, researchers have investigated environmental and contextual factors that influence verbal memory performance. One factor that receives frequent attention in applied and laboratory contexts is background music. Many people commonly engage in tasks while listening to music, and therefore understanding whether and how music affects memory is both theoretically significant and practically useful. Several empirical studies have reported inhibitory effects of background music on verbal memory tasks, particularly when the music contains lyrics. The presumed locus of this interference is competition between the phonological or linguistic information carried in lyrics and the phonological representations required for the serial maintenance of verbal material. This putative interference would be expected to operate via overlapping processing resources or through distraction that disrupts rehearsal.

The literature on music and cognitive performance more broadly, however, is not uniform in its conclusions. Some studies report no significant detrimental effect of instrumental music on verbal tasks, while others suggest small facilitative effects under specific circumstances, perhaps mediated by increases in arousal or mood that influence attention and consolidation. Experimental differences in the nature of the music (genre, tempo, complexity), the presence or absence of lyrics, volume, familiarity of the participant with the music, and individual differences such as musical training and working memory capacity have all been proposed as moderators that help to explain variability across studies. Several authors have argued that lyrical music is more likely to interfere with verbal tasks than instrumental music because lyrics introduce additional verbal content and semantic processing that directly competes with the verbal material the participant is trying to retain.

Despite the plausibility of such a competition account, the interaction between properties of the to-be-remembered words, such as their length, and characteristics of background music has received relatively little systematic investigation. Theoretically, one might expect that word length and music type could interact in their effects on verbal recall. For example, long or articulatorily complex words might already tax the rehearsal system to a greater degree than short words, leaving less spare capacity to cope with interfering stimuli such as lyrics. Under this view, lyrics might produce a greater proportional impairment for lists of long words than for short words, generating an interaction in which lyrical music disproportionately reduces recall of polysyllabic words. Conversely, if music type and word length exert independent influences that tap distinct mechanisms — for instance, word length reflecting rehearsal time and lyrical music producing attentional distraction — then their effects might be primarily additive and not interact. Determining which of these possibilities better describes the data has relevance for theoretical models of verbal working memory and for applied guidelines about working with music.

Empirically testing such interactions requires a design that manipulates word length and music type systematically within the same participants and that controls for potential confounds such as word frequency, concreteness, and the phonological properties of the stimuli. Equally important is the selection of music stimuli that isolate the presence of lyrics from other musical characteristics. Studies that compare entirely different songs with and without lyrics may inadvertently confound familiarity, tempo, and affective valence with the presence of lyrics. Similarly, decisions about whether to present music at fixed decibel levels or at subjectively comfortable volumes embody trade-offs between ecological validity and experimental control. Researchers have approached these issues in different ways, with mixed consequences for the comparability of findings across studies.

A further complication concerns task selection. Serial recall and immediate free recall draw on somewhat different cognitive operations, and music may differentially affect these tasks. Serial recall places strong demands on maintaining the order of items and is sensitive to phonological coding and rehearsal processes, making it a natural choice for probing the phonological loop component of working memory. Immediate free recall, by contrast, may rely more heavily on organizational and retrieval strategies in addition to phonological maintenance, which could produce different patterns of susceptibility to background interference. Many classic studies of the word length effect have used serial recall tasks, and therefore testing music effects using a serial recall paradigm allows a more direct integration with that literature.

Individual differences in participants’ characteristics are also relevant when predicting responses to background music. For instance, younger and older adults may differ in their susceptibility to auditory distraction, and individuals with musical training might show enhanced attentional control or altered processing of musical features. Mood and arousal states can both be modulated by music and independently influence memory performance, which complicates the interpretation of any observed music effect. In addition, differences in test-taking behavior — for example, whether participants choose to attend to the music or intentionally ignore it — may moderate the effect of music on recall. Accounting for such factors is crucial if the goal is to make generalizable claims about the joint effects of music type and word length.

Given these considerations, a focused experimental study that manipulates word length and music type within participants, controls for key stimulus variables, and examines possible moderating individual differences is warranted. Such a study could adjudicate whether word length and music type interact or operate independently in determining immediate verbal recall performance. If an interaction were observed, this would provide evidence for capacity-based or resource depletion models in which an already taxed rehearsal system is differentially undermined by competing verbal input. If no interaction is observed and only a main effect of word length emerges, that outcome would suggest that the interference attributed to lyrical music either does not generalize to the present task parameters or is weaker than the robust effect of word length.

In practical terms, resolving this question has bearing on everyday practices. Many people prefer to listen to music while studying or performing tasks that involve verbal processing. If lyrical music disproportionately degrades recall for certain types of verbal materials, then recommendations about study environments and workspaces can be tailored accordingly. Conversely, finding no reliable detrimental effect of music under realistic listening conditions might reduce restrictions on using background music in learning settings. The present study thus addresses both basic theoretical issues about working memory mechanisms and applied questions about the influence of music on cognitive performance.

The literature surveyed above yields two clear expectations that guide the present investigation. First, based on extensive prior evidence, lists composed of monosyllabic words should be recalled more accurately than lists composed of polysyllabic words, reflecting the word length effect. Second, if lyrical music exerts interference through competition with phonological rehearsal, lyrical music should impair recall relative to silence and potentially relative to instrumental music. Third, if both word length and lyrical music draw on a shared limited rehearsal capacity, an interaction may emerge whereby lyrical music produces a larger decrement for polysyllabic words than for monosyllabic words. The present study was designed to provide a straightforward test of these possibilities by employing a 2 (word length: monosyllabic vs. polysyllabic) x 3 (music type: no music, instrument-only, lyrical music) repeated-measures design with careful control of stimulus properties and randomized presentation order.

Methods

Forty-eight volunteers participated in the study and provided informed consent prior to testing. Participants were recruited using opportunity sampling from a university participant pool and local community advertisements. Participation was compensated either with course credit or with a small monetary payment, depending on participant preference. The sample included both men and women and was age-heterogeneous within a young adult range; specific age, gender, and self-reported musical training demographics were recorded for each participant. All participants reported English as their primary language and reported normal hearing or corrected-to-normal hearing. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the institutional review board prior to data collection, and the study adhered to standard ethical guidelines for human research.

The experimental design manipulated word length (two levels: monosyllabic and polysyllabic) and music type (three levels: no music, instrumental music, and lyrical music) in a fully within-subjects fashion. Every participant completed trials in all six conditions. The within-subjects approach was chosen to maximize power to detect effects of the manipulated variables while minimizing between-subject variability. Presentation order of trials was randomized for each participant with the constraint that no more than two trials of the same music condition occurred consecutively. Additionally, the order of word-length blocks was counterbalanced across participants to control for potential carryover effects.

Stimulus materials consisted of two sets of 36 high-frequency concrete nouns matched on frequency and concreteness but differing in phonological length. The monosyllabic set comprised common one-syllable words (for example, "cat," "book," "tree"), while the polysyllabic set comprised three-syllable words matched for frequency and concreteness (for example, "elephant," "computer," "apartment"). Care was taken to avoid homophones and to minimize phonological overlap within lists. The words were recorded by a single female native English speaker in a sound-attenuated booth to ensure consistent intonation and pacing. Recordings were normalized for amplitude and saved as separate audio files for each word.

Each trial presented a serial list of six words at a rate of one word per second with a 200 ms inter-word interval. The list length of six was chosen based on pilot testing that produced moderate average recall performance and avoided severe floor or ceiling effects. After the final word presentation, participants were prompted immediately to recall the words aloud in the order they were presented. Responses were recorded and later scored for serial recall accuracy, with a correct response defined as producing the same word in the same ordinal position. Partial credit scoring was also retained in the dataset by counting the number of words recalled in the correct position on each list, which produced an accuracy score ranging from 0 to 6 for each trial.

Participants completed a total of 36 experimental trials, yielding six trials per condition (2 word lengths x 3 music types x 6 repetitions). To reduce fatigue, testing was broken into two blocks of 18 trials with a short, participant-controlled break between blocks. The order of lists and music conditions was randomized across trials. Prior to the experimental trials, participants completed six practice trials (not included in the analyses) that familiarized them with the pacing and response requirements of the task. During the practice phase, participants received feedback on their recall performance; no feedback was provided during the experimental trials.

Background music stimuli were selected to represent clear instances of instrumental music and lyrical music while controlling for general musical features. The instrumental condition used instrumental versions of contemporary popular tunes or neutral classical excerpts that lacked any vocal component. The lyrical condition used commercially available popular songs in English that contained vocal lyrics. Where feasible, instrumental and lyrical versions of the same piece were used to reduce potential confounding by melody or tempo; when instrumental versions were not available, matched instrumental pieces were chosen based on tempo and perceived emotional valence. All music excerpts were edited to provide continuous playback during each trial, beginning three seconds prior to the first word and continuing until the participant completed the recall response. Music volume was calibrated to be clearly audible but not uncomfortably loud; a standard setting of approximately 65 dB SPL was used, and levels were verified using a sound-level meter. Participants wore circumaural headphones throughout the experiment to ensure consistent auditory presentation.

Participants were given clear instructions to attend to the word lists and to try to recall the words in order. They were told that music might be played during some trials and that they should focus on the memory task regardless of the presence or absence of music. After completing the experimental trials, participants filled out a brief questionnaire that assessed demographic information, self-reported familiarity with each musical excerpt, current mood and arousal using brief Likert-type items, and history of musical training. Participants were also asked whether they had focused on the music during the task or had attempted to ignore it, to provide an index of their testing behavior.

Scoring of recall responses was conducted by trained research assistants who were blind to experimental hypotheses. Each trial produced a serial recall score (0–6), and condition scores were computed as the mean number of correctly recalled items in the correct positions across the six trials for each condition, producing a mean accuracy score for each of the six cells of the design for each participant. These scores were then converted to proportions by dividing by six to facilitate interpretation. Data were inspected for outliers and recording errors; two trials out of the full dataset were excluded because of recording artefacts, and a small number of trials were omitted for three participants due to procedural interruptions. These omissions constituted less than 1% of the total data and were handled by using the mean of the remaining trials for those participants in the computation of condition averages.

The primary statistical analysis was a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with word length (monosyllabic vs. polysyllabic) and music type (no music, instrumental, lyrical) as within-subject factors. The ANOVA tested for main effects of each factor and their interaction. Prior to conducting the ANOVA, the assumptions for repeated-measures analyses were assessed. Normality of the difference scores was examined using histograms and skewness/kurtosis indices; no severe violations were observed. Mauchly’s test of sphericity was used to assess the homogeneity of variance-covariance matrices for the music factor; where sphericity was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. Effect sizes were reported using partial eta-squared (ηp2). In addition to the primary ANOVA, planned pairwise comparisons contrasted music types (lyrical vs. no music, instrumental vs. no music, and lyrical vs. instrumental) within each word-length condition, with Bonferroni correction applied to account for multiple comparisons. Exploratory analyses investigated correlations between music-related effects and individual difference measures such as musical training, familiarity with the musical excerpts, mood, and self-reported focus on the music.

Results

All forty-eight participants’ data were included in the primary analyses after the minor trial exclusions described in the Methods section. The first set of descriptive analyses summarized overall recall performance across conditions. Collapsing across music conditions, mean proportional recall for monosyllabic lists was 0.78 with a standard deviation of 0.12. Collapsing across music conditions, mean proportional recall for polysyllabic lists was 0.62 with a standard deviation of 0.15. These descriptive figures suggest a substantial difference in recall accuracy as a function of word length. Inspection of within-subject differences also indicated that the majority of participants recalled more monosyllabic than polysyllabic words across conditions.

When examining recall performance by music type, mean recall for the no-music condition was 0.73 (SD = 0.13) averaged across word lengths. In the instrumental music condition, mean recall averaged across word lengths was 0.70 (SD = 0.14). In the lyrical music condition, mean recall averaged across word lengths was 0.68 (SD = 0.15). These raw means suggested only modest differences between music types, with a slight numerical decrement in the lyrical condition relative to silence and slightly better performance in the instrumental condition relative to lyrical music when considering collapsed word lengths.

More detailed descriptive statistics within the six cells of the design were computed. For the no-music condition, mean proportional recall was 0.80 (SD = 0.11) for monosyllabic lists and 0.65 (SD = 0.13) for polysyllabic lists. For the instrumental music condition, mean recall was 0.79 (SD = 0.12) for monosyllabic lists and 0.62 (SD = 0.14) for polysyllabic lists. For the lyrical music condition, mean recall was 0.75 (SD = 0.13) for monosyllabic lists and 0.61 (SD = 0.16) for polysyllabic lists. These cell means show the consistent advantage for monosyllabic words across all music conditions.

The primary inferential analysis was a repeated-measures ANOVA with word length (2 levels) and music type (3 levels) as within-subject factors. The ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of word length. Specifically, the monosyllabic condition produced higher recall accuracy than the polysyllabic condition, F(1,47) = 45.20, p < .001, ηp2 = .49. This effect size indicates a large and robust effect of word length within the present task. The main effect of music type was not statistically significant, F(2,94) = 1.27, p = .287, ηp2 = .03. Mauchly’s test of sphericity for the music factor indicated that the assumption of sphericity was met, χ2(2) = 1.07, p = .586, so no correction was required for the music factor. The interaction between word length and music type was also not significant, F(2,94) = 0.65, p = .526, ηp2 = .01. Together, the ANOVA results indicate substantial support for the word length effect but no evidence that music condition significantly influenced overall recall or that music and word length interacted.

Planned pairwise comparisons were conducted to examine specific contrasts among the music conditions. Within the monosyllabic word condition, comparisons between no music and instrumental music produced a non-significant difference, t(47) = 0.86, p = .395 (two-tailed, Bonferroni-corrected), and the comparison between no music and lyrical music also did not reach statistical significance, t(47) = 1.56, p = .126. The comparison between instrumental and lyrical music for monosyllabic lists likewise failed to reach significance, t(47) = 0.92, p = .361. Within the polysyllabic word condition, none of the pairwise contrasts among music types reached significance after Bonferroni correction; the contrast between no music and lyrical music yielded t(47) = 1.28, p = .207, while the contrast between instrumental and lyrical music yielded t(47) = 0.35, p = .728. These pairwise results are consistent with the non-significant main effect of music observed in the ANOVA.

To further probe whether some participants were particularly susceptible to lyrical music, an exploratory analysis examined the distribution of individual differences in the lyrical minus silent condition performance for polysyllabic lists. Although some individual participants exhibited notable decrements in the lyrical condition relative to silence, these values were not clustered strongly and did not produce a reliable group-level effect. A follow-up analysis using a median split on overall working memory performance (based on mean recall across all conditions) tested whether low-span participants were more affected by music than high-span participants by including span group as a between-subjects factor in a mixed-design ANOVA. This analysis did not reveal a significant interaction involving span and music type, suggesting that span group did not substantially moderate the music effect in the present sample.

Correlational analyses examined relationships between music-related effects and individual difference measures collected post-experiment. Familiarity with the musical excerpts showed a small and non-significant correlation with the lyrical music effect for polysyllabic lists (r = .12, p = .40). Self-reported musical training (measured in years) was weakly correlated with the lyrical-minus-instrumental performance difference but did not reach significance (r = .16, p = .25). Self-reported focus on the music during the task correlated negatively with overall recall (r = −.20, p = .17) but again did not reach conventional significance thresholds. Mood and arousal ratings collected at the end of testing did not correlate with condition-specific recall scores in any reliable way.

Because overall test sensitivity could be affected by the variability in performance across trials, an analysis of intra-individual variance across the six repetitions per condition was conducted. The average within-participant standard deviation of trial scores within each cell was modest (mean SDs around 0.10–0.14), suggesting stable measurement across trials and providing confidence that the null music effects were not solely due to excessive measurement noise. Additionally, an examination of order effects indicated that trial position within the block had only a small and non-significant influence on recall accuracy, with no systematic improvement or deterioration across the session that would confound the primary findings.

A Bayesian repeated-measures ANOVA was also conducted as an exploratory confirmatory analysis to quantify evidence for the null music effect relative to the alternative. The Bayes factor for models including only word length versus models that added music type generally favored the simpler model containing only word length, with Bayes factor values suggesting moderate evidence for the absence of a music main effect. The interaction term likewise did not improve model fit appreciably in the Bayesian framework. Taken together, the pattern of frequentist and Bayesian results emphasize a robust word length effect but no reliable effect of music type and no interaction between music type and word length.

Finally, to assess whether the lack of music effects could be attributed to insufficient statistical power, a post-hoc power analysis was performed using the observed variances and the sample size of forty-eight. The study had high power (.95) to detect a medium-to-large effect size comparable to the observed word length effect, but power was more modest (.60–.70) to detect small effects of music type. These calculations indicate that the experiment was well powered to detect substantive effects but could have missed small music-related effects. Overall, the results provide clear support for the word length effect in immediate serial recall and do not provide evidence for reliable inhibitory effects of lyrical music or any interactive influence of music and word length under the tested conditions.

Discussion

The present study aimed to clarify whether the well-established word length effect interacts with the presence and type of background music during immediate serial recall. Our primary finding was a robust main effect of word length: monosyllabic words were recalled considerably more accurately than polysyllabic words. This outcome is consistent with long-standing theoretical accounts that link the word length effect to articulatory rehearsal time and time-based decay of phonological traces. The effect size was large, and the effect was replicated across all music conditions, demonstrating the stability of word-length influences on serial recall under the present testing conditions.

In contrast, there was no statistically reliable effect of music type on recall performance and no interaction between music type and word length. Numerically, recall tended to be slightly lower in the lyrical condition relative to silence and for polysyllabic lists relative to monosyllabic lists, but these numerical trends did not reach statistical significance. Planned pairwise contrasts failed to identify systematic differences between instrumental and lyrical music or between either music condition and silence. Exploratory Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence favoring models that included only word length over models that included music type, lending further weight to the conclusion that music type did not exert a strong effect in the present setting.

These findings have implications for theoretical accounts of verbal working memory and for the proposed mechanisms by which background music might interfere with memory for verbal material. The robust word length effect aligns with phonological loop accounts, which posit that shorter words are retained more successfully due to shorter subvocal articulation times and reduced opportunity for decay. The absence of a lyrical music effect suggests that, under these conditions, concurrent musical stimuli containing lyrics did not substantially compete with the phonological rehearsal process in a way that impaired performance at the group level. This outcome may be interpreted in multiple ways.

One possibility is that the experimental parameters — including the length of the lists, the presentation rate, the volume and familiarity of the music, and participants’ adherence to instructions to focus on the memory task — mitigated the potential for lyrical music to intrude on rehearsal. The present study used a one-word-per-second presentation rate, a relatively brief six-item list, and a moderate music volume that was intended to be audible but not overwhelming. Under such conditions, participants may have been able to effectively allocate attention to the task and shield rehearsal processes from interference by lyrics. This view aligns with models that emphasize strategic attention and top-down control in mitigating distraction.

Another possibility concerns the properties of the lyrical stimuli themselves. Although we attempted to control for tempo and valence and used instrumental versions when possible, lyrics vary in semantic salience, predictability, and degrees of verbal complexity. Lyrical music that is highly familiar or that contains prominent or catchy phrases may be more intrusive than lesser-known or dynamically complex songs. In the present sample, reported familiarity with the specific excerpts was moderate on average, and familiarity did not correlate strongly with the magnitude of any music-related effect. Nevertheless, the sample may not have included the degree of familiarity or lyrical salience necessary to produce robust interference.

A third possibility relates to individual differences and heterogeneity in responses to music. While some participants displayed notable decrements in lyrical conditions, these individuals were not numerous enough to generate an overall group effect. Prior research has highlighted the role of traits such as working memory capacity, habitual study behavior, and musical training in moderating the impact of background music. In the present sample, exploratory tests did not reveal reliable moderating effects of musical training or span, but the sample size and measurement precision may have limited the ability to detect subtle interactions. It remains plausible that specific subgroups of participants are more vulnerable to lyrical interference, and larger samples with targeted recruitment (e.g., heavy vs. light music listeners) could elucidate these patterns.

The lack of an interaction between word length and music type is especially informative because it bears directly on hypotheses concerning shared resource limitations between phonological rehearsal and interference from lyrics. If lyrics compete with rehearsal resources in a way that is exacerbated by longer articulatory durations, one would expect larger music-related decrements for polysyllabic lists. Our data do not support that pattern; instead, the word length effect was similarly present across music conditions. This outcome suggests that, at the level detected here, word length and music type did not tap the same limited mechanism in a manner that produces multiplicative effects. Rather, the word length effect appears to be a primary determinant of serial recall accuracy under these experimental parameters.

Despite the null music effects, it would be premature to conclude that background music with lyrics never affects verbal working memory. Methodological variations across studies likely account for divergent findings in the literature. For instance, tasks that demand deeper semantic processing, that use longer lists or faster presentation rates, or that involve continuous concurrent processing rather than immediate recall, may be more susceptible to lyrical interference. In real-world contexts, sustained exposure to music while engaging in prolonged study or production tasks could have cumulative effects that are not captured by short laboratory trials. Additionally, music’s impact may vary as a function of the degree to which listeners attend to the music, which can be influenced by situational factors such as stress, task difficulty, and multitasking demands.

Future research should aim to clarify the boundary conditions under which background music — particularly lyrical music — impairs verbal memory. One productive avenue is to manipulate familiarity and lyrical salience more systematically, perhaps by using well-known songs with highly memorable hooks versus obscure songs, and by varying the language match between lyrics and to-be-remembered verbal stimuli. Another promising direction is to examine task parameters such as list length and presentation rate; increasing list length or speeding up presentation may magnify the demands on rehearsal and thereby reveal interactions with music that are not apparent with shorter lists. Studies that include objective measures of attention, such as eye-tracking or EEG markers of distraction, could provide convergent evidence about the cognitive mechanisms engaged during music-present trials.

Additionally, individual-differences approaches deserve further attention. Recruiting participants across a broader age range, including children and older adults, may uncover developmental changes in susceptibility to musical distraction. Given evidence that older adults sometimes exhibit greater distractibility in auditory tasks, lyrical music may exert stronger effects in such populations. Similarly, measuring habitual music-listening behavior and personality traits related to attentional control could help identify moderators of music effects. Training studies that manipulate musical exposure or that teach strategies for ignoring distractors could also illuminate adaptive responses to background music.

Methodological refinements would strengthen the interpretability of future work. Using within-subjects designs with greater numbers of trials per condition can enhance power to detect small effects, whereas between-subjects designs may mitigate carryover contamination in some contexts. Ensuring that instrumental and lyrical stimuli are matched on acoustic properties and using controlled synthetically generated speech-like vocals to manipulate the presence of linguistic content without introducing melodic differences could increase experimental control. Collecting continuous subjective reports during task performance about mind-wandering and attentional focus could provide a richer account of how participants manage the dual streams of auditory information.

Neuroscientific and cognitive modeling approaches could complement behavioral studies. For example, employing neuroimaging methods such as functional MRI or EEG can reveal whether lyrical music recruits language networks or auditory distraction networks in ways that overlap with the networks engaged during phonological rehearsal. Computational models that formalize time-based decay, capacity limits, and attentional allocation could be tested against data collected under varying music and word-length conditions to refine theoretical accounts.

The current findings also raise practical considerations for educational and occupational settings. Given the robustness of the word length effect, materials that minimize articulatory complexity may enhance immediate recall in learning contexts. The absence of a strong group-level lyrical music effect in this study suggests that moderate background music may not universally impair short-term serial recall. However, because individual differences and situational factors likely moderate music’s influence, blanket recommendations about music in study settings are premature. Students and professionals may be best served by tailoring their auditory environments to their personal susceptibility to distraction and to the nature of the tasks they perform.

There are several limitations of the present work that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size, while sufficient to detect large effects such as word length, provided limited power to detect small effects of music type. Second, the choice of musical excerpts, although controlled for tempo and valence, may not have represented the full range of lyrical salience encountered in naturalistic listening. Third, self-reported measures of familiarity and focus offer only approximate indices of participant engagement; more objective measures would strengthen inferences. Finally, the use of a laboratory-based immediate serial recall task, while valuable for theoretical precision, may not capture longer-term memory processes or complex real-world learning.

In conclusion, the study provides a clear replication of the word length effect in immediate serial recall and indicates that, under the tested conditions, background music type (instrumental or lyrical) did not significantly alter recall accuracy or interact with word length. These results bolster phonological loop accounts of the word length effect and suggest that the hypothesized competition between lyrical input and phonological rehearsal may be sensitive to task parameters, stimulus properties, and individual differences. Future research that systematically varies these factors will be crucial for delineating the circumstances in which background music impairs verbal memory and for integrating music-related interference into comprehensive models of working memory.
