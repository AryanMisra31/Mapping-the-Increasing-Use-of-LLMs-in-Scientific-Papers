Introduction

Working memory is a central construct in cognitive psychology and refers to the system that temporarily holds and manipulates information for ongoing cognitive tasks. Working memory capacity is limited and has been shown to predict performance across a wide range of cognitive domains, including reading comprehension, problem solving, and reasoning. Over the last several decades researchers have sought to elucidate the factors that influence working memory performance, including individual differences in attention, processing speed, and experience-dependent changes in cognitive control. One line of inquiry concerns the relationship between bilingualism and working memory performance. Bilingualism has been proposed to influence cognitive control processes because bilingual individuals habitually manage two linguistic systems, switching between them and inhibiting one while using the other. Such habitual demands on attentional control and selection have led some investigators to hypothesize that bilinguals may develop enhanced executive functions, including aspects of working memory. The proposition that bilingualism confers cognitive advantages has been debated vigorously, and the empirical literature contains both reports of bilingual advantages and null or mixed findings. It is therefore important to examine the precise conditions under which bilingualism might influence working memory performance and to integrate this inquiry with formal models of working memory. A particularly influential theoretical framework for understanding working memory is that advanced by Barrouillet and colleagues, who have emphasized the role of attention and time constraints in the maintenance of information. Barrouillet and colleagues argue that when attention is captured by a concurrent processing task, memory maintenance is impaired in proportion to the amount of time attention is diverted from storage. This perspective is often referred to as a time-based resource-sharing account, and it highlights the dynamics of attention allocation between processing and storage. Under this account, working memory performance in complex tasks is determined in part by the fraction of time during which attention can be devoted to refreshing representations in memory. Memory performance will therefore depend on the duration and frequency of processing episodes that compete for attention. The Barrouillet framework suggests that variations in attentional control could modulate the extent to which processing demands disrupt memory, and it thereby creates a testable link between individual differences in attentional control, such as those hypothesized in bilinguals, and working memory performance. In light of this theoretical linkage, studies that combine complex span paradigms with manipulations of processing duration and attentional capture are well suited to evaluate whether bilingual experience yields differences in working memory maintenance. Research on bilingualism and working memory has employed a variety of tasks, including simple span tasks that minimize processing demands and complex span tasks that interleave storage and processing. Simple span tasks, such as digit span, primarily test the short-term retention and rehearsal of sequences, whereas complex span tasks assess the ability to maintain items in memory while attention is periodically engaged by a secondary task. If bilinguals have enhanced attentional control or more efficient switching, one might predict a bilingual advantage to be most prominent in complex span tasks that require frequent shifts of attention between processing and storage. Conversely, if bilingual effects are limited to components of executive control not heavily implicated in the particular sharing dynamics addressed by Barrouillet and colleagues, then no advantage may be observed on time-based resource-sharing paradigms. Empirical results to date have been mixed, with some studies reporting bilingual advantages on certain executive function tasks and others failing to replicate those findings. Several methodological factors have been proposed to account for these discrepancies, including heterogeneity in bilingual populations, differences in task demands, varying sample sizes, and potential confounds related to socioeconomic status, education, and language proficiency. Given these complexities, studies that explicitly manipulate processing load and measure memory performance with tasks derived from the time-based resource-sharing tradition can provide clearer tests of whether bilingual experience leads to measurable differences in the dynamics of attention and memory. In addition to task selection, it is important to consider how bilingualism is operationalized. Bilinguals differ along multiple dimensions, including age of acquisition, proficiency in each language, frequency of use, and context of language switching. Some individuals acquire two languages early in life and use both regularly in similar contexts, whereas others learn a second language later and use it predominantly in academic or occupational settings. Such heterogeneity may moderate any relationship between bilingualism and cognitive outcomes. In an ideal study of bilingualism and working memory, participants would be characterized on relevant bilingualism dimensions, and analyses would examine whether these dimensions predict performance beyond simple group membership. The current study was designed to integrate these theoretical and methodological considerations by investigating whether bilingualism is associated with superior working memory performance on tasks that tax attention in a time-based manner. We recruited a sample of university students identified as monolingual or bilingual and administered a set of tasks adapted from Barrouillet and colleagues’ reading span and reading digit-span paradigms. These tasks are well suited to probe the time-sharing dynamics that are central to the Barrouillet framework because they require participants to maintain sequences of digits while intermittently performing a reading-based processing task that captures attention for variable durations. Participants completed both a simple reading digit-span task and a more demanding reading span variant that included more frequent or longer processing episodes, thereby creating within-subject variations in processing load. The logic of the experiment was straightforward: if bilingual experience confers advantages in the allocation of attention under competing demands, bilingual participants should show smaller decrements in memory performance as processing demands increase relative to monolinguals. Conversely, if bilingualism does not influence the core mechanisms of time-based resource sharing, the two groups should perform similarly across levels of processing load. Our design included control measures to reduce potential confounds. Participants were drawn from the same university population to reduce variability in educational background. We collected self-report measures of age, gender, parental education, and language history to examine whether group differences could be explained by these variables. We also administered a brief screening for nonverbal reasoning and reading proficiency to ensure groups did not differ systematically on these dimensions. The primary outcome measures were the proportion of correctly recalled items in the reading digit-span tasks and the maximal correct span achieved in the simple digit-span task. Secondary outcome measures included error types, order errors, and response latencies on the processing components. These additional measures allowed us to probe whether any observed group differences were specific to storage performance or reflected broader differences in processing speed or accuracy. We preregistered the primary hypotheses and analytic approach prior to data collection, specifying group comparisons and planned interactions between group and processing load. The primary hypothesis was that bilinguals would show superior working memory maintenance, evidenced by higher recall scores and greater resistance to processing-induced forgetting compared to monolinguals. A secondary hypothesis was that effect sizes, if present, would be modulated by bilingual experience variables such as age of acquisition and frequency of use. In this set of analyses we also planned to apply Bayesian statistical methods to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis should group differences not emerge. The application of Bayesian methods would allow conclusions that went beyond failure to reject the null by estimating support for the absence of meaningful group differences. Prior meta-analytic summaries of bilingual advantage research have highlighted the importance of adequately powered samples and careful matching on potential confounds. A sample size of 24 participants per group was selected as a balance between practical constraints and the need for reasonably precise estimates of group differences in a within-subjects design. Although larger samples are always preferable, the study was intended to contribute to the cumulative evidence base by providing a careful test using theoretically motivated tasks. The present study thus combined a theoretically grounded task design, careful participant characterization, and pre-specified analyses to address a focused question about the relationship between bilingualism and time-based working memory maintenance. By doing so, the study aimed to clarify whether bilingual experience is associated with measurable changes in the dynamics of attention and memory that are central to the Barrouillet account. The expectations and analytic plan were clear: estimate group differences in recall accuracy and span, test the group by processing-load interaction, examine correlations with bilingualism dimensions, and apply Bayesian inference to assess evidence for the null or alternative hypotheses. The results of these analyses would inform both the empirical debate on bilingual advantage and the theoretical claims about how attention allocation influences maintenance in working memory. Given the mixed prior literature and the specific demands of the tasks used here, we remained open to the possibility of a null result, but we noted that such a result would be theoretically informative if it emerged with reasonable precision and after controlling for potential confounds. Ultimately, the study was designed to assess whether habitual bilingual experience translates into improved capacity to protect maintained information from attentional capture during concurrent processing, as operationalized in reading-based complex span tasks.

Methods

Participants were 48 students recruited from the university community and were assigned to groups on the basis of self-reported language experience. There were 24 participants who identified as monolingual English speakers and 24 who identified as bilingual in English and another language. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 26 years, with a mean age of 20.4 years across the sample. The gender composition was balanced as far as possible; in the monolingual group there were 13 women and 11 men, and in the bilingual group there were 14 women and 10 men. All participants were enrolled at the same university and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological or psychiatric conditions. Participants received course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. Bilingual participants varied in the age of acquisition of their second language and in self-reported proficiency and frequency of use; these variables were recorded via a detailed language history questionnaire administered during the session. The language questionnaire asked about age at first exposure to the second language, self-rated speaking and reading proficiency on a 1-to-7 scale, and approximate daily or weekly use estimates. Parental education was assessed to provide an index of socioeconomic background, and there were no substantial differences between groups in average parental education level. A brief nonverbal reasoning measure was administered to screen for gross group differences in general ability; scores did not differ significantly between groups. The experimental tasks were adapted from the reading digit-span and reading span paradigms developed in the time-based resource-sharing literature. The core memory items were digits presented auditorily in sequences of varying length. Digits from 1 to 9 were used, presented in a fixed tempo such that each digit occupied 800 ms including a brief inter-stimulus interval. Two primary task conditions were administered: a simple reading digit-span condition that minimized processing demands and a complex reading span condition in which digits were interleaved with short reading comprehension prompts that required participants to read a sentence and judge its sensibility. In the complex condition, the reading component captured attention for a variable duration, creating intervals during which attention could not be devoted to refreshing memory traces. The sentences for the processing component were balanced in length and syntactic complexity and were presented visually on a screen for a duration calibrated to be attentionally demanding yet solvable within a short interval. Participants responded to the sensibility judgments via a two-button response pad. The experiment included multiple trials at each sequence length ranging from 3 to 8 digits. Each participant completed practice trials to ensure comprehension of task instructions and to familiarize participants with the pace of digit presentation and reading tasks. The order of trials was randomized within blocks to minimize order effects, and the order of the simple and complex conditions was counterbalanced across participants. All testing took place in individual testing rooms under quiet conditions. Each experimental session lasted approximately 60 minutes including questionnaire completion, practice, and the main tasks. For scoring, the primary dependent variable in the digit-span tasks was the proportion of digits correctly recalled in the correct serial position across trials at each list length. In addition, a maximum correct span score was computed for each participant defined as the longest list length at which the participant achieved at least 80% correct recall across trials. For the complex condition, processing accuracy on the sensibility judgments and response latencies were recorded and analyzed to ensure that participants engaged with the processing task and to investigate potential speed-accuracy trade-offs. Error analyses classified memory errors into omission errors, intrusions, and order errors to evaluate whether groups differed in qualitative error profiles. All responses and stimuli were logged via experimental software and later exported for analysis. Prior to hypothesis testing, data were screened for outliers and compliance with task instructions. No participants were excluded for failure to follow instructions or for extreme performance, and all 48 datasets were included in the analyses. Data were inspected for normality, and transformations were applied where necessary to meet assumptions for parametric tests; however, most variables conformed adequately to normal distributions. The primary statistical tests consisted of independent-samples t-tests comparing groups on overall recall proportions and maximal span, and repeated-measures ANOVAs testing for group by processing-load interactions across sequence lengths and condition types. Effect sizes were computed using Cohen’s d for t-tests and partial eta-squared for ANOVAs to provide standardized estimates of the magnitude of observed effects. To quantify evidence for the null hypothesis in the case of non-significant results, Bayesian t-tests were conducted for primary group comparisons. Bayes factors were computed using default priors to estimate relative support for the null versus alternative hypotheses. In addition to group-level analyses, correlational analyses were conducted within the bilingual sample to examine relations between language experience variables and memory performance. These correlations included age of acquisition, self-rated proficiency, and frequency of use as predictors of recall proportions and span scores. Multiple regression analyses were performed to evaluate whether such bilingualism variables predicted working memory performance after adjusting for potential covariates such as age and nonverbal reasoning scores. Statistical significance was set at alpha = .05 for primary tests, with Bonferroni corrections applied where multiple comparisons were performed to control familywise error. Analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and all steps of data processing and analysis were documented prior to conducting inferential tests. The analytic plan conformed to the preregistered hypotheses and included exploratory follow-up analyses to clarify any unexpected patterns. All reported degrees of freedom, test statistics, p-values, effect sizes, and confidence intervals reflect final analyses on the full sample of 48 participants.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary outcome measures are presented first. Mean proportion correct across the simple digit-span condition was 0.79 (SD = 0.11) for monolinguals and 0.77 (SD = 0.12) for bilinguals. Mean proportion correct across the complex reading span condition was 0.74 (SD = 0.13) for monolinguals and 0.72 (SD = 0.14) for bilinguals. The maximal correct span averaged 5.3 items (SD = 0.9) for monolinguals and 5.1 items (SD = 1.0) for bilinguals. Processing accuracy on the sensibility judgments in the complex condition was 0.93 (SD = 0.05) for monolinguals and 0.92 (SD = 0.06) for bilinguals, indicating high engagement in the processing component for both groups. Mean response latencies on the processing judgments did not differ substantially between groups, with monolinguals averaging 812 ms (SD = 124) and bilinguals averaging 829 ms (SD = 139). Visual inspection of the distribution of scores suggested no major departures from normality in the primary measures. Independent-samples t-tests were conducted to test the primary between-group differences. For the simple digit-span proportion correct, the difference between groups was not significant, t(46) = 0.54, p = .59, Cohen’s d = 0.16, 95% CI for the mean difference = [-0.06, 0.10]. For the complex reading span proportion correct, the between-group difference was also not significant, t(46) = 0.55, p = .58, Cohen’s d = 0.16, 95% CI = [-0.08, 0.12]. For maximal correct span, the t-test indicated no significant group difference, t(46) = 0.69, p = .49, Cohen’s d = 0.20, 95% CI = [-0.37, 0.09]. Processing accuracy on the sensibility judgments did not differ between groups, t(46) = 0.53, p = .60. Response latencies on processing trials showed no significant difference either, t(46) = 0.46, p = .65. The absence of significant group differences in the primary measures was further evaluated using Bayes factors. Bayesian t-tests yielded BF10 = 0.31 for the simple digit-span proportion correct, BF10 = 0.34 for complex reading span proportion correct, and BF10 = 0.37 for maximal correct span. These Bayes factors indicate moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis of no group difference for the primary measures under the default priors. Repeated-measures ANOVAs were used to probe effects of sequence length and condition and whether such effects interacted with group. A 2 (group: monolingual vs bilingual) × 6 (sequence length: 3–8) repeated-measures ANOVA on proportion correct in the complex condition revealed a highly significant main effect of sequence length, F(5,230) = 98.2, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .68, reflecting declining recall with longer lists. The main effect of group was not significant, F(1,46) = 0.33, p = .57, partial eta-squared = .01. The group by sequence length interaction was not significant, F(5,230) = 0.72, p = .61, partial eta-squared = .02. Parallel analyses in the simple condition showed the expected main effect of sequence length, F(5,230) = 85.5, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .65, but again no main effect of group, F(1,46) = 0.29, p = .59, and no group by length interaction, F(5,230) = 0.84, p = .53. These ANOVA results indicate that both groups exhibited similar declines in performance as memory load increased and that bilinguals did not demonstrate a reduced impact of increased load relative to monolinguals. Error type analyses were conducted to determine whether qualitative differences in memory errors emerged across groups. The proportion of omission errors, intrusions, and order errors were calculated across complex condition trials. Both groups showed similar error profiles, with omission errors being the most common error type, followed by order errors and then intrusions. Chi-square tests comparing the distribution of error types between groups were non-significant, χ2(2) = 1.34, p = .51. A further analysis examined whether processing performance influenced memory performance differently across groups. Trials were categorized by processing accuracy, and only processing-correct trials were used in a follow-up comparison of recall. The group difference remained nonsignificant when restricting analyses to processing-correct trials, t(46) = 0.62, p = .54. Correlational analyses within the bilingual sample evaluated relations between language experience variables and memory outcomes. Age of acquisition of the second language correlated weakly and non-significantly with complex span proportion correct, r = -0.11, p = .61, and with maximal correct span, r = -0.14, p = .54. Self-rated proficiency correlated modestly but non-significantly with complex span, r = 0.18, p = .40. Frequency of daily use of the second language also showed no significant correlation with memory measures. Multiple regression analyses were performed to assess whether bilingualism variables predicted memory performance after adjustment for covariates. A regression predicting complex span proportion correct from age, nonverbal reasoning, and frequency of second language use yielded no significant unique contribution from frequency of use, β = 0.10, t(20) = 0.45, p = .66. Nonverbal reasoning emerged as a modest predictor in the regression, β = 0.31, t(20) = 2.02, p = .057, indicating a trend-level association with memory performance across the sample. Group differences remained non-significant after controlling for nonverbal reasoning and parental education in ANCOVA models, with group effects showing F-values below 1 in adjusted models. Exploratory analyses applied equivalence testing to assess whether the observed group differences could be considered practically negligible. Two one-sided tests (TOST) were performed with bounds set to Cohen’s d = ±0.50 as a threshold for a medium effect. For both the simple and complex span measures, the TOST procedure indicated that the observed differences fell within the equivalence bounds, supporting the interpretation that large or medium effects of bilingualism on these working memory measures were unlikely in this sample. Finally, inspection of individual participant data did not reveal any subgroup of bilinguals with markedly superior performance; instead performance was distributed similarly across both groups. Taken together, the statistical evidence indicates that bilingualism was not associated with robust differences in working memory performance as measured by the reading digit-span and reading span tasks implemented in this study. The pattern of results was consistent across multiple analytic approaches, and Bayesian analyses suggested moderate support for the null hypothesis.

Discussion

This study examined whether bilingualism is associated with superior performance on working memory tasks that explicitly tax the allocation of attention in a time-based manner. We used reading digit-span and reading span paradigms adapted from the time-based resource-sharing tradition to probe the dynamics of attention allocation between processing and storage. Across a sample of 48 university students divided into 24 monolinguals and 24 bilinguals, we observed no significant group differences in recall proportions, maximal correct span, error profiles, or processing response latencies. The absence of observable bilingual advantages persisted across simple and complex conditions, as well as across sequence lengths, and Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence favoring the null hypothesis over the presence of a meaningful group effect. These findings have several implications for the ongoing debate about the cognitive consequences of bilingualism and for theoretical accounts of working memory that emphasize attentional dynamics. First, with regard to the bilingual advantage hypothesis, our results add to the growing number of studies reporting null findings in which bilingualism, at least as operationalized in heterogeneous adult university samples, does not translate into measurable advantages on certain executive function or working memory measures. The tasks we used were specifically designed to probe whether habitual bilingual management of two languages leads to enhanced ability to protect maintained information from attentional capture during interleaved processing episodes. Under the time-based resource-sharing account, improved control over attention allocation would be expected to mitigate processing-induced forgetting and to yield a smaller decrement in recall as processing load increases. We did not observe such a mitigation effect, and bilingual participants showed declines in recall with increased sequence length and processing demands that were statistically indistinguishable from monolingual participants. Second, with regard to theoretical models of working memory, the Barrouillet framework emphasizes the competitive demands on attention during interleaved processing and storage. Our results suggest that the putative attentional benefits of bilingual experience do not manifest in the specific attentional dynamics measured by these time-based span tasks, at least in this sample. This implies that bilingualism may not uniformly enhance the attentional processes that support refreshing of memory traces during brief processing interruptions, or that the magnitude of any such enhancement is small relative to inter-individual variability in the sample. It is possible that bilingual advantages, when present in other studies, reflect improvements in other facets of cognitive control, such as conflict monitoring, response inhibition, or task switching under particular task demands, rather than broader enhancements in the short-term allocation of attention implicated by the TBRS model. Third, methodological factors must be considered when interpreting null results in bilingualism research. Our participant sample comprised university students with a range of bilingual backgrounds, including variation in age of acquisition, proficiency, and frequency of use. Heterogeneity in bilingual experience can attenuate group-level effects if only a subset of bilinguals attains the type and degree of language management that might influence cognitive control. In our correlational analyses within the bilingual group, we did not find significant relations between self-reported bilingual experience variables and working memory outcomes, but our modest sample size limits power to detect such moderating influences. Future work with larger samples and finer-grained measures of bilingual experience, including objective proficiency tests and ecological language-use measures, would be better positioned to determine whether particular bilingual subgroups exhibit cognitive differences. Fourth, the tasks chosen for this study, while theoretically motivated by time-based resource-sharing theory, represent only one class of working memory measures. It remains possible that bilingual advantages, if they exist, are more likely to appear on other types of tasks or under different testing conditions. For example, tasks that involve greater interference from competing stimuli, tasks requiring rapid alternation between distinct cognitive sets, or tasks that draw more heavily on verbal rehearsal strategies might reveal different patterns. The specific nature of the processing task interleaved with memory items may also influence outcomes; in our design the processing component was linguistic in nature (reading sensibility judgments), which could have differential effects on bilinguals depending on their proficiency in the language of testing. Fifth, the role of developmental timing and duration of bilingual exposure warrants additional attention. Some theories posit that cognitive adaptations arising from bilingual experience are more pronounced when bilingualism begins early in life and when both languages are used extensively across diverse contexts. Our bilingual participants included individuals who acquired their second language at varying ages, and it is plausible that early balanced bilinguals might differ from late bilinguals in ways that affect working memory. However, even analyses restricted to bilinguals reporting early acquisition did not reveal consistent performance advantages in our sample, though these subgroup analyses were underpowered. Sixth, the effect sizes observed in this study were small and consistent with the notion that any bilingual advantage for the specific time-based memory dynamics examined here is likely to be modest. The equivalence testing suggested that medium or large effects are unlikely in this population and on these measures. This has implications for the broader field because it highlights the need for adequately powered studies to detect small effects and for meta-analytic synthesis to accumulate evidence across studies. Researchers should therefore plan for larger sample sizes if they aim to detect small but potentially meaningful cognitive consequences of bilingual experience. Seventh, the null finding reported here does not imply that bilingualism has no cognitive consequences whatsoever. Rather, it suggests that bilingualism does not confer a robust advantage in the specific working memory maintenance dynamics assessed by reading digit-span and reading span tasks in a university sample. Other cognitive domains or task contexts may nevertheless reveal differences. For instance, studies examining real-world multitasking, sociolinguistic flexibility, or domain-general task switching might uncover bilingual-related benefits not captured by laboratory span measures. Eighth, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The sample size, while sufficient to detect medium-sized effects, was limited for detecting small effects or interactions involving bilingual experience variables. The assessment of bilingualism relied principally on self-report measures rather than on objective proficiency tests or on real-time measures of language switching in naturalistic contexts. The processing component was in the dominant language of the participants, which might reduce the relevance of bilingual control mechanisms if both groups process the sentences with similar facility. Future studies should consider employing processing tasks in the participants’ non-dominant or second language to test whether bilinguals show differential resilience to interference in that context. Ninth, an important avenue for future research is to integrate longitudinal designs that track cognitive change as a function of changing language use. Most studies, including the present one, are cross-sectional and therefore cannot conclusively establish causal links between bilingual experience and cognitive outcomes. Longitudinal or training studies that manipulate language exposure or task-switching demands could provide clearer evidence about causality and about the potential for bilingual experience to induce cognitive plasticity. Tenth, neurophysiological measures could illuminate mechanisms that are invisible to behavioral measures alone. Even in the absence of behavioral differences, bilinguals and monolinguals may exhibit distinct patterns of neural activation or connectivity during complex span tasks, indicating compensatory or more efficient processing strategies. Incorporating electrophysiological or functional neuroimaging techniques alongside behavioral tasks would help to map the neural correlates of any subtle cognitive differences tied to bilingualism. Eleventh, the present findings underscore the importance of theoretical specificity in claims about bilingual advantages. Broad assertions that bilingualism universally enhances executive function are not supported by our data, and claims should be constrained to clearly defined cognitive processes and task contexts. The Barrouillet time-based resource-sharing model provides an explicit account of how attentional capture by processing tasks impacts maintenance, but our data suggest that routine bilingual experience may not systematically alter the parameters of this sharing process. Twelfth, replication and cumulative evidence building remain crucial. Single studies, whether reporting advantages or null results, cannot settle complex questions about individual differences that emerge from diverse life experiences. We encourage researchers to preregister analytic plans, to share data, and to coordinate multi-site studies that can amass larger and more diverse samples. Thirteenth, there may be interactions between bilingualism and other individual difference variables, such as working memory strategy use, personality traits, or motivational factors, that moderate cognitive outcomes. Future work should examine these potential moderators to develop a richer, more nuanced model of how bilingual experience interfaces with cognition. Fourteenth, it would also be valuable to consider ecological validity in task selection. Laboratory tasks often isolate specific cognitive processes, but they may not capture the complexity of cognitive control as deployed in real-world bilingual language use. Designing tasks that better mirror naturalistic language switching and selection demands could be informative. Fifteenth, despite the limitations, the strengths of the present study include a theoretically motivated task design, careful documentation of bilingual experience, and the use of multiple analytic approaches including Bayesian and equivalence testing. These methodological features increase confidence that the observed null effects for group differences were not merely statistical artifacts but reflect a substantive absence of large bilingual advantages on the specific measures employed here. Sixteenth, taken together, the findings suggest that bilingualism does not produce a robust effect on the time-based dynamics of working memory maintenance as assessed by reading digit-span and reading span tasks in this university sample. Seventeenth, the broader implication is that researchers and practitioners should be cautious about generalizing claims of bilingual cognitive enhancement without specifying the cognitive domain, the task contexts, and the characteristics of the bilingual population in question. Eighteenth, future research should prioritize larger, more diverse samples, objective measures of bilingual experience, longitudinal designs, and multimodal assessment methods to map the boundaries of any cognitive effects of bilingualism. Nineteenth, with such an agenda, the field can move beyond equivocal findings toward a more detailed and mechanistic understanding of how life-long experiences like bilingualism shape specific components of cognition. Twentieth, in closing, the data presented here contribute to this cumulative enterprise by providing a rigorous test of the hypothesis that bilingualism enhances the attentional processes that protect working memory from processing-induced forgetting, and by demonstrating that in this sample and with these tasks such enhancement was not observed.
