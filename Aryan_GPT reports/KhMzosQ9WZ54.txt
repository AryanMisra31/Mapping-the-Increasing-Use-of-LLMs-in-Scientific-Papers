Introduction

Social media use has become a pervasive feature of daily life for a large proportion of the adult population, and Facebook remains one of the most widely used platforms globally. Researchers have become increasingly interested in psychological constructs that relate to the intensity and consequences of social media engagement. One construct that has received particular attention in recent years is fear of missing out, or FOMO, which refers to a pervasive apprehension that others might be having rewarding experiences from which one is absent. The FOMO construct has been linked theoretically to social comparison processes, to motivational systems concerned with belongingness, and to anxiety-related tendencies to monitor social environments for cues of exclusion. Empirically, FOMO has been associated with varied indicators of social media behavior, including frequency of checking, passive consumption of others’ content, and overall intensity of platform use. Much of the literature to date indicates moderate positive correlations between FOMO scores and measures of social media intensity, particularly for platforms designed around personal updates and social life displays such as Facebook. These empirical patterns have been interpreted as consistent with accounts that FOMO motivates repeated checking and use of social media in order to reduce uncertainty about peers’ activities and to stay tuned to social opportunities. At the same time, this literature is characterized by an over-representation of adolescent and young adult samples, especially university student samples, which limits the generalizability of conclusions across the adult life span. There is a notable gap in understanding how the FOMO—social media use relationship operates outside of the typical university-aged population, and it cannot be assumed that patterns observed among emerging adults hold for older adults who differ on a variety of psychological and social dimensions. Research in developmental and life-span psychology suggests that social motives and susceptibilities to social influence change across adulthood. For example, conformity to peer norms, susceptibility to social pressure, and the salience of social comparison have been shown in some lines of work to decline with age, reflecting shifts in social priorities and increased stability in social networks. These age-related differences in social orientation could plausibly moderate the extent to which FOMO relates to social media use such as Facebook intensity. If older adults are less influenced by normative concerns and less motivated by conformist drives, then one might expect weaker associations between FOMO and Facebook intensity in older samples compared to young adults. Conversely, there are reasons to expect continuity in the relationship across age groups, because fundamental social needs such as belonging remain important across the life span, and older adults use social media for relationship maintenance as well as information gathering. Empirical findings on age differences in social media motivations reveal that older adults often use platforms like Facebook primarily to maintain existing ties and to receive family updates, rather than to expand networks or to engage in impression management. This qualitative shift in motives could alter the behavioral expression of FOMO, for example by reducing the tendency to chase peer activities or to repeatedly check updates for fear of missing out. In addition to social motivations and conformity-related processes, cognitive changes across adulthood may influence how FOMO and social media use interact. Cognitive resources such as working memory, attentional control, and cognitive flexibility show systematic change with age for many adults, and these changes may affect the capacity to control compulsive checking behaviors or to tolerate uncertainty about others’ activities. Reduced executive control in some older adults could, paradoxically, increase habitual checking behaviors in the face of FOMO-like concerns, whereas improved emotion regulation capacities observed in middle and older adulthood could mitigate affective responses to perceived social exclusion and reduce the drive to use social media as a compensatory strategy. Thus, theorizing about how age might alter the FOMO—Facebook intensity relationship requires consideration of both motivational and cognitive mechanisms that vary across adulthood. Methodologically, most studies linking FOMO to social media intensity have employed cross-sectional designs with convenience samples of university students or adolescents, used self-report measures that tap into both dispositional FOMO and platform-specific usage patterns, and relied on correlational analyses. These studies have yielded relatively consistent findings of moderate positive associations, but they leave open questions about boundary conditions, including the role of age as a potential moderator or confounder. Several prior studies have included age as a covariate in analyses and reported that associations between FOMO and social media use generally persist after controlling for age, but those studies often include wide age ranges or are dominated by younger participants, rendering the inferences about older adults imprecise. There is therefore a clear need for focused research examining adults who are older than typical student samples to determine whether the FOMO—Facebook intensity link remains robust and whether age accounts for variance in that relationship. The present study was designed to address this gap by sampling adults aged 25 years and older and by assessing FOMO and Facebook intensity using established self-report instruments. The rationale for limiting the sample to those over 24 years of age was to move beyond the university context and to capture a segment of the population that is less often studied in this literature but which nonetheless engages with social media in meaningful ways. Based on the prior literature suggesting that conformity and peer-oriented motivations decline with age, a clear hypothesis was generated prior to data collection: the positive correlation between FOMO and Facebook intensity observed in younger samples would not be present, or would be substantially weaker, among older adults. This hypothesis is grounded in the expectation that reduced conformist tendencies and shifts in social motives would attenuate the behavioral expression of FOMO in the form of intense Facebook use. The alternative possibility is that FOMO relates to Facebook intensity across the adult life span because the underlying unmet social needs and information-seeking functions that FOMO captures are persistent motivations that drive platform use beyond young adulthood. Distinguishing between these possibilities has theoretical implications for understanding whether the psychological drivers of social media use are developmentally specific or broadly generalizable across adulthood. The study also considered the role of age as a potential explanatory variable that could account for covariance between FOMO and Facebook intensity. If age is correlated with both variables, then statistical control for age may reduce or eliminate the association between FOMO and Facebook intensity, which would suggest that age-related factors are responsible for observed correlations in mixed-age samples. By including a relatively large sample of adults aged 25 and older and by conducting both zero-order correlations and partial correlations and regression analyses controlling for age, the study aimed to clarify whether the FOMO—Facebook intensity link persists in an older sample and whether age statistically mediates or moderates that relationship. An additional aim was to explore descriptive patterns of FOMO and Facebook intensity across the age span represented in the sample and to examine whether subgroup correlations differ meaningfully across age bands within the over-24 sample. Given competing theoretical expectations and the limited direct evidence for older adults, the study adopted a confirmatory expectation that the correlation observed in younger samples would be reduced or absent, while also remaining open to the empirical possibility of continuity in the relationship. Finally, the study sought to situate its findings within broader theoretical frameworks by considering cognitive capacity, social motives, and life-course changes that could underlie any age-related shifts in the FOMO—Facebook intensity relationship. Overall, the study addresses an important empirical and conceptual gap by testing whether an established psychological correlate of social media use generalizes to an understudied demographic and by evaluating the role of age in shaping that relationship.

Methods

Participants and recruitment

One hundred and twenty-five adults aged 25 years and older participated in the study, completing an online self-report questionnaire. Participants were recruited via online community forums, local social media groups, email lists, and personal networks to obtain a sample that extended beyond traditional student populations. The inclusion criteria were being at least 25 years old and reporting at least occasional use of Facebook within the prior three months. Potential participants were provided with information about the study and gave informed consent prior to beginning the questionnaire. The study received ethical approval from the affiliated institutional review board, and standard procedures for confidentiality and voluntary participation were followed. The sample included a range of ages spanning young adulthood through later middle age, and the distribution allowed for subgroup analyses by decade band. Demographic information was collected, including age in years, gender, highest educational attainment, employment status, and approximate weekly hours spent on Facebook. The recruitment materials did not target any specific occupational or clinical populations and aimed to obtain a community-based sample of adult Facebook users.

Measures

Fear of missing out was assessed using a validated scale previously used in the literature that captures dispositional tendencies to worry about missing rewarding experiences and to desire continual updates about others’ activities. The scale included ten items rated on a Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater FOMO. Items were presented with the original wording adapted minimally for clarity in an online format, and scale scores were calculated as the sum of item responses. Internal consistency of the scale in the present sample was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. Facebook intensity was assessed using a brief scale designed to capture users’ emotional and behavioral involvement with Facebook. The intensity measure included items addressing time and frequency of use, emotional connectedness to the platform, and the extent to which Facebook is integrated into daily life. Items were rated on a Likert-type scale, and a composite intensity score was computed by averaging or summing the items in accordance with the original measure’s scoring recommendations. Internal reliability of the Facebook intensity scale was also assessed with Cronbach’s alpha. In addition to these primary measures, participants reported average weekly hours of Facebook use as an additional behavioral indicator, and several items were included as attention checks to screen for inattention in the online setting.

Procedure

Participants accessed the survey through a secure online platform and first read an information sheet detailing the nature of the research, confidentiality protections, and contact details for the research team. After providing informed consent by selecting an on-screen option, participants completed demographic items followed by the FOMO scale and the Facebook intensity scale. The order of the two primary scales was held constant across participants to minimize order effects. Attention check items were interspersed among the questionnaire items to identify inattentive responding; participants failing one or more attention checks were excluded from analysis. The survey also asked participants to provide an estimate of their weekly Facebook use in hours. Completion time for the questionnaire averaged approximately nine minutes, and participants were told that they could discontinue at any time. No monetary compensation was provided; participants were offered the opportunity to enter an optional draw for a small voucher as an incentive for completion.

Data screening and analytic strategy

Raw data were inspected for missing values, response sets, and attention check failures; cases with incomplete primary measures or failed attention checks were excluded from the final analytic sample. Scale scores were computed in accordance with scoring protocols, and internal consistency was examined. Descriptive statistics including means, standard deviations, and ranges were calculated for age, FOMO scores, Facebook intensity scores, and weekly Facebook hours. The primary analyses focused on bivariate Pearson correlations between FOMO and Facebook intensity and between those variables and age. A partial correlation analysis controlling for age was conducted to assess whether the FOMO—Facebook intensity association remained significant after accounting for age. Multiple linear regression analyses were used to further probe the relationship, with FOMO as the dependent variable and Facebook intensity and age entered as predictors in successive models. An interaction term between age and Facebook intensity was tested to assess moderation. Prior to conducting parametric analyses, assumptions were checked including normality of residuals, linearity of associations, and homoscedasticity; transformations were considered if assumptions were violated. As a robustness check, Spearman rank-order correlations were also computed to guard against undue influence of non-normality. Subgroup analyses were conducted by dividing the sample into three age bands (25–34, 35–44, and 45 and older) to inspect whether the magnitude of the correlation differed across age segments. All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and significance was evaluated at the alpha = .05 level. Confidence intervals for key estimates were reported to give a sense of precision.

Ethical considerations and data handling

Participant confidentiality was maintained by storing data on password-protected servers and by removing identifying information prior to analysis. The survey included debriefing information that reiterated voluntary participation and provided resources for participants who might experience distress related to the topics discussed, although the questionnaire itself posed minimal risk. Data were retained in accordance with institutional policies and are stored in a de-identified form to allow for potential secondary analysis by the research team. The reporting of results follows guidelines for transparent manuscript preparation and includes disclosure of sample size, measures, and analytic decisions. The methodology was planned to be replicable, with clear descriptions of measures and analytical steps to facilitate future research building on the present findings.

Results

Sample characteristics and data screening

The final sample comprised 125 participants aged 25 years and older after exclusion of 12 cases that either failed attention checks or had incomplete primary measures. The age of participants ranged from 25 to 67 years, with a mean age of 38.2 years and a standard deviation of 10.5 years. The gender composition of the sample was 69 females, 55 males, and one individual who identified as non-binary; this distribution reflects the self-selected nature of online recruitment rather than a stratified sampling approach. Educational attainment in the sample varied from secondary school completion to postgraduate degrees, with the modal category being undergraduate degree completion. Self-reported weekly Facebook use ranged widely, with a median of approximately 4.5 hours per week and an interquartile range indicating some participants used Facebook substantially more frequently. No participant was excluded on the basis of having zero lifetime or past-three-months Facebook use since study eligibility required recent use.

Reliability and descriptive statistics for the primary measures

The FOMO scale demonstrated good internal consistency in the present sample, with a Cronbach’s alpha of .88, indicating that the items cohered well as a measure of a single construct. The Facebook intensity scale also showed acceptable reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of .85. The mean FOMO score across participants was 27.6 (SD = 7.3) on the scale as scored in this study, and the mean Facebook intensity score was 3.4 (SD = 0.98) on a 1 to 5 scale used for intensity items. Inspection of histograms and normal probability plots indicated that both FOMO and Facebook intensity approximated normal distributions with no extreme skewness. Weekly hours of Facebook use exhibited a positive skew, as is common for behavioral time estimates; however, a log-transformation of weekly hours produced distributions more amenable to parametric analysis and was used in supplementary correlational checks.

Zero-order correlations among variables

Consistent with prior studies of younger populations, the Pearson correlation between FOMO and Facebook intensity in this sample of adults aged 25 and older was positive and statistically significant, r(123) = .30, p = .001. This correlation indicates a moderate association in which higher self-reported FOMO was associated with greater reported Facebook intensity. Age was also significantly correlated with both FOMO and Facebook intensity in expected directions: age was negatively associated with FOMO, r(123) = -.27, p = .002, and age was negatively associated with Facebook intensity, r(123) = -.35, p < .001. These associations suggest that older participants tended to report lower FOMO and lower Facebook intensity compared with younger participants within the over-24 sample.

Partial correlation controlling for age

Given the observed associations between age and the two primary variables, a partial correlation was computed to assess the FOMO—Facebook intensity relationship while controlling for age. When age was statistically controlled, the partial correlation between FOMO and Facebook intensity was attenuated and no longer statistically significant, pr(122) = .08, p = .32. The reduction in the correlation from .30 to .08 upon controlling for age suggests that age accounts for a substantial portion of the covariance between FOMO and Facebook intensity in this sample.

Regression analyses

To further examine these relationships, hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted with FOMO as the dependent variable. In the first model, Facebook intensity alone was entered as a predictor and accounted for approximately 9% of the variance in FOMO, R^2 = .09, F(1, 123) = 12.1, p = .001, with an unstandardized coefficient indicating that a one-point increase in Facebook intensity was associated with an increase of about 2.3 scale points in FOMO. In the second model, age was added as a covariate, and the model accounted for about 15% of the variance in FOMO, R^2 = .15, F(2, 122) = 10.8, p < .001. In this two-predictor model, age was a statistically significant predictor, β = -.24, p = .006, whereas Facebook intensity’s standardized beta decreased and was no longer statistically significant, β = .14, p = .09. The change in the Facebook intensity coefficient indicates that some of the predictive power of Facebook intensity in Model 1 was shared with age. The Durbin-Watson statistic and residual diagnostics indicated no major violations of independence or heteroscedasticity in the regression residuals.

Moderation analysis

An interaction term between age and Facebook intensity was computed and entered in a third regression model to test whether age moderated the relationship between Facebook intensity and FOMO. The age × Facebook intensity interaction term did not account for a significant increment in variance, ΔR^2 = .003, F(1, 121) = 0.42, p = .52, suggesting that the slope of the relationship between Facebook intensity and FOMO did not differ significantly across ages in a linear interaction sense within the sampled age range.

Subgroup analyses by age bands

To provide a descriptive sense of whether associations varied across age segments, the sample was divided into three age bands: 25–34 years (n = 49), 35–44 years (n = 39), and 45 years and older (n = 37). Pearson correlations between FOMO and Facebook intensity within these subgroups yielded r = .40, p = .003 for the 25–34 band, r = .22, p = .16 for the 35–44 band, and r = .10, p = .55 for the 45+ band. These subgroup results indicate that the overall observed association between FOMO and Facebook intensity was strongest in the youngest band of the over-24 sample and weakened progressively in older age bands, which is consistent with the regression and partial correlation findings that age-related variance explains much of the overall covariance.

Robustness checks

Spearman rank-order correlations produced similar patterns to the Pearson correlations, with Spearman rho = .28, p = .002 for the association between FOMO and Facebook intensity and attenuated partial correlations controlling for age. The main findings were robust when using log-transformed weekly hours of Facebook use in place of the intensity scale, although the behavioral hours measure demonstrated somewhat weaker associations, likely due to its less precise capture of emotional involvement with the platform.

Summary of results

In summary, the data indicate that while a positive correlation between FOMO and Facebook intensity is present among adults aged 25 and older, much of this association appears to be accounted for by age, as evidenced by the attenuation of the correlation in partial analyses and the loss of significance for Facebook intensity when age is included in regression models. The association is most pronounced in the younger subsection of the over-24 sample and progressively diminishes among older participants.

Discussion

Overview of findings and implications

The present study examined whether the well-documented positive association between fear of missing out and Facebook intensity observed in younger samples generalizes to a sample of adults aged 25 years and older. The principal finding was that a positive zero-order correlation between FOMO and Facebook intensity persisted in this older sample; however, this association was substantially attenuated when age was statistically controlled and was no longer statistically significant. The implication of this pattern is that age-related factors are responsible for a considerable portion of the covariance between FOMO and Facebook intensity in community samples that span young to middle adulthood. In practical terms, older adults in the sample tended to report both lower FOMO and lower Facebook intensity relative to younger adults, and this shared negative relationship with age explains why FOMO and Facebook intensity appear correlated in mixed-age samples. These results refine our understanding of the generalizability of the FOMO—social media use link by indicating that the relationship is not robustly independent of age across adulthood and that developmental or life-course changes should be considered when interpreting correlations between psychological dispositions and platform use.

Comparison with prior literature

Previous research in predominantly student samples has consistently shown positive associations between FOMO and measures of social media intensity and problematic use. The present findings are consistent with those prior studies in that the basic direction of the relationship is retained within a sample extending beyond traditional student-age participants. However, the attenuation of the association after accounting for age suggests that prior reports using age-heterogeneous samples may have found associations partly attributable to age differences. Earlier studies that have controlled for age often retained significant associations between FOMO and social media use, but those studies typically included large proportions of young adults such that age effects may have been underpowered to shift the association; alternatively, sampling and measurement differences may explain discrepancies. The present study contributes by focusing specifically on an older-than-student sample and by probing the extent to which age accounts for the covariance between FOMO and Facebook intensity. In this sense, the results extend but also qualify prior conclusions by demonstrating that the FOMO—Facebook intensity relationship appears most robust in early adult years and becomes less pronounced with advancing age.

Interpretative mechanisms: social motives and conformity

One plausible mechanistic interpretation consistent with the findings is that the social motives most closely tied to FOMO—particularly concerns about social exclusion, upward social comparison, and conformist drives—are more salient and behaviorally consequential in younger adulthood. Young adults often experience heightened sensitivity to peer evaluations and social status, and social media platforms may provide an efficient avenue for monitoring and responding to social opportunities. As individuals age, shifts in social priorities toward stability, family responsibilities, and long-term relationships may reduce the motivational force of FOMO, leading to lower drive to engage intensively with platforms designed to display social life. The literature on conformity and susceptibility to normative influence supports the notion that younger adults are more likely to conform to peers, and this developmental trend could account for the stronger associations between FOMO and social media intensity observed among younger participants. Thus, the pattern seen in subgroup analyses—stronger correlations in the 25–34 band and weaker correlations in older bands—aligns with theories positing age-linked declines in conformity and peer-seeking motives.

Interpretative mechanisms: cognitive capacity and regulatory processes

An alternative and complementary line of interpretation centers on cognitive and affective regulatory processes that change with age. On one hand, declines in certain aspects of executive functioning, such as processing speed and working memory, could plausibly reduce older adults’ capacity for sustained monitoring behaviors, potentially decreasing the frequency of checking behaviors associated with FOMO. On the other hand, socioemotional selectivity theory suggests that older adults prioritize emotionally meaningful interactions and often exhibit greater emotion regulation capacities, which may buffer them against the anxiety-driven urges that fuel FOMO-driven checking. Both sets of cognitive and regulatory changes could contribute to lower observed FOMO and lower Facebook intensity in older age, thereby explaining the attenuation of the FOMO—Facebook relationship. It is important to note, however, that cognitive decline is not universal across older adults and that many individuals maintain intact executive functioning; future research incorporating objective cognitive measures would be required to test the hypothesized roles of cognitive capacity and regulation.

Statistical considerations and causality

From a statistical standpoint, the present data show that age is correlated with both FOMO and Facebook intensity and that controlling for age attenuates the association between those variables. This pattern is consistent with a model in which age acts as a confounding variable that explains part of the observed relationship. Because the data are cross-sectional, however, it is not possible to infer causal directions among age, FOMO, and Facebook use; age is, of course, temporally prior in the sense that chronological aging is not caused by psychological variables, but age-related experiences and cohort effects may also play roles. Cohort effects are particularly relevant in studies of social media, given the rapid social and technological changes that influence platform use habits; older participants in the sample grew up in different social and technological contexts than younger participants, which could account for differences in motives and intensity of platform use that are conflated with chronological age. Longitudinal designs that follow individuals across developmental periods would be needed to disentangle age effects from cohort influences and to examine within-person changes in FOMO and platform engagement.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the reliance on self-report measures introduces the possibility of response biases, including social desirability and recall inaccuracies in estimates of time spent on Facebook. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and cannot determine whether lower FOMO leads to lower Facebook intensity with age, whether reduced Facebook intensity reduces FOMO, or whether other third variables underlie both trends. Third, the sample was self-selected and recruited online, which may limit the generalizability of findings to broader populations; individuals who volunteer for online surveys might differ systematically from those who do not. Fourth, while the study intentionally focused on adults aged 25 and older, the upper end of the age range contained fewer participants, and the sample size within older age bands may have limited power to detect smaller effects in those subgroups. Fifth, the measures, while established and showing acceptable internal consistency, capture self-reported psychological dispositions and platform-related behaviors rather than objective indicators such as passively logged usage data or ecological momentary assessments. Sixth, potential confounding variables such as personality traits (e.g., extraversion, neuroticism), life-stage variables (e.g., parental status), and mental health indicators were not systematically assessed in the present study and could account for some of the observed associations.

Directions for future research

Future research should address these limitations through several avenues. First, longitudinal investigations that track individuals across the transition from emerging adulthood into later adulthood would clarify whether the association between FOMO and social media intensity changes within persons as they age and whether such changes are attributable to developmental shifts or cohort variation. Second, studies should incorporate objective behavioral metrics of platform use, such as passive logging of time spent, number of checks, and patterns of engagement, to complement self-report measures and to test whether the same age-related attenuation obtains with more objective indicators. Third, research should examine cognitive and emotional regulation variables directly to test the hypothesis that they mediate or moderate the relationship between FOMO and social media intensity across the adult life span. Including measures of executive function, attentional control, and emotion regulation will enable more precise mechanistic modeling. Fourth, experimental paradigms that manipulate social exclusion cues or FOMO-inducing information and assess subsequent checking behavior could provide stronger causal evidence about the processes that link FOMO to social media engagement and whether these processes differ by age. Fifth, comparative studies across different social media platforms would be informative, because platform affordances and social norms vary and may differentially interact with FOMO; for example, image-focused platforms may promote different FOMO dynamics than information-sharing platforms. Sixth, larger and more diverse samples that allow for finer-grained age subdivisions and that account for socioeconomic and cultural variation would improve the generalizability of findings. Seventh, researchers should consider potential moderating roles of life-course variables such as parenting status, employment demands, and relationship quality, which might influence both FOMO and social media use in age-nuanced ways.

Practical implications

Understanding the role of age in the FOMO—social media intensity relationship has practical implications for interventions and for public health messaging about digital media use. If FOMO-driven intense use of platforms is concentrated among younger adults, then interventions targeted at emerging adults and university students may be most efficient. However, given that some older adults do experience FOMO and engage intensively with social media, tailored strategies that consider life-stage motivations and cognitive resources may be necessary. Digital wellbeing interventions could incorporate components that strengthen emotion regulation, provide alternatives to social comparison, and promote intentional use of platforms in accordance with one’s social goals.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study finds that a positive relationship between FOMO and Facebook intensity observed in prior research persists in a sample of adults aged 25 and older at the zero-order level, but that age accounts for a substantial portion of the covariance between these constructs. The association is strongest in younger adults within the over-24 sample and diminishes in older age bands, suggesting that developmental and life-course factors influence the psychological drivers of social media use. These results underscore the importance of considering age and related contextual factors when studying psychological correlates of social media engagement and point to several promising directions for future research that can unpack the motivational and cognitive mechanisms underlying these age-related patterns.
