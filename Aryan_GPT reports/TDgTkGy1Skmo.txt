Introduction:
Human cognition routinely brings together language and perception. Words and sentences often evoke sensory and motor representations. This capacity to recruit perceptual systems during language comprehension is a central topic in cognitive psychology. The question of how language and perception interact has been framed in multiple theoretical traditions. One influential perspective proposes that language comprehension involves perceptual simulation, in which modal systems that support perception and action are engaged during understanding. Another perspective argues for more abstract, amodal propositional representations that do not require recruiting perceptual systems. Empirical work over the last several decades has provided support for both positions, creating an ongoing debate. A key empirical strategy has been to show that language about spatial orientation, motion, shape, or color can influence subsequent perceptual tasks. For example, reading a sentence that describes an upward motion can speed response times to an upward pointing arrow presented soon afterwards. Such effects have been interpreted as evidence for modality-specific simulation. However, the literature is complex and not without conflicting findings. A recurring concern is the degree to which these language-perception effects depend on individual differences. Some researchers have suggested that the propensity to generate vivid mental images might moderate the extent to which language recruits perceptual systems. If so, people with more vivid visual imagery should show larger language-to-perception compatibility effects. If not, the perceptual consequences of language could be relatively automatic and independent of conscious imagery ability. The vividness of visual imagery questionnaire (VVIQ) has often been used to measure individual differences in imagery vividness. The VVIQ asks participants to form mental images of common scenes or objects and to rate the vividness on Likert scales. Scores on this instrument have been correlated with a range of cognitive and neural measures, but they remain self-report and subject to potential reporting biases. Even so, the VVIQ is widely used as a practical index of imagery ability in behavioral studies. Another experimental paradigm that has been used to probe language-perception interactions involves manipulating implied orientation in sentences and measuring effects on subsequent perceptual discrimination. One influential example is the 'head-up, foot-down' manipulation reported by Estes, Verges, and Barsalou. In that line of work, sentences that imply a particular spatial orientation for a human figure (e.g., head-up vs head-down) were shown to facilitate detection or discrimination of images congruent with the implied orientation. Such results have been taken to indicate that sentence comprehension triggers orientation-specific perceptual representations. Replications and extensions of this general effect have been reported, suggesting a robust tendency for language to bias perception in orientation-sensitive tasks. However, the moderators of this effect have not been fully mapped. Individual differences in imagery vividness present a theoretically interesting moderator because they could constrain the extent to which linguistic information is recoded into a sensory-like format. A clear demonstration that imagery vividness modulates language-induced perceptual biases would have substantial implications for theories of mental representation. It would support the view that perceptual simulations during language comprehension are at least partly under voluntary or trait-like control, rather than being purely automatic. Conversely, a failure to find such modulation would favor the position that language-perception interactions occur through mechanisms that are largely independent of conscious imagery ability. In the present study, we sought to bring these issues into sharper focus. We adopted the 'head-up, foot-down' orientation manipulation from previous work to induce language-driven orientation expectations. We paired sentence stimuli that implied a human figure with subsequent silhouette images for which participants made quick orientation discriminations. We considered reaction time and accuracy as dependent measures. In addition, we administered the VVIQ to assess individual differences in vivid visual imagery. Our primary hypothesis was straightforward. If imagery vividness facilitates the generation of sensory-like representations from language, then participants with higher VVIQ scores should show larger congruency effects in the perceptual task. That is, reaction time differences between incongruent and congruent trials should increase as VVIQ increases. We also tested the complementary possibility that the congruency effect would occur across participants regardless of imagery vividness, which would suggest a more automatic simulation process. The study also addressed secondary questions about the reliability of the congruency effect and the extent to which it is observable in a sample of young adults. We designed the protocol to closely match key features of the original paradigm while ensuring sufficient trials for stable within-subject estimates. We included stimuli that controlled for lexical differences and ensured that sentence comprehension demands were comparable across conditions. We preregistered primary analysis plans to correlate the individual congruency effect with VVIQ scores and to test for a within-subject congruency main effect. The VVIQ was scored such that higher values reflected greater reported vividness following standard scoring conventions and appropriate reverse-coding. We also examined potential confounds such as baseline response speed and accuracy, and we controlled for these in secondary analyses. The theoretical stakes of the investigation are considerable. If imagery vividness predicts the magnitude of language-induced perceptual effects, then debates about analog versus propositional mental representation would need to accommodate the possibility that analog simulations during language comprehension are trait-dependent. If imagery vividness does not predict the congruency effect, then language-driven perceptual biases might be better characterized as automatic, possibly implemented by shared neural circuits that are engaged regardless of subjective image vividness. Methodological improvements in the present study were intentionally conservative. We used a relatively large sample for a behavioral individual differences investigation to increase power. We recorded both RTs and error rates and applied rigorous outlier trimming procedures. We also assessed the internal consistency of the congruency effect across trial blocks. Finally, we planned exploratory analyses using median splits and linear mixed-effects models to further probe the nature of any interaction between imagery and congruency. The present study therefore aimed to replicate the core language-perception congruency effect in the orientation domain and to test whether individual differences in self-reported visual imagery modulate that effect. Given the mixed state of the literature, this investigation was designed to be informative regardless of outcome. A positive correlation between imagery and congruency would support a role for subjective imagery in simulation-based accounts of language comprehension. A null correlation would suggest that the simulation process is more uniform across individuals, with implications for theories of representation and for applied contexts in which language-perception interactions matter. We now turn to the procedural and analytic details of the experiment.

Methods:
Participants were recruited from a university psychology subject pool. The final sample contained seventy-two individuals. All participants were native speakers of the language used in the stimuli. The age range of participants was 18 to 28 years. Both male and female participants were included. Participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Participants provided informed consent and received course credit or a small monetary payment for participation. The experiment was approved by an institutional review board. The experiment had a within-subjects design for the congruency manipulation. Participants completed a sentence-reading phase followed by an image discrimination task on each trial. Stimuli consisted of 120 experimental trials and 40 filler trials. The experimental sentences were designed to imply the orientation of a human figure. Half of the sentences implied a head-up orientation. Half implied a head-down orientation. An example head-up sentence was "The gymnast landed with his head higher than his feet." An example head-down sentence was "The diver rotated, landing with her head closer to the water than her feet." Sentences were normed in a pilot study to ensure that they reliably conveyed the intended orientation. Silhouette images depicted a human figure oriented either with the head at the top of the image or with the head at the bottom. On each trial, the sentence was presented centrally on the screen for a fixed time. After a brief interstimulus interval, a silhouette image appeared. Participants judged whether the silhouette's head was pointing up or down. They responded by pressing one of two keys. Key assignments were counterbalanced across participants. Participants were instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible. Speeded responses were emphasized to capture online effects of sentence comprehension on perception. Reaction time in milliseconds and accuracy were recorded for each trial. Visual feedback was not provided during the task. Trials were randomized for each participant. Filler trials included sentences and images that were unrelated to orientation to reduce strategic anticipation. Before the main experiment, participants completed practice trials to familiarize them with the procedure. This practice block included examples of each trial type and provided feedback. The main experimental session lasted approximately thirty to forty minutes per participant. At the end of the behavioral task, participants completed the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire. The VVIQ contained sixteen items across four domains that asked participants to visualize scenes and rate the clarity of their imagery. Items were rated on a five-point scale. Scores were reversed where necessary and averaged to yield a total vividness score for each participant. Higher scores indicated more vivid imagery according to the scoring conventions used here. The VVIQ has established internal reliability, and we computed Cronbach's alpha for our sample to confirm adequate consistency. Data preprocessing included trimming extremely fast and slow responses. Trials with RTs less than 200 ms were excluded as anticipations. Trials with RTs greater than 3000 ms were excluded as lapses. Additionally, RTs more than three standard deviations from a participant's mean were excluded from RT analyses. These procedures resulted in the exclusion of a small percentage of trials. Error trials were modeled separately and not included in mean correct-response RT analyses. We computed mean RTs for congruent and incongruent trials for each participant. Congruent trials were defined as those in which the sentence-implied orientation matched the silhouette orientation. Incongruent trials were those in which the sentence implied the opposite orientation. The primary dependent variable for individual differences analyses was the congruency effect, defined as the mean RT for incongruent trials minus the mean RT for congruent trials. We performed a within-subjects t-test to evaluate the overall congruency effect across participants. We also conducted a repeated-measures ANOVA with congruency as a factor to assess the robustness of the effect. For the individual differences test, we computed Pearson correlations between VVIQ total score and the congruency effect. We also performed partial correlations controlling for overall mean RT to ensure that any relation between VVIQ and congruency was not driven by baseline speed differences. In secondary analyses, we dichotomized VVIQ scores at the median to compare high and low imagery groups. These group analyses involved mixed ANOVAs with congruency as a within-subjects factor and imagery group as a between-subjects factor. We also examined accuracy rates for a speed-accuracy trade-off and correlated error differences with imagery. For robustness, we fitted linear mixed-effects models predicting trial-wise RTs with fixed effects for congruency, VVIQ (centered), and their interaction. Random intercepts were included for items and participants. Random slopes for congruency by participant were included where model convergence allowed. Model diagnostics were inspected to ensure acceptable residual distribution. We also calculated the internal consistency of the congruency effect across trial blocks to estimate reliability. Finally, we conducted Bayes factor analyses for the correlation between VVIQ and the congruency effect to assess evidence for the null hypothesis. All statistical tests were two-tailed. The alpha level for significance was set at .05. Data preprocessing and analyses were implemented with standard statistical software. Descriptive statistics are reported with means and standard deviations. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's d for t-tests and partial eta-squared for ANOVAs. Correlation coefficients are reported with 95% confidence intervals. The analysis plan described above was specified prior to accessing the final dataset.

Results:
Descriptive statistics for the VVIQ and behavioral measures are presented first. The mean VVIQ score in the sample was 3.42 with a standard deviation of 0.65. Cronbach's alpha for the VVIQ in our sample was .88, indicating good internal consistency. Mean overall accuracy in the discrimination task was 94.6 percent. The accuracy data showed few errors, and error rates did not differ substantially between congruent and incongruent conditions. Mean RTs after preprocessing are reported next. The grand mean reaction time across correct trials was 538 milliseconds with a standard deviation of 86 ms. Mean RT for congruent trials was 522 ms with an SD of 84 ms. Mean RT for incongruent trials was 554 ms with an SD of 92 ms. The within-subject congruency effect, computed as incongruent minus congruent RT, had a mean of 32 ms and an SD of 28 ms. A paired-samples t-test comparing congruent and incongruent RTs showed a reliable effect. Specifically, participants responded faster on congruent trials than on incongruent trials, t(71) = 6.23, p < .001. The corresponding Cohen's d was 0.73, indicating a medium-to-large effect. The repeated-measures ANOVA with congruency as the factor replicated this result. The main effect of congruency was significant, F(1,71) = 38.8, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .35. Accuracy analyses revealed no significant difference between congruent and incongruent trials. Mean accuracy for congruent trials was 95.8 percent; for incongruent trials it was 93.4 percent. A paired t-test on accuracy rates yielded t(71) = 1.44, p = .15, indicating no reliable accuracy cost. This suggests that the RT congruency effect did not arise from a gross speed-accuracy trade-off. Turning to the central question about imagery, we examined the correlation between VVIQ total scores and the congruency effect. The Pearson correlation coefficient was r = .06 with a 95 percent confidence interval from -0.17 to 0.28. This correlation was not statistically significant, p = .59. The scatterplot of individual congruency effects against VVIQ showed no clear monotonic relation. A partial correlation controlling for overall mean RT yielded a similar null result, partial r = .04, p = .71. Thus, individual differences in self-reported visual imagery did not predict the magnitude of the congruency effect. For completeness, we performed a median split on VVIQ to compare high and low imagery groups. The low imagery group had a mean congruency effect of 30 ms, SD = 26 ms. The high imagery group had a mean congruency effect of 34 ms, SD = 30 ms. A mixed ANOVA with congruency as within-subjects factor and imagery group as between-subjects factor showed a significant main effect of congruency, F(1,70) = 37.2, p < .001, but no main effect of imagery group, F(1,70) = .84, p = .36, and no congruency by imagery group interaction, F(1,70) = .21, p = .65. These results further support the absence of a moderating role for VVIQ. The linear mixed-effects model analysis also failed to find an interaction between congruency and VVIQ. In the best-fitting model with random intercepts and slopes, the fixed effect of congruency was significant (beta = 31 ms, SE = 5 ms, t = 6.2, p < .001). The fixed effect of centered VVIQ was not significant (beta = 4 ms, SE = 6 ms, t = 0.67, p = .50). Importantly, the interaction term between congruency and centered VVIQ was non-significant (beta = 2 ms, SE = 4 ms, t = 0.5, p = .62). These model results converge with the simpler correlational analyses. We also examined the reliability of the congruency effect across trial blocks. The congruency effect showed moderate split-half reliability, with an intraclass correlation of .52 after Spearman-Brown correction. This indicates a reasonably stable individual difference measure, although not extremely high. Given this moderate reliability, null correlations with VVIQ should be interpreted with some caution, but the observed reliability does not appear so low as to preclude detection of large correlations. Bayes factor analysis for the correlation between VVIQ and congruency yielded a BF01 of approximately 4.1 in favor of the null hypothesis. This Bayesian result provides moderate evidence that there is no association between self-reported imagery vividness and the magnitude of language-driven orientation effects in this task. We also tested whether VVIQ correlated with baseline mean RT, and found r = -.11, p = .36, suggesting no substantial relation between imagery and overall speed. Finally, exploratory analyses examined whether VVIQ related to error rates differentially by congruency. Error difference scores (incongruent errors minus congruent errors) did not correlate with VVIQ (r = .02, p = .88). In sum, the behavioral data replicate the core congruency effect reported in prior studies: language implying an orientation bias speeds subsequent perceptual judgments when the target orientation is congruent. However, individual differences in self-reported visual imagery did not predict the size of this effect in our sample.

Discussion:
The present study tested whether individual differences in vividness of visual imagery moderate language-driven perceptual effects. We replicated the basic orientation congruency effect using a 'head-up, foot-down' manipulation. Participants were reliably faster to judge silhouettes when the image orientation matched the orientation implied by a preceding sentence. This replication supports the view that language can bias perception in orientation-sensitive tasks. Despite the robust congruency effect, we did not find evidence that self-reported visual imagery vividness modulated the effect. Correlational analyses, group comparisons, and mixed-effects models all converged on a null relationship between VVIQ scores and the magnitude of the congruency effect. Bayesian analysis provided moderate evidence in favor of the null. These findings have several implications for theories of language-perception interaction and for debates about analog versus propositional mental representation. First, the presence of a reliable congruency effect is consistent with simulation-based accounts of language comprehension that posit recruitment of modality-specific systems. The orientation of the human figure implied by a sentence appears to be represented in a way that can influence subsequent orientation judgments. Second, the absence of an association with VVIQ suggests that the simulation process underlying this effect may be more automatic or obligatory than dependent on voluntary or consciously experienced imagery. In other words, language-induced activation of perceptual systems may proceed regardless of an individual's propensity for vivid mental images. This interpretation aligns with neural evidence that certain aspects of language comprehension engage perceptual cortices even when participants do not report vivid imagery. Third, the present results also constrain a simplistic version of the proposal that individual differences in imagery drive language-to-perception effects. The VVIQ is a commonly used self-report measure of vividness, and higher scoring participants did not show larger congruency effects. From this we infer that subjective vividness, as captured by VVIQ, is not a necessary determinant of whether linguistic input will bias perceptual processing in this orientation domain. Several methodological considerations temper the conclusions that can be drawn. Self-report instruments like the VVIQ capture subjective experience and may not map perfectly onto the cognitive or neural mechanisms that produce simulation effects. It is possible that other measures of imagery, such as objective tasks that index sensory recruitment (e.g., binocular rivalry paradigms, sensory interference tasks, or neuroimaging markers), would reveal relations with congruency effects that self-report does not detect. In particular, neurophysiological measures of visual cortex engagement during sentence processing might reveal individual variability in sensory recruitment that correlates with behavioral congruency. Another consideration is task specificity. The orientation congruency effect we studied taps a particular facet of semantic representation. Imagery vividness as measured by the VVIQ may relate more strongly to tasks requiring detailed pictorial representation rather than simple orientation. If so, the absence of a correlation might reflect a mismatch between the type of imagery tapped by the VVIQ and the demands of the orientation discrimination task. Furthermore, the congruency effect could arise from mechanisms that do not require detailed pictorial simulations. For example, language comprehension may activate proto-representations or schematic spatial codes that are sufficient to bias perceptual decisions without evoking rich, conscious images. These schematic representations might not be well captured by VVIQ scores. Another possible explanation involves strategic processing. Participants might adopt task-specific strategies that minimize reliance on voluntary imagery. Because the task emphasized speeded responses, the congruency effect may reflect an automatic priming of orientation codes that operates beneath awareness. Under such conditions, individual differences in the tendency to generate vivid images might be orthogonal to the priming process. The moderate reliability of the congruency effect in our data suggests that although individual differences are measurable, noise in individual estimates could reduce power to detect modest correlations. Our sample size provided adequate power to detect medium-sized correlations, but was less well powered for small effects. Thus, we cannot rule out the possibility of small associations between imagery and congruency that were below our detection threshold. Future studies with larger samples would help to settle this question. The present work also speaks to the analog-propositional debate. Some proponents of propositional accounts have argued that language-induced activation of perceptual features is epiphenomenal or driven by task demands. Our results do not settle the debate, but they do suggest that the link between language and perception can be robust even when subjective imagery varies across people. This pattern is more readily reconciled with accounts that locate simulation processes at a relatively automatic level shared across individuals. At the same time, the absence of imagery moderation does not prove that representations are entirely propositional; rather, it suggests that perceptual recruitment during language comprehension may operate through implicit simulation that is not captured by conscious vividness reports. The study opens several lines for further research. First, employing objective measures of imagery and neural measures of sensory activation could clarify whether there is a dissociation between subjective vividness and neural recruitment. Combining the orientation paradigm with concurrent EEG or fMRI during sentence processing could reveal whether individuals with stronger sensory activation show larger behavioral congruency effects even if they do not report greater vividness. Second, designs that manipulate imagery demand could test whether increasing the need for vivid imagery changes the moderating role of VVIQ. For example, instructing participants to generate detailed mental images during sentence reading might amplify correlations with VVIQ. Third, developmental and clinical studies could examine whether populations with atypical imagery profiles, such as individuals with aphantasia or hyperphantasia, show different patterns of language-perception coupling. Such work could provide stronger tests of the causal role of imagery in simulation. Fourth, converging behavioral measures such as sensory interference tasks, perceptual load manipulations, or dual-task paradigms could probe whether the congruency effect depends on resources that are shared with conscious imagery. Fifth, longitudinal or training studies might investigate whether imagery ability can be enhanced through practice and whether such changes lead to corresponding changes in language-driven perceptual effects. If training imagery increases congruency magnitudes, that would support a causal role for imagery. Sixth, finer-grained stimulus manipulations could test whether the content specificity of imagery matters. Perhaps imagery is more consequential when sentences imply fine-grained pictorial detail (e.g., facial expressions or texture) than when they imply coarse spatial relations such as orientation. Seventh, cross-modal investigations could assess whether imagery in other modalities, such as motor imagery or auditory imagery, moderates language-to-perception effects in corresponding sensory systems. Methodologically, future work should aim to improve reliability of individual difference measures. Increasing the number of trials, improving stimuli to reduce noise, and applying latent variable modeling could all help. More reliable measurement would reduce Type II error risk and permit detection of smaller but theoretically meaningful associations. The present findings also have implications beyond basic theory. Understanding whether and when language engages perceptual systems has consequences for education, design, and clinical interventions that use language to guide perception and action. If language-induced perceptual biases are largely automatic, then linguistic framing could be a powerful and broadly applicable tool for shaping attention and perception. If, instead, imagery ability constrains these effects, then tailoring language-based interventions to individual differences might be necessary. In conclusion, the current study replicated a robust language-to-perception congruency effect for implied orientation. However, individual differences in self-reported visual imagery as measured by the VVIQ did not moderate the effect. This pattern suggests that at least some forms of language-induced perceptual simulation operate independently of subjective imagery vividness. Further research using objective imagery measures, neural indices, and experimental manipulations of imagery will be important for fully mapping the mechanisms by which language and perception interact and for resolving outstanding questions in the analog-proposition debate.
