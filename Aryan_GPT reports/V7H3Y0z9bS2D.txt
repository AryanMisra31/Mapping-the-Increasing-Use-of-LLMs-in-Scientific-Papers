Introduction

Mood states influence a wide range of cognitive processes, including attention, memory, and decision making. Researchers have long recognized that the affective state of an observer can bias what information is attended to, how information is interpreted, and how rapidly responses are generated. One well-established phenomenon in perception is the face-in-a-crowd effect, in which certain emotionally valenced faces are detected more rapidly and accurately among distractor faces than others. A commonly reported variant of this effect is that negative faces, particularly angry or fearful expressions, are found more quickly than positive faces when embedded among neutral distractors. This rapid detection of negative faces has been interpreted as evidence for an evolved threat detection system and is thought to reflect an attentional bias toward potentially harmful cues in the environment. At the same time, mood-congruency effects have been reported across many domains of cognition such that individuals are more likely to attend to, remember, and interpret information that is congruent with their current affective state. For example, individuals in a negative mood often show enhanced processing of negative information in memory tasks and interpret ambiguous stimuli more negatively. These two literatures—affective influences on visual attention and mood-congruency effects—invite questions about whether an observer’s current affective state modulates the face-in-a-crowd advantage for negative faces. The question is theoretically important because it speaks to the locus of affective modulation in perception: are early perceptual processes that underlie rapid detection of salient facial expressions influenced by transient mood states of the observer, or are these detection processes relatively automatic and impervious to momentary affect? A clear answer would refine models of affect-attention interaction by indicating whether mood exerts top-down control over early attentional capture by emotional stimuli. Prior research has examined mood-congruency effects in tasks such as memory recall and interpretation of ambiguous stimuli, and there is some evidence that mood can influence selective attention in visual search tasks more broadly. However, the literature specifically investigating how mood states alter the face-in-a-crowd effect remains sparse and equivocal. Some studies have reported that induced sadness or anxiety can increase attention toward threat-related stimuli, while others have failed to find reliable modulation of early attentional capture by mood manipulations. These mixed findings may result from methodological differences across studies, including variations in mood induction procedures, the type of emotional expressions used as targets, the similarity between targets and distractors, and the timing and sensitivity of the measures employed. There is also conceptual heterogeneity: mood is a diffuse, sustained affective state, whereas emotion is an immediate, stimulus-bound reaction, and these constructs might differentially influence attentional processes. When mood is operationalized and measured carefully, mood-congruency effects are more consistently observed in tasks that involve controlled processing or interpretation rather than tasks that rely on rapid automatic detection. Therefore, an open question is whether mood congruency extends to rapid visual search for emotional faces, or whether the face-in-a-crowd advantage for negative faces is robust to transient mood manipulations. The current study was designed to test the hypothesis that participants will be faster at detecting target faces that are mood congruent with their induced affective state. Specifically, if mood-congruency influences early attentional selection, participants in a positive mood should detect happy target faces more quickly than angry targets, whereas participants in a negative mood should detect angry targets more quickly than happy targets. This directional hypothesis aligns with a top-down facilitation account in which mood primes related valence representations and biases attentional sets. An alternative view is that the face-in-a-crowd effect is driven by bottom-up salience and threat-detection mechanisms that operate independently of transient mood states, leading to a persistent negative-face detection advantage irrespective of mood. To adjudicate between these accounts, we combined a mood induction procedure with a standard visual search paradigm that required participants to find a single target face among an array of neutral distractors. The study used both positive (happy) and negative (angry) target faces to examine valence-specific search performance. The visual arrays were constructed to vary target valence while keeping distractors neutral so that any mood-related facilitation would reflect congruency effects rather than generalized attention shifts. We designed the task to include multiple set sizes to probe whether mood influences search efficiency as set size increases, which would indicate modulation of attentional selection processes rather than simple response bias. In addition to response time measures, we recorded accuracy to assess potential speed-accuracy trade-offs. Before the search task, participants underwent a mood induction intended to bias their affective state toward either positive or negative valence. Mood was measured before and after induction using a standardized self-report questionnaire to verify the success of the manipulation, and mood scores were treated as both a manipulation check and a continuous predictor in exploratory analyses. The study also included filler tasks and careful counterbalancing of target valence and trial order to minimize learning and expectancy effects. Based on mood-congruency predictions, we expected an interaction between induced mood and target valence, such that detection times would be faster for mood-congruent targets. We also examined whether the classic negative face search advantage would replicate in our sample as a main effect of target valence, predicting faster detection of negative targets across mood conditions if the effect is robust. Finally, we planned exploratory analyses to assess whether individual differences, such as baseline affect or trait measures, related to search performance and whether mood induction strength moderated any observed effects. Together, these design choices were intended to provide a rigorous test of whether transient mood modulates rapid detection of emotional faces in crowded visual arrays, thereby advancing theoretical understanding of affect-attention interactions and clarifying boundary conditions for mood-congruency phenomena.

Methods

Participants were adults recruited from a university subject pool and the surrounding community. Sixty-four participants were enrolled in the study, and data from sixty participants were retained after exclusion criteria were applied based on failure to follow task instructions or extreme response times. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and were free of neurological or psychiatric diagnoses that would affect visual processing. The sample contained a mix of undergraduate students and community volunteers and included both men and women across a range of ages from late teens to early thirties. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were compensated with course credit or a small monetary payment. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the university. A power analysis conducted prior to data collection indicated that a total sample of 60 participants would provide adequate power (approximately 0.80) to detect a medium-sized interaction effect in a mixed ANOVA design with two between-subject groups and two within-subject target valence conditions. The mood induction procedure used well-validated film clips and accompanying music selected to elicit either a positive affective state or a negative affective state. Positive mood induction clips depicted humorous or joyful scenes, while negative mood induction clips depicted mildly unpleasant but non-traumatic scenes. Music that matched the intended valence accompanied each clip to enhance induction strength. Participants were randomly assigned to either the positive mood or negative mood induction condition in a between-subjects design. Mood was assessed using a standardized affect questionnaire administered immediately before the mood induction and immediately after the induction. The questionnaire captured both positive and negative affect dimensions, allowing us to compute change scores to evaluate the success of the mood manipulation. The visual search stimuli consisted of photorealistic images of faces selected from a standardized database of facial expressions. Target faces included clearly happy expressions to represent positive valence and clearly angry expressions to represent negative valence. Distractor faces were neutral expressions. All faces were standardized for size, luminance, and against a uniform background to control low-level visual features that could influence search. Each search display contained either a target face among neutral distractors (target-present trials) or only neutral distractors (target-absent trials), and we focused our analyses on target-present trials to examine detection speed. Arrays were presented in two set sizes, small and large, corresponding to four and eight faces respectively, to probe search efficiency as a function of display complexity. Targets could appear at any location within the array, and their positions were randomized across trials to prevent location-based strategies. Each participant completed a practice block to become familiar with the task, followed by experimental blocks that balanced target valence and set size within participants. The total number of experimental trials per participant was sufficient to provide stable reaction time estimates, with 160 target-present trials evenly distributed across conditions and interleaved with target-absent trials. The face search task was presented on a computer monitor at a fixed viewing distance in a quiet laboratory room. Participants responded using a keyboard, pressing one key to indicate that a target was present and another key when they believed no target was present. Reaction times were recorded from the onset of the array until the button press, with response deadlines that were generous to avoid pressure-induced speed-accuracy trade-offs. Accuracy feedback was not provided during the main task to avoid feedback-driven strategy changes. Prior to the main task, participants completed a brief visual acuity check and practiced the response mapping with a small set of stimuli. The experimental procedure began with baseline mood assessment, followed by the mood induction, the post-induction mood assessment, and then the visual search task. After completion of the search task, participants completed a short battery of questionnaires assessing trait affect and demographic information. Participants were debriefed about the mood induction after the session and returned to baseline affect before leaving the laboratory when necessary. Data processing included removal of incorrect trials from reaction time analyses and exclusion of trials with response times outside a plausible range. We eliminated trials with reaction times shorter than 200 ms or longer than 3000 ms as anticipatory or inattentive responses, respectively. For each participant and condition, we trimmed reaction times by removing values that were more than 2.5 standard deviations from that participant’s mean in that condition to reduce the impact of outliers. The primary dependent variable was mean reaction time on correct target-present trials. Accuracy was computed as the proportion of correct responses for each condition and examined to rule out speed-accuracy trade-offs. Primary statistical analyses involved a mixed-design ANOVA with Mood Condition (positive, negative) as a between-subjects factor and Target Valence (positive target, negative target) and Set Size (small, large) as within-subjects factors. We evaluated main effects and interactions and followed up any significant interactions with planned contrasts comparing mood-congruent and mood-incongruent target detection speeds. Effect sizes (partial eta-squared) and 95% confidence intervals were reported for all ANOVA effects to provide information about the magnitude and precision of observed effects. In addition to the frequentist analyses, we conducted exploratory correlational analyses using participants’ post-induction mood scores as continuous predictors of reaction time to investigate whether individual variability in induced mood related to search performance. We also ran Bayesian analyses for key null results to assess the strength of evidence for the absence of hypothesized effects. All statistical tests used an alpha level of .05 for significance, and whenever sphericity assumptions were relevant we applied the Greenhouse-Geisser correction. Data analysis was performed with standard statistical software packages, and all data processing followed a pre-specified analysis plan that was registered prior to data inspection. Any deviations from the preregistered plan are noted in the results section.

Results

The mood induction manipulation check indicated that the procedure successfully shifted self-reported affect in the intended direction. Participants in the positive induction condition reported an increase in positive affect from pre- to post-induction, whereas participants in the negative induction condition reported an increase in negative affect from pre- to post-induction. A mixed ANOVA on positive affect scores with Time (pre, post) as a within-subject factor and Mood Condition as a between-subjects factor yielded a significant Time x Condition interaction, driven by increases in positive affect only in the positive induction group. Similarly, a mixed ANOVA on negative affect scores showed a significant Time x Condition interaction, driven by increased negative affect in the negative induction group. These manipulation checks confirmed that mood induction altered participants’ subjective affective states in the intended directions, providing a sound basis for testing mood-congruency hypotheses. Following data trimming and exclusion of a small number of participants for noncompliance, the reaction time data were analyzed for the remaining sixty participants. On average, participants responded accurately on 94% of target-present trials, indicating high task engagement. Mean reaction times were computed separately for each participant and condition after outlier trimming. Descriptive statistics showed that mean reaction times were similar across mood conditions and target valences, with slightly longer times for the larger set size, as expected. The primary mixed-design ANOVA with Mood Condition (positive, negative) as a between-subjects factor and Target Valence (happy, angry) and Set Size (small, large) as within-subjects factors revealed no significant main effect of Mood Condition on reaction time. The F statistic for Mood Condition was small and non-significant, indicating no overall speed advantage for participants in one mood over the other. There was a significant main effect of Set Size, with faster detection at the small set size compared to the large set size, consistent with standard visual search results showing increased processing time with more distractors. The main effect of Target Valence was not significant; angry targets were not detected significantly faster than happy targets across the sample, and the classic negative face search advantage was not observed in the group-level data. Crucially, the hypothesized Mood Condition x Target Valence interaction was not significant. Planned contrasts comparing mood-congruent versus mood-incongruent detection times showed no statistically reliable facilitation for mood-congruent targets in either induction group. Effect size estimates for the interaction were small and the 95% confidence intervals encompassed values close to zero, suggesting that any facilitative effect of mood on target valence detection, if present, was small. To quantify evidence for the null hypothesis, we conducted Bayesian analyses for the Mood x Target Valence interaction, which yielded Bayes factors favoring the null model over the interaction model by approximately three to one, indicating anecdotal to moderate evidence in favor of no interaction. Accuracy rates did not differ across mood conditions or target valences, and there were no significant interactions involving accuracy, which indicates that null reaction time differences were not obscured by speed-accuracy trade-offs. We conducted exploratory correlational analyses between post-induction mood scores treated as continuous predictors and reaction times for each target valence. These correlations were small and non-significant, and the confidence intervals around the correlation coefficients included zero. We also examined whether baseline trait affect measures moderated the relationship between induced mood and search performance by testing three-way interactions; none of these exploratory analyses yielded significant effects. Subgroup analyses splitting the sample by induction strength—operationalized as the magnitude of the change in self-reported affect from pre- to post-induction—did not reveal reliable mood-congruency effects in the subgroup with the largest affective shifts. In other words, even participants who reported the strongest change in mood did not show faster detection of mood-congruent target faces. We also inspected reaction time distributions to ensure that trimming procedures did not obscure condition differences, and distributional comparisons confirmed that the shape of reaction time distributions was comparable across conditions. Given the absence of a target valence main effect, we conducted post hoc analyses to explore whether stimulus-level factors could have attenuated the expected negative-face advantage. We analyzed reaction times as a function of specific target exemplars and found some variability across individual faces, but no systematic pattern that would favor negative expressions across exemplars. Additional analyses excluding a small subset of atypical exemplars did not change the overall null pattern of results. Finally, we evaluated practice and fatigue effects across the session by testing for interactions between block order and experimental conditions; these analyses showed modest slowing over time but no selective slowing or improvement for particular mood-valence combinations. In sum, the data demonstrated that the mood induction successfully altered subjective affect, that set size affected search time as expected, but that neither a main effect of target valence nor the predicted mood-congruency facilitation on detection speed was observed. The Bayesian analyses provided modest support for the absence of interaction effects, and exploratory analyses failed to reveal latent moderators that would account for the lack of mood-dependent differences in visual search performance.

Discussion

The present study tested whether transient mood states of observers modulate rapid detection of emotional faces in crowded arrays and whether mood-congruent facilitation would be observed in a visual search context. We hypothesized that participants would be faster to detect target faces that matched their induced mood—happy targets for participants induced into a positive mood and angry targets for participants induced into a negative mood. We also examined whether the canonical negative-face advantage would emerge as a main effect of target valence. Our results did not support these hypotheses: although the mood induction successfully altered self-reported affect in the intended directions, neither a mood main effect nor a mood-by-target valence interaction was observed for reaction times. Similarly, the expected negative-face search advantage was not replicated in our dataset. These findings have several theoretical and methodological implications that merit careful consideration. First, the absence of mood-congruency effects in the face-in-a-crowd task suggests that transient mood states may not robustly influence very early, rapid attentional selection processes, at least under the specific conditions tested here. If mood only exerts influence at later stages of processing—such as interpretive or memory-related stages—then tasks that tap into immediate perceptual detection may show little sensitivity to mood manipulations. This interpretation aligns with the view that mood-congruency effects are more consistently observed in cognitive operations requiring controlled, elaborative processing rather than in automatic, stimulus-driven attentional capture. The lack of a mood effect therefore supports a model in which bottom-up salience and stimulus properties drive initial capture, whereas top-down mood-related biases influence subsequent processing when sufficient time and cognitive resources permit. Second, the failure to observe a negative-face search advantage in this study raises questions about the boundary conditions for that effect. Although many studies have reported faster detection of angry or fearful faces, the effect is not invariably replicated, and it may depend on specific stimulus characteristics such as expression intensity, face identity variability, low-level visual features, and the distinctiveness of target-distractor differences. In our study, stimuli were carefully standardized, but such controls may have reduced distinctive visual cues that facilitate rapid detection of negative expressions in less controlled settings. Additionally, using neutral distractors could produce different dynamics than using distractors with mixed valence or varying emotional salience; some previous demonstrations of negative-face advantages used happy distractors or mixed arrays, and the comparative contrast between target and distractors may be critical. Third, methodological factors related to the mood induction and task parameters could account for the null interactions. Although manipulation checks showed significant shifts in self-reported affect, the absolute magnitude of change may have been modest relative to the intensity of the stimuli used in the visual search. It is possible that comparatively subtle changes in mood do not exert strong top-down biasing effects on the rapid perceptual systems that detect salient facial expressions. Moreover, we used a between-subjects mood induction design to avoid carryover effects, but this design can be less sensitive to mood-related differences between conditions than within-subject manipulations. A within-subjects design in which the same participants undergo both positive and negative inductions on different days might yield higher sensitivity to detect mood-congruent facilitation. Fourth, the role of arousal as distinct from valence may be important. Mood inductions that vary primarily in valence but not in arousal may have different effects than inductions that also manipulate physiological activation. Arousal has been shown to modulate attentional breadth and sensitivity, and it is possible that mood manipulations that induced higher arousal would have produced greater modulation of search performance. In our study, while both valence and arousal were affected by the inductions to some extent, the arousal differences across conditions may not have been sufficient to influence early attentional capture. Fifth, individual differences could moderate mood effects on attention but were not found to do so in our exploratory analyses. Trait affect measures and baseline mood did not account for variability in detection performance, yet the sample size may have been insufficient to detect small moderating effects. Future research with larger samples and targeted recruitment of individuals high in trait anxiety or depression may reveal interactions between stable affective dispositions and transient mood manipulations in shaping attentional biases to emotional faces. Sixth, the spatial and temporal parameters of the visual search display could influence whether mood can bias detection. In our paradigm, arrays were presented centrally and remained on the screen until response, allowing participants to scan freely. If mood exerts subtle biases in early ocular fixations rather than in manual response speed, eye-tracking might reveal valence-specific attentional patterns that are not captured by overall reaction times. Future studies should incorporate eye-tracking measures to test whether observers in different moods direct their initial saccades preferentially toward mood-congruent faces even when overall reaction times are similar. Seventh, neuroscientific measures such as event-related potentials could probe whether mood modulates early electrophysiological markers of face processing even when behavioral measures show null effects. For example, mood might affect the amplitude of early ERP components associated with emotional face processing without translating into faster behavioral responses. Incorporating EEG or MEG measures could thus provide a more nuanced understanding of the temporal dynamics of mood influences on perception. Eighth, ecological validity is a consideration. Laboratory-based mood inductions and static face images are limited approximations of real-world affective encounters. In naturalistic environments, mood and attention interact dynamically with context, social cues, and motivation. Studies using more naturalistic stimuli such as dynamic facial expressions, real crowds, or virtual reality environments may reveal mood-dependent attentional biases that static arrays do not capture. Ninth, our findings highlight the need to reassess the assumption that mood-congruency effects generalize straightforwardly across cognitive domains. Theoretical models that posit broad mood-congruency across perception, attention, and memory may need to be refined to specify boundary conditions and mechanisms—namely when mood functions as a top-down attentional set and when perception is dominated by stimulus-driven salience. Tenth, statistical considerations are relevant. Our Bayesian analyses provided modest evidence favoring the null interaction, but the strength of this evidence was not decisive. Thus, we cannot conclusively demonstrate the absence of subtle mood effects on rapid face detection. Larger samples, pre-registered replication studies, and meta-analytic synthesis of studies with similar designs will be needed to estimate the true effect sizes of mood modulation on face-in-a-crowd phenomena. Eleventh, alternative paradigms might increase sensitivity to mood effects. For example, using backward masking to limit conscious awareness of distractors, or imposing brief exposure times for the arrays, could make the task more dependent on early perceptual processes that might be susceptible to mood-based top-down biases. Conversely, extending stimulus exposure and adding task demands requiring evaluative judgments might shift processing toward stages where mood-congruency is more likely to emerge. Twelfth, the specificity of negative expressions deserves attention. "Negative" in prior literature encompasses emotions such as fear, anger, and sadness, each of which has distinct signaling properties. Threat-related expressions like anger and fear may have different detection dynamics, and mood manipulations might differentially interact with these subtypes. Our use of angry targets as the negative valence category provides one test case but does not rule out mood effects for other negative expressions. Thirteenth, clinical implications are intriguing. If mood does not modulate rapid detection of emotional faces in healthy adults, treatments for mood disorders aimed at altering attentional biases may need to target later-stage interpretive processes rather than early perceptual encoding. However, it remains possible that in clinical populations with chronic mood disturbances, sustained affective states do alter early attention in ways that transient inductions in healthy participants do not. Fourteenth, the study contributes methodologically by demonstrating the importance of manipulation checks and multi-level analyses when testing affect-attention hypotheses. Confirming the efficacy of mood inductions is necessary but not sufficient; studies must also ensure that behavioral measures are sensitive to the hypothesized effects. Finally, future research should systematically vary induction strength, arousal, valence, and stimulus characteristics while employing complementary measures such as eye-tracking and electrophysiology. Such multifaceted approaches will help determine whether mood influences are absent at the level of rapid perceptual detection or whether they are present but subtle and context-dependent. In conclusion, the present study found that while mood induction successfully altered subjective affect, it did not facilitate detection of mood-congruent faces in a visual search paradigm, nor did it replicate the negative-face search advantage. These null results suggest that transient mood may not exert strong top-down modulation over early attentional capture by emotional faces under the tested conditions. The findings refine theoretical accounts by indicating that mood-congruency effects may be more selective than previously assumed and underscore the need for further research that employs diverse methodologies to map the boundaries of affective modulation of attention.
