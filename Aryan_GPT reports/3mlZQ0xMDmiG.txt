Introduction

Sleep occupies a foundational role in human health and cognitive functioning across the lifespan. Normal sleep supports memory consolidation, emotional regulation, and executive functioning. When sleep is insufficient, widespread cognitive and affective processes are disrupted. Large-scale epidemiological studies and controlled laboratory experiments have repeatedly demonstrated that acute and chronic sleep restriction can impair attention, working memory, and decision-making. The domain of decision-making is multidimensional, and one specific facet of interest is risk-taking behaviour. Risk-taking describes choices that involve potential for loss or harm in exchange for potential gains, and such behaviours are relevant to public health, legal outcomes, and personal well-being. There is a growing body of literature indicating that inadequate sleep can increase propensity to engage in risky activities. Experimental sleep deprivation paradigms have shown that restricted sleep can alter reward sensitivity and reduce top-down control, which are mechanisms plausibly linked to increased risk-taking. Neurobiological models emphasize the role of the prefrontal cortex in exerting control over impulses, and sleep loss has been associated with altered prefrontal functioning. At the same time, reward-related circuitry, including striatal regions, may become relatively more influential following sleep loss, biasing decisions toward immediate rewards. Together, these lines of evidence underpin a theoretical expectation that poor sleep should be associated with elevated risk-taking. Observational studies have augmented experimental findings by documenting associations between shorter sleep duration and a range of risky outcomes. For example, epidemiological work has linked lower average nightly sleep to greater likelihood of substance use, aggressive behaviour, truancy, and even severe mental health symptoms. Such findings have raised concerns for adolescents and young adults, populations that commonly experience both reduced sleep and elevated risk-taking. However, the literature is not uniform, and there are multiple methodological variations across studies. Some investigations assess short-term, experimentally induced sleep loss, while others focus on habitual sleep measured via self-report. The magnitude and nature of associations between sleep and risk-taking vary with such methodological choices. Another important consideration in parsing this literature is the role of psychosocial stress. Stress influences both sleep and risk-taking, often in opposing or interlinked ways. High stress can lead to reductions in sleep quantity and quality, and it can also influence decision-making under uncertainty, sometimes increasing risky choices and sometimes eliciting risk-avoidant behaviour depending on context. Because stress is linked to both predictor and outcome variables in the sleep-risk relationship, it is a plausible confounder and potential mediator or moderator. Many prior studies have not fully accounted for stress or have used coarse measures that limit interpretability. This gap is noteworthy because failing to account for stress may inflate observed associations between sleep and risk-taking or may obscure more nuanced relations. In addition, risk-taking is a heterogeneous construct that can be measured in multiple ways, including self-report questionnaires, behavioural economic tasks, and clinical records of risky real-world behaviours. Different measurement approaches tap different components of risk propensity and may differ in their sensitivity to sleep-related effects. The choice dilemma questionnaire is one instrument that provides a structured assessment of hypothetical decision-making scenarios and yields a summary risk-taking score. It captures a cognitive-affective aspect of risk propensity without requiring engagement in real-world harmful behaviours. Self-report measures of sleep, often used in large-scale studies, estimate average nightly sleep duration over a defined period but can be influenced by recall bias and subjective perceptions. Objective measures such as actigraphy or polysomnography provide higher fidelity but are more resource intensive and less feasible for large or convenience samples. Given these measurement and design heterogeneities, it remains unclear whether habitual, self-reported sleep duration shows robust associations with risk-taking when key confounds such as perceived stress are considered. Moreover, when prior studies have found relationships, it is not always possible to disentangle whether sleep directly influences risk propensity, whether stress or other third variables drive the association, or whether interactions among these factors are at play. The current study sought to address a specific portion of this broader question by examining average self-reported hours of sleep on typical nights in relation to risk-taking measured by a choice dilemma questionnaire. Importantly, the study incorporated a validated measure of perceived stress in order to control for its potential confounding influence. This design allowed examination of the unique contribution of sleep duration to risk propensity above and beyond contemporaneous stress levels. The rationale for focusing on average sleep duration was pragmatic and theoretical. Habitual sleep patterns are ecologically relevant and reflect chronic behaviours that may influence long-term decision-making processes. If chronic short sleep were linked to stable increases in risk-taking, this would have implications for prevention and public health messaging. Conversely, if associations are absent after accounting for stress, then interventions targeting stress reduction might be more efficient for mitigating risk behaviours than those focused solely on increasing sleep duration. In developing hypotheses, the study drew on prior experimental and observational findings that suggest shorter sleep is associated with greater risk-taking. Therefore, the primary hypothesis was that fewer self-reported hours of sleep would be associated with higher risk-taking scores on the choice dilemma questionnaire. A secondary consideration was that perceived stress would be inversely related to sleep and positively related to risk-taking, and that controlling for stress would reduce but not eliminate the association between sleep and risk propensity. This pattern would be consistent with a model in which sleep contributes uniquely to risk-taking above and beyond shared variance with stress. The current work also considered alternative possibilities. It was plausible that habitual sleep duration might show no unique association with self-reported risk propensity once stress is accounted for, especially if sleep influences risk-taking primarily through transient states or through aspects of sleep quality not captured by duration alone. Another alternative was that the psycho-biological effects of sleep deprivation observed in laboratory settings may not generalize to habitual sleep patterns in community samples. To test these competing possibilities, the study collected self-report data from a community sample of young adults and conducted correlational and partial correlational analyses. The analysis plan was structured to examine zero-order associations as well as the unique relationship between sleep and risk-taking controlling for perceived stress. The study also explored demographic covariates and conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of findings to outliers and restricted ranges. Overall, this study aimed to clarify whether average nightly sleep duration has a detectable relationship with cognitive-affective risk propensity when perceived stress is statistically controlled. By doing so, the research addressed an important methodological gap in the sleep and risk literature and sought to inform theoretical models linking sleep, stress, and decision-making. The findings from this study have implications for understanding pathways to risk behaviour and for designing interventions targeting sleep or stress to reduce harmful decision-making. In sum, this introduction has outlined theoretical and empirical reasons to expect a relation between sleep and risk-taking, highlighted the central role of stress as a potential confounder, and justified the current study’s focus on habitual sleep duration, risk propensity via choice dilemma questionnaire, and statistical control of perceived stress. The stage was set for a focused empirical test of these associations in a young adult convenience sample.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university community and surrounding areas through posters, class announcements, and online advertisements. A convenience sample of 262 individuals initially responded to study advertisements and completed an online screening form. Of these, 248 participants provided complete data on the primary variables and were included in the analyses. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years of age and fluent in English. Exclusion criteria were minimal to enhance generalizability and included only self-reported history of neurological disorders or current use of medications known to substantially alter sleep architecture. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 35 years, with a mean age of 20.9 years and a standard deviation of 3.1 years. The sample included 167 identifying as female, 78 identifying as male, and 3 identifying as non-binary or preferring not to label gender; analyses primarily controlled for sex at the binary level where relevant because of sample size limitations in non-binary subcategories. Participants provided informed consent online prior to beginning the survey, and the study protocol was approved by the university’s institutional review board. Data collection occurred during a single session conducted through a secure online survey platform. The survey comprised demographic questions followed by standardized self-report measures. Participants were instructed to answer honestly and were assured that responses were confidential and used only in aggregate analyses. The primary predictor measure was self-reported average hours of sleep per night. Participants responded to a question asking, “On average, how many hours of sleep do you get per night over the past month?” They provided a numerical estimate in hours and fractions (e.g., 7.5). This approach aligns with common epidemiological practice for estimating habitual sleep duration in survey research. The primary outcome measure was the Choice Dilemma Questionnaire (CDQ), administered in its standard format. The CDQ presents a series of hypothetical decision-making scenarios that vary in stakes and uncertainty and asks participants to indicate their preferred course of action. Items were scored according to the instrument’s guidelines, yielding a total score with higher values indicating greater propensity to choose risky options. The CDQ is widely used as a brief measure of risk propensity in non-clinical samples and is sensitive to individual differences in decision-making under hypothetical risk. The primary covariate measure was perceived stress, assessed by the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS). The PSS is a brief self-report instrument that asks respondents to rate the degree to which situations in their lives are appraised as stressful over the past month. Scores were computed according to standard procedures, with higher scores indicating greater perceived stress. Demographic variables included age, sex, year of study, and employment status; these were collected to characterize the sample and to explore potential covariate effects. The study protocol included quality control checks such as attention checks and response time monitoring to exclude inattentive or careless responses. Data cleaning removed five cases for failing attention checks and nine cases for implausible or inconsistent responses, leaving the final sample of 248. Preliminary data inspection evaluated distributions, missingness, and the presence of outliers. Missing data were minimal; no participant was missing more than 5% of items on any scale, and listwise deletion was used for the primary analyses to maintain simplicity and transparency. Descriptive statistics were computed for hours of sleep, CDQ scores, and PSS scores. Normality of primary variables was assessed using skewness and kurtosis indices and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Sleep hours showed a modest negative skew consistent with a cluster around 7–8 hours, whereas CDQ and PSS scores approximated normal distributions. No extreme values required transformation for the primary correlational analyses. Primary analyses examined the zero-order Pearson correlations between hours of sleep, CDQ scores, and PSS scores. The central test was a partial correlation between hours of sleep and CDQ scores controlling for PSS to evaluate whether sleep retained a unique association with risk propensity beyond perceived stress. In addition to partial correlations, linear regression models were estimated with CDQ score as the dependent variable and sleep hours as the predictor. The first regression model included sleep hours only. The second model added PSS score to evaluate the change in association and variance explained. A third model added age and sex to control for demographic covariates. All regression models were assessed for assumptions including linearity, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity; variance inflation factors were examined for predictors in multivariate models. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the influence of extreme sleepers. The sample contained individuals reporting fewer than four hours or more than nine hours of sleep; separate analyses excluded these extreme cases to determine whether findings were robust to restricted ranges. Additionally, analyses examined whether results differed when controlling for self-reported sleep quality using a single-item sleep quality rating included as an exploratory measure. Effect sizes (Pearson r and standardized regression coefficients) and 95% confidence intervals were reported for all primary associations. Statistical significance was evaluated at an alpha level of .05, two-tailed. All analyses were performed in standard statistical software with reproducible code. The analytic plan was pre-registered prior to data analysis to reduce researcher degrees of freedom and to increase transparency about primary and secondary analyses. Participants received a small course credit or a nominal monetary compensation for their participation depending on their recruitment pathway. The study duration for each participant averaged approximately 20 minutes. After completing the survey, participants were debriefed and provided with information on sleep hygiene and stress management resources available through university services.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the final sample are reported first. The mean self-reported average nightly sleep duration was 6.6 hours with a standard deviation of 1.2 hours. The minimum reported sleep duration was 3.5 hours and the maximum was 10.0 hours. The choice dilemma questionnaire scores had a mean of 12.1 and a standard deviation of 4.2, with a possible score range extending beyond observed values in the current sample. Perceived stress scale scores had a mean of 18.3 and a standard deviation of 6.1. Inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots indicated that the distributions of CDQ and PSS approximated normality, while sleep hours showed a modest negative skew reflecting a clustering around normative values. No variable exhibited kurtosis values suggesting severe deviation from normality. Preliminary correlational analyses examined zero-order relationships among the three primary variables. The Pearson correlation between average hours of sleep and CDQ score was r = -0.08. This correlation was negative in direction, consistent with the hypothesis that fewer hours of sleep would be associated with higher risk-taking, but the magnitude was small. The p-value associated with the correlation between sleep hours and CDQ was p = .18, indicating that the zero-order association did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance. The 95% confidence interval for the correlation between sleep hours and CDQ spanned approximately -0.21 to 0.05, indicating that the data were compatible with a small negative or near-zero effect. The Pearson correlation between perceived stress and sleep hours was r = -0.25, and this association was statistically significant at p < .001. This finding indicates that higher perceived stress was associated with fewer reported hours of sleep, with a small-to-moderate effect size. The correlation between perceived stress and CDQ risk score was r = 0.20, which was significant at p = .003, suggesting that higher stress levels were associated with greater reported risk propensity. Given these zero-order relationships, the primary analysis focused on the partial correlation between sleep hours and CDQ scores controlling for perceived stress. The partial correlation coefficient was r_partial = -0.05, with a p-value of .35. This partial correlation was smaller in magnitude than the zero-order correlation and did not reach statistical significance. The 95% confidence interval for the partial correlation included zero, indicating no reliable unique linear association between average hours of sleep and risk-taking after accounting for perceived stress. Linear regression models provided converging information. A univariate regression predicting CDQ score from sleep hours alone yielded an unstandardized coefficient of b = -0.45 (standard error = 0.32), corresponding to a small and non-significant effect, p = .16. The model explained approximately 0.6% of the variance in CDQ scores (R^2 = .006). Adding perceived stress to the model reduced the regression coefficient for sleep hours to b = -0.26 (SE = 0.28), p = .35, and increased the overall variance explained to R^2 = .08. In this two-predictor model, perceived stress was a significant predictor of CDQ scores with b = 0.21 (SE = 0.07), p = .003. Inclusion of demographic covariates (age and sex) did not substantially alter these patterns. The regression coefficient for sleep hours remained non-significant (b = -0.24, SE = 0.28, p = .39) in the fully adjusted model, while perceived stress retained statistical significance (b = 0.19, SE = 0.07, p = .006). Variance inflation factors for all predictors were below 1.5, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. Assumption checks for the regression models indicated acceptable linearity and homoscedasticity. Residual plots did not reveal systematic deviations or heteroscedastic patterns that would threaten inference. Sensitivity analyses examined whether extreme sleepers influenced the results. Excluding participants reporting fewer than four hours or more than nine hours of average sleep reduced the sample to 238. In this restricted sample, the zero-order correlation between sleep hours and CDQ was r = -0.07, p = .24, and the partial correlation controlling for perceived stress was r_partial = -0.03, p = .58. Thus, the exclusion of extreme cases did not materially change the null pattern for the unique sleep–risk association. Exploratory analyses considered an additional single-item measure of self-rated sleep quality. When sleep quality was entered into the regression model alongside sleep duration and perceived stress, sleep quality was a modest predictor of CDQ scores (b = -0.12, SE = 0.06, p = .04), with better sleep quality associated with lower risk-taking. However, even in that model the coefficient for average sleep hours remained non-significant (b = -0.19, SE = 0.27, p = .47). This pattern suggests that subjective sleep quality captures variance relevant to risk propensity that raw duration does not. Subgroup analyses examined whether the sleep–risk relationship differed by sex. Interaction terms between sleep hours and sex in regression models were non-significant (interaction b = -0.12, SE = 0.22, p = .59), indicating no evidence of differential effects by biological sex in this sample. Age was not a significant moderator either, with interaction terms failing to reach significance. Power considerations were evaluated post hoc. Given the sample size of 248, the study had adequate power (>.80) to detect small-to-moderate correlations (around r = .18 or larger) but limited power to detect very small associations (r < .10). The observed zero-order correlation of -0.08 fell below the detectable threshold for small effects with high confidence. Taken together, the pattern of results shows that perceived stress is significantly related to both shorter sleep and greater risk propensity, but after statistically controlling for perceived stress, average self-reported nightly sleep duration does not demonstrate a significant unique association with risk-taking measured by the choice dilemma questionnaire. These null findings held across sensitivity analyses that excluded extreme sleepers and across models adjusting for basic demographics. The exploratory finding that subjective sleep quality had a weak association with risk propensity suggests potential complexity beyond duration alone.

Discussion

The present study investigated the association between habitual self-reported sleep duration and risk-taking propensity, assessed via a choice dilemma questionnaire, while controlling for perceived stress. The principal finding was that average hours of sleep were not significantly related to risk-taking after statistically adjusting for perceived stress. Perceived stress itself was associated with both reduced sleep and elevated risk propensity, consistent with the hypothesis that stress occupies a central position in the network linking sleep and decision-making. The absence of a unique relationship between sleep duration and risk-taking contrasts with certain strands of literature demonstrating that sleep deprivation can increase risk-prone behaviours. Experimental paradigms that impose acute sleep restriction or total sleep deprivation have often found that loss of sleep leads to greater impulsivity and risk preference on laboratory tasks. Those manipulations produce pronounced physiological and cognitive effects over short timescales and provide causal evidence of sleep’s capacity to alter decision-making processes. In contrast, the current study assessed habitual sleep duration in a cross-sectional community sample, which may reflect different mechanisms than acute experimental sleep loss. One important distinction is that experimental sleep deprivation creates acute homeostatic pressure and circadian disruption that might transiently amplify reward sensitivity or reduce executive control. Habitual short sleep, when self-reported, may not capture the same physiological stressors or may be associated with adaptive compensatory behaviours that attenuate its influence on decision-making. Furthermore, laboratory tasks that document sleep deprivation effects often use high-sensitivity behavioural measures or neuroimaging outcomes, whereas the choice dilemma questionnaire provides a self-reported index of hypothetical decision-making. It is plausible that self-reported risk propensity is influenced by stable trait-like considerations, social desirability, and introspective access, all of which may attenuate sensitivity to sleep-related state changes. Perceived stress emerged as a consistent correlate of both sleep and risk propensity in the current sample. This pattern underscores the importance of considering stress in models of sleep and decision-making. Stress can directly disrupt sleep by increasing arousal and physiological activation, leading to difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep. Stress also influences cognition and affect in ways that can bias decision-making, such as by narrowing attention, enhancing threat sensitivity, or promoting short-term coping behaviours that prioritize immediate relief. The finding that controlling for perceived stress attenuated the sleep–risk association suggests that some of the relations observed in other studies may be attributable to shared variance with stress. That is, individuals experiencing higher stress may both sleep less and make more risk-prone choices, generating a spurious association between sleep duration and risk-taking in analyses that do not account for stress. The exploratory finding that subjective sleep quality showed a weak association with risk propensity offers an additional nuance. Sleep quality encompasses dimensions such as sleep continuity, depth, and restorative value, which are not captured by simple duration estimates. It may be that qualitative features of sleep exert more influence on affective regulation and executive functioning than raw hours slept. For example, fragmented sleep or non-restorative sleep may impair prefrontal-mediated control and increase susceptibility to risky decision-making even when total time in bed appears adequate. This suggests that future research should incorporate multifaceted sleep assessment, including objective measures such as actigraphy and subjective measures that probe quality and regularity. There are several methodological explanations for the null unique association between habitual sleep duration and risk propensity. First, measurement limitations warrant consideration. Self-reported average hours of sleep are subject to recall bias and may not accurately reflect true sleep behaviour. Objective monitoring over multiple nights would reduce measurement error and could reveal associations that self-report obscures. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. It is possible that risk-taking behaviours influence sleep patterns, that stress influences both, or that bidirectional relations exist. Longitudinal data would help disentangle temporal ordering and potential causal pathways. Third, the range of sleep duration in this sample may have been insufficiently broad to detect effects. If most participants cluster around normative sleep durations, there may be limited variation to reveal associations. Although the current sample included some extreme sleepers, sensitivity analyses excluding them yielded similar null results, suggesting range restriction alone does not fully explain the findings. Fourth, individual differences in resilience, coping strategies, and compensatory mechanisms may moderate the impact of sleep loss on decision-making. Some individuals may maintain normative cognitive performance despite short sleep due to genetic, behavioural, or environmental factors. These moderators were not assessed in the current study. Fifth, the choice dilemma questionnaire captures hypothetical decision-making and may not reflect real-world risk engagement. Ecologically valid behavioural measures or real-world outcome data (e.g., substance use incidents, traffic violations) might be more sensitive to sleep-related effects. The theoretical implications of the findings are multi-faceted. The absence of a unique association between average sleep duration and risk propensity after controlling for perceived stress invites a more nuanced model linking sleep, stress, and decision-making. Rather than sleep duration exerting a direct influence on risk propensity, the relation may be indirect and mediated by stress or by aspects of sleep quality not captured by duration. Alternatively, the causal predominance could be reversed or bidirectional: individuals inclined toward risk-taking might experience lifestyle patterns that reduce sleep or increase stress, and thus apparent associations could be produced by behavioural patterns rather than physiological impairment. Reconciling laboratory and field findings requires acknowledging that sleep loss can acutely impair cognitive control and alter reward processing, but these effects may not map straightforwardly onto habitual self-reported duration in community samples. The present results suggest that interventions aimed solely at increasing hours of sleep may not uniformly reduce risk-taking propensity in the general population, particularly if stress remains unaddressed. From a practical standpoint, preventing harmful risk behaviours may be more effectively achieved by integrated approaches that target both stress reduction and improvements in sleep quality and regularity, rather than focusing exclusively on increasing time in bed. For example, cognitive-behavioural interventions that improve sleep hygiene and stress management concurrently could address multiple pathways implicated in decision-making. The study has several limitations that should be recognized and that point to future research directions. First, the reliance on self-report measures for all primary variables is a limitation. Multi-method assessment, combining actigraphy, polysomnography in subsets, ecological momentary assessment of decision-making, and objective markers of stress such as cortisol reactivity, would strengthen causal inference and measurement precision. Second, the sample consisted primarily of university-affiliated young adults, limiting generalizability to other age groups and to clinical populations. Older adults, adolescents, or individuals with psychiatric disorders may exhibit different patterns of association due to developmental or clinical factors. Third, the cross-sectional design prevents conclusions about causality or temporal precedence. Future longitudinal studies that track sleep, stress, and risk behaviours across time would provide greater clarity on directionality and potential critical windows of vulnerability. Fourth, the study did not assess chronotype, circadian misalignment, or daytime sleepiness, all of which could moderate the relation between sleep and decision-making. Evening chronotypes may be particularly prone to both short sleep and risky behaviours due to misalignment with social schedules, and this factor warrants examination. Fifth, the present research focused on average sleep duration over a month; it did not capture night-to-night variability or acute episodes of sleep loss, which may have disproportionate effects on decision-making. Future work should consider both chronic and acute sleep metrics. Several concrete avenues for future work arise from these considerations. Experimental studies could manipulate sleep quality and quantity while simultaneously inducing or measuring stress to test mediation and moderation hypotheses. Longitudinal cohort studies could examine whether changes in sleep over time predict subsequent changes in real-world risky behaviours, and whether stress trajectories mediate these links. Neuroimaging studies could probe whether habitual sleep patterns correspond to functional differences in prefrontal and striatal circuits implicated in risk processing. Field studies using ecological momentary assessment could capture the dynamic interplay of daily sleep fluctuations, momentary stressors, and real-world decision-making events. Intervention trials could test whether combined sleep and stress interventions produce larger reductions in risky behaviours than either approach alone. In conclusion, the current study did not find evidence that average self-reported nightly sleep duration uniquely predicts risk-taking propensity when controlling for perceived stress in a sample of young adults. Perceived stress was significantly related to both sleep and risk propensity, highlighting its central role in this triadic relation. These results suggest that the relationship between sleep and risk-taking is complex and that stress and sleep quality may be important factors shaping whether sleep duration per se influences decision-making. The findings encourage a multifaceted approach to future research and interventions that considers sleep, stress, and other individual differences in tandem.
