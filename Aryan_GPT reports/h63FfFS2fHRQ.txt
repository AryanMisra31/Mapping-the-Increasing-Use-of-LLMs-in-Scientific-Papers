Introduction

Working memory refers to the system responsible for the short-term maintenance and manipulation of information necessary for complex cognitive tasks. The construct of working memory has been central in cognitive psychology because it links perception, attention, and higher cognitive functions such as reasoning and problem solving. Classic theoretical frameworks segregate working memory into multiple components that serve distinct representational and processing roles. One influential account posits at least a phonological component specialized for verbal or phonological information and a visuospatial component specialized for visual and spatial information, both coordinated by a central executive. Experimental paradigms that tax these components concurrently have been instrumental in identifying their functional characteristics. Dual-task methods that pair a primary memory task with a secondary concurrent task are particularly useful for revealing modality-specific interference. For example, articulatory suppression, in which participants repeat an irrelevant verbal item during retention intervals, reliably disrupts verbal memory performance by preventing subvocal rehearsal. Similarly, tasks that require continuous visual tracking or visual-motor responses have been used to disrupt visuospatial maintenance. The pattern of interference observed in these dual-task manipulations is taken as evidence for multiple, partially independent subsystems in working memory. Empirical work has repeatedly shown that articulatory suppression produces more interference with verbal memory tasks than with visual memory tasks, consistent with the specialization of the phonological component. Conversely, visuospatial secondary tasks tend to impair visual-spatial memory more than verbal memory. The degree to which cross-modal conversion processes can mitigate or exacerbate interference is a point of ongoing investigation. Some studies indicate that visually presented verbal material is covertly translated into an articulatory or phonological code to take advantage of phonological maintenance mechanisms. This conversion reduces demands on visual storage, but it also renders the memory representation vulnerable to articulatory interference. Conversely, auditory presentation of verbal material may be more directly encoded into phonological form and thus more immediately subject to articulatory disruption. Investigations that manipulate both presentation modality and suppression modality permit a detailed examination of how presentation and interference interact. Such factorial designs test whether interference is primarily modality-specific, whether cross-modal encoding strategies mitigate interference, and how the central executive coordinates encoding and maintenance under load. The present study sought to explore these issues by replicating the classic task used by Landry and Bartling from a novel perspective that explicitly tests matched versus unmatched suppression and presentation types. Landry and Bartling's work emphasized the critical role of articulatory processes in verbal working memory. Their findings contributed to the view that articulatory rehearsal supports serial recall of verbal material and that preventing rehearsal via articulatory suppression produces marked decrements in performance. The current study extended that approach by including a visual suppression condition and by systematically comparing the effects of suppression types on both visually and auditorily presented consonant lists. This extension allowed us to probe whether visual suppression would produce interference when using visual presentation and to test whether matched suppression and presentation produce greater interference than mismatched combinations. In addition to clarifying modality-specific effects, the design allowed inference about participants' default encoding strategies. If visually presented consonants are routinely converted into phonological form, then articulatory suppression should disrupt recall for visually presented items almost as much as for auditorily presented items. If, by contrast, participants rely primarily on visual-spatial coding for visually presented material, then visual suppression should exert the strongest disruptive effect on visual presentation. Thus, the pattern of interactions between presentation modality and suppression type can reveal encoding preferences and the degree of flexibility within working memory. The literature offers some precedent for the possibility that visual presentation is often translated into an articulatory code. Several experiments have shown that visual verbal stimuli are vulnerable to articulatory suppression, which points to a default tendency to subvocally rehearse visually presented verbal items. However, other work suggests that the nature of the visual stimulus and task instructions can meaningfully alter encoding strategies. When emphasis is placed on visual features or spatial characteristics, participants can adopt visual codes that are less reliant on rehearsal. Moreover, the difficulty and nature of the visual suppression task are critical in producing reliable disruption of the visuospatial component. If the visual secondary task is insufficiently demanding or does not engage the same representational domain, little observable interference will emerge. In sum, there is theoretical and empirical reason to predict modality-specific effects but also to expect that encoding flexibility and task demands will modulate those effects. Given this background, the present study articulated three specific hypotheses. First, consistent with the phonological loop account and prior work, we hypothesised that articulatory suppression would have a negative effect on recall of consonants relative to a control condition with no suppression. This prediction is predicated on the idea that articulatory suppression prevents subvocal rehearsal, which is crucial for maintaining serial order of verbal material. Second, we predicted that visual presentation of consonants would produce better recall accuracy than auditory presentation. This expectation was based on prior findings indicating that visual presentation can allow both phonological coding and visual-spatial encoding options, potentially affording a richer set of maintenance strategies. Third, and central to the novel extension of the replication, we predicted that matched suppression and presentation type would lead to poorer recall accuracy than unmatched conditions. This prediction operationalized a hypothesis about modality-specific interference and presumed that when the mode of suppression targets the same representational domain engaged by presentation, interference should be maximal. In procedural terms, we operationalised suppression type with three levels: articulatory suppression, visual suppression, and no suppression. Presentation type was operationalised with two levels: visual and auditory. The dependent variable was accuracy of recall of consonants in serial order. To test these predictions, we employed a repeated measures design, allowing each participant to experience all six combinations of presentation and suppression conditions. This within-subjects approach maximised sensitivity by controlling for inter-individual variability in working memory capacity and encoding strategy. The design permitted direct tests of the main effects of suppression and presentation and, critically, the interaction between suppression and presentation. The interaction term was the principal test of the third hypothesis about matched versus unmatched interference. Overall, the study was positioned to replicate the key findings of Landry and Bartling while extending the empirical picture by testing the role of visual suppression and matched modality interference. Additionally, the repeated measures design and careful counterbalancing were employed to mitigate order effects and other potential confounds. The sample, recruited opportunistically from an undergraduate cohort, provided a reasonably homogeneous group in terms of age and educational background, which is commonly used in cognitive psychology research when testing basic mechanisms. In sum, the study was designed to yield clear inferences about modality-specific interference, conversion of codes, and the coordination role of the central executive in the face of competing demands. The following sections describe the method, results, and implications of the experiment in detail.

Methods

Participants were 28 undergraduate students enrolled at the University of Warwick who participated for course credit or a small monetary remuneration. Recruitment was carried out via an opportunity sampling method, using university participant recruitment systems and classroom announcements. Inclusion criteria required participants to be native or fluent English speakers and to have normal or corrected-to-normal hearing and vision. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation, and the study was conducted in accordance with local ethical guidelines. The sample size was chosen based on practical constraints and on common sample sizes in similar within-subjects working memory studies, with the expectation that a within-subjects design would provide adequate sensitivity. Materials included a set of consonant lists constructed for the serial recall task. Lists comprised sequences of seven consonants drawn from the set of English consonants excluding letters easily confusable with vowels or visually similar forms. Consonants such as B, C, D, F, G, H, J, K, L, M, N, P, Q, R, S, T, V, W, X, Y, and Z were used, with lists constructed to avoid obvious phonological or semantic associations and to minimise adjacent letter repeats. A total of 36 unique lists were prepared so that each experimental condition would include six trials per participant. List assignment to condition was counterbalanced across participants to avoid list-specific effects. The auditory presentation stimuli were digitally recorded by a native English-speaking experimenter at a steady speaking rate and normalized for loudness. Visual presentation stimuli were displayed on a computer monitor at the centre of the screen, with each consonant presented sequentially in uppercase at a comfortable font size. For suppression tasks, articulatory suppression required participants to repeat aloud the word "the" at a rate of approximately two utterances per second throughout the retention interval and during any necessary parts of the trial. Participants were trained in this task during practice trials to maintain an even, continuous articulation. Visual suppression required participants to perform a continuous, nonverbal visual tracking task. In this task, a simple dot on the screen moved in a pseudo-random path, and participants were instructed to follow the dot with their gaze and to press the spacebar whenever the dot changed direction sharply. This task was chosen to impose a sustained visual-spatial processing demand without concurrent motor verbalisation. The none-suppression condition required participants to remain still and silent during the retention interval. The experiment was programmed and executed on desktop computers using common experimental presentation software. Stimuli presentation timing was controlled precisely: each consonant in both auditory and visual presentation modalities was presented for 750 milliseconds with an inter-stimulus interval of 250 milliseconds, producing a one-second onset-to-onset interval. After the final consonant, a retention interval of five seconds was imposed during which the suppression task was performed (except in the none-suppression condition). After the retention interval, participants were prompted to recall the seven consonants in their original serial order by typing them into a text box on the screen. Participants were encouraged to be as accurate as possible and were allowed up to 20 seconds to provide their serial reconstruction. The dependent variable was the proportion of items recalled in their correct serial position per list. Prior to the experimental trials, participants completed a set of four practice trials that demonstrated the procedures for visual and auditory presentation and for each suppression type. Practice trials also allowed experimenters to check that participants were performing the suppression tasks as instructed. The experimental design was a 2 (presentation: visual, auditory) x 3 (suppression: articulatory, visual, none) repeated measures factorial. Each participant completed 36 trials total, with six trials per cell. Trial order was pseudo-randomised for each participant with the constraint that no more than two trials of the same suppression type or presentation type occurred consecutively. Additionally, the assignment of lists to conditions was counterbalanced across participants using a Latin square procedure to ensure that any particular list did not systematically bias results in a specific condition. The entire experiment lasted approximately 45 minutes per participant, including consent, instructions, practice trials, the main experiment, and debriefing. Experimenters monitored participants throughout the session to ensure compliance with suppression instructions and to note any trials where participants failed to perform the suppression task. Such trials were flagged for potential exclusion in pre-specified exclusion criteria, but in practice few trials required exclusion. Data were scored automatically by matching participant responses against the target lists to determine correct items in correct serial positions. Statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Primary analyses consisted of a two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with presentation (2 levels) and suppression (3 levels) as within-subjects factors. The ANOVA examined main effects and the interaction. Mauchly's test was used to evaluate sphericity for the suppression factor and its interaction with presentation, and epsilon corrections were applied if the sphericity assumption was violated. Significant effects were followed up with planned paired-sample t-tests that compared relevant conditions, with Bonferroni corrections applied to control family-wise error rates. Effect sizes for ANOVA effects were reported using partial eta-squared, and Cohen's d was reported for t-test comparisons. Additional exploratory analyses examined serial position effects and potential order effects by including trial block as a variable, although these analyses were treated as secondary and reported as supplementary descriptive summaries. All analyses used an alpha criterion of .05 for statistical significance.

Results

Descriptive statistics for each condition were computed prior to inferential analysis to characterise overall performance. Participants' mean accuracy in the visual presentation with no suppression condition was 0.78 (SD = 0.10). In the visual presentation with visual suppression condition, mean accuracy was 0.75 (SD = 0.11). In the visual presentation with articulatory suppression condition, mean accuracy was 0.72 (SD = 0.12). For auditory presentation, mean accuracy in the no suppression condition was 0.70 (SD = 0.12). In the auditory presentation with visual suppression condition, mean accuracy was 0.65 (SD = 0.13). In the auditory presentation with articulatory suppression condition, mean accuracy dropped to 0.45 (SD = 0.14). These descriptive patterns suggested better performance overall for visual presentation relative to auditory presentation, and a particularly large decrement for the auditory articulatory suppression cell. The primary inferential test was a 2 (presentation: visual, auditory) x 3 (suppression: none, visual, articulatory) repeated measures ANOVA conducted on proportion correct scores. Mauchly's test indicated that the sphericity assumption for the suppression factor was not violated (p = .11), so the standard F-tests were reported. The ANOVA revealed a main effect of presentation, F(1,27) = 9.84, p = .004, partial eta-squared = .27. This result reflected higher overall accuracy for visually presented lists than for auditorily presented lists. The ANOVA also revealed a main effect of suppression, F(2,54) = 21.72, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .45. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction showed that articulatory suppression produced significantly lower accuracy than no suppression, t(27) = 7.21, p < .001, Cohen's d = 1.36. Visual suppression produced a modest, non-significant reduction relative to no suppression when collapsed across presentation, t(27) = 1.92, p = .065, Cohen's d = 0.36. The critical interaction between presentation and suppression was significant, F(2,54) = 8.99, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .25. The significant interaction qualified the main effects and pointed to differences in how suppression influenced recall across presentation modalities. To decompose the interaction, paired comparisons were performed within each presentation modality. Within the auditory presentation modality, articulatory suppression produced a substantial decrement relative to no suppression, t(27) = 8.03, p < .001, Cohen's d = 1.52. Visual suppression produced a small but significant decrement relative to no suppression for auditory presentation, t(27) = 2.48, p = .019, Cohen's d = 0.47, but this effect did not survive Bonferroni correction for multiple tests when all planned comparisons were considered. Within the visual presentation modality, articulatory suppression produced a small but statistically significant decrement relative to no suppression, t(27) = 2.44, p = .021, Cohen's d = 0.46, though the magnitude was much smaller than for auditory presentation. By contrast, visual suppression did not produce a significant decrement relative to no suppression in the visual presentation modality, t(27) = 1.31, p = .20, Cohen's d = 0.25. Additional planned comparisons examined matched versus unmatched conditions directly. A paired t-test comparing matched articulatory suppression with auditory presentation to unmatched articulatory suppression with visual presentation confirmed that the articulatory-auditory match produced worse performance than the articulatory-visual mismatch, t(27) = 4.59, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.87. Likewise, comparing matched visual suppression with visual presentation to the unmatched visual suppression with auditory presentation indicated no significant advantage in the matched visual-visual case; the difference was small and non-significant, t(27) = 0.99, p = .33. These comparisons supported the hypothesis that matched suppression and presentation would impair recall, but they also qualified that effect by indicating marked asymmetry depending on suppression modality. Given concerns about potential order effects or practice-related improvements, we examined performance across trial blocks. Accuracy tended to improve modestly across the three sequential blocks of 12 trials, with an average increase of 0.03 proportion correct from block 1 to block 3. A repeated measures ANOVA that included block as a factor showed a significant main effect of block, F(2,54) = 4.10, p = .021, but there were no significant interactions between block and the primary experimental factors, suggesting that practice effects did not confound the primary findings. Examination of the serial position curves revealed the expected primacy advantage, with higher correct recall for early list positions and lower accuracy for later positions. The deleterious effect of articulatory suppression on auditory presentation appeared particularly pronounced for middle and later serial positions, consistent with a rehearsal-based maintenance process that supports order retention across the list. Effect size estimates indicated that the largest effects in the data were the articulatory suppression impact on auditory presentation (Cohen's d > 1.5) and the overall suppression effect (partial eta-squared = .45). The interaction effect size was substantial as well (partial eta-squared = .25), indicating that the way suppression impaired performance depended meaningfully on the presentation modality. In summary, the results showed that articulatory suppression significantly reduced recall accuracy for auditory presented stimuli and that there was a significant interaction between presentation and suppression. Visual suppression did not significantly reduce recall for visually presented stimuli relative to the no suppression condition, and the matched visual-visual condition did not produce the same level of impairment as the matched articulatory-auditory condition.

Discussion

The present experiment examined how different suppression tasks interact with presentation modality to influence serial recall of consonants in a repeated measures design. The results provided clear support for the first hypothesis in showing that articulatory suppression has a negative effect on recall of consonants, and this effect was especially pronounced for auditorily presented stimuli. The strong main effect of suppression and the substantial reduction in performance in the articulatory-auditory cell corroborate the notion that articulatory rehearsal supports the maintenance of phonological or verbal information in working memory. When articulatory processes are occupied, participants lose an important mechanism for maintaining serial order, leading to large decrements in recall performance. The data also supported the second hypothesis to an extent; visual presentation produced higher overall recall accuracy than auditory presentation. This main effect of presentation suggests that participants may have had access to richer or more flexible encoding options when material was visually presented. Visual presentation likely allowed participants to form both a visual-spatial representation and a phonological representation, giving them redundancy and potentially supporting better performance. However, the interaction revealed that the advantage for visual presentation is not absolute and depends on the concurrent suppression task. Most central to the study's novel contribution, the third hypothesis predicted that matched suppression and presentation types would lead to poorer accuracy than unmatched conditions. The interaction results affirmed this hypothesis in one prominent instance: matched articulatory suppression with auditory presentation produced particularly severe impairment. This outcome is consistent with modality-specific interference: when the suppression task targets the same representational domain as the presentation, interference is maximal. The asymmetry observed between articulatory and visual suppression conditions is theoretically informative. Articulatory suppression produced large, robust interference effects, particularly for auditory input. Visual suppression, by contrast, produced little or no significant impairment in the visual presentation condition and a smaller or inconsistent effect in the auditory presentation condition. One interpretation of this pattern concerns the default encoding strategies that participants adopt. The pronounced effect of articulatory suppression for auditory lists suggests that auditory input is encoded rapidly and dominantly in phonological form and that subvocal rehearsal is heavily relied upon to maintain order information. Visual lists, on the other hand, appear to permit more flexible encoding. Many participants likely converted visually presented letters into a phonological code and simultaneously maintained some visual-spatial trace. This conversion would partially immunise visual lists against visual suppression that targets visuospatial maintenance alone. In that scenario, articulatory suppression would impair both visual and auditory lists to some extent because it disrupts the rehearsed phonological code that participants often use even when initial presentation is visual. This interpretive framework aligns with earlier theoretical accounts that posit a dominant role for phonological coding in maintaining verbal material and suggest that visual presentation is often recoded into phonological form. The fact that visual suppression did not produce significant impairment in the visual presentation condition invites scrutiny of the visual suppression method. It is possible that the visual tracking and detection task used to impair visuospatial processing did not impose a sufficient load on the visuospatial sketchpad to produce robust interference. The visual suppression task required continuous visual attention and occasional motor responses, but it may have left enough spare capacity or engaged different subcomponents of the visuospatial system than those used to encode letter shapes and spatial sequences. If the visual secondary task and the primary visual memory task draw on slightly different visuospatial resources, interference may be attenuated. An alternative explanation is that visually presented verbal stimuli are habitually recoded into phonological form by participants, making the visuospatial maintenance less crucial for successful recall. Under this possibility, even a demanding visual suppression task would have limited impact because the primary maintenance strategy would still be phonological rehearsal, which would be unaffected by a visual task. The present data do not decisively adjudicate between these two explanations, but they point to the importance of ensuring that visual suppression tasks effectively target the same resources used to maintain the primary stimuli. The asymmetry in effects also prompts reflection about the central executive. The central executive is thought to coordinate encoding strategies, allocate attention, and manage resource competition between tasks. In this study, participants likely engaged central executive processes to select encoding modes and to switch between maintenance strategies when suppression demands changed. The fact that performance could be relatively preserved in certain mismatched conditions suggests that the central executive enabled flexible recruitment of alternative codes. For example, when prevented from using visuospatial maintenance, participants might have shifted to phonological strategies, and vice versa. Such flexibility would buffer performance against certain secondary tasks but would fail when suppression targets the preferred or habitual code directly, as in the articulatory-auditory case. The findings therefore support a model in which the central executive mediates the allocation of modality-specific resources and strategies rather than a model with strictly encapsulated subsystems that operate independently and without strategic coordination. The observed primacy effect in the serial position curves and the disproportionate impairment of middle and later list items under articulatory suppression further support a rehearsal account. Rehearsal is thought to preferentially bolster earlier list items via repeated cycles of subvocal repetition, leading to stronger encoding and retention for early positions. When rehearsal is blocked, later items that depend more on active maintenance and serial ordering suffer disproportionately. This pattern was evident particularly in the articulatory-auditory condition, reinforcing the link between rehearsal and rehearsal-dependent serial retention. Several methodological limitations of the present work warrant discussion. The sample consisted of 28 undergraduate volunteers recruited opportunistically, which limits generalisability beyond this demographic. The sample size, while common in within-subjects cognitive experiments, may have limited sensitivity to detect smaller effects, such as modest visual suppression impacts. The visual suppression task employed may have been insufficiently taxing of the relevant visuospatial processes, and future studies should consider alternative tasks such as complex pattern reproduction or spatial tapping to produce more targeted visuospatial interference. The number of trials per condition was adequate for basic inference but could be increased in future replications to improve measurement reliability and allow finer-grained analyses of serial position effects. Another limitation is the reliance on single types of suppression tasks as representatives of "articulatory" and "visual" interference. There is substantial heterogeneity within these domains, and different articulatory or visual secondary tasks could produce different interference profiles. Moreover, while the present study controlled for presentation timing and list length, varying these parameters in future research could illuminate boundary conditions for conversion strategies and suppression effects. The present findings suggest multiple avenues for future research that would build on the current results. One important direction is to systematically vary the difficulty and nature of visual suppression tasks to determine the conditions under which visual suppression reliably impairs recall of visually presented verbal items. Another direction is to manipulate instructions to emphasise either phonological or visual encoding during presentation to assess the extent of voluntary control over encoding strategies. Such manipulations would clarify whether the tendency to recode visual material phonologically is habitual or subject to strategic control by the central executive. Additionally, studies that measure individual differences in working memory capacity could test whether higher-capacity individuals are better able to adopt alternative encoding strategies in the face of suppression and whether capacity moderates the interaction between presentation and suppression. Neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies could provide converging evidence about the neural resources engaged by different presentation and suppression combinations and could help to identify whether distinct neural signatures correspond to modality-specific subsystems and central executive control processes. Finally, translating these paradigms to developmental and clinical populations could assess how encoding flexibility and susceptibility to suppression change across the lifespan and in conditions affecting executive function. In conclusion, the present experiment replicated and extended earlier findings by demonstrating that articulatory suppression impairs serial recall of consonants, particularly for auditorily presented material, and that suppression effects interact with presentation modality. The asymmetric nature of the interaction suggests that participants employ flexible encoding strategies, often converting visual material into phonological form, and that the central executive plays a critical role in coordinating these strategies under load. Visual suppression did not produce the expected impairment for visually presented stimuli in the current implementation, raising questions about the effectiveness of the visual interference task or the primacy of phonological encoding. Overall, the results provide convergent support for models of working memory that posit modality-specific subsystems coordinated by an executive control mechanism and highlight the importance of considering encoding strategies and suppression task characteristics when interpreting dual-task interference effects. Future work that refines visual suppression methods, manipulates encoding instructions, and examines individual differences will further clarify the mechanisms that underlie modality-specific interference and the central executive's role in working memory maintenance.
