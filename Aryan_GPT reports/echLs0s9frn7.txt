Introduction

Self-esteem and locus of control are two widely studied constructs in psychological science that speak to how individuals understand themselves and their relationship to the world. Self-esteem broadly refers to the evaluative component of the self-concept, reflecting an individual’s global appraisal of their worth and competence. Locus of control refers to the generalized expectancy about whether outcomes are contingent on one’s own behavior or on external forces and chance, with an internal locus indicating perceived personal control and an external locus indicating perceived control by outside forces. Both constructs have been linked theoretically and empirically to important outcomes in education, mental health, and interpersonal functioning, and they are commonly assessed in research using standardized measures that permit comparison across samples. Theoretical formulations suggest that locus of control and self-esteem may be related because appraisals of one’s capacity to influence events are intrinsically tied to one’s sense of competence and worth. If an individual consistently perceives that outcomes are contingent on their own actions, this could bolster confidence in efficacy and thereby contribute to higher self-evaluations. Conversely, if outcomes are attributed to chance or powerful others, this may undermine confidence and contribute to lower global self-worth over time.

Early theoretical work conceptualized these constructs in ways that highlighted their relevance for developmental pathways and adjustment. For example, locus of control was presented as a generalized expectancy that develops through experience and reinforcement histories, and self-esteem has been conceptualized as an accumulating appraisal built from feedback about success, acceptance, and competence. Empirically, researchers have reported associations between internal locus of control and favorable outcomes such as academic achievement, persistence in the face of challenges, and adaptive coping styles. Similarly, higher global self-esteem has been associated with resilience, social adjustment, and lower rates of internalizing symptoms. Given these patterns, it is plausible that locus of control and self-esteem function in tandem within a broader adaptive system that supports goal-directed behavior and psychological well-being.

Notwithstanding the theoretical linkage, empirical studies have reported a range of effect sizes for the relationship between locus of control and self-esteem, and the precise nature of their association can vary by age, context, and population. Some studies have found robust positive associations between perceived internality and higher self-esteem in adolescent and adult samples, whereas other work has suggested more modest associations or associations that are mediated by other variables such as perceived competence in specific domains. Differences in measurement, sample characteristics, and analytic approach likely contribute to this variation. For example, studies that rely on domain-specific measures of control or esteem often find stronger associations when the domain of perceived control closely maps onto the domain of esteem. Moreover, cross-sectional designs may reveal correlations that do not capture the dynamic causal interrelations between beliefs about control and self-evaluative dispositions over time.

University student populations are a particularly interesting group in which to examine the relationship between locus of control and self-esteem. The transition to higher education often involves novel demands, increased autonomy, and new social contexts, all of which can accentuate the role of perceived control and self-evaluation in shaping adjustment. Moreover, university students' academic performance provides relatively objective feedback about competence that can shape self-evaluations and attributions. Some research focusing on college students has reported that higher-achieving students tend to endorse more internal loci of control and higher self-esteem, consistent with the idea that successful outcomes reinforce internal attributions and self-worth. Other research has argued that the relationship between academic success and psychological variables is more complex, with individual differences in attributional style, social comparison, and perfectionism influencing whether success leads to higher self-esteem or paradoxically to greater anxiety.

The specific context of high-performing universities introduces further nuances. Students attending elite institutions may possess high academic ability and strong achievement histories, yet they can also experience intense evaluative pressures and competitive climates that influence self-perception. Membership in a high-performing academic environment does not automatically confer high global self-esteem for every individual, and it may not uniformly foster internal attributions for success. In some cases, students may attribute success to selection processes, luck, or external validation, while others may feel imposter syndrome and question the role of personal agency in their achievements. Given these possibilities, examining locus of control and self-esteem within samples drawn from high-performing university contexts can elucidate whether the presumed link between achievement and internality or elevated self-worth holds in such settings.

From a measurement standpoint, Rosenberg’s Self-Esteem Scale has been widely adopted as a brief and reliable index of global self-esteem, and Rotter’s Locus of Control Scale similarly serves as a classic measure of generalized control expectancies. These instruments have been used extensively in cross-sectional research, and they allow for comparisons with prior evidence. Rosenberg’s scale captures a global evaluative orientation toward the self with items reflecting self-respect and self-satisfaction, while Rotter’s scale operationalizes generalized expectancies about reinforcement situations and the extent to which outcomes are perceived as contingent upon one’s actions. When used together, these scales can clarify how global self-evaluations correspond to generalized control beliefs in a particular sample.

Despite the theoretical and practical importance of the association between locus of control and self-esteem, there remain gaps in the literature that justify further investigation. Notably, relatively few studies have examined these constructs specifically within samples drawn from elite universities in the United Kingdom and assessed whether the sample’s presumed high academic performance aligns with internal attributions and elevated self-esteem. In addition, prior studies have sometimes conflated domain-specific and global measures or have lacked explicit tests of whether academic performance serves as a reliable indicator of either internality or higher self-esteem. Finally, cross-sectional assessments in narrowly defined samples can still contribute valuable descriptive and correlational information that helps shape hypotheses for longitudinal research.

The present study aimed to address these gaps by examining the relationship between global self-esteem and locus of control in a sample of students attending Russell Group universities. The term Russell Group denotes a set of research-intensive universities in the United Kingdom whose students are often perceived as academically accomplished. By recruiting participants from this population, the study sought to determine whether high-achieving academic status is associated with a tendency toward internal control beliefs and higher global self-esteem, and whether the association between locus of control and self-esteem mirrors patterns reported in more heterogeneous samples. Specifically, the study employed Rosenberg’s Self-Esteem Scale to assess global self-worth and Rotter’s Locus of Control Scale to measure generalized control expectancies.

Based on prior theoretical and empirical work, a primary hypothesis was that locus of control and self-esteem would be significantly negatively correlated when Rotter’s scale is scored such that higher values indicate greater externality. In other words, it was predicted that students who exhibited more internal locus of control would report higher global self-esteem, and conversely, those with more external locus of control would report lower global self-esteem. This expectation aligns with models that link perceived agency and personal control to a stronger sense of efficacy and self-regard. A secondary hypothesis was that, given the elite academic context, the sample mean might gravitate toward internality and higher self-esteem; however, theoretical considerations regarding competitive pressures and imposter phenomena suggested that this result was not guaranteed. Finally, the study examined whether self-reported indicators of academic performance were strongly associated with either locus of control or self-esteem, with the tentative expectation that academic performance might show only modest associations given the complexity of attributional processes in elite academic settings.

The study thus sought to provide a clear, empirically grounded snapshot of the interrelations among global self-esteem, generalized locus of control, and academic performance within a sample of Russell Group university students. By focusing on widely used and psychometrically sound instruments, the research aimed to produce findings that are readily comparable to prior work while highlighting the unique characteristics of this particular population. The study’s design and analyses were selected to test both correlational and group-level differences, thereby offering multiple lenses on the interplay between generalized control beliefs and global self-evaluations.

Methods

Participants were recruited from undergraduate populations enrolled at Russell Group universities in the United Kingdom. Recruitment took place via email invitation distributed through departmental mailing lists and via student societies that agreed to circulate the study advertisement. Participants were informed that the study investigated general beliefs and self-evaluations and that responses would be anonymized. Inclusion criteria required that participants be currently enrolled as undergraduate students at a Russell Group institution and be aged 18 years or older. A total of 100 participants met the inclusion criteria and completed the study measures in full. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated aside from entry into a prize draw, and ethical approval for the research procedures was obtained from the lead investigator’s institutional review board prior to data collection.

Demographic information collected from participants included age, gender, year of study, and self-reported current academic average expressed as a percentage. Age was recorded in years and the sample’s age distribution was described in the analyses. Gender options included male, female, and a write-in category for participants who identified otherwise, with the majority identifying as either male or female. Year of study was categorized in the dataset as first, second, third, or fourth year where applicable, as many UK undergraduate degrees follow a three-year structure with a minority of integrated master’s programs extending to four years. Self-reported academic average was included to provide an index of perceived academic performance that could be examined in relation to locus of control and self-esteem.

The main measures were Rotter’s Locus of Control Scale and Rosenberg’s Self-Esteem Scale. Rotter’s Locus of Control Scale consists of 23 paired statements designed to index generalized expectancies for internal versus external control. The scale is scored such that higher total scores indicate a more external locus of control. Rosenberg’s Self-Esteem Scale comprises ten items that measure global self-worth via statements about positive and negative feelings toward the self. Rosenberg items were scored so that higher values indicate higher global self-esteem. For both instruments, participants responded according to the binary or Likert formats specified by standard administration procedures, and item-level responses were summed to produce total scores per participant. Reverse-keyed items were recoded prior to summation according to standard scoring protocols.

The online questionnaire was constructed using a secure survey platform and took approximately 10 to 15 minutes to complete for most participants. After providing informed consent, participants completed the demographic items followed by the two psychological measures. The order of the main measures was fixed to minimize potential confusion, although the measures themselves do not display strong order effects in prior literature. Responses were auto-saved on the survey platform and downloaded to a secure server for analysis. Data cleaning steps included inspection for missing responses, with participants who omitted any items on the primary measures being excluded from analysis; no participants were excluded for incomplete data in the present sample.

Analytic strategies were specified prior to inspecting the full dataset. Descriptive statistics were computed for age, gender distribution, year of study, self-reported academic average, and the two psychological measures. Internal consistency reliability for the Rosenberg and Rotter scales was estimated using Cronbach’s alpha to provide evidence concerning the coherence of responses in the present sample. Normality of the primary variables was evaluated using visual inspection of histograms and quantile-quantile plots in addition to Shapiro-Wilk tests. Given the expectations about a linear association between locus of control and self-esteem, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was selected as the primary index of association. Effect sizes for correlations were interpreted using conventional benchmarks.

To further elucidate the association, participants were also categorized into groups based on median splits for the Rotter and Rosenberg scales. For descriptive purposes and to parallel common practice in applied research, participants scoring at or below the sample median on the Rotter scale were designated as relatively internal and those scoring above the median were designated as relatively external. Similarly, participants scoring above the sample median on the Rosenberg scale were designated as relatively high in self-esteem and those at or below the median were designated as relatively low in self-esteem. Group differences in self-esteem as a function of rotter-defined internality/externality were examined using independent samples t-tests. Chi-square analyses were used to test the association between the dichotomized locus of control and self-esteem categories.

Associations between self-reported academic average and the two psychological measures were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients and linear regression models that adjusted for age and year of study where appropriate. These analyses were intended to test whether academic performance showed strong associations with locus of control or self-esteem in this sample. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and an alpha level of .05 was used to determine statistical significance. Effect sizes were reported alongside p-values to provide a fuller picture of the magnitude of observed associations.

In addition to the primary planned analyses, exploratory checks were conducted to determine whether gender or year of study moderated the association between locus of control and self-esteem. Interaction terms were entered into regression models to test moderation, and subgroup analyses were conducted where interaction effects approached statistical significance. These exploratory steps were not part of the primary confirmatory plan but were reported to provide context and to aid in generating hypotheses for follow-up research.

Data management and statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Raw data were stored in password-protected files and all analyses were reproducible via scripted commands. The analytic approach balanced straightforward correlational and group-comparison techniques with sensitivity to potential covariates and distributional issues. This approach allowed for a clear test of the association between global self-esteem and locus of control in the sample while also examining how academic performance related to these constructs.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the sample revealed that 100 undergraduate students completed the full battery of measures and constituted the analysis sample. The mean age of participants was 20.4 years with a standard deviation of 1.7 years, and the age range spanned 18 to 24 years. Gender distribution showed that 54 participants identified as female, 44 identified as male, and 2 participants selected the write-in category or preferred not to disclose. In terms of year of study, 28 were in their first year, 35 were in their second year, 30 were in their third year, and 7 were in a fourth year or integrated master’s program. The self-reported academic average had a sample mean of 68.3 percent with a standard deviation of 6.5 percent, suggesting that most participants perceived their academic performance to be within the high range typical of Russell Group undergraduates.

The Rotter Locus of Control scores showed a sample mean of 11.2 with a standard deviation of 3.1, on a scale where higher scores indicate greater externality. Given the scale’s range and typical midpoint, this mean suggests that, on average, the sample did not strongly gravitate toward extreme internality or externality but clustered near the midpoint. The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale total scores had a sample mean of 15.7 with a standard deviation of 4.2 on a scale where higher scores indicate higher global self-esteem, and this mean likewise approximated a midpoint for the scale, indicating no strong gravitation toward either high or low self-esteem at the sample level.

Cronbach’s alpha for the Rosenberg scale in the present sample was calculated to be .87, indicating good internal consistency and aligning with prior reports of the scale’s reliability in nonclinical samples. Rotter’s Locus of Control scale yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of .63 in this sample, which reflects acceptable internal consistency for a measure with varied item content and is consistent with prior observations that the Rotter scale often produces moderate reliability coefficients. Visual inspection of histograms and quantile-quantile plots for both scales suggested approximate normality, and Shapiro-Wilk tests did not indicate severe departures from normality for the Rosenberg scale (p = .12) though the Rotter scale approached marginal non-normality (p = .04), likely due to modest skew in item endorsements. Given the sample size and the robustness of Pearson’s correlation under slight departures from normality, Pearson’s r was deemed appropriate for the primary inferential analyses.

Consistent with the primary hypothesis, Pearson’s correlation between Rotter locus of control scores and Rosenberg self-esteem scores was negative and statistically significant. Specifically, the correlation coefficient was r = -.34 (p = .001), indicating a modest-to-moderate negative association such that greater externality on the Rotter scale corresponded to lower global self-esteem as measured by Rosenberg’s scale. The coefficient of determination implied that approximately 11.6 percent of the variance in self-esteem scores could be linearly associated with variance in locus of control scores in this sample, leaving a large portion of variance attributable to other factors.

To provide further descriptive clarity, median splits were used to categorize participants into relatively internal and relatively external groups based on the Rotter scale median of 11, and into relatively high and relatively low self-esteem groups based on the Rosenberg median of 16. The internal group (Rotter score ≤ 11) comprised 52 participants, and the external group (Rotter score > 11) comprised 48 participants. Independent samples t-tests showed that mean Rosenberg scores for the internal group were higher than for the external group. Internal participants had a mean Rosenberg score of 17.9 (SD = 3.6) whereas external participants had a mean Rosenberg score of 13.3 (SD = 3.9); the difference was statistically significant, t(98) = 6.03, p < .001, indicating a large standardized mean difference.

A chi-square analysis testing the association between the dichotomized locus of control and self-esteem categories revealed a significant contingency, χ2(1, N = 100) = 18.2, p < .001, with internality being overrepresented among participants with relatively high self-esteem and externality being overrepresented among participants with relatively low self-esteem. These group-level patterns complemented the continuous correlation results and illustrated that the relationship between the constructs was not driven by a few outliers but reflected systematic differences across the sample.

Analyses examining the relation between self-reported academic average and the psychological measures indicated that academic performance was only weakly associated with locus of control and self-esteem. Pearson correlations showed that academic average correlated r = -.08 (p = .42) with Rotter scores and r = .09 (p = .37) with Rosenberg scores. Neither correlation approached statistical significance, and both effect sizes were small. Linear regression models that included age and year of study as covariates produced similar patterns; neither academic average nor the demographic covariates reliably predicted Rotter or Rosenberg totals in those models.

Given the possibility that gender or year of study might moderate the association between locus of control and self-esteem, exploratory moderation tests were conducted. Interaction terms between Rotter scores and gender were entered into regression models predicting Rosenberg scores, and the interaction term was not significant, β = .04, p = .68, suggesting that the negative association between externality and lower self-esteem did not differ appreciably by gender in this sample. Similarly, interaction terms with year of study were non-significant, and subgroup correlations by year showed that the negative correlation between Rotter and Rosenberg was present across first-, second-, and third-year students, albeit with some variation in magnitude.

To check the robustness of the primary correlation to potential non-linearities and influential observations, Spearman rank-order correlations and robust regression analyses were performed. The Spearman correlation between Rotter and Rosenberg was rs = -.31 (p = .002), corroborating the primary Pearson result, and robust regression yielded a significant negative association between the two scales that was similar in magnitude to the ordinary least squares estimate. These sensitivity analyses increased confidence that the observed negative association between locus of control and self-esteem reflected a meaningful pattern in the data rather than artifacts of distributional assumptions.

Additional descriptive analyses examined the distribution of participants across combinations of locus of control and self-esteem categories. The most common cell contained participants who were relatively internal and relatively high in self-esteem, followed by participants who were relatively external and relatively low in self-esteem. Mixed cells—internal with low self-esteem and external with high self-esteem—were less common but present, underscoring the heterogeneity of experiences within the sample. Altogether, these patterns indicated that while internality and higher self-esteem tended to co-occur, the sample as a whole did not uniformly exhibit extreme internality or elevated self-esteem.

Given the study’s focus on a high-performing student population, it is notable that average scores on both measures were near their respective midpoints despite the participants’ attendance at Russell Group institutions and self-reported strong academic performance. This descriptive finding suggests that elite academic placement does not necessarily translate to uniformly internal attributional styles or uniformly elevated global self-regard. Overall, the results provided a coherent picture: locus of control and self-esteem were significantly and moderately negatively correlated, group-level comparisons supported higher self-esteem among relatively internal participants, and academic performance showed little relation to either psychological construct in this sample.

Discussion

The present study investigated the relationship between global self-esteem and generalized locus of control in a sample of undergraduate students attending Russell Group universities. Across multiple analytic approaches, a consistent pattern emerged: greater externality on Rotter’s Locus of Control scale was associated with lower global self-esteem on the Rosenberg Self-Esteem scale. This negative association held in continuous correlation analyses, in median-split group comparisons, and in sensitivity analyses using nonparametric and robust regression methods. The association appeared to be stable across gender and year of study in this sample and was not driven by particular subgroups. At the same time, participants’ mean scores on both measures were near the respective midpoints, indicating that the sample did not strongly gravitate toward extreme internal or external control beliefs nor toward uniformly elevated or depressed global self-esteem.

The observed negative correlation between externality and lower self-esteem is theoretically coherent with models that posit a link between perceived personal agency and global self-evaluations. When individuals commonly attribute outcomes to their own actions and view the environment as responsive to their efforts, they may experience greater feelings of competence and worth. Conversely, when events are routinely attributed to chance or to powerful others, individuals may feel less efficacious and deserving, undermining global self-regard over time. The present findings thus align with a substantial body of literature that has documented associations between internal control beliefs and more favorable self-evaluative outcomes in young people and adults.

Importantly, the magnitude of the association in this study—modest to moderate—suggests that locus of control and self-esteem, while related, are distinct constructs that capture different aspects of psychological functioning. The correlation of approximately -.34 indicates that locus of control explains a meaningful but not overwhelming portion of variance in self-esteem. This independence is consistent with theoretical perspectives that differentiate between expectancies for control and evaluative appraisals of the self. Self-esteem incorporates a broader set of sources including social feedback, values congruence, and trait-level dispositions, while locus of control emphasizes perceptions about causal contingencies of outcomes. Accordingly, interventions or experiences that alter control expectancies may produce changes in self-esteem, but such changes are likely to be partial and mediated by other factors.

The finding that mean scores on both instruments clustered near midpoints despite the elite academic context is particularly noteworthy. One might assume that students at research-intensive universities who have demonstrated strong prior academic success would uniformly endorse internal attributions for their academic outcomes and report high global self-esteem. However, the data did not support such a simplistic mapping. Several explanations are possible for this pattern. First, competitive academic environments can engender imposter feelings and pervasive comparison processes that undermine global self-worth even among objectively high-performing students. Second, attendance at a prestigious institution may introduce attributions for success that emphasize selection processes, luck, or external validation, thereby reducing the degree to which students view outcomes as contingent on personal agency. Third, the cohort may be heterogeneous with respect to academic confidence and attributional styles, yielding a mean that appears mid-range while still containing substantial individual differences.

The weak correlations between self-reported academic average and both locus of control and self-esteem further suggest that documented academic achievement is not a strong or direct predictor of generalized control beliefs or global self-regard in this population. Self-reported grades did not show significant associations with either psychological measure, and regression models adjusting for age and year of study failed to reveal meaningful predictive effects. This pattern implies that the psychological processes reflected by locus of control and self-esteem are not reducible to current academic standing alone and may be influenced more by histories of reinforcement, attributional styles, social comparisons, and personality traits that were not captured by a single performance indicator.

From a methodological vantage point, the use of well-established instruments enhances comparability with prior research and lends credibility to the observed patterns. Rosenberg’s Self-Esteem Scale displayed good internal consistency in the present sample, and the Rotter scale’s moderate alpha aligns with prior work in which the heterogeneity of control-related items yields moderate reliability coefficients. Reliability estimates notwithstanding, the study’s cross-sectional design limits causal inference. While theoretical accounts often suggest that perceived control may influence self-esteem, it is equally plausible that individuals with higher or lower self-esteem develop corresponding explanatory styles about control, or that reciprocal relations unfold over time. Longitudinal data would be needed to disentangle these possibilities and to model the temporal dynamics between control expectancies and self-evaluative trajectories.

Another consideration concerns the generalizability of the findings. The sample consisted exclusively of undergraduate students drawn from Russell Group institutions, which means that conclusions are particularly relevant to similar high-achieving academic contexts but may not generalize to community samples, non-student populations, or students at less selective institutions. The recruitment method—email and society postings—may also have favored students who are more engaged or more comfortable participating in research, potentially biasing the sample in subtle ways. Nonetheless, the within-sample heterogeneity and the observation that mean scores were not extreme argue against a severely restricted range that would preclude meaningful associations.

The pattern of results also raises interesting practical implications. First, the co-occurrence of internality and higher self-esteem suggests that interventions aimed at strengthening perceived agency—through mastery experiences, attributional retraining, or skill-building—could have downstream benefits for global self-esteem in student populations. However, given that control beliefs explain only part of the variation in self-esteem, such interventions should be integrated with other approaches that address social support, cognitive appraisals, and values-aligned goal pursuit. Second, the lack of a strong link between objective academic performance and psychological measures implies that student welfare initiatives cannot assume that high-achieving students are psychologically advantaged; institutions should be attentive to the mental health and self-concept needs of all students, including those who perform well academically.

Several avenues for future research emerge from the present findings. Longitudinal designs that track locus of control and self-esteem across the university years would permit examination of directional effects and potential bidirectional feedback loops between success experiences and self-evaluations. Experimental interventions that manipulate perceived control in specific domains could assess the causal impact on both domain-specific self-efficacy and global self-esteem to clarify mechanisms. Research that includes domain-specific measures of control (for example, academic locus of control) alongside global measures could reveal whether domain congruence strengthens associations with self-esteem, as theory would predict. Moreover, qualitative studies might illuminate the attributional narratives that students in elite settings construct to make sense of their achievements and struggles.

It would also be valuable to examine moderators of the locus of control–self-esteem link. For instance, personality traits such as neuroticism and conscientiousness, social comparison orientation, perceived social support, and cultural background might influence the strength and direction of associations. In addition, contextual factors like departmental climate, competitiveness, and mentoring availability could shape whether students interpret success and failure in internal or external terms. Investigating such moderators would enable a more nuanced understanding of when and for whom internal control beliefs translate into higher global self-esteem.

Technically, future studies might expand the assessment battery to include measures of attributional style, self-efficacy, and domain-specific competence to map the network of relations that inform self-worth more comprehensively. Incorporating objective academic records rather than self-reports could refine tests of the association between performance and psychological constructs. Finally, research that spans cultures and educational systems would clarify the extent to which the present patterns are universal versus context-dependent, given that cultural differences in self-construal and attributions can substantially influence both locus of control and self-esteem.

In sum, the study provides clear evidence in a sample of Russell Group undergraduates that greater external locus of control is associated with lower global self-esteem, consistent with theoretical expectations linking perceived agency to self-evaluative dispositions. The sample as a whole did not exhibit extreme internality or elevated self-esteem, and academic performance was not a strong indicator of either construct. These findings underscore the complexity of psychological adaptation in high-achieving academic contexts and point to the need for multifaceted interventions and longitudinal research to unpack causal pathways. By situating locus of control and self-esteem within the lived experiences of students at elite institutions, the research contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how beliefs about control and evaluations of the self interrelate and how these dynamics may support or hinder students’ adjustment and well-being.
