Introduction

The impact of media on psychological well-being has been a central concern in psychological research for several decades. Scholars and practitioners alike have noted that contemporary media environments present idealized visual and narrative portrayals of gendered appearance and interpersonal relationships. These portrayals are often repetitive and widely disseminated, which has led to concerns that exposure to such content may shape viewers’ beliefs, expectations, and self-perceptions. One domain in which media influence has been most intensively studied is body image, where a consistent body of work points to associations between media consumption and dissatisfaction with body shape, weight, and appearance. The tendency for individuals to compare themselves to media images and to internalize cultural appearance ideals has been proposed as a central mechanism linking media exposure to negative body image. Social comparison theory proposes that people evaluate their own appearances by comparing themselves to others, and media images provide a readily available set of comparison targets that are often unrealistically idealized. Cultivation theory further suggests that repeated exposure to particular messages in the media shapes viewers’ perceptions of social reality, including norms about attractiveness and relationship ideals. A number of theoretical perspectives converge on the prediction that higher levels of media exposure, particularly to appearance-focused content, will be associated with more negative evaluations of one’s own body and appearance-related self-esteem.

Romantic beliefs comprise another psychological domain that has been discussed in relation to media exposure. Romantic beliefs include a set of attitudes and expectations about love, relationships, and partner behavior, which may be framed as romantic idealism or endorsement of romantic myths. Media—through movies, television, romantic novels, and increasingly through social media narratives—portray romantic relationships in stylized and often idealized ways. These portrayals may promote beliefs such as the notion of a single perfect partner, the idea that love should be effortless or destiny-driven, and the expectation that outward displays of romantic passion are central to relationship success. Exposure to romanticized media narratives can function as a form of social learning, offering scripts for how romantic relationships should unfold. The cultivation of romantic beliefs through media exposure is theorized to affect how individuals form expectations about partners, resolve conflicts, and assess the adequacy of their own romantic experiences. Accordingly, prior work has documented associations between media exposure and more romanticized relationship expectations and beliefs, suggesting that media can play a role in shaping romantic schemas.

Despite the separate literatures on media effects on body image and on media effects on romantic beliefs, the interface among romantic beliefs, media exposure, and body image has been less systematically examined. It is reasonable to expect there to be functional links among these domains because romantic beliefs often specify expectations about partner attributes and romantic scenarios in which appearance can be foregrounded. For example, if media promote a notion that partners should be physically ideal or that romantic success hinges on aesthetic appeal, then endorsement of romantic beliefs could be associated with heightened pressure to conform to appearance ideals. Conversely, individuals with strong romantic beliefs may be more attentive to media portrayals of romance, and this selective attention could expose them to a higher frequency of appearance-focused messages. Thus, theoretical models could predict either direct associations between romantic beliefs and body image or indirect associations mediated by media exposure and social comparison processes.

Existing empirical work has linked romantic experiences to body image, but it has not consistently linked romantic beliefs per se to body image. Studies examining the relationship between experiences in romantic relationships—such as receiving appearance-based feedback from partners or being objectified within intimate relationships—have reported that negative romantic experiences can correspond with worsening body image and appearance-related self-esteem. For example, critical comments from partners or relationship rejection have been associated with lower body satisfaction. These findings suggest that interpersonal dynamics within romantic contexts can play a substantial role in shaping body image. However, romantic experiences and romantic beliefs are conceptually distinct constructs: beliefs reflect endorsed schemas and expectations, whereas experiences reflect actual interpersonal events and behaviors. Because media both shapes beliefs and provides a context for social comparison, a comprehensive understanding of body image may require considering media exposure, romantic beliefs, and romantic experiences together.

A related issue concerns gender differences in media effects and body image outcomes. Women have traditionally been the main focus of research on body dissatisfaction because cultural appearance standards for women tend to be more prescriptive and are more frequently emphasized in mainstream media. Empirical findings often show stronger associations between media exposure and body dissatisfaction for women relative to men. Men are also influenced by media ideals, but the specific content and standards (e.g., muscularity, leanness) differ and may be less consistently emphasized in some media formats. Furthermore, gender socialization processes and the differential value placed on appearance for women and men may amplify the psychological impact of media messages for women. Given these patterns, it is important to examine whether associations among media exposure, romantic beliefs, and negative body image differ by gender.

The existing literature therefore points to several coherent predictions but also reveals important gaps. First, while media exposure is reliably linked to negative body image, the extent to which this association generalizes across different demographic groups and media types remains an open question. Second, romantic beliefs are plausibly related to both media exposure and body image, but empirical tests of the exact nature of these relationships have been limited. Third, although romantic experiences have been associated with body image outcomes, the relationship between romantic beliefs and body image has not been thoroughly investigated. Fourth, interactions among media exposure, romantic beliefs, and gender have not been comprehensively mapped, leaving room for studies that consider these variables simultaneously.

The present study was designed to address these gaps by examining the bivariate associations among media exposure, romantic beliefs, and negative body image in a single sample. In doing so, the study aims to clarify whether romantic beliefs are correlated with media exposure and with body image, and to determine whether the well-documented association between media exposure and negative body image replicates in the current sample. In addition, the study examines whether any observed associations differ by gender, given prior evidence suggesting stronger media-body links among women. The design is correlational and observational, which allows for the detection of patterns of association but not causal claims. Nonetheless, the study can contribute to theory by testing whether romantic beliefs operate as an independent correlate of body image or whether media exposure accounts for most of the variance associated with negative body image.

To operationalize these constructs, standard questionnaires were used to assess romantic beliefs and body image, and a study-specific media exposure questionnaire was developed to capture the degree to which participants consume and attend to appearance- and romance-focused media content. The romantic beliefs instrument measures the degree of endorsement of romantic ideals and myths, while the body image measure captures dissatisfaction with body weight, appearance, and the impact of these concerns on self-esteem. The media exposure questionnaire was designed to be broad in scope, indexing frequency and intensity of exposure across television, film, magazines, and online social media, with particular attention to items that emphasize appearance and romantic narratives.

Three primary hypotheses guided the analyses. First, consistent with a substantial literature, it was hypothesized that higher media exposure would be associated with more negative body image. Second, consistent with prior research linking media to romantic schema formation, it was hypothesized that higher media exposure would be associated with stronger endorsement of romantic beliefs. Third, because romantic beliefs specify expectations that might amplify appearance concerns in romantic contexts, it was hypothesized that stronger romantic beliefs would be associated with more negative body image. Exploratory analyses also tested whether the predicted associations varied by gender, with an expectation that associations between media exposure and negative body image would be stronger among women.

By investigating these relationships in a single dataset, the present study sought to clarify the extent to which romantic beliefs are implicated in appearance-related self-evaluations and whether media exposure remains a robust correlate of negative body image when romantic beliefs are considered. The approach taken here therefore aims to bridge related but often separately studied literatures on media effects, romantic cognition, and body image, providing a more integrated perspective on how cultural and interpersonal cognitions might combine to shape appearance-related self-perceptions.

Methods

Participants were 100 adults recruited through a combination of university participant pools and online community advertisements. Recruitment targeted individuals aged 18 to 35 years to capture a cohort that is both heavy consumers of popular media and at a developmental stage when romantic relationships and body image concerns are salient. A total of 100 participants completed the study in its entirety and were included in the analyses. Of these participants, 62 identified as female, 36 identified as male, and 2 identified as nonbinary or preferred not to specify; the nonbinary or unspecified participants were retained in aggregated descriptive statistics but were excluded from gender-stratified inferential comparisons due to limited sample size in that category. The mean age of participants was 22.7 years with a standard deviation of 3.9 years. Participants reported a range of ethnic and socioeconomic backgrounds representative of the local university and surrounding communities, and all participants were fluent in English.

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the institutional review board of the hosting university prior to data collection. Participants provided informed consent electronically before beginning the online survey. Participation was voluntary and anonymous; participants received either course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. The survey was administered via an online platform, and participants were free to discontinue at any time without penalty. A debriefing statement explaining the study’s aims and providing mental health resource information was displayed at the conclusion of the survey.

Measures included three questionnaires administered in a fixed order to minimize potential order effects: the Romantic Beliefs Scale, the Body Weight, Image, and Self-Esteem Evaluation Questionnaire (Revised), and the Media Exposure Questionnaire developed for the present study. Participants also provided demographic information including age, self-reported height and weight (from which body mass index, BMI, was calculated), gender, and relationship status.

The Romantic Beliefs Scale is a self-report instrument that assesses endorsement of common romantic ideals and myths. Items reflect beliefs such as the idea of “soulmates,” that love should be effortless, and that a person’s romantic partner should meet most emotional needs. For the purposes of this study, the scale comprised 15 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Item scores were summed to produce a total score with a possible range of 15 to 75, where higher scores indicate stronger endorsement of romantic beliefs. Prior versions of similar scales have shown acceptable internal consistency and construct validity in analogous samples, and the present study calculated internal consistency for the current sample.

The Body Weight, Image, and Self-Esteem Evaluation Questionnaire (Revised) was used to capture negative body image and its impact on self-esteem. This revised instrument includes subscales assessing dissatisfaction with body weight, dissatisfaction with appearance, and the extent to which weight and appearance influence self-worth. The questionnaire for this study contained 20 items rated on a 5-point scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely). Responses were summed to yield a composite score ranging from 20 to 100, with higher scores indicating more negative body image and greater appearance-related impacts on self-esteem. As with the romantic beliefs measure, internal consistency for the body image questionnaire was computed in the current sample.

The Media Exposure Questionnaire was developed by the research team to assess the frequency and intensity of exposure to media content that emphasizes appearance and romantic narratives. The questionnaire included 18 items covering a variety of media channels, including television shows, films, magazines, and social media platforms. Items asked participants to report the frequency of exposure (e.g., hours per week) to different media types and to rate the extent to which they attend to appearance-related content and romantic narratives within those channels. Responses were provided on a combination of frequency scales and Likert-type scales; to create an overall media exposure index, items were standardized and aggregated so that higher composite scores reflected greater exposure and attention to appearance- and romance-focused media content. The Media Exposure Questionnaire’s development included item piloting with a small sample prior to data collection to ensure clarity and relevance; internal consistency was also evaluated in the full sample.

Procedures for scoring included reverse-scoring of items where necessary and computation of total summed or averaged scores for each instrument. Prior to inferential analyses, data were screened for outliers, missing data, and departures from normality. Cases with missing data greater than 20% on any scale were excluded; the final sample consisted of the 100 participants who completed the scales fully. Item-level missing responses within otherwise complete scales were handled using mean imputation for that participant on the relevant scale if missingness was less than 10% for that scale. Distributional checks included inspection of histograms and calculation of skewness and kurtosis statistics; where distributions were substantially non-normal, nonparametric checks were performed as supplementary analyses.

Analytic strategies involved both descriptive and inferential statistics. Initial analyses described sample characteristics and provided means, standard deviations, and internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) for each scale. Pearson product-moment correlations were computed to evaluate bivariate associations among media exposure, romantic beliefs, and body image scores in the full sample. To address gender differences, correlations were computed separately for women and men, and differences between correlation coefficients were tested using Fisher r-to-z transformations. Multiple regression analyses were conducted to examine the unique contribution of media exposure and romantic beliefs in predicting negative body image, with gender and BMI included as covariates in subsequent models. Interaction terms (e.g., Media Exposure × Gender; Romantic Beliefs × Gender) were entered into regression models to test whether associations differed by gender.

Supplementary analyses included partial correlations controlling for age and BMI to assess whether associations among the main variables persisted after accounting for these potential confounds. Additional exploratory analyses examined whether relationship status (single vs partnered) moderated the associations between romantic beliefs and body image, given that romantic beliefs might have different psychological relevance for individuals who are currently in relationships compared to those who are not.

All statistical tests were two-tailed and interpreted at an alpha level of .05. Effect sizes were reported using standard conventions (e.g., Pearson’s r, Cohen’s d for mean comparisons, and standardized beta coefficients for regression analyses). Statistical analyses were conducted using commonly available statistical software, and data and syntax were archived in accordance with institutional policies to enable reproducibility of the analyses.

Results

Descriptive statistics and reliability estimates were calculated for all primary measures in the sample of 100 participants. The Romantic Beliefs Scale showed acceptable internal consistency with Cronbach’s alpha equal to .81. The Body Weight, Image, and Self-Esteem Evaluation Questionnaire (Revised) also demonstrated good internal consistency with an alpha of .87. The Media Exposure Questionnaire composite exhibited acceptable internal consistency with an alpha of .78. These estimates indicate that the scales functioned reliably in the current sample and supported the use of total scores in subsequent analyses.

Means and standard deviations for the primary variables were as follows. The mean Romantic Beliefs Score was 38.4 with a standard deviation of 9.6, indicating a moderate level of endorsement of romantic beliefs in the sample. The mean Body Image Score was 46.9 with a standard deviation of 12.3, indicating a moderate level of negative body image on average. The mean Media Exposure composite score was standardized such that the sample mean was approximately zero with a standard deviation of 1.0 by construction. Self-reported BMI in the sample had a mean of 23.7 and a standard deviation of 4.1; BMI values ranged from 17.5 to 36.4.

Initial bivariate Pearson correlations among the three main constructs for the full sample are reported next. Media Exposure was positively correlated with Body Image Score, indicating that greater media exposure was associated with more negative body image. Specifically, the correlation between Media Exposure and Body Image was r = .30, p = .002, which represents a small-to-moderate effect size. In contrast, the correlation between Media Exposure and Romantic Beliefs was small and not statistically significant, r = .12, p = .22. Similarly, the correlation between Romantic Beliefs and Body Image was r = .09, p = .34, which was not statistically significant. Thus, in the overall sample, only Media Exposure and Body Image showed a significant association.

Given theoretical expectations regarding gender differences, separate correlations were computed for women and men to assess whether the observed associations differed by gender. For the women (n = 62), the correlation between Media Exposure and Body Image was stronger and statistically significant, r = .42, p < .001. This larger correlation suggests a moderate effect, indicating that among women, higher media exposure was notably associated with more negative body image. Among men (n = 36), the correlation between Media Exposure and Body Image was small and not significant, r = .05, p = .73. To test whether the difference in these correlations across gender was statistically significant, a Fisher r-to-z transformation was conducted. The difference between the female and male correlation coefficients was significant, z = 2.05, p = .04, indicating that the association between Media Exposure and Body Image was significantly stronger for women than for men in this sample.

Correlations of Romantic Beliefs with the other two variables were also examined by gender. For women, Romantic Beliefs and Media Exposure were correlated at r = .14, p = .27, which was not significant. For men, Romantic Beliefs and Media Exposure were correlated at r = .09, p = .61, also non-significant. The correlation between Romantic Beliefs and Body Image was r = .10, p = .42 for women and r = .06, p = .70 for men, neither of which reached significance. Thus, across gender-stratified analyses, Romantic Beliefs showed no significant associations with either Media Exposure or Body Image.

Multiple regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the unique contribution of Media Exposure and Romantic Beliefs in predicting negative body image, while accounting for potential confounding variables. In the first regression model for the full sample, Body Image Score was regressed on Media Exposure and Romantic Beliefs. Media Exposure emerged as a significant predictor (standardized beta = .29, p = .003), while Romantic Beliefs did not (standardized beta = .09, p = .33). This model accounted for approximately 11% of the variance in Body Image Score (adjusted R^2 = .11).

A second regression model added gender and BMI as covariates and included interaction terms to test for moderation by gender. In this model, Media Exposure remained a significant predictor (standardized beta = .28, p = .004). Gender (coded female = 1, male = 0) was a non-significant predictor when entered without interactions (standardized beta = .07, p = .45). BMI had a significant positive association with negative body image (standardized beta = .22, p = .02), indicating that higher BMI was associated with more negative body image. The interaction term Media Exposure × Gender was significant (standardized beta = .19, p = .03), suggesting that the effect of media exposure on body image was more pronounced for women. The interaction term Romantic Beliefs × Gender was not significant (standardized beta = .05, p = .62). The inclusion of interactions improved model fit, and the model explained approximately 18% of the variance in Body Image Score (adjusted R^2 = .18).

Partial correlations controlling for age and BMI were computed to examine whether the observed associations were robust to these covariates. The partial correlation between Media Exposure and Body Image in the full sample, controlling for age and BMI, was r_partial = .27, p = .006, indicating that the association remained significant after accounting for these variables. Partial correlations between Romantic Beliefs and Body Image, and between Romantic Beliefs and Media Exposure, remained nonsignificant after controlling for age and BMI (both p > .20).

Supplementary exploratory analyses examined whether relationship status moderated the association between Romantic Beliefs and Body Image. An interaction term (Romantic Beliefs × Relationship Status) was entered into a regression predicting Body Image Score, controlling for Media Exposure. Neither the main effect of Romantic Beliefs nor the interaction with relationship status reached significance (p > .20), suggesting no evidence that being single versus partnered altered the (non-significant) association between romantic beliefs and body image in this sample.

To assess the robustness of the main finding, nonparametric Spearman rank-order correlations were computed as sensitivity checks due to modest skewness in the Body Image distribution. The Spearman correlation between Media Exposure and Body Image in the full sample was rho = .28, p = .004, consistent with the Pearson correlation and confirming that the association was not an artifact of distributional assumptions. Gender-stratified Spearman correlations similarly reflected the pattern observed with Pearson correlations: a significant rho = .40, p = .001 among women and a non-significant rho = .06, p = .66 among men.

Finally, descriptive comparisons suggested that women reported slightly higher mean Media Exposure scores than men, but this difference did not reach statistical significance in an independent-samples t-test (t(96) = 1.47, p = .14, Cohen’s d = 0.30). Women did report significantly higher Body Image scores (indicating more negative body image) than men (t(96) = 2.68, p = .009, Cohen’s d = 0.55), consistent with prior literature suggesting elevated body dissatisfaction among women.

In summary, the results indicate a significant positive association between media exposure and negative body image in the overall sample, with this association concentrated among female participants. Romantic beliefs did not show significant correlations with either media exposure or body image in this dataset. The association between media exposure and body image persisted after controlling for age and BMI and was corroborated by nonparametric sensitivity checks.

Discussion

The present study examined the interrelationships among media exposure, romantic beliefs, and negative body image in a sample of 100 young adults. The primary findings were twofold. First, consistent with prior literature, greater exposure to appearance- and romance-focused media was associated with more negative body image scores overall. Second, this association was primarily observed among women: the correlation between media exposure and negative body image was moderate and statistically significant for female participants but negligible and non-significant for male participants. In contrast, romantic beliefs did not show significant associations with either media exposure or negative body image in the current sample. These results suggest that media exposure is a relevant correlate of negative body image, particularly among women, whereas the endorsement of romantic beliefs per se may not be directly implicated in appearance dissatisfaction in this cohort.

The finding that media exposure correlates positively with negative body image aligns with a substantial body of theoretical and empirical work. The present results are consistent with social comparison theorizing, which predicts that consumption of idealized media images fosters upward comparisons that can undermine self-evaluations of appearance. The observed gender difference, whereby women demonstrated a stronger media-body association than men, is also consonant with prior research indicating that cultural appearance ideals and media representations exert a disproportionate influence on women due to gendered socialization and the heavier emphasis on physical attractiveness for women in cultural narratives. The significant Media Exposure × Gender interaction observed in the regression analysis further supports the interpretation that media exposure interacts with gender-linked social and cultural factors to influence body image.

The lack of a significant association between romantic beliefs and body image warrants careful interpretation. At face value, the null finding implies that endorsement of romantic ideals, such as belief in soulmates or destiny-driven love, may not be directly related to an individual’s satisfaction with their body or the degree to which they tie self-worth to appearance. This suggests that beliefs about romantic relationships may operate independently of appearance-related self-evaluations for many individuals. One plausible explanation is that romantic beliefs reflect a cognitive schema about relational processes that does not necessarily place appearance at the center of one’s valuation of a partner or oneself. In other words, a person may endorse romantic myths without simultaneously valuing or investing heavily in their physical appearance as a core component of self-worth.

Another explanation for the absence of a relationship between romantic beliefs and body image is measurement specificity. Romantic beliefs, as assessed by the scale used in this study, focus on general expectations about the nature of romantic love and partnership rather than on appearance-specific romantic ideals. If the aspects of romantic cognition that are most closely related to body image are those that emphasize partner appearance or attractiveness as essential to romantic success, a broader romantic beliefs scale might not capture these nuances. Thus, a measure tailored to assess the degree to which romantic beliefs explicitly emphasize physical attractiveness as central to partner selection might reveal different patterns. This points to an avenue for future research to incorporate more fine-grained measures that distinguish between general romantic idealism and appearance-centric relationship beliefs.

Additionally, prior research linking romantic experiences to negative body image suggests that lived interpersonal events, such as receiving critical comments from a romantic partner or experiencing relationship rejection, may function differently from abstractly held romantic beliefs. Romantic experiences are proximal social events that can directly affect one’s body image through interpersonal feedback and emotional consequences. Romantic beliefs, by contrast, may exert influence more indirectly and perhaps only under specific conditions, such as when individuals perceive discrepancies between their beliefs and their lived experiences. It is possible that romantic beliefs moderate the impact of romantic experiences on body image, rather than exerting a direct effect. For instance, holding strong romantic ideals might amplify the negative impact of appearance-related partner criticism because such criticism may be interpreted as a failure to live up to one’s romantic script. Testing such moderated mediation models would be an important direction for future work.

The gender-specific pattern observed in this study is noteworthy and merits further elaboration. Women in the sample exhibited both higher negative body image scores and a stronger association between media exposure and negative body image. This pattern supports the idea that cultural messages about beauty and attractiveness are more salient and consequential for women in shaping self-evaluation. The lack of a significant association among men may reflect several factors. Men may be exposed to different kinds of appearance ideals (e.g., muscularity rather than thinness) that were not captured adequately by the Media Exposure Questionnaire, which emphasized general appearance- and romance-focused media. Alternatively, men in the present sample may be less likely to internalize media images in ways that negatively affect their self-esteem, possibly due to gendered expectations about the relative importance of appearance. Finally, measurement limitations in assessing male-focused appearance concerns could have attenuated the observed associations among men. Future studies should ensure that media exposure measures and body image instruments are sensitive to the particular appearance ideals and body image concerns that are most relevant for men, including muscularity-oriented concerns and weight-training-related body ideals.

The correlation between media exposure and negative body image remained significant after controlling for age and BMI, indicating that the observed relationship was not simply a function of these demographic or constitutive variables. BMI itself was associated with body image, which is consistent with a large literature documenting that higher BMI is often linked to greater body dissatisfaction. Importantly, the media effect persisted above and beyond the contribution of BMI, suggesting that media-related pressures and comparisons add explanatory power in accounting for individual differences in body image.

The study’s design and methodology have several strengths that lend credibility to the findings. The use of validated instruments for romantic beliefs and body image, the development of a media exposure measure targeted to appearance- and romance-focused content, and the inclusion of gender-stratified analyses all contribute to a nuanced examination of the relationships of interest. The internal consistency values for the primary measures were adequate, supporting the reliability of the constructs as operationalized in this study.

Nevertheless, there are important limitations that temper the conclusions that can be drawn. First and foremost, the correlational and cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although media exposure was associated with negative body image, it is not possible from this design to determine whether media exposure causes increases in body dissatisfaction, whether individuals with higher body dissatisfaction seek out more media content that reinforces appearance ideals, or whether a bidirectional relationship exists. Longitudinal and experimental designs will be necessary to clarify the directionality of effects.

Second, the Media Exposure Questionnaire, while developed to capture relevant content, relied on self-report and aggregate item standardization. Self-reported media exposure may be subject to recall bias, social desirability, or imprecision in estimating consumption habits. Future work could improve on this limitation by incorporating objective measures of media exposure, such as digital trace data or ecological momentary assessment, which can provide more granular and accurate estimates of exposure and engagement.

Third, the sample size, while adequate for detecting moderate effects, limits the ability to test more complex models or to detect smaller effect sizes, especially when conducting subgroup analyses. The small number of participants identifying as nonbinary or preferring not to specify gender precluded meaningful analyses for gender-diverse individuals. Research with larger and more diverse samples is needed to assess the generalizability of the present findings and to permit more fine-grained analyses across gender identities, sexual orientations, and cultural backgrounds.

Fourth, the Romantic Beliefs Scale captured general romantic idealism rather than appearance-focused romantic beliefs. If the hypothesized link between romantic cognition and body image operates specifically through beliefs that place heavy emphasis on physical attractiveness as a precondition for romantic success, then the present measure may have lacked the specificity needed to detect such an association. Future studies should consider including scales that assess the role of partner appearance in romantic beliefs, as well as measures of appearance-related relationship expectations.

Fifth, the study did not directly measure romantic experiences, which prior research suggests can be influential for body image. Including measures of actual relational events—such as partner criticism, body-related comments from partners, experiences of rejection, or relationship satisfaction—would enable tests of mediation or moderation models whereby romantic experiences could serve as pathways linking media exposure or romantic beliefs to body image outcomes.

Given these limitations, several clear directions for future research emerge. Longitudinal studies following participants over time would allow for stronger inferences about the temporal ordering of media exposure and body image changes. Experimental manipulations that vary exposure to appearance- or romance-focused media content could provide causal tests of media effects and identify mechanisms such as social comparison or internalization. Including measures of body-related romantic beliefs and of romantic experiences would permit tests of whether beliefs shape the impact of experiences on body image or vice versa. Examining mediators such as appearance investment, self-objectification, and social comparison tendencies could illuminate the psychological processes through which media exposure influences body image. Further, research should strive to use more ecologically valid measures of media exposure, including platform-specific consumption and content coding, to ascertain which types of media (e.g., social media influencers, reality television, fashion magazines) are most strongly related to negative body image.

In practical terms, the findings reinforce the relevance of media literacy and critical viewing interventions, particularly for young women, as potential strategies to buffer against media-related body dissatisfaction. Programs that foster critical engagement with media messages, reduce the internalization of unrealistic appearance ideals, and provide tools to counteract upward social comparisons may be beneficial in reducing the negative impact of media on body image. Clinicians working with individuals presenting with body image concerns should consider assessing media consumption patterns and incorporating media-focused interventions into treatment plans.

In conclusion, the current study replicates and extends existing work by demonstrating that higher exposure to appearance- and romance-focused media is associated with more negative body image, predominantly among women, while showing that general romantic beliefs are not directly correlated with body image or media exposure in this sample. These findings contribute to an integrated understanding of the cultural and cognitive correlates of body dissatisfaction and highlight the importance of gender-specific analyses. Future research employing longitudinal, experimental, and more nuanced measurement approaches will be necessary to disentangle causal pathways and to identify effective interventions to mitigate media-related body image risks.
