Introduction

Cooperation is a foundational topic in psychological science because it touches on how people coordinate behavior for mutual benefit in social settings. Over the past several decades researchers have documented that cooperation can emerge even in one-shot anonymous interactions in laboratory settings. A particularly influential line of work has argued that cooperative responses are, in many contexts, intuitive or automatic, whereas selfish responses require deliberation and reflection. This assertion has been advanced on the basis of dual-process theorizing that distinguishes between fast, intuitive processes and slow, deliberative processes that draw on controlled cognition. The idea that intuition favors cooperation has strong theoretical appeal because it suggests that humans may be adapted or socially trained to default to prosocial strategies in everyday interaction. If cooperation is a readily accessible response, then experimental manipulations that encourage rapid responding should reveal higher rates of cooperative behavior than manipulations that encourage deliberation.

A now widely cited series of experiments by Rand, Greene, and Nowak has provided empirical support for the intuition-for-cooperation hypothesis. In those studies, participants who were placed under time pressure or asked to respond quickly in one-shot social dilemmas were more likely to contribute to public goods or cooperate in prisoner’s dilemma–type tasks than participants who were given time to deliberate. The reported effects were robust across several samples and have stimulated a large literature testing the generality and boundary conditions of the effect. Some subsequent work has replicated the basic effect, whereas other research has questioned its robustness, the exact mechanisms that produce it, and the types of contexts in which it emerges. Thus the literature contains support for intuitive cooperation, but also important caveats that motivate further exploration of moderators and individual differences.

One potentially important source of individual differences that has not been extensively examined in this context concerns people’s prior experience making high-stakes, rapid decisions in real-world domains. Athletic participation, particularly at competitive levels, requires frequent, rapid decisions under pressure that are embedded in social and strategic contexts. Athletes must often make split-second choices that balance self-interest, team objectives, and social norms. Repeated exposure to these decision contexts could plausibly shape the cognitive and affective heuristics that actors bring to unrelated decision tasks. For instance, athletes who play team sports regularly negotiate cooperation and competition within their teams and with opponents; their default responses in social dilemmas might become tuned by that experience. Similarly, athletes who primarily engage in individual sports face different incentive structures and norms, which might translate into different default orientations when faced with a cooperation-versus-self-interest decision in a laboratory task. If training and experience in sport tune the content of intuitive responses, then the intuition-for-cooperation effect might be moderated by sport experience.

Despite the plausible links between sport experience and social decision-making, relatively little experimental work has directly examined whether participation in sport moderates the extent to which people are intuitively cooperative. The extant literature on sports psychology documents that participation in sport is associated with a host of social and cognitive outcomes, including enhanced teamwork skills, better stress regulation, and refined rapid decision-making skills. Team sports are frequently highlighted for their role in the development of social identity and collective norms that prioritize in-group cooperation. Individual sports, by contrast, may emphasize self-reliance, individual accountability, and strategic self-interest in competitive settings. Yet both team and individual sports require athletes to make rapid decisions within complex, socially embedded environments. Thus one might expect sport participation to shape default responses in cooperation tasks, but the direction of that effect is theoretically ambiguous.

There are at least two plausible hypotheses for how sport experience could moderate intuition-for-cooperation effects. One hypothesis is that sports players, especially team-sport players, will show stronger intuitive cooperation because they have internalized cooperative norms and practiced rapid coordination with teammates. Under this account, the decision habits developed in team sports in which instantaneous, cooperative behavior is rewarded would generalize to unrelated decision tasks such that quick responses favor prosociality. A competing hypothesis is that sports experience, particularly in competitive or individualistic sports, trains athletes to adopt quick strategic or self-protective heuristics that prioritize personal gain when stakes are salient. If this were the case, sports players might be less prosocial under time pressure than non-sports players or might show a reduced difference between intuitive and deliberative responses because their intuitions are more strategic and less prosocial.

Another way sport participation could affect intuitive cooperation is by reducing the gap between intuitive and reflective choices. That is, repeated practice making fast decisions in sporting contexts could produce a convergence between what feels intuitive and what deliberation leads to, yielding more similar decisions regardless of whether participants are prompted to respond quickly or to deliberate. If athletes’ immediate responses are already informed by an internalized strategy accrued through sports participation, then introducing time pressure may not substantially shift behavior. This would manifest as an interaction between time condition and sport participation such that the intuition-for-cooperation effect is strong among non-athletes but attenuated among athletes.

The present study sought to investigate these possibilities by directly replicating the central manipulation used by Rand, Greene, and Nowak and testing sport participation as an individual-difference moderator. The research was designed to achieve two core aims. The first aim was to replicate the main effect previously reported: that intuitive decision-making, operationalized via a time pressure manipulation, increases cooperative behavior in a one-shot public goods game. Replication of this effect in a new sample is a necessary first step before evaluating moderators. The second aim was to test whether self-reported sports participation—coded into categories for no sport, individual sports, team sports, or both individual and team sports—moderates the relationship between decision mode (intuitive vs. reflective) and cooperative contributions in the public goods game.

To pursue these aims we designed an online study with a sample of 104 participants who completed a one-shot public goods game under either a time pressure or a time delay condition. Participants also indicated their sport participation status via a brief questionnaire and provided basic demographics. We preregistered (for transparency we describe our analytic plan in the methods) a two-way analysis to examine the main effect of time condition and the interaction of time condition with sport participation. We also planned several follow-up contrasts to compare each sport group to the no-sport group and to examine whether team and individual sports differed from each other.

There are several reasons why examing sport participation as a moderator is both theoretically meaningful and empirically important. First, sport is a common activity worldwide and thus its potential to influence default social heuristics has broad implications for understanding population-level variation in cooperation. Second, sport participation blends cognitive and social components—decision speed, teamwork norms, competitive incentives—that make it a rich context for shaping both intuitive and reflective processes. Third, the question of whether real-world experience can alter the content of intuitive responses speaks directly to debates about the malleability of intuition and the degree to which intuitive cooperation reflects evolved, fixed dispositions versus learned, context-sensitive heuristics.

Beyond theoretical importance, the empirical exploration of sport participation addresses a practical gap. Much of the intuition-for-cooperation literature has focused on laboratory manipulations and broad population samples without detailed attention to the kinds of training and experience that participants bring to the task. By systematically including a measure of sport participation and classifying participants into meaningful subgroups, we aim to provide new insight into boundary conditions for the intuition-for-cooperation effect and to open a door for future work that interrogates how other forms of real-world practice shape default social behavior.

Given the mixed predictions in the literature and the exploratory nature of some of the sport-related hypotheses, we framed our sports analyses as theoretically motivated but exploratory. Specifically, we predicted that the main replication would hold—intuitive decision-making would increase cooperation overall compared with reflective decision-making. For sport participation we refrained from making a single directional prediction across all sport categories because of the competing theoretical rationales described above. Instead, we tested whether sport participation moderates the main effect and whether the pattern of moderation differs between individual and team sports. We also explored whether participants who reported both individual and team participation differed from those who only reported one form of sport involvement.

In sum, the present study aims to replicate a widely discussed experimental effect concerning intuition and cooperation and to extend that literature by examining whether sport experience alters the pattern of intuitive versus reflective social decisions. The findings have the potential to clarify the generality of the intuition-for-cooperation effect and to demonstrate how domain-specific training can shape the cognitive heuristics that guide social behavior. If sports participation attenuates the intuition-for-cooperation effect, this would suggest that intuition is malleable and shaped by real-world contexts; if sport participation amplifies or leaves the effect unchanged, different implications for theory and future research would follow. We proceed by describing our methods, reporting the statistical analyses and outcomes, and discussing the implications and possible future directions that arise from the results.

Methods

Participants and recruitment

One hundred and four participants were recruited to take part in an online experiment that involved a one-shot social dilemma decision. Participants were recruited through an online recruitment platform and were eligible if they were 18 years old or older and fluent in English. All participants provided informed consent before participating in the study. The sample size of 104 was chosen to be similar in scale to many published experimental replications in this area and to provide modest statistical power for detecting main effects and interactions. Participants were compensated for their time with a fixed payment plus the potential for bonus earnings determined by the outcome of the public goods game.

Demographics and sport participation assessment

Participants completed a brief demographic questionnaire prior to the experimental task. Demographic variables included age, gender, and self-reported familiarity with economic games. Sport participation was assessed with a single-item question that asked participants to indicate whether they currently played no sport, participated in individual sports, participated in team sports, or participated in both individual and team sports. Participants were instructed to consider recreational and competitive involvement and to include activities they participated in regularly during the past year. Categories were self-selected by participants; no further attempt was made to verify the depth or intensity of participation. The sport participation variable was treated as categorical with four levels.

Experimental task: public goods game

Participants completed a one-shot public goods game that was modeled on paradigms commonly used in the literature testing the intuition-for-cooperation hypothesis. Each participant was endowed with a fixed amount of experimental currency for the round. Participants were informed that they could contribute any portion of their endowment to a public pot; contributions to the pot would be multiplied by a factor greater than one and then divided equally among a putative group of four players. The payoff structure was described in a clear and simple manner; the exact numbers (endowment and multiplier) were presented numerically and participants had to pass an attention check that required them to report the payoff consequences for specific contribution levels before proceeding. The game was explicitly framed as a one-shot interaction with anonymous other participants such that reputation and future interactions were not possible. Instructions included examples to illustrate the payoff trade-off between selfish and cooperative choices.

Time condition manipulation

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two decision mode conditions: a time pressure condition designed to promote intuitive responding and a time delay condition designed to promote reflective responding. Random assignment was implemented by the online survey platform’s randomization function. In the time pressure condition participants were asked to make their contribution decision within 10 seconds of the decision screen being displayed; a visible countdown timer was presented to emphasize the time constraint. If a participant did not submit a decision within the time window the decision screen automatically advanced and their last recorded input was used. In the time delay condition participants were instructed to think carefully and to wait at least 10 seconds before submitting their response; a timer indicated that the minimum delay period was in effect and the response button remained disabled during that period. After the 10-second delay, participants could submit their decision at their leisure. These timing parameters were chosen to be broadly consistent with the time constraints used in prior work examining intuitive versus reflective decision-making in economic games.

Attention checks and manipulation checks

To ensure that participants understood the structure of the public goods game, an attention check was included in the instructions. Participants who failed the attention check were excluded from primary analyses. In addition, we included a self-report manipulation check after the decision asking participants to indicate whether they felt they responded intuitively or deliberately on a Likert scale; this measure was used descriptively to confirm that the time manipulation influenced subjective experience. Decision times were recorded as an objective measure of compliance with the time manipulation and were compared across conditions.

Procedure

Participants completed the study online in a single session that lasted approximately 10 to 15 minutes. After providing consent, participants completed demographic questions and the sport participation item. Next they read the public goods game instructions, completed the attention check, and were randomly assigned to a time condition. They completed the public goods decision under their assigned time constraint and then answered the manipulation check and a small number of additional filler items about their decision strategy. Finally, participants were debriefed and paid.

Exclusions and data cleaning

Data were screened for incompleteness and for failed attention checks. Participants who failed the attention check embedded in the game instructions were excluded from the primary analyses to ensure that the results reflected participants who understood the payoff structure of the task. In addition, participants who did not follow the timing requirements conspicuously (for example, in the time pressure condition taking more than 30 seconds before responding) were flagged for sensitivity analyses, though they were retained in the primary intention-to-treat analyses. Reaction time data were inspected for extreme outliers indicative of inattentive responding.

Analytic approach

The primary dependent variable was the amount contributed to the public pot expressed as a proportion of the endowment. We used a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test the main effect of time condition (time pressure vs. time delay), the main effect of sport participation (four levels), and their interaction. When appropriate, planned contrasts compared each sport group to the no-sport group and compared team versus individual sport participants. We report means and standard deviations for each group and condition, effect sizes (partial eta squared for ANOVA and Cohen’s d for pairwise contrasts), and p-values. We supplemented ANOVA models with regression analyses that controlled for demographic covariates such as age and gender. Manipulation checks included comparisons of response times and self-reported deliberation across time conditions using t-tests. Sensitivity analyses excluded participants who failed attention checks or who exhibited extreme response times to confirm the robustness of the findings.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the appropriate institutional review board. Participants were informed of their rights, including the right to withdraw at any time, and were compensated for participation. Data were collected anonymously and stored in secure servers in accordance with data protection protocols.

Results

Sample description and exclusions

One hundred and four participants began the study. Three participants were excluded from the primary analyses because they failed the attention check embedded in the public goods game instructions. The final analytic sample therefore consisted of 101 participants. The demographic characteristics of the sample were broadly consistent with typical online convenience samples: the mean age was in the mid twenties, and the sample included a mix of genders. Sport participation distribution for the 101 included participants was as follows: 28 participants reported no sport participation, 24 reported primarily individual sports, 30 reported primarily team sports, and 19 reported participation in both individual and team sports. Random assignment to the time condition yielded comparable group sizes across time pressure and time delay conditions; randomization checks showed no significant differences in age, gender, or sport distribution across the two conditions.

Manipulation checks

Objective response times differed markedly across the time pressure and time delay conditions as intended. Participants in the time pressure condition had a mean decision time of approximately 8.5 seconds (SD ≈ 2.1) compared with a mean decision time of approximately 23.4 seconds (SD ≈ 10.8) in the time delay condition. An independent-samples t-test confirmed that this difference in decision times was significant, t(99) = -14.2, p < .001. Subjective reports of whether participants felt they responded intuitively or deliberately also differed across conditions. On a 7-point scale where higher values reflected more deliberation, participants in the time delay condition reported higher deliberation (M ≈ 5.1, SD ≈ 1.3) than those in the time pressure condition (M ≈ 3.2, SD ≈ 1.5). This difference was also statistically significant, t(99) = 7.9, p < .001. These checks indicate that the time manipulation operated as intended and affected both objective and subjective aspects of responding.

Primary ANOVA: main effects and interaction

We conducted a 2 (time condition: time pressure vs. time delay) × 4 (sport participation: none, individual, team, both) ANOVA on the proportion of the endowment contributed to the public pot. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of time condition, F(1,93) = 6.21, p = .014, partial eta squared = .063, indicating that participants in the time pressure condition contributed more on average than participants in the time delay condition. The main effect of sport participation was not statistically significant at conventional levels, F(3,93) = 1.78, p = .157, partial eta squared = .054. Importantly, the interaction between time condition and sport participation was significant, F(3,93) = 3.05, p = .033, partial eta squared = .090, indicating that the effect of time condition on contributions differed across sport participation groups.

Descriptive statistics by group and condition

To illustrate the interaction, we present the mean contribution proportions for each sport group in each time condition. Among participants reporting no sport participation, those in the time pressure condition contributed substantially more (M ≈ .62 of endowment, SD ≈ .18) than those in the time delay condition (M ≈ .41, SD ≈ .22), yielding a large effect size for this comparison, Cohen’s d ≈ 1.07. By contrast, among participants reporting participation in team sports, the difference between time pressure (M ≈ .53, SD ≈ .24) and time delay (M ≈ .49, SD ≈ .19) was small and did not reach statistical significance, Cohen’s d ≈ .19. Participants reporting individual sports also showed a smaller time effect (time pressure M ≈ .56, SD ≈ .21; time delay M ≈ .50, SD ≈ .23), Cohen’s d ≈ .29. Participants who reported both individual and team sports likewise showed a small difference across conditions (time pressure M ≈ .57, SD ≈ .20; time delay M ≈ .51, SD ≈ .18), Cohen’s d ≈ .32. These descriptive statistics show that the intuition-for-cooperation effect was pronounced among non-sports participants but attenuated among those who reported participating in sports.

Planned contrasts: non-sports vs. sports groups

Planned contrasts tested whether the time pressure effect differed between participants reporting no sport and participants who reported any sport participation (i.e., combining individual, team, and both groups). The contrast comparing no-sport participants to all sports participants was significant for the interaction with time condition, b = .17 (SE = .07), t = 2.43, p = .017, indicating that the increase in contributions under time pressure relative to delay was significantly larger for non-sports participants than for sports participants. Pairwise comparisons confirmed that the time pressure versus delay difference within the no-sport group was statistically significant, t(26) = 3.91, p < .001, whereas within the combined sports group the time pressure versus delay difference was not statistically significant, t(71) = 1.61, p = .112.

Comparisons among sport subgroups

We also examined whether the pattern of attenuation varied across the three sport subgroups. A set of follow-up contrasts compared team sport participants to individual sport participants and to those who reported both types of sport participation. None of these contrasts reached conventional levels of statistical significance. The interaction between time condition and the contrast comparing team versus individual sports yielded b = -.03 (SE = .08), t = -.38, p = .704, and the interaction comparing team versus both sports yielded b = -.04 (SE = .09), t = -.44, p = .663. These nonsignificant contrasts suggest that the attenuation of the intuition-for-cooperation effect among sports participants was broadly similar across individual, team, and both-sport groups in this sample.

Regression analyses controlling for demographics

To assess the robustness of the interaction, we estimated linear regression models predicting contribution proportion from time condition, sport participation (dummy-coded), their interactions, and control variables including age and gender. The key interaction terms remained significant when controlling for these covariates, indicating that the moderation effect of sport participation could not be explained by simple demographic differences. In the model, the time pressure main effect remained significant (b = .09, SE = .04, p = .014), and the interaction between time pressure and no-sport status was significant (b = .17, SE = .07, p = .019). Gender and age were not significant predictors in the model.

Sensitivity analyses

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure that the results were not driven by outliers or participants who failed to comply strongly with the timing manipulation. Excluding three participants in the time pressure condition whose response times exceeded 30 seconds did not materially change the pattern of results: the time pressure main effect remained significant and the interaction with sport participation remained significant. Similarly, including the three participants who had been excluded for failing the attention check in a robustness check did not change the substantive conclusions, although the effect sizes were slightly attenuated. Analyses that treated contribution as a dichotomous variable (full versus partial or zero contribution) produced consistent results, with the odds of making a higher contribution under time pressure significantly increased among non-sports participants but not among sports participants.

Exploratory analyses: subjective manipulation check and strategy reports

Exploratory analyses examined whether subjective reports about decision strategy explained the interaction between time condition and sport participation. In a mediation-style analysis we regressed contribution on time condition, sport participation, subjective deliberation, and the interaction terms. Subjective deliberation was negatively associated with contribution, but including it in the model did not eliminate the significant interaction between time condition and sport participation. Reports of using fairness or strategic considerations as self-reported decision strategies were also considered; those who endorsed fairness motives reported higher contributions overall, but again these self-reports did not fully account for the sport moderation effect.

Summary of results

In summary, the primary replication objective was supported: across the full sample participants in the time pressure condition contributed more to the public pot than those in the time delay condition. Crucially, however, this intuition-for-cooperation effect was not uniform across participants. It was pronounced among participants who reported no sport participation but was attenuated among participants who reported playing individual sports, team sports, or both. The attenuation did not differ significantly between sport subtypes in the present sample. These effects were robust to several sensitivity checks and to inclusion of demographic covariates.

Discussion

The present study had two primary aims: to replicate the intuition-for-cooperation effect reported in previous research and to test whether sport participation moderates the relationship between decision mode and cooperative behavior. The findings provide a nuanced answer to both aims. First, we successfully replicated the main finding that promoting intuitive responding via time pressure leads to higher cooperative contributions in a one-shot public goods game relative to a deliberation-promoting time delay. This replication supports the basic dual-process account that rapid, intuitive processes can favor prosocial choices in anonymous social dilemmas. Second, and importantly, we found that the strength of this intuition-for-cooperation effect depends on participants’ sport participation status. Participants who reported no sport participation exhibited a large increase in cooperation under time pressure relative to delay, whereas participants who reported involvement in individual or team sports demonstrated only minimal differences between the two decision modes. This moderation implies that sports experience influences the content or accessibility of intuitive responses in social dilemmas.

These findings have several important theoretical implications. They suggest that the intuitive cooperation effect is not immutable across individuals and contexts; instead, it is sensitive to life experience and domain-specific training. The attenuation among sports participants can be interpreted in multiple, not mutually exclusive, ways. One interpretation is that sports training—especially when it involves frequent rapid decisions in socially embedded contexts—aligns intuitive responses more closely with deliberative strategies. In other words, athletes may develop decision habits such that what feels immediate and intuitive already incorporates considerations that would otherwise require deliberation for non-athletes. Under this "habit-convergence" account, the time manipulation has less leverage over behavior among athletes because the cognitive content of their intuition is already shaped by prior practice.

A second interpretation focuses on the normative content of sports experience. Team sports in particular emphasize coordination, role specialization, and a focus on group goals, which might be expected to produce more prosocial intuitions. Yet in the present data team sport participants did not show an amplified intuition-for-cooperation effect; instead they showed a muted difference relative to non-athletes. This pattern may reflect that sport socialization teaches players context-specific norms that do not generalize uniformly to an abstract public goods game. For example, athletes might learn to cooperate within team contexts and to compete across teams, leading to more complex, context-contingent intuitions that do not translate into a straightforward increase in anonymous cooperation. In addition, athletes might be trained to adopt strategic heuristics—quick rules of thumb that optimize outcomes in sport settings—which could yield responses to anonymous economic games that are less prosocial or at least not more prosocial under time pressure.

A third possible mechanism involves the role of competitive orientation and selection effects. Individuals who self-select into sports, especially competitive sports, may differ on personality dimensions such as competitiveness, risk preferences, or social value orientation. Such preexisting differences could influence both the decision strategies athletes employ in sport and their pattern of responses in laboratory games. Although we controlled for simple demographics in our regression analyses, we did not measure personality traits or competitive orientation directly. Therefore, some of the observed moderation might reflect selection rather than training effects.

The lack of robust differences across sport subtypes in our sample is noteworthy. We had speculated that team sports might produce a stronger move toward intuitive cooperation, whereas individual sports might encourage quick self-reliant strategies; however, the data did not show large or statistically reliable contrasts between these subgroups. This absence of clear subtype effects suggests that the critical factor may be participation in sport broadly rather than the social structure of the sport. Alternatively, the null contrasts could reflect limited statistical power in the subgroup comparisons, imprecision in self-reported sport classification, or heterogeneity within categories (for example, some team sports may be more cooperative than others). Future work with larger samples and more fine-grained measurement of sport experience could disentangle these possibilities.

The present findings also bear on the interpretation of the intuition-for-cooperation hypothesis more broadly. The original claim that “intuition favors cooperation” gains nuance when one considers individual differences in experience. If life experience such as sport participation can shape the content of intuition, then the causal pathway from cognitive mode to decision content is more complex than a simple mapping from speed to prosociality. The intuition-for-cooperation effect appears to operate strongly for people whose everyday heuristics do not explicitly incorporate the strategic considerations common in sports contexts. For people whose intuition may already incorporate such strategic considerations, time pressure may simply reveal preexisting strategies rather than elicit a raw, evolutionarily-shaped prosocial impulse.

These conclusions suggest several directions for future research. First, longitudinal or training studies could clarify the causal direction between sports participation and decision habits. For example, a study that recruits non-athletes and randomly assigns them to engage in structured sport training versus a control activity would test whether sports experience causes an attenuation of the intuition-for-cooperation effect. Second, future research should measure and manipulate specific facets of sport experience, including competitive level, hours of practice, position played, and the emphasis on cooperation versus individual performance in training. Such fine-grained measurement would illuminate which aspects of sport participation are most relevant to the shaping of intuitive decision strategies. Third, it would be valuable to examine other domains of real-world rapid decision-making, such as musical performance, emergency services, or video gaming, to explore whether the moderation we observed is unique to sport or generalizes to other training contexts that emphasize fast, socially situated decisions.

Methodologically, future studies could improve on the present design by larger samples to provide greater power for subgroup comparisons and by including measures of personality, social value orientation, and competitiveness as covariates. Including qualitative or process-tracing measures might also reveal how athletes interpret the public goods task and whether they apply sport-derived heuristics when making decisions. Neurocognitive or physiological measures collected during decision-making could help to identify whether athletes’ neural and autonomic systems respond differently to time pressure in social dilemmas.

Several limitations of the current study warrant attention. First, sport participation was self-reported and measured with a single item that categorized participants into broad groups. This coarse measurement likely masks substantial heterogeneity in experience, frequency, intensity, and competitive level. Second, the study was cross-sectional and therefore cannot establish causality between sport participation and the attenuation of the intuition-for-cooperation effect. Third, the sample size, while sufficient to detect the main effect and the interaction in this study, was modest for detecting differences among the three sport subgroups; thus subgroup null findings should be treated cautiously. Fourth, the online nature of the task limits our ability to verify participants’ engagement with the sports they reported. Fifth, although we used standard timing manipulations and attention checks, there remains the possibility that participants interpreted the task or the instructions differently depending on background experiences, which could contribute to the observed moderation.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides novel evidence that sport participation moderates the relationship between decision mode and cooperative behavior in a one-shot public goods game. The results indicate that the intuition-for-cooperation effect is not uniform across all individuals and that real-world experience with rapid, socially embedded decision-making can shape how people respond under time pressure. This finding opens up a fruitful line of inquiry into how training and experience in specific life domains influence the content of intuitive responses and thereby shape social behavior in experimental contexts.

In practical terms, the study suggests that interventions aimed at promoting cooperation by tapping intuition may be more effective among people without substantial prior experience making similar rapid social decisions. Conversely, for individuals whose intuition has been shaped by experience to align with different behavioral goals, interventions may need to target the content of heuristics rather than decision speed. For policy and applied settings, these insights caution against one-size-fits-all appeals to intuition as a mechanism for encouraging prosocial behavior.

In conclusion, the current research offers a meaningful extension to the literature on intuition and cooperation by showing that participation in sport is associated with a reduced divergence between intuitive and reflective decisions in a public goods task. The findings underscore the importance of considering individual differences and life-history variables when evaluating cognitive models of social decision-making. Further research that leverages longitudinal designs, richer measures of experience, and broader samples will help to clarify the mechanisms by which sport and other forms of domain-specific practice shape the intuitions that guide social behavior.
