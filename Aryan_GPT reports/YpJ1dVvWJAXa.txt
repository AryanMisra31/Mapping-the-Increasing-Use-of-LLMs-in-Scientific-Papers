Introduction

Mental health problems are common among university students and represent a growing concern for higher education institutions. Many students experience symptoms of anxiety, depression, and stress during their undergraduate years. The transition to university life often coincides with a peak period for the onset of several mental health conditions. University students face unique stressors such as academic pressure, moving away from home, financial concerns, and changes in social support networks. These stressors can combine with pre-existing vulnerabilities to increase the likelihood of mental health difficulties. At the same time, help-seeking behavior for mental health problems among students is frequently low. A substantial gap exists between the number of students experiencing mental health difficulties and the number who access professional support. Several barriers to help seeking have been identified, including stigma, limited mental health literacy, perceived self-reliance, and concerns about confidentiality. Social support has been recognized as a key factor that can facilitate coping and encourage help seeking. Students with stronger perceived social support are more likely to disclose difficulties and to reach out to friends, family, or professionals. Conversely, poor social support is associated with reduced likelihood of seeking help. Self-concept and cultural or personal beliefs about independence can also shape decisions to seek help. Individuals with a strong independent self-conception may feel that they should deal with problems on their own and therefore may be less inclined to seek external support. These psychological and social factors interact in complex ways to shape help-seeking intentions and behaviors. Educational exposure to psychological concepts could plausibly influence these mechanisms. Students studying psychology gain knowledge about mental health, therapeutic approaches, and the importance of help seeking as part of their curriculum. This knowledge could translate into greater mental health literacy, reduced stigma, and increased willingness to access support. For this reason, study discipline has been proposed as a potential contributor to differences in help-seeking intentions among students. In particular, psychology undergraduates might be expected to show higher intentions to seek help due to their training and increased familiarity with mental health issues. At the same time, there are reasons to expect that such differences may not emerge. Knowledge acquired in academic settings does not always translate into personal behavior or attitudes, and students studying psychology may also experience unique pressures, such as perceived expectations to manage their own mental health. Moreover, familiarity with clinical concepts might paradoxically increase self-diagnosis and either encourage or inhibit formal help seeking depending on individual responses. Non-psychology students, by contrast, represent a heterogeneous group with varying levels of exposure to mental health education, but they may receive curriculum-based support in other disciplines or have access to the same university-wide wellbeing resources. The literature investigating differences in help-seeking intentions by academic discipline is mixed. Some accounts suggest greater openness among psychology students, while others report no clear advantage for those with psychology training. Methodological differences between studies, such as small sample sizes, variability in measures used, and differences in the operationalization of help seeking, complicate the formation of firm conclusions. Common instruments used to assess help-seeking intentions include vignette-based questionnaires that simulate a specific problem scenario and ask participants about their likelihood of seeking different types of help. The General Help Seeking Questionnaire and vignette adaptations are widely used to capture preferences for professional and non-professional sources of support. These instruments typically differentiate between formal help sources, such as general practitioners and mental health professionals, and informal sources like friends and family. They also allow calculation of composite scores that reflect overall help-seeking intentions. In addition to situational measures, self-report instruments assessing perceived social support and self-construal provide a broader context for understanding help-seeking propensity. Perceived social support measures capture the extent to which individuals believe they have reliable emotional and instrumental assistance, while self-construal scales indicate the degree to which people define themselves in terms of autonomy versus relational interdependence. Together, these constructs can help explain why some students are more willing to seek help than others. From a practical standpoint, understanding whether students from different degree disciplines differ in their intentions to seek help is important for designing targeted interventions. If psychology students are significantly more likely to seek help, then interventions might need to be prioritized for non-psychology cohorts. Conversely, if there is no difference, that would suggest the need for broad-based interventions across disciplines. Given that universities often allocate resources for mental health promotion and that cost-effective targeting of these resources is desirable, empirical evidence on discipline-related differences can inform policy and practice. The current experiment was designed to address these questions by comparing the help-seeking intentions of undergraduate psychology students with those of non-psychology undergraduates. The design used an adapted vignette version of a commonly used help-seeking instrument to measure intentions to seek professional support, intentions to seek non-professional support, and an overall help-seeking score that combined professional and non-professional sources. The sample comprised students from universities in the United Kingdom, reflecting a typical undergraduate population in terms of age and stage of study. In addition to the primary comparison by degree course, the study assessed perceived social support and independent self-construal as supplementary variables. This decision was motivated by theoretical accounts that emphasize the role of social networks and self-identity in decisions to seek help. The primary hypothesis was that psychology students would report higher intentions to seek professional help than non-psychology students. A secondary hypothesis was that psychology students would report higher overall help-seeking intentions. It was anticipated that social support would be positively correlated with non-professional help-seeking intentions. It was also anticipated that stronger independent self-conceptions would be associated with lower intentions to seek help. The study was designed to provide a clear test of the role of degree course in help-seeking intentions while controlling for relevant social and self-related factors. The use of a vignette-based measure was intended to anchor responses to a concrete situation and reduce variability due to hypothetical interpretation. The instrument was adapted to include a comprehensive list of both professional and non-professional help sources to allow calculation of distinct subscales. The inclusion of both psychology and non-psychology students of similar undergraduate levels sought to minimize confounding due to year of study or age. Ethical approval was obtained from a university ethics committee prior to data collection. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and all participants provided informed consent. The recruitment strategy targeted a balanced sample size between the two groups to facilitate comparisons using independent samples t-tests. The analytic approach included checks of statistical assumptions and exploratory correlational analyses to contextualize any differences or similarities observed between groups. The study therefore aimed to contribute to the evidence base on whether degree course is associated with intentions to seek help for mental health problems among undergraduates. The results have potential implications for the design and targeting of mental health support and educational interventions in universities. By assessing both professional and informal help-seeking intentions and including measures of social support and self-construal, the study sought to provide a nuanced view of the factors that may shape students’ likelihood of reaching out when they are distressed. The remainder of this article describes the methods, reports the results, and discusses the implications for practice and future research.

Method

Participants were 60 undergraduate students recruited from universities in the United Kingdom. Thirty participants were enrolled in undergraduate psychology programmes and thirty participants were enrolled in undergraduate programmes in non-psychology disciplines. Participants were recruited via online study recruitment platforms and departmental noticeboards. Recruitment materials invited students to take part in a short online survey about reactions to hypothetical scenarios and attitudes toward seeking help. Inclusion criteria required participants to be aged 18 years or older and to be currently enrolled as an undergraduate student. Exclusion criteria included inability to understand English sufficiently to complete the questionnaire and prior formal training in counseling or psychotherapy. Participants who reported current professional qualifications in mental health were excluded to avoid confounding effects. The final sample comprised thirty psychology students and thirty non-psychology students. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 24 years. The mean age of psychology students was 20.1 years and the mean age of non-psychology students was 20.4 years. The sample included participants of diverse gender identities, with 36 identifying as female, 22 identifying as male, and 2 identifying as non-binary. Gender distributions were similar across the two groups. Participants self-reported ethnicity, and the sample reflected a range of ethnic backgrounds typical of UK universities. All participants received a small monetary voucher as reimbursement for their time. The study was conducted online using a secure survey platform. After providing informed consent, participants completed a brief demographics section and then read a standardized vignette describing a hypothetical situation in which a student experienced persistent and distressing psychological symptoms. The vignette depicted a plausible scenario of elevated anxiety, sleep disruption, reduced academic functioning, and feelings of isolation over several weeks. The scenario was deliberately neutral with respect to specific diagnostic labels to focus on intentions rather than clinical knowledge. After reading the vignette, participants completed an adapted version of a vignette-based general help-seeking questionnaire. The instrument listed a range of potential help sources and asked participants to indicate how likely they would be to seek help from each source if they were experiencing the problems described in the vignette. The list contained eight sources classified as professional and eight sources classified as non-professional. Professional sources included general practitioner, university counseling service, clinical psychologist, psychiatrist, occupational health service, campus nurse, private therapist, and telephone/online mental health service. Non-professional sources included partner, parent, friend, roommate, university student rep, religious leader, informal peer support group, and online peer forum. For each source participants rated their likelihood of seeking help on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very unlikely) to 7 (very likely). The adapted instrument allowed computation of a professional help-seeking subscale, a non-professional help-seeking subscale, and an overall help-seeking score that averaged responses across all listed sources. The professional subscale score ranged theoretically from 1 to 7 and the non-professional subscale had the same range. The overall help-seeking score represented the mean across all sources. The adapted questionnaire included brief instructions to anchor responses to the scenario described and emphasized personal likelihood rather than perceived social norms. Two attention-check items were embedded in the questionnaire to ensure participants were reading the vignette and items carefully. Participants who failed either attention check were excluded from the analysis. In addition to the help-seeking instrument, participants completed a brief perceived social support measure. This measure asked participants to rate the extent to which they felt they had supportive people they could rely on in times of need. Ratings were on a 7-point scale and produced a mean social support score. A self-construal measure was also included to assess the degree of independent self-conception. The instrument contained items reflecting autonomy, self-reliance, and preference for independent problem solving. Responses were recorded on a 7-point scale and averaged to produce an independent self-construal score. All measures were presented in the same online session and the order of the help-seeking questionnaire and the supplementary measures was randomized across participants to minimize order effects. The entire survey took approximately 15 to 20 minutes to complete. Data collection took place over a period of four weeks during the academic term. The study received ethical approval from the sponsoring university’s research ethics committee and complied with data protection regulations. After data collection, responses were screened for completeness and for implausible response patterns. Two participants were removed for failing attention checks and one participant was removed for incomplete data, leaving 60 valid cases. Descriptive statistics were computed for demographic and scale variables. Group equivalence on age and gender was assessed using independent t-tests and chi-square tests respectively. The primary analyses compared the psychology and non-psychology groups on professional help-seeking intentions, non-professional help-seeking intentions, and overall help-seeking intentions. Independent samples t-tests were specified for each comparison. Prior to conducting t-tests, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed. Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to examine the distribution of scores for each group and each dependent variable. Levene’s tests were used to evaluate equality of variances between groups. Where assumptions were met, standard t-tests were reported. For completeness, non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests were also computed when any assumption was marginal. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d to quantify the magnitude of observed differences. Two-tailed tests were used throughout and alpha was set at .05. In addition to the primary between-group comparisons, exploratory analyses examined correlations between perceived social support, independent self-construal, and the help-seeking subscales. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed when the variables met distributional assumptions and Spearman correlations were computed otherwise. Finally, hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted as exploratory tests to see whether social support and self-construal accounted for variance in help-seeking intentions beyond the contribution of degree course. Degree course was entered as a dummy variable in the first step and social support and self-construal were entered in the second step. The change in R-squared and the statistical significance of individual predictors were examined. All analyses were conducted in a standard statistical package and diagnostic plots were inspected for influential observations and violations of assumptions. Results were reported with means, standard deviations, test statistics, degrees of freedom, p-values, confidence intervals where appropriate, and effect size estimates for clarity.

Results

Descriptive statistics indicated that the two groups were broadly similar on demographic characteristics. The mean age of the combined sample was 20.3 years. The psychology group had a mean age of 20.1 years and the non-psychology group had a mean age of 20.4 years. An independent samples t-test showed that the age difference between groups was not statistically significant. Gender distribution did not differ significantly between the two groups. The majority of participants identified as female in both groups. Ethnic composition of the sample reflected a range of backgrounds and did not differ markedly between groups. There were no significant baseline differences between groups on the supplementary measures of social support and independent self-construal. The mean perceived social support score for the psychology group was 4.11 on the 7-point scale. The mean perceived social support score for the non-psychology group was 3.98. An independent samples t-test indicated that this difference was not statistically significant. The mean independent self-construal score for the psychology group was 4.28. The mean independent self-construal score for the non-psychology group was 4.35. There was no significant difference between groups on this measure. These equivalence checks suggested that any observed differences in help-seeking intentions were unlikely to be attributable to systematic group differences on basic demographic or supplementary variables. Turning to the primary dependent variables, the professional help-seeking subscale had acceptable internal consistency across the sample. The Cronbach’s alpha for the professional items was within an acceptable range, indicating coherence of the subscale. The non-professional subscale also demonstrated acceptable internal consistency. The overall help-seeking score, being a composite of the two subscales, demonstrated a reliability coefficient consistent with the constituent parts. The distributional checks prior to hypothesis testing showed that the professional help-seeking scores for both groups did not deviate substantially from normality according to Shapiro-Wilk tests. Levene’s test for equality of variances on the professional subscale was non-significant, indicating that the assumption of homogeneity of variance was met. An independent samples t-test comparing psychology and non-psychology students on professional help-seeking intentions yielded a non-significant difference. The mean professional help-seeking score for the psychology group was 3.87 with a standard deviation of 1.12. The non-psychology group had a mean of 3.54 with a standard deviation of 1.21. The t-test produced t(58) = 1.23 and a two-tailed p-value of .22. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference ranged from -0.28 to 0.88. The observed Cohen’s d for this comparison was 0.32, indicating a small effect size. These results suggested that psychology students did not differ significantly from non-psychology students in their reported likelihood of seeking professional help in response to the vignette. For the non-professional help-seeking subscale, assumption checks also supported the use of the t-test. Shapiro-Wilk tests were non-significant for each group on this variable and Levene’s test indicated equal variances. The mean non-professional help-seeking score for psychology students was 4.62 with a standard deviation of 1.05. The mean score for the non-psychology group was 4.58 with a standard deviation of 1.11. The independent samples t-test produced t(58) = 0.18 and a two-tailed p-value of .86. The 95% confidence interval for the difference between means ranged from -0.50 to 0.58. The Cohen’s d for this comparison was 0.05, indicating a negligible effect size. This pattern indicated near-identical self-reported likelihoods of seeking help from informal sources such as friends or family across the two groups. The overall help-seeking score produced similar findings. The psychology group’s mean overall score was 4.25 with a standard deviation of 0.92. The non-psychology group’s mean overall score was 4.06 with a standard deviation of 0.98. The t-test comparing overall help-seeking intentions yielded t(58) = 0.68 and a two-tailed p-value of .50. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference included zero, ranging approximately from -0.37 to 0.73. The Cohen’s d for overall help seeking was 0.17, again indicating a small effect size. The non-significant findings across professional, non-professional, and overall help-seeking measures suggested that enrollment in an undergraduate psychology programme was not associated with higher self-reported intentions to seek help in the hypothetical vignette scenario compared to enrollment in non-psychology programmes. As a robustness check, non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests were computed for the primary comparisons. These non-parametric tests produced p-values consistent with the t-test results and did not alter the interpretation of non-significant group differences. In order to explore the role of perceived social support and independent self-construal, correlations were computed between these variables and the help-seeking subscales. Across the combined sample, perceived social support correlated positively with non-professional help-seeking intentions. The Pearson correlation coefficient was r = .35, and this association was statistically significant with a p-value of .007. This result indicated that participants who perceived greater social support were more likely to report that they would seek help from friends, family, or other informal sources in the vignette. Perceived social support also correlated positively with overall help-seeking intentions, although this association was smaller and approached but did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance, with r = .23 and p = .07. The correlation between perceived social support and professional help-seeking intentions was smaller and not statistically significant, with r = .20 and p = .13. Independent self-construal showed a modest negative correlation with non-professional help-seeking intentions across the sample. The Pearson correlation was r = -.28 and the p-value was .03, indicating a statistically significant association. This pattern suggested that participants with a stronger independent self-conception were less likely to indicate that they would seek help from informal sources. Independent self-construal was negatively correlated with overall help-seeking intentions at a similar magnitude but the result did not reach statistical significance, with r = -.18 and p = .16. The correlation between independent self-construal and professional help seeking was weak and non-significant. To further probe the predictors of help-seeking intentions, hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted. For professional help seeking as the dependent variable, degree course entered in the first step did not account for a significant portion of variance. Adding perceived social support and independent self-construal in the second step produced a small increase in explained variance but neither predictor reached conventional significance in the model predicting professional help seeking. The final model for professional help seeking explained a modest proportion of variance and did not yield robust predictors in this sample. When non-professional help seeking was the dependent variable, degree course again failed to contribute significantly. However, adding perceived social support and independent self-construal in the second step produced a statistically significant change in R-squared. Within that model, perceived social support emerged as a significant positive predictor and independent self-construal emerged as a significant negative predictor. This pattern was consistent with the bivariate correlation results. The final model predicting non-professional help seeking accounted for a modest but meaningful proportion of the variance in intentions. Post-hoc power considerations suggested that the sample size of 30 participants per group provided adequate power to detect medium-sized effects but limited power for small effects. Using conventional thresholds, the study had approximately seventy percent power to detect an effect size of around d = 0.5. Consequently, small differences between groups may not have been detected in this sample. Taken together, the results indicated that psychology and non-psychology undergraduates reported similar intentions to seek help for the vignette scenario across professional, non-professional, and overall indices. The supplementary analyses highlighted the importance of perceived social support and independent self-construal as correlates of informal help-seeking intentions but did not show that degree course influenced these associations.

Discussion

The present study examined whether undergraduate students studying psychology differ from their peers in other disciplines in their intentions to seek help for mental health problems as described in a vignette. The primary finding was that no statistically significant differences emerged between psychology and non-psychology students on professional, non-professional, or overall help-seeking intentions. This pattern was consistent across parametric and non-parametric tests and was supported by effect size estimates that were small or negligible. The absence of a significant advantage in help-seeking intentions among psychology students is notable given theoretical expectations that familiarity with mental health concepts might translate into greater willingness to access support. One interpretation of the null result is that academic exposure to psychology does not automatically translate into personal behavioral intentions regarding help seeking. Knowledge and understanding of psychological problems, while potentially increasing mental health literacy, may not overcome other barriers such as stigma or concerns about self-reliance. Another interpretation is that psychology students may acquire clinical knowledge but simultaneously develop stronger evaluative standards for what constitutes an appropriate reason to seek help, which might dampen self-perceived need to access professional services. A third possibility is that the curriculum-based knowledge effects are offset by other factors that are common across student groups, such as perceived pressures, confidentiality concerns, or resource constraints. The correlational findings offer additional insight into the social and identity-related factors associated with help-seeking intentions. Perceived social support was positively associated with non-professional help-seeking intentions, suggesting that students who feel supported are more likely to turn to friends, family, or peers when distressed. This finding is consistent with theoretical accounts that emphasize the enabling role of supportive networks in facilitating disclosure and informal help seeking. Independent self-construal was negatively associated with non-professional help-seeking intentions, indicating that students who emphasize personal autonomy and self-reliance may be less inclined to seek help from informal sources. The pattern of associations was stronger for non-professional help seeking than for professional help seeking. This difference suggests that social support and self-identity factors may play a relatively larger role in decisions to consult friends or family than in decisions to seek professional assistance. The regression analyses further indicated that social support and independent self-construal together accounted for a modest amount of variance in non-professional help-seeking intentions beyond the effect of degree course. These findings imply that intervention strategies designed to increase help seeking might usefully focus on enhancing perceived social support and addressing norms around self-reliance. The lack of differences between psychology and non-psychology students has implications for the targeting of mental health promotion in universities. If discipline-based variation in help-seeking intentions is minimal, then university-wide interventions may be more appropriate than discipline-specific programmes. Universal programmes aimed at improving mental health literacy, reducing stigma, and increasing awareness of services could benefit the student body as a whole. In addition, interventions that strengthen social connectedness and encourage supportive peer engagement might be particularly effective in increasing informal help seeking. The current results caution against assuming that students enrolled in psychology programmes are inherently more likely to seek help. The curriculum alone may not be sufficient to change personal help-seeking intentions, and educational content might need to be supplemented by experiential or skills-based components that explicitly address help-seeking behavior. It is important to consider limitations when interpreting these findings. The sample size, although comparable to many experimental studies in psychology, was modest and limited the power to detect small effects. A larger sample may have detected subtle differences between groups if they exist. The cross-sectional and self-report nature of the data restricts causal inference and raises the possibility of response biases. The vignette-based approach, while useful for anchoring responses to a standard scenario, may not fully capture the complexity of real-world situations in which students decide to seek help. Individuals’ stated intentions can diverge from actual behavior, particularly in the presence of situational barriers such as appointment availability, financial constraints, or acute crises. The adapted help-seeking measure used in the study was assembled to provide professional and non-professional subscales, and while internal consistency was acceptable, further validation work would enhance confidence in its psychometric properties. The sample was drawn from UK universities and may not generalize to students in other cultural contexts where attitudes toward help seeking and norms of independence differ. Additionally, the non-psychology group was heterogeneous with respect to disciplines and curricular content, and this heterogeneity may have obscured subtle differences that could exist between psychology students and particular non-psychology fields. Future research could address these limitations by recruiting larger and more diverse samples and by including behavioral indices of help seeking such as service utilization records or longitudinal follow-up to assess whether intentions predict subsequent behavior. Qualitative methods could be used to explore the nuanced reasons why students, including those studying psychology, may not seek help despite knowledge of mental health issues. Such qualitative work could identify barriers such as fears about judgment, concerns about career implications, or uncertainty about the effectiveness of services. Experimental or intervention studies could test whether targeted educational modules that include personal narratives, role-playing, or facilitated reflections on help seeking produce changes in intentions and behavior. It would also be valuable to examine whether integrating help-seeking skills into psychology curricula alters students’ own likelihood of accessing support. The current study’s finding that perceived social support and independent self-construal are associated primarily with informal help-seeking intentions suggests that interventions aiming to foster supportive networks may be particularly fruitful. Universities could consider developing structured peer-support programmes, mentoring schemes, and social initiatives that reduce isolation and create safe opportunities for disclosure. Training peers to respond appropriately and to signpost professional services might improve help-seeking pathways. Policy implications extend to the allocation of resources within universities. Since psychology students did not show elevated help-seeking intentions relative to their peers, mental health services and promotional efforts should not be restricted based on discipline. Rather, institutions should adopt inclusive approaches that address common barriers across the student population. Another implication is that increasing availability of professional services may not in itself be sufficient to increase utilization. Service promotion should be paired with efforts to enhance perceived accessibility, confidentiality, and acceptability of professional help. The role of independent self-construal in inhibiting informal help seeking highlights the need to address cultural and identity-based norms that valorize self-reliance. Interventions might include reflective exercises that normalize the act of seeking help and reframe it as a form of strength rather than weakness. Campaigns that use messages congruent with diverse cultural conceptions of the self could be more effective at reaching students who value independence. The theoretical contribution of the study lies in its demonstration that academic exposure to psychology does not necessarily lead to increased intentions to seek help. This finding refines assumptions about the protective role of mental health knowledge and suggests that cognitive understanding needs to be coupled with social and identity-related interventions to influence help-seeking behavior. The observed links between social support and informal help seeking support models that position social networks as central facilitators of early help seeking. Future work should examine the dynamic interplay between knowledge, social context, self-identity, and help-seeking behaviors across time. Longitudinal studies could clarify whether help-seeking intentions change as students progress through their degrees and whether exposure to curricular or extra-curricular mental health initiatives modifies these trajectories. Studies that compare students across different cultural contexts or educational systems could illuminate how macro-level factors shape the relationships documented here. In conclusion, the present study found no evidence that undergraduate psychology students have greater intentions to seek professional or informal help for distressing symptoms than their non-psychology peers. The results underscore the importance of broad-based, campus-wide approaches to mental health promotion and highlight perceived social support and independent self-construal as important correlates of informal help seeking. Addressing the multifaceted barriers to help seeking will likely require integrated interventions that combine mental health education, stigma reduction, social support enhancement, and culturally sensitive messaging. Future research that is larger, longitudinal, and mixed-methods in design will help to refine these recommendations and guide effective policy and practice in higher education settings.
