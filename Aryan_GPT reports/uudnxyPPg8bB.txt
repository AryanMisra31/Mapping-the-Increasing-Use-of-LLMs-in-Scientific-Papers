Introduction

Human memory is not a unitary faculty but a set of interrelated processes that change across the lifespan. Episodic memory, or the ability to remember specific events and the context in which they occurred, declines with age in many, though not all, domains. One stable finding in cognitive aging research is that older adults often show more difficulty forming and retrieving associations between separate elements of an event than they do recalling individual items. This pattern has been articulated as the associative deficit hypothesis, which posits that aging is associated with a selective decline in the ability to bind or associate together discrete pieces of information into coherent episodic representations. The associative deficit hypothesis has received support across a range of experimental paradigms, including paired-associate learning, face-name binding tasks, and complex scene memory tasks. Researchers have argued that associative processes impose additional demands on hippocampal-dependent binding mechanisms and on the coordination of prefrontal cortical systems that support strategic encoding and retrieval. These additional demands make associative memory particularly vulnerable to age-related neurobiological change.

Although the associative deficit hypothesis has been influential and supported by many studies, there is variability in the magnitude of the deficit across samples and tasks. Some of this variability may be attributable to individual differences in brain organization and in factors that influence interhemispheric communication. One such individual difference is handedness. Handedness is a behavioral index of lateralization of brain function, and it is commonly assessed on a continuum from strongly right-handed to strongly left-handed. Left-handed and mixed-handed individuals have been reported to show less lateralized brain organization for language and other cognitive functions, and to exhibit greater interhemispheric connectivity on some neurophysiological measures. A growing body of literature has linked reduced lateralization or greater bilateral activation to cognitive performance advantages in certain tasks, particularly when compensatory bilateral recruitment can support processing in the presence of localized decline.

A specific line of work has explored associations between handedness and memory performance. Several studies have found that people who are mixed-handed or left-handed sometimes show enhanced episodic memory performance relative to strongly right-handed individuals. The proposed mechanism is that greater interhemispheric communication in mixed- or left-handed individuals facilitates the integration of distributed representations, which may support some forms of mnemonic processing. This proposal suggests an intriguing possibility: if handedness reflects differences in the degree of functional lateralization and interhemispheric connectivity, then mixed- or left-handed older adults might be relatively protected against age-related associative deficits. In other words, handedness could be a moderator of the associative deficit, such that older mixed- or left-handed people would show a smaller age-related decrement in associative memory performance than strongly right-handed older people.

Empirical tests of this moderating hypothesis are limited. Some studies that have examined handedness and memory have focused on younger samples or on overall memory rather than on dissociations between item and associative memory. Other studies have used small samples or have not directly tested interactions among age, handedness, and memory type. The theoretical rationale for expecting handedness to moderate the associative deficit rests on several assumptions that bear scrutiny. First, it assumes that mixed- or left-handedness is associated with neural and cognitive organization that facilitates the binding of information. Second, it assumes that the mechanisms that produce age-related associative deficits are at least partly amenable to compensation by alternative functional configurations, such as enhanced interhemispheric transfer. Third, it assumes that the behavioral measures used to assess associative memory are sensitive to the hypothesized protective effects.

There are reasons to be cautious about the expectation that handedness would attenuate the associative deficit. Age-related changes in memory have been linked to structural and functional decline in the medial temporal lobes, reductions in white matter integrity in long-range tracts that support hippocampal-prefrontal coordination, and alterations in neurotransmitter systems. These widespread biological changes may not be readily offset by differences in lateralization that manifest behaviorally as handedness. In addition, handedness itself is an imperfect proxy for underlying neuroanatomical features; individuals classified as mixed-handed on the basis of self-report may show heterogeneous neural organization. Nonetheless, handedness remains an accessible behavioral marker that has been associated with cognitive outcomes and that could plausibly relate to associative memory performance in older adults.

Given the gaps and ambiguities in the literature, it is valuable to examine the interaction of age, handedness, and memory type in a controlled experimental design that directly tests whether handedness moderates the age-related associative deficit. Such a test requires a sample including both younger and older adults, reliable measurement of handedness that allows for categorization into strongly right-handed and mixed/left-handed groups, and a memory task that provides separate measures of item memory (recognition or recall of single items) and associative memory (memory for pairings among items). An experimental design that includes age and handedness as between-subjects factors and memory type as a within-subjects factor allows for a direct test of whether age-related differences are disproportionately larger on associative than item memory and whether handedness moderates this pattern.

In this study, we implemented a 2 (age: younger, older) x 2 (handedness: strongly right-handed, mixed/left-handed) x 2 (memory type: item, associative) mixed design. We selected word lists and paired-associate materials that are standard in the aging literature and that have been shown previously to produce reliable associative deficits. Handedness was assessed with a standardized inventory and scores were used to classify participants into groups reflecting strongly right-handed and mixed/left-handed profiles. We matched groups on basic demographic variables as far as possible and screened older adults for cognitive impairment to ensure that age differences reflect normative aging rather than pathological decline.

Based on prior research and theoretical considerations, we had two primary predictions. First, we predicted an age by memory type interaction reflecting the associative deficit, with older adults showing disproportionately impaired associative memory compared to item memory relative to younger adults. Second, we predicted that handedness would moderate this interaction, such that mixed/left-handed older adults would show a reduced associative deficit relative to strongly right-handed older adults. We also explored main effects of handedness and potential age by handedness interactions on item memory, recognizing that prior literature has reported episodic memory advantages for mixed/left-handed individuals in some contexts.

The present study makes three contributions. It directly tests whether handedness moderates the associative deficit in a single dataset that includes both younger and older adults. It uses standardized memory materials and a well-validated handedness inventory to operationalize the key constructs. Finally, the mixed design allows for powerful within-subjects comparisons of item and associative memory and for tests of higher-order interactions. If handedness were to moderate the associative deficit, it would have implications both for our understanding of individual differences in cognitive aging and for models that link lateralization and interhemispheric communication to mnemonic processes. If handedness does not moderate associative decline, the findings would constrain accounts that attribute protective effects to lateralization and would point to the need to identify other factors that support associative binding in aging.

In addition to the primary tests, we conducted several auxiliary analyses. We examined the effect of treating handedness as a continuous predictor to assess whether a graded relationship between handedness score and associative performance exists. We also controlled for education and sex in supplementary analyses to evaluate potential confounds. To assess the robustness of null findings, particularly for the moderation hypothesis, we computed Bayes factors alongside frequentist tests. Finally, we examined response times and response bias metrics as exploratory measures that could provide insight into strategic differences across groups.

The overall logic of the study is straightforward: by comparing item and associative performance across age and handedness groups in a within-subjects memory design, we can determine whether the associative deficit is present and whether it is differentially expressed in older adults who vary in handedness. This approach leverages the strengths of mixed designs in terms of statistical power for within-subjects contrasts while retaining the ability to examine between-subjects moderators. The study thus can provide new evidence on whether a simple behavioral marker of lateralization is associated with preserved associative memory in aging.

We emphasize that whether the associative deficit replicates in this sample is itself an important empirical question given variation in task parameters and sample characteristics across studies. Therefore, analysis of the primary age by memory interaction is a critical test. The moderation hypothesis is more speculative but motivated by converging evidence linking handedness, lateralization, and memory. If supported, it would suggest that interindividual differences in hemispheric organization relate to the integrity of binding processes in aging. If not supported, it would indicate that associative decline in older adults is relatively invariant to handedness as measured behaviorally.

In sum, the present study sought to replicate the associative deficit effect and to test whether mixed or left-handedness buffers older adults against associative decline. The study used established materials and procedures, included younger and older participants screened for cognitive health, applied both categorical and continuous approaches to handedness, and employed a pre-registered analytic plan that included both frequentist and Bayesian methods. The analyses reported below focus first on descriptive statistics and group comparisons, then on the critical mixed-model ANOVA tests, and finally on a set of supplementary analyses aimed at probing the robustness and scope of the findings. Together, these analyses provide a comprehensive assessment of whether handedness moderates age-related associative memory decline.

Methods

Participants were 120 community-dwelling volunteers recruited through university subject pools, community centers, and online advertisements. Participants were allocated to four groups based on age and handedness: younger strongly right-handed, younger mixed/left-handed, older strongly right-handed, and older mixed/left-handed. Each group comprised 30 participants for a total sample size of 120. The younger group ranged in age from 18 to 30 years, and the older group ranged from 65 to 80 years. Participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and were fluent in English.

We screened older participants using the Mini-Mental State Examination to exclude potential cases of cognitive impairment. All older participants scored 27 or above on the MMSE, indicating no evidence of dementia. Participants provided informed consent and were compensated with a modest payment or course credit. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board.

Handedness was assessed with the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory, a widely used self-report measure that yields a laterality quotient from -100 (strongly left-handed) to +100 (strongly right-handed). For categorical grouping, we classified participants as strongly right-handed if they scored +80 or higher on the laterality quotient. Participants scoring +79 or below were classified as mixed/left-handed for the purposes of the between-subjects factor. This cutoff was chosen to capture a conservative definition of strong right-handedness while grouping mixed-handed and left-handed participants together into a broader non-strong-right category. In supplemental analyses we treated the laterality quotient as a continuous predictor to test for graded relationships.

Educational attainment and self-reported health were recorded as potential covariates. Demographic variables including sex, years of education, and reported use of reading glasses were collected. We attempted to match age groups on years of education and sex distribution but allowed some variability that we controlled for in secondary analyses.

Materials consisted of word lists drawn from standardized norms for concreteness and frequency. Two sets of 48 words each were used and were matched for lexical properties. For associative encoding, we randomly paired words within each list to form 24 word pairs per list. The study task was designed to generate both item memory and associative memory measures within the same experimental session.

The procedure consisted of an encoding phase followed by two test phases. At encoding, participants were presented with single words and word pairs in interleaved trials. Each trial began with a fixation cross for 500 ms, followed by a stimulus presented centrally for 3000 ms. For single-word trials, participants were instructed to decide whether the word referred to a living or nonliving thing and to press a corresponding key. For pair trials, participants were instructed to form a mental association between the two words and to rate the vividness of the imagined association on a 1-4 scale using the keyboard. The interleaving of single-item and pair trials ensured that encoding processes for both item and associative memory occurred in a similar temporal context.

After a 10-minute filled delay during which participants completed unrelated questionnaires, memory was tested in two separate blocks. Item memory was assessed first using a recognition test consisting of 72 trials: the 48 studied words plus 24 novel lures matched for frequency and concreteness. Participants made old/new judgments and indicated confidence on a three-point scale. Associative memory was then assessed using a forced-choice associative recognition test in which studied pairs were presented along with recombined lure pairs. On each trial, participants saw two pairs on the screen and indicated which of the two pairs represented a studied pairing. The order of item and associative tests was held constant across participants to avoid contamination of associative judgments by item retrieval strategies, although additional analyses examined whether order effects might influence outcomes.

Memory scores were computed as proportion correct for item recognition and for associative recognition. For item recognition, we derived corrected recognition (hits minus false alarms) as a primary metric to account for response bias. For associative recognition, proportion correct on forced-choice trials served as the primary outcome. Reaction times for correct responses were recorded as exploratory measures. We also computed signal detection indices (d') for item recognition as a supplementary metric.

Prior to analysis, data were screened for outliers and violations of assumptions. No participant had more than 10% missing data on the key measures. Trials with response times less than 200 ms or exceeding 3 standard deviations above the participant's mean were excluded from reaction time analyses but retained for accuracy analyses. We verified normality of residuals and homogeneity of variance for the primary dependent variables. Mauchly's test of sphericity was not applicable because the within-subjects factor had two levels.

The primary analysis was a mixed-model 2 (age: younger, older) x 2 (handedness: strongly right-handed, mixed/left-handed) x 2 (memory type: item, associative) analysis of variance with memory type as the within-subjects variable and age and handedness as between-subjects variables. The dependent variable was memory performance expressed as proportion correct, with item scores corrected for false alarms. This approach tests the critical interaction between age and memory type that operationalizes the associative deficit and allows for examination of a three-way interaction with handedness.

Supplementary analyses included treating handedness as a continuous predictor in multiple regression frameworks predicting associative-minus-item difference scores. We also ran ANCOVAs controlling for years of education and sex to evaluate potential confounding influences. Where appropriate, effect sizes are reported as partial eta-squared for ANOVA effects and Cohen's d for t-test contrasts. For tests where null effects were of inferential interest, we computed Bayes factors using default priors to quantify the strength of evidence in favor of the null relative to the alternative.

All statistical tests were two-tailed and used an alpha level of .05 as the threshold for significance. The analyses were conducted in standard statistical software. Data and analysis code are archived and available on request to promote transparency and reproducibility.

Results

The sample comprised 120 participants divided evenly across the four age-by-handedness groups, with 30 participants in each cell. The younger group had a mean age of 22.4 years with a standard deviation of 3.1 years. The older group had a mean age of 71.2 years with a standard deviation of 4.3 years. Years of education did not differ significantly across the four groups; the overall mean years of education was 15.8 with a standard deviation of 2.6 years. Sex distribution was approximately balanced across groups, with 63 females and 57 males in the total sample.

Edinburgh Handedness Inventory scores differed as expected between the categorical groups. Strongly right-handed participants had a mean laterality quotient of +92.6 (SD = 5.3). Mixed/left-handed participants had a mean laterality quotient of +11.4 (SD = 55.1), reflecting the heterogeneity within this group that included both left-handed and mixed-handed individuals. As noted in the methods, the categorical cutpoint was +80. There was no significant correlation between laterality quotient and age within our sample.

Descriptive statistics for the primary memory measures are presented next. For item recognition (corrected recognition hits - false alarms), younger strongly right-handed participants had a mean corrected recognition of .62 (SD = .11). Younger mixed/left-handed participants had a mean corrected recognition of .64 (SD = .12). Older strongly right-handed participants had a mean corrected recognition of .48 (SD = .13). Older mixed/left-handed participants had a mean corrected recognition of .50 (SD = .12). For associative recognition (proportion correct on forced-choice associative trials), younger strongly right-handed participants had a mean of .79 (SD = .08). Younger mixed/left-handed participants had a mean of .81 (SD = .07). Older strongly right-handed participants had a mean of .66 (SD = .09). Older mixed/left-handed participants had a mean of .67 (SD = .10).

These descriptive results show an age-related decline on both item and associative measures and suggest that associative memory is lower than item memory, especially in older adults. To test the predicted associative deficit and the moderating role of handedness, we conducted the primary mixed ANOVA with memory type (item, associative) as the within-subjects factor and age and handedness as between-subjects factors.

The omnibus ANOVA produced a significant main effect of age, F(1,116) = 38.76, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .250, indicating that younger participants outperformed older participants overall. There was also a significant main effect of memory type, F(1,116) = 148.02, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .561, reflecting better performance on associative recognition than on corrected item recognition across the sample when scaled in the manner used. Importantly, the age x memory type interaction, which indexes the associative deficit, was marginally significant, F(1,116) = 3.57, p = .061, partial eta-squared = .030. The direction of the interaction was consistent with the associative deficit hypothesis, with older adults showing a relatively larger decrement in associative compared to item memory than younger adults.

The main effect of handedness was not significant, F(1,116) = 1.09, p = .299, partial eta-squared = .009. Critically, the three-way interaction among age, handedness, and memory type was non-significant, F(1,116) = 0.44, p = .509, partial eta-squared = .004, indicating that handedness did not significantly moderate the age-related associative deficit in this sample. The age x handedness interaction on overall memory performance was likewise non-significant, F(1,116) = 0.67, p = .415, partial eta-squared = .006.

Given the marginal nature of the age x memory interaction, we conducted planned simple effects to probe the pattern. For younger participants, item versus associative performance differed modestly but substantially, t(59) = 6.29, p < .001, Cohen's d = 0.81, indicating that associative recognition was higher than item corrected recognition in the younger group with the particular scoring metrics used. For older participants, the difference between associative and item performance was larger, t(59) = 10.44, p < .001, Cohen's d = 1.35. The difference in the magnitude of the within-subjects contrast between older and younger participants reflects the marginal interaction reported above.

We also conducted between-group t-tests that compared younger and older adults separately for item and associative measures. For item memory, younger adults outperformed older adults, t(118) = 6.10, p < .001, Cohen's d = 1.11. For associative memory, the age difference was also significant, t(118) = 7.58, p < .001, Cohen's d = 1.38. The age-related effect sizes were larger for associative memory than for item memory, consistent with an associative deficit, although the formal interaction test only reached marginal significance.

Next, we examined whether handedness had any main or interactive effects when treated as a continuous predictor. We computed an associative deficit index defined as associative proportion correct minus corrected item recognition for each participant and regressed this index on age group (dummy-coded), laterality quotient (centered), and their interaction. The regression showed a significant main effect of age group, b = -0.14, SE = 0.018, t = -7.78, p < .001. The main effect of laterality quotient was not significant, b = 0.0004, SE = 0.0005, t = 0.80, p = .424. The age group by laterality quotient interaction was also non-significant, b = -0.0002, SE = 0.0007, t = -0.29, p = .771. These regression results reinforce the conclusion that, in this sample, handedness score did not predict the associative deficit nor did it interact with age.

Because null effects can reflect insufficient power, we computed Bayes factors for the critical three-way interaction. Using default priors, the Bayes factor in favor of the null over the alternative for the three-way interaction was approximately 4.2, indicating moderate evidence for the null hypothesis relative to the alternative. For the age x memory interaction, the Bayes factor in favor of the alternative over the null was about 1.6, indicating only weak evidence for the associative deficit in this sample.

We examined whether education or sex might obscure a moderation effect of handedness by including these variables as covariates in an ANCOVA. When years of education and sex were included, the three-way interaction remained non-significant, F(1,112) = 0.51, p = .478, partial eta-squared = .005. The age x memory interaction remained marginal, F(1,112) = 3.41, p = .067, partial eta-squared = .030. Thus, controlling for these covariates did not change the substantive pattern.

Exploratory reaction time analyses revealed expected main effects of age, with older adults responding more slowly on correct trials for both item and associative tests, F(1,116) = 55.18, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .322. There were no reliable effects involving handedness on reaction time measures. Response bias analyses on item recognition indicated a more conservative criterion in older adults compared to younger adults, though this did not interact with handedness.

We also inspected the distribution of laterality quotient within the mixed/left-handed group and found substantial heterogeneity, with some participants strongly left-handed and some more ambidextrous. A post hoc analysis restricting the non-strong-right group to only those with laterality quotients <= -60 (i.e., clearly left-handed) reduced the non-strong-right sample size and did not reveal a significant age x memory x handedness interaction, though statistical power was reduced in this restricted analysis.

Finally, we checked for potential outliers at the participant level. No participants had z scores greater than 3.0 on the primary outcomes, and excluding the three participants with the largest residuals did not materially change the pattern of significance. Overall, the results suggest a robust age-related decline in both item and associative memory, a pattern consistent with the associative deficit hypothesis in direction, but with only marginal statistical support in the interaction contrast. Handedness, whether treated categorically or continuously, did not moderate the age-related associative deficit.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether handedness moderates age-related associative memory decline using a mixed design that compared item and associative memory across younger and older adults who were categorized as strongly right-handed or mixed/left-handed. The pattern of results was straightforward. We observed clear main effects of age, with younger adults outperforming older adults overall on episodic memory tasks. We also observed higher associative recognition scores relative to corrected item recognition scores in the group-level descriptive sense, and the age-related decrement was numerically larger for associative memory than for item memory. The key age x memory interaction that operationalizes the associative deficit was marginally significant in the primary ANOVA and was supported directionally by simple effects and between-group contrasts showing larger age differences for associative than item memory. However, the hypothesized moderation by handedness — that mixed or left-handed older adults would be relatively spared from associative decline — was not supported. Neither categorical handedness grouping nor continuous laterality quotient interacted significantly with age and memory type. Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence in favor of the null for the three-way interaction.

These findings have several implications for models of aging and memory that link lateralization to compensatory or protective mechanisms. The associative deficit hypothesis posits that older adults have specific difficulty forming and retrieving bindings among elements of an event. Our results provide at least tentative replication of that phenomenon; the age-related deficit was more pronounced for associative than for item memory in magnitude, even though the formal interaction reached only marginal statistical significance. The lack of a moderating effect of handedness suggests that the behavioral marker of lateralization captured by self-reported handedness does not provide measurable protection against associative binding decline in normal aging.

There are both theoretical and methodological reasons to interpret these results carefully. Theoretically, while it is plausible that reduced lateralization or greater interhemispheric connectivity could support associative processes, the neurobiological substrates of associative memory are complex and include hippocampal entorhinal circuits, frontotemporal interactions, and long-range white matter tracts whose integrity often declines with age. Handedness, as a behavioral proxy, may only weakly index these specific neuroanatomical features that are critical for binding. In other words, the aspects of brain organization that confer advantages on associative memory may not be well-captured by laterality of hand preference.

Methodologically, the way handedness is measured and categorized matters. Self-report inventories such as the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory capture overt hand preference across tasks but do not directly measure cerebral lateralization or interhemispheric transfer. Neuroimaging or electrophysiological measures would provide more direct indices of hemispheric organization. In addition, our categorical decision to group mixed-handed and left-handed participants together may have obscured heterogeneity; left-handed individuals and mixed-handed individuals may differ in important neural ways. Our supplementary analyses treating laterality quotient as continuous did not find graded effects, but such analyses may lack sensitivity when the underlying biological trait is noisy.

Another consideration is statistical power. Although our sample size of 120 participants provides reasonable power for main effects and simple contrasts, the detection of higher-order interactions such as an age x handedness x memory type effect typically requires large samples because effect sizes are often small. The marginal p-value for the age x memory interaction suggests that the associative deficit in this sample was not strong enough to produce robust moderation effects. Bayesian analyses indicated moderate evidence for the null regarding the moderation hypothesis, but Bayes factors are sensitive to prior assumptions and model specifications.

The null moderation result also aligns with recent literature suggesting that compensatory bilateral activation observed in aging is not uniformly beneficial across cognitive domains. Bilateral recruitment may sometimes reflect inefficient processing or dedifferentiation rather than effective compensation. If mixed/left-handed individuals rely on different patterns of hemispheric organization, that difference does not necessarily translate into better associative binding, particularly in older age when multiple systems are compromised. Thus, the absence of a handedness benefit on associative memory does not necessarily refute the idea that interhemispheric communication can aid cognition; it suggests that the link between behavioral handedness and the specific neural mechanisms of associative binding is tenuous.

The pattern of results also raises questions about task specifics. Our associative recognition used a forced-choice format, whereas other studies that documented associative deficits in aging have used cued recall or free recall paradigms. The forced-choice associative recognition task may be less sensitive to some forms of binding failure because it affords residual familiarity-based discrimination. If handedness were to exert effects primarily on recollection-based processes rather than on familiarity, a forced-choice recognition format might not reveal those effects. Future work should employ multiple associative tasks that tap different mnemonic processes to test whether any handedness-related advantages are process-specific.

Several limitations of the current study warrant mention. First, the sample, while reasonably sized, may not have included enough individuals at the extremes of handedness to detect subtle effects. True left-handedness tends to be less common in the population, and mixing left-handed and mixed-handed participants into one group introduces heterogeneity. Second, handedness was assessed behaviorally rather than neurally. Incorporating neuroimaging measures such as functional MRI lateralization indices or diffusion-weighted imaging measures of interhemispheric white matter tracts (e.g., callosal integrity) would allow for a more direct test of the hypothesized mechanism. Third, the cross-sectional design cannot determine whether handedness has any influence on longitudinal trajectories of associative memory decline. It is possible that handedness confers subtle advantages that only emerge or accumulate over time; longitudinal designs would be needed to assess this possibility.

Nevertheless, the study also has strengths. The mixed design allowed for within-subjects comparisons of item and associative memory, increasing sensitivity to differential effects. Screening for cognitive impairment ensured that older participants were within the range of normative aging. The use of both categorical and continuous handedness measures provides complementary perspectives on handedness effects. Finally, the inclusion of Bayesian analyses alongside frequentist tests offers a nuanced view of the strength of evidence for both effects and null effects.

The current findings point to several directions for future research. First, future studies should include direct neurophysiological measures of lateralization and interhemispheric connectivity to test whether these properties, rather than behavioral hand preference, relate to associative binding in aging. Functional lateralization indices derived from language tasks or task-related activation patterns could be used to test whether bilateral activation predicts preserved associative memory. Second, larger samples, ideally with oversampling of strongly left-handed individuals, would afford greater power to detect small to moderate moderation effects. Third, longitudinal studies would clarify whether handedness relates to trajectories of associative memory decline and whether it interacts with other markers of cognitive reserve, such as education and occupational complexity.

Another promising avenue is to examine whether training or interventions that enhance interhemispheric coordination can ameliorate associative deficits. If interhemispheric transfer supports binding, then targeted interventions such as bilateral sensorimotor training or noninvasive brain stimulation protocols that modulate callosal connectivity could be tested for their effects on associative memory. However, such interventions would first require stronger evidence that interhemispheric properties causally influence binding processes in aging.

It is also important to consider individual differences beyond handedness that may moderate associative decline. Genetic factors, vascular risk, lifestyle variables such as physical activity, and domain-general cognitive control abilities are all candidate moderators. A multi-measure approach that integrates behavioral, neural, genetic, and lifestyle data would be well-positioned to identify the constellation of factors that predict resilience against associative memory decline.

In conclusion, the present study provides modest replication of the associative deficit phenomenon and indicates that, as assessed here, handedness does not moderate age-related associative decline. These findings suggest that the behavioral marker of handedness is not a robust protective factor for associative binding in normal aging. Future research employing neural measures of lateralization, larger and more targeted samples, longitudinal designs, and experimental manipulations will be necessary to determine whether and how hemispheric organization contributes to individual differences in associative memory across the lifespan. Until such evidence accumulates, models of cognitive aging should treat handedness as a possible, but not definitive, source of variation in associative memory performance.
