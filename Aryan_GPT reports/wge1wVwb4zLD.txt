Introduction

Self-esteem is a core psychological construct that influences mental health, social behavior, and overall well-being. Self-esteem can shape how individuals interpret social interactions and respond to online and offline feedback. The rise of social media platforms has introduced novel social environments that can shape self-evaluations in ways that were not possible in previous generations. Social media platforms vary in their design, affordances, and typical patterns of use, and these differences may influence their psychological effects. Investigating relationships between social media use and self-esteem is therefore important for understanding contemporary psychosocial functioning. Current everyday life is saturated with social media, making the study of their psychological correlates broadly relevant to clinicians, researchers, and the public. Platforms such as Facebook and Instagram are widely used and have distinct features that may differentially relate to self-esteem outcomes. Facebook has emphasized social networks and reciprocal connections for many years, whereas Instagram foregrounds visual content, image curation, and a follower model that can be more asymmetrical. These platform distinctions suggest that simple aggregate statements about "social media" may miss important heterogeneity in effects. Prior work has shown mixed results regarding the relationship between social media and self-esteem. Some studies report that social media use is associated with lower well-being and reduced self-esteem, particularly when use involves passive consumption or social comparison. Other studies suggest that social media can provide social support, affirmation, and opportunities for positive self-presentation that bolster self-esteem. The mixed pattern in the literature suggests that details such as the type of use, the platform, and the social context are likely moderators. For example, active engagement that produces supportive feedback could increase self-esteem, while passive scrolling and upward social comparisons could decrease it. Similarly, receiving likes and comments on posts can produce feelings of social validation, yet the degree to which such validation influences self-esteem may depend on the platform and the individual's preexisting traits. Despite these nuanced possibilities, relatively few studies have directly compared platform-specific metrics such as likes, friends, and time spent across two large platforms in a single sample. The present study addresses this gap by examining Facebook and Instagram use in relation to self-esteem using a correlational survey design. Facebook and Instagram differ in social structure: Facebook traditionally emphasizes mutual friendships and networks of acquaintances, while Instagram often relies on a follower model and curated visual self-presentation. These structural differences could plausibly affect how social metrics relate to self-esteem. For instance, the number of friends on Facebook may reflect real-world social network size and obligations, whereas the number of followers on Instagram might reflect popularity, professional curation, or superficial engagement. Likes represent a form of social feedback that is lightweight and quantifiable; they can act as cues of social approval but are also subject to variability and strategic presentation. Time spent on a platform reflects exposure and engagement and may index either beneficial social connection or problematic overuse and rumination. There are theoretical reasons to expect different relationships between self-esteem and these social media metrics. Social cognitive theories posit that self-evaluations are shaped by social feedback and comparison processes. Under this view, the amount and valence of social feedback (e.g., likes) should be related to self-esteem. Interpersonal theories argue that the quality of connections matters more than quantity, suggesting that sheer numbers of friends or followers may be less important than the nature of interactions. Uses and gratifications perspectives suggest that motivations for use (e.g., information seeking, social connection, self-presentation) moderate outcomes. The extant literature includes findings that greater time on social media is sometimes associated with lower self-esteem and poorer well-being, potentially due to increased opportunities for negative social comparison or displacement of offline activities. At the same time, receiving positive feedback such as likes and supportive comments has been associated with temporary boosts in mood and feelings of acceptance for some users. However, these effects are not consistent across platforms or across individual differences. Some research indicates that visible social metrics such as follower counts and likes can produce anxiety and competition, especially for users who place high importance on external validation. Other work finds little association between follower counts and self-concept, suggesting that numbers alone do not determine self-worth. Given these mixed findings, a careful empirical comparison across platform-specific features could clarify how different social media behaviors are linked to self-esteem. The present study therefore tested specific hypotheses about the links between Facebook and Instagram metrics and self-esteem scores. The study was designed as a correlational survey that measured self-reported average daily time spent on Facebook and Instagram, the number of Facebook friends and Instagram followers, and typical likes received on recent posts. We pre-specified hypotheses that receiving more likes would be positively associated with higher self-esteem across both platforms, on the assumption that social approval would bolster self-evaluations. We also hypothesized that larger networks, operationalized as Facebook friends and Instagram followers, would be positively associated with self-esteem, reflecting greater social resources and perceived connectedness. Finally, based on prior literature showing associations between heavy social media use and lower well-being, we hypothesized that greater time spent on social media would be associated with lower self-esteem. The rationale for these hypotheses combined expectations from social feedback theories and the accumulated empirical work linking heavy social media use to poorer self-outcomes. In addition to these primary predictions, we planned exploratory analyses to test whether platform differences would moderate the relationships. Specifically, we sought to determine whether the association between likes and self-esteem would be stronger on platforms that emphasize reciprocal friendship (Facebook) versus platform that emphasize curated visual self-presentation (Instagram). We also examined whether the relationship between network size and self-esteem differed across platforms. The rationale here was that friends on Facebook may reflect more reciprocal and meaningful ties, whereas followers on Instagram may be less personally connected and more oriented toward visibility. Finally, we planned to control for demographic variables such as age, gender, and socioeconomic status because these variables can be related to both social media behavior and self-esteem. The study thus combined theoretically informed hypotheses with broad exploratory aims. Given the ubiquity of social media and the potential for both benefit and harm, understanding these relationships has applied significance. Clinicians working with clients who report distress related to social media might tailor interventions differently depending on which platform behaviors are most strongly linked to low self-esteem. Researchers can also use platform-specific findings to refine theories about online social behavior and its psychological consequences. In light of the mixed and sometimes contradictory prior findings, we believed that a medium-sized, well-characterized sample with specific platform metrics would contribute useful data. We designed the survey to capture concrete usage metrics such as minutes per day and approximate counts of friends/followers and average likes, rather than global hours or vague frequency estimates. We further assessed self-esteem using a validated instrument to ensure reliable measurement of the psychological outcome. Because the study was correlational, causal claims were explicitly avoided, but associations could inform theory and clinical practice. The study tested three pre-registered hypotheses: first, that greater likes on posts would be positively associated with higher self-esteem on both Facebook and Instagram; second, that larger numbers of friends and followers would be positively associated with higher self-esteem; and third, that greater time spent on the platforms would be associated with lower self-esteem. We also conducted exploratory regressions to determine the unique contribution of each metric when controlling for other variables. These decisions were intended to illuminate whether the form of social feedback (likes), the scale of the social network (friends/followers), or the quantity of engagement (time) was most strongly related to self-esteem. By comparing Facebook and Instagram directly in the same sample, the study sought to clarify whether platform affordances correspond to distinct psychological profiles. Overall, the introduction has presented the theoretical background, the empirical ambiguities in existing work, and the specific hypotheses and rationale that motivated the present study. The remainder of the article describes the methods used to collect and analyze the data, reports the results of the primary and exploratory tests, and discusses the implications of the findings for theory, practice, and future research.

Methods

Participants and recruitment were conducted using an online research panel designed to sample adults who regularly use social media. Participants were required to be at least 18 years old. The recruitment message indicated that respondents would complete a survey about social media use and general well-being. A total of 362 individuals initiated the survey. After applying data quality filters and removing incomplete responses, the final analytic sample comprised 318 participants. The sample size of 318 provided reasonable statistical power to detect small-to-moderate correlations in the range typically observed in social media research. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 54, with a mean age of 28.7 years and a standard deviation of 6.9 years. The sample included a mix of genders; 58% identified as female, 40% as male, and 2% as nonbinary or another gender identity. Participants reported a variety of educational backgrounds and employment statuses. The sample was broadly representative of young adult social media users, which was the target population for this study. The study obtained ethics approval from the institutional review board of the sponsoring institution. All participants provided informed consent prior to beginning the survey and were able to withdraw at any time. Participants received modest compensation through the panel's standard reward system. Measures included demographic information, platform-specific usage metrics, and a validated self-esteem scale. Demographic items captured age, gender, highest education completed, employment status, and an approximate indicator of socioeconomic status using self-reported income brackets. These demographic covariates were included to control for potential confounding in the analyses. Platform-specific usage metrics were assessed with concrete, self-reported items. For Facebook, participants reported their approximate number of friends on their primary Facebook account. For Instagram, participants reported their approximate number of followers on their primary Instagram account. Participants were instructed to estimate these counts to the best of their ability and to update the estimated values based on their most recent memory of account settings. We acknowledged that self-reported counts could be imprecise but believed that participants could provide reasonable approximations that are meaningful for correlational analyses. Likes were assessed by asking participants to estimate the average number of likes they received on their most recent public posts over the past month on each platform. We operationalized likes as the mean number of likes per post received on posts the participant made that were visible to their network. Time spent was assessed by asking participants to estimate their average daily time spent on each platform in minutes. Participants were instructed to think about a typical weekday and to consider both mobile and desktop usage. We offered categorical ranges for time but also allowed free numeric entry to enable more precise analyses; the final variable used a numeric minute estimate. Self-esteem was measured using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES), a widely used 10-item self-report measure that assesses global self-worth. Participants indicated their agreement with each item on a four-point Likert scale. Items were scored and summed such that higher scores reflected greater self-esteem. The RSES has demonstrated reliability and validity in diverse samples, and in the present sample the scale showed strong internal consistency, with a Cronbach's alpha of 0.87. Prior to analysis, data cleaning steps were taken. Responses with excessive missing data, straight-line responding, or contradictory answers were flagged and reviewed. Participants who failed attention checks embedded in the survey were excluded from the final analysis. Continuous variables were inspected for skewness and outliers. The distributions of friends, followers, and likes were right-skewed, as is common for count-like social metrics. To address skewness, we applied a log(1 + x) transformation to these variables for correlation and regression analyses; descriptive statistics are reported on the raw scale for interpretability but inferential tests used the transformed variables to better meet the assumptions of parametric tests. Time spent was inspected for implausible values; entries above 600 minutes per day were individually examined and removed if judged unreliable. Missing data were minimal; for participants with a small amount of missing item-level data on the RSES, we used mean imputation across the scale; participants with multiple missing RSES items were excluded. All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Primary analyses consisted of Pearson product-moment correlations between self-esteem scores and each social media metric (likes, friends/followers, time) for both Facebook and Instagram. Secondary analyses included multiple linear regression models predicting self-esteem from all platform metrics simultaneously, controlling for demographic covariates (age, gender, education, and income bracket). We tested for multicollinearity among predictors by examining variance inflation factors (VIFs) and found no problematic collinearity (VIFs < 2.2). We also conducted exploratory interaction tests to examine whether the relationship between likes and self-esteem differed by platform. For these interaction tests, we created a combined dataset with platform as a within-subjects indicator and used mixed-effects regression models that included random intercepts for participants. Model assumptions (linearity, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals) were checked graphically and via standard tests. Residuals were approximately normally distributed in the primary regression models after log-transforming skewed predictors. Effect sizes are reported using standardized regression coefficients and correlation coefficients. Statistical significance was evaluated using a two-tailed alpha of .05. Where multiple tests increased the chance of Type I error, we interpreted patterns of results cautiously and emphasized effect sizes rather than binary significance. In addition to hypothesis-testing analyses, we conducted robustness checks. These included repeating key regressions without transformed predictors, excluding extreme outliers, and testing whether controlling for total combined time across platforms altered the relationships. We also conducted subgroup analyses by gender and age tertile to see if relationships were moderated by these variables. All robustness checks are reported in the results section. The methods were designed to balance feasibility with measurement precision in a survey context. By collecting multiple platform-specific metrics in the same sample, we aimed to parse platform differences in their associations with self-esteem. Ethical considerations included protecting participant anonymity and ensuring that demographic and social media metrics were stored securely. The study intentionally did not attempt to access participants' real account data to preserve privacy and to keep the study economical; instead, it relied on self-reported estimates, a limitation we acknowledge and address in the discussion. Overall, the methods provided the basis for testing our pre-registered hypotheses about likes, friends/followers, time spent, and their associations with self-esteem across Facebook and Instagram.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the analytic sample are presented first. The mean Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale score was 22.9 with a standard deviation of 4.8 on the 10-40 scoring range. Facebook friends had a median of 350 and a mean of 412, with a wide distribution and several high-end outliers. Instagram followers had a median of 420 and a mean of 512, again with a skewed distribution. Average likes per post reported on Facebook had a mean of 27.4 and a median of 18. Average likes per post on Instagram had a mean of 96.5 and a median of 58. Reported average daily time on Facebook was 48.2 minutes (SD = 36.9), while daily time on Instagram averaged 71.3 minutes (SD = 52.1). Raw distributions showed positive skew for friends, followers, and likes, motivating the log(1 + x) transformation used in inferential analyses. Cronbach's alpha for the RSES was 0.87, indicating acceptable internal consistency in this sample. Bivariate correlations between self-esteem and the transformed social media metrics revealed several notable patterns. Self-esteem had a small but significant positive correlation with log-transformed average Facebook likes (r = .18, p = .002). By contrast, self-esteem did not correlate significantly with log-transformed average Instagram likes (r = .03, p = .57). Self-esteem correlated negatively with log-transformed Facebook friends count (r = -.15, p = .01). The correlation between self-esteem and log-transformed Instagram followers was small and negative but did not reach conventional significance (r = -.09, p = .10). Self-esteem was negatively correlated with time spent on Facebook (r = -.25, p < .001), consistent with the hypothesis about time spent and lower self-esteem. The correlation between self-esteem and time spent on Instagram was small and non-significant (r = -.08, p = .13). These bivariate patterns showed that Facebook likes were positively associated with self-esteem while Facebook friends and Facebook time were negatively associated with self-esteem. Instagram likes were not associated with self-esteem, and Instagram followers showed at most a weak negative relationship. To further probe these associations, we conducted multiple linear regression analyses predicting self-esteem. Model 1 included all Facebook metrics (log friends, log likes, and time spent) and controlled for age, gender, education, and income. In Model 1, log Facebook likes remained a significant positive predictor of self-esteem (standardized β = .14, p = .01). Log Facebook friends remained a significant negative predictor (standardized β = -.11, p = .03). Time spent on Facebook was a significant negative predictor of self-esteem (standardized β = -.21, p < .001). The model accounted for 12% of the variance in self-esteem (adjusted R^2 = .12). Model diagnostics indicated acceptable fit and no substantial multicollinearity among predictors. Model 2 included Instagram metrics (log followers, log likes, and time spent) with the same demographic controls. In Model 2, log Instagram likes were not a significant predictor of self-esteem (standardized β = .02, p = .68). Log Instagram followers also did not significantly predict self-esteem in the multivariate model (standardized β = -.05, p = .35). Time spent on Instagram was a non-significant negative predictor (standardized β = -.07, p = .18). The variance explained by Model 2 was modest, with adjusted R^2 = .05. Comparing the two platform-specific models suggested stronger associations between Facebook metrics and self-esteem than between Instagram metrics and self-esteem. To determine whether the platform differences in likes were statistically robust, we conducted an interaction test using a mixed-effects model on the combined dataset of likes with platform as a within-subjects factor. In this mixed model, the interaction between platform (Facebook vs. Instagram) and log likes in predicting self-esteem was significant (interaction β = -.16, p = .02), indicating that the positive association between likes and self-esteem was stronger for Facebook than for Instagram. This interaction remained significant when controlling for age, gender, and time spent on each platform. We also examined whether the negative association between friends/followers and self-esteem was platform-specific. An interaction test revealed that the negative relation between network size and self-esteem was more pronounced for Facebook friends than for Instagram followers (interaction β = -.09, p = .04). In other words, the negative relationship held for Facebook but was weaker and non-significant for Instagram. Next, we ran a combined regression predicting self-esteem from all six social metrics (Facebook and Instagram friends/followers, likes, and time spent) simultaneously along with demographic covariates. In this comprehensive model, log Facebook likes (β = .12, p = .02) and time on Facebook (β = -.17, p < .001) remained significant predictors. Log Facebook friends approached significance in this model (β = -.08, p = .07). Instagram metrics were non-significant when taking Facebook metrics into account. The combined model explained 14% of the variance in self-esteem (adjusted R^2 = .14). We inspected potential moderation by gender by adding interaction terms between gender and each social metric in exploratory models. None of the gender interactions reached significance after Bonferroni correction for multiple tests, indicating that the basic pattern of associations was similar across gender categories in this sample. Age moderation tests showed one modest interaction: the negative association between Facebook time and self-esteem was stronger in younger participants (ages 18-24) relative to older participants (ages 35+), though the interaction effect was small (interaction β = -.10, p = .04) and should be interpreted cautiously. Robustness checks supported the main patterns. Excluding extreme outliers in friends and followers counts did not materially change the direction or significance of the associations. Running models on untransformed predictors produced similar patterns but with slightly larger standard errors due to skew; the key inferences remained the same. Controlling for total combined time across platforms reduced the magnitude of the association between Facebook time and self-esteem slightly but the relationship remained significant (β = -.15, p = .002). We also tested whether self-esteem might predict social media metrics (reverse direction) using cross-sectional mediation-style models; these analyses are exploratory given the study's correlational design. They suggested that low self-esteem was associated with greater time on Facebook, but the cross-sectional nature prevents causal interpretation. Overall, the results revealed several unexpected patterns relative to our pre-registered hypotheses. The predicted positive association between likes and self-esteem held for Facebook but not for Instagram. The predicted positive association between network size and self-esteem did not hold; instead, Facebook friends were negatively associated with self-esteem and Instagram followers were not significantly related. The predicted negative association between time spent and self-esteem was confirmed for Facebook but not for Instagram. These findings were consistent across multiple analytic approaches and robust to sensitivity checks. Effect sizes were generally small to moderate, with the strongest single predictor being time spent on Facebook, which showed the most consistent and substantial negative association with self-esteem. The combined set of social media variables explained a modest but meaningful proportion of variance in self-esteem, indicating that platform-specific online behaviors are one of many factors that relate to self-evaluations. Taken together, the results suggest that platform affordances and the social meaning of specific metrics shape their associations with self-esteem.

Discussion

The present study examined associations between platform-specific social media metrics and global self-esteem in a sample of adult social media users. The results provided a nuanced picture in which Facebook and Instagram showed different patterns of association with self-esteem. Specifically, Facebook likes were positively related to self-esteem, whereas Instagram likes were not. Conversely, Facebook friends were negatively related to self-esteem and Instagram follower counts showed no significant association. Time spent on Facebook was negatively associated with self-esteem, while time on Instagram was not significantly related. These results offer several theoretical and practical implications. The finding that Facebook likes were positively correlated with self-esteem supports theories emphasizing the role of social feedback in shaping self-evaluations. Likes on Facebook may function as discrete signals of acceptance and belonging from one's reciprocal social network, and these signals may reinforce positive self-appraisals. The reciprocity and personal connections typical on Facebook might make likes more meaningful to self-concept than likes obtained on a platform that emphasizes polished visual presentation and a potentially more anonymous follower base. That an effect emerged for Facebook likes but not Instagram likes suggests that the psychological meaning of identical interface features can vary by platform context. This platform-specificity challenges the tendency in some prior research to treat social media as a monolithic category and underscores the need to parse platform affordances. The lack of a positive relationship between Instagram likes and self-esteem was notable. Instagram is a platform where curated image-based self-presentation and conspicuous metrics such as follower counts are common, yet in this study likes on Instagram did not predict self-esteem. One possible explanation is that Instagram users may experience likes as less diagnostic of social acceptance because the platform's culture encourages broad liking or strategic engagement. Alternatively, Instagram's visual emphasis might foster social comparison processes that attenuate the positive impact of likes on self-evaluations. Users may view likes on Instagram as less personal or more performative, reducing their capacity to bolster global self-esteem. Another key finding was the negative association between Facebook friends and self-esteem. This pattern runs contrary to an initial hypothesis that larger networks would be associated with greater social resources and higher self-esteem. Instead, a larger number of Facebook friends was associated with lower self-esteem in bivariate correlations and in some multivariate models. Several interpretations are possible. Large friend counts might increase the complexity of social obligations and the potential for negative social comparisons, which could erode self-esteem. Alternatively, those with lower self-esteem might seek to compensate by accumulating more social connections online, a pattern consistent with compensatory internet use theories. The cross-sectional design cannot disentangle directionality, but the negative association challenges simplistic assumptions that "more friends is always better." The fact that Instagram follower counts did not show a parallel negative association suggests that the social meaning of network size differs across platforms. Instagram followers may include many strangers, influencers, or passive watchers, and therefore high follower counts may not be as burdensome or comparison-inducing as large Facebook friend networks that more likely include acquaintances and real-life contacts. The most robust and consistent finding was the negative relationship between time spent on Facebook and self-esteem. This result aligned with prior literature linking heavy social media use to poorer mental health outcomes and lower well-being. Time on Facebook may increase exposure to social comparison opportunities, negative affective content, or rumination, all of which can negatively affect self-esteem. It may also displace offline social interactions and activities that support self-worth. Importantly, the negative association was stronger among younger participants in exploratory tests, suggesting that developmental factors or cohort-specific engagement styles may amplify vulnerability. The absence of a significant negative association between time on Instagram and self-esteem was somewhat surprising given concerns about image-based platforms promoting social comparison. This discrepancy might reflect differences in typical user behavior; for example, Instagram use might be more selectively curated and tied to identity signaling for some users, whereas Facebook browsing may be more passive and mixed with exposure to a wider array of social content. Another possibility is that the psychological consequences of time spent are mediated by the nature of the activity (active vs. passive use) rather than by platform per se. Our measures captured overall time but did not distinguish between active posting, direct messaging, or passive scrolling. Therefore, future work should incorporate more granular measures of use modalities to better explain platform differences. The interaction tests confirmed that the association between likes and self-esteem differed by platform, which further underscores the importance of context in interpreting social feedback. Practical implications stem from these platform-specific patterns. Clinicians working with clients distressed about social media might target different behaviors depending on which platform seems most problematic. For example, reducing passive Facebook browsing time could be a useful strategy given its consistent negative association with self-esteem. At the same time, encouraging meaningful online interactions that produce authentic social support could help clients harness the potential benefits of likes and positive feedback—particularly in contexts where feedback is reciprocal and relationships are substantive. The study also suggests that interventions should not assume that increasing network size will enhance self-esteem. Instead, clinicians could focus on the quality of connections and the individual's motives for accumulating friends or followers. Theoretically, the findings contribute to debates about whether visible social metrics are inherently detrimental or beneficial for self-evaluations. The mixed pattern—positive associations for one platform's likes, negative associations for friend counts and time spent—indicates that visible metrics can have both adaptive and maladaptive correlates depending on social context and usage patterns. This complexity argues for more nuanced models that incorporate platform affordances, user motivations, and the quality of social ties. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First and foremost, the study's cross-sectional correlative design precludes causal inference. While we found associations between specific metrics and self-esteem, we cannot determine whether social media use causes changes in self-esteem or whether preexisting self-esteem influences social media behavior. Longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to establish directionality. Second, the study relied on self-reported metrics for friends, followers, likes, and time spent. Although participants could provide reasonable estimates, objective digital trace data would provide more precise measurement and reduce potential reporting biases. Future studies that combine self-report with platform-derived behavioral logs would strengthen inference. Third, the sample was drawn from an online panel and may not generalize to all populations, particularly older adults or those who do not use social media frequently. The sample was concentrated among young adults, which was intentional but limits generalizability. Fourth, the measures of likes and time did not capture the valence of feedback or the emotional responses to feedback. Not all likes are experienced equally; qualitative aspects of interactions such as supportive comments, negative messages, and the emotional importance of particular relationships likely moderate effects. Future research should assess subjective appraisals of feedback and the emotional context of interactions. Fifth, the analyses treated platform metrics as independent predictors, but there may be complex interdependencies and nonlinearities. For example, the psychological impact of likes may vary at different thresholds of follower count, or the effects of time spent may depend on the balance of active versus passive use. Advanced modeling approaches, such as moderated mediation or person-centered analyses, could reveal more nuanced patterns. Despite these limitations, the study contributes novel evidence that identical social media features can relate to self-esteem in different ways across platforms. The differing associations for Facebook and Instagram highlight the need for platform-specific theorizing and intervention approaches. Future research directions include conducting longitudinal studies to test causal directions, integrating objective behavioral data, and examining mediators such as social comparison, perceived social support, and motives for use. Experimental designs could manipulate feedback or time spent to test causal effects on self-esteem and related outcomes. Additionally, research could explore individual difference moderators such as trait vulnerability to social comparison, baseline self-esteem, and social anxiety to see who is most affected by specific platform features. Cross-cultural research would also be valuable, as norms and uses of social media vary internationally and could shape the meaning of likes, friends, and followers. Another important direction is to investigate how platform changes and algorithmic curation influence the psychological impact of social metrics. Social media platforms continually evolve, and features such as hiding likes or altering feed algorithms could change how users interpret feedback. Evaluating the psychological consequences of design changes could inform ethical platform design and public policy. Researchers might also examine interventions that encourage healthier patterns of use, such as digital literacy training, time-use limits, or prompts to reflect before posting. Clinical trials testing whether reducing passive use or fostering authentic engagement improves self-esteem would be particularly informative. Finally, qualitative work could deepen understanding by capturing users' subjective narratives about the meaning of likes, friends, and followers in their lives. Such qualitative insights can guide the development of more precise survey instruments and interventions. In conclusion, the present study demonstrated that social media metrics have nuanced and platform-specific associations with self-esteem. Facebook likes were positively associated with self-esteem, whereas Instagram likes were not. Facebook friends and time spent on Facebook were negatively associated with self-esteem. These patterns suggest that the psychological impact of social media depends on platform affordances, the social meaning of visible metrics, and patterns of engagement. Clinicians and researchers should attend to platform-specific behaviors rather than treating social media use as a unitary construct. Future work using longitudinal and objective measures will be essential to clarify causal mechanisms and to translate findings into effective interventions that promote psychological well-being in an increasingly digital social world.
