Introduction

Human behavior is shaped by a complex interplay of dispositional tendencies and situational factors. One dispositional tendency that has received considerable attention is risk taking, defined broadly as the propensity to engage in behaviors that entail the possibility of loss or harm in exchange for potential gains or desired outcomes. Risk taking is not a unitary construct; it manifests differently across domains such as financial decisions, health and safety, recreational activities, social interactions, and ethical choices. Researchers have sought to capture this multidimensionality using instruments that differentiate between domain-specific tendencies and overall propensity. Sport participation offers a particularly informative context in which to examine risk taking because sport inherently involves physical risk, competitive uncertainty, and reward contingencies. Individuals who engage in sports regularly may be more likely to seek novel or arousing experiences, tolerate physical discomfort, and prioritize potential gains associated with performance. At the same time, sporting environments may socialize participants into particular risk norms or reinforce specific decision-making styles. Thus, sports provide an opportunity to examine how dispositional risk taking relates to behavioral engagement, as well as how participation contexts might shape or be shaped by risk propensity.

University students represent a key population for examining the association between sport participation and risk taking. They are at an age where identity formation, social experimentation, and leisure choices coalesce, and where participation in organized and informal sports is common. Many students choose to participate in sports at recreational, intramural, or competitive levels, and others opt out of organized physical activity. Differences between these groups may reflect pre-existing personality traits, responses to social opportunities, or bidirectional influences between personality and behavior. Therefore, studying student samples can yield insights not only into the correlational relationship between sport participation and risk taking but also into plausible mechanisms that underlie group differences.

Prior research has linked personality traits with sporting outcomes and participation patterns. For example, some investigators have argued that traits associated with the broad domain of extraversion and sensation seeking are more common among athletes and are conducive to sports that reward assertiveness, novelty seeking, and tolerance for uncertainty. Other work has focused on the role of conscientiousness in training adherence and long-term performance development. A more targeted stream of research has considered the relationship between specific risk-related personality measures and sporting contexts. In this vein, Allen and Laborde provided evidence suggesting a strong impact of personality traits on sporting performance. Their findings emphasize that dispositional characteristics, including tendencies toward risk or approach behaviors, can influence both selection into sport and performance outcomes within sport. Such findings raise two key types of questions. First, are people who participate in sport more inclined toward risk taking than those who do not participate? Second, if such differences exist, do they reflect dispositional selection, socialization processes within sport, or a combination of both? Answering these questions requires careful measurement and analysis.

One widely used tool for measuring risk taking across domains is the Domain-Specific Risk-Taking (DOSPERT) scale. The DOSPERT captures self-reported likelihood of engaging in risky behaviors in multiple domains and has been used to assess both overall and domain-specific risk propensities. Its multidimensionality makes it well suited for investigations of sport-related risk because it can distinguish between general thrill-seeking tendencies and domain-specific proclivities, such as health/safety risk taking, which may be directly relevant to sporting contexts. Using a measure like the DOSPERT allows researchers to ask whether athletes differ from non-athletes in generalized risk taking or whether differences are concentrated in domains most relevant to sport.

Studies that compare athletes and non-athletes have produced mixed results, possibly because of differences in sample populations, operational definitions of athletic participation, and risk-taking measures used. Some research suggests that athletes show greater sensation seeking and risk acceptance, particularly in high-contact or extreme sports. Other studies have found minimal differences or have documented that differences are domain-specific rather than global. A potential contributor to mixed findings is heterogeneity in what is meant by “sports people.” For example, elite athletes, recreational athletes, and those who participate in non-contact or team sports may differ systematically in their attitudes toward risk. Thus, clear operational definitions and sample characterization are necessary to interpret group comparisons meaningfully.

Another important consideration is the potential role of gender. Males and females often differ on average in self-reported risk-taking tendencies, although the magnitude and direction can vary by domain. Because sport participation rates and sport types often differ by gender in university samples, observed group differences may be confounded by gender composition. Therefore, any comparison between sports participants and non-participants must account for gender distribution and possibly examine interaction effects.

Beyond simple group comparisons, it is informative to examine whether the degree of sport participation, such as hours per week of sporting activity, is associated with risk-taking propensity. If a dose-response relationship is observed, such that greater time spent in sport is associated with higher risk-taking scores, that pattern could be interpreted as consistent with either selection or socialization mechanisms. For example, individuals higher in risk-taking may seek out more sport participation; alternatively, repeated exposure to sporting contexts may increase comfort with risk or change risk-related attitudes. Longitudinal designs are necessary to establish directionality, but cross-sectional associations can help identify promising avenues for longitudinal or experimental follow-up.

The present study sought to address gaps in the literature by comparing overall and domain-specific risk-taking tendencies between university students who participate in sports regularly and those who do not participate. The primary aim was to establish whether there is a difference in risk taking between sports people and non-sports people within a university student sample. Operationally, “sports people” were defined as students who reported regularly participating in organized or informal sport for at least three hours per week over the previous six months, including membership on a university team or regular club participation. “Non-sports people” were defined as students who reported engaging in less than one hour per week of organized or informal sport over the same period. These definitions were chosen to create a meaningful behavioral contrast in participation while minimizing overlap between groups.

Because risk taking is multifaceted, we employed the DOSPERT to assess both overall risk propensity and domain-specific tendencies. The DOSPERT includes subscales that assess ethical risks, financial risks (investment and gambling), health/safety risks, recreational risks, and social risks. We predicted that sports participants would show elevated overall risk-taking scores relative to non-participants. We further predicted that differences would be most pronounced on recreational and health/safety domains, given the physical and novelty-seeking elements of many sports. We also examined whether hours of sport participation would be associated with DOSPERT scores, controlling for demographic variables, and whether gender would moderate group differences.

To situate the current study within the broader theoretical landscape, it is important to consider mechanisms that might underlie group differences. One possibility is personality-driven selection: individuals with higher dispositional risk taking may be more likely to choose sporting activities that provide arousal and uncertain outcomes. Another possibility is that participation in sport socializes individuals to accept and manage risk, for instance through repeated exposure to controlled risk environments, coaching that emphasizes risk assessment, or peer influences that valorize daring behavior. A third possibility is bidirectional influence, where initial selection is reinforced by socialization, leading to enhanced risk acceptance over time. Distinguishing among these mechanisms was beyond the scope of the present cross-sectional study, but the pattern of associations between degree of participation and risk scores may provide clues.

Given the practical implications of any relationship between sport participation and risk taking, the study also considered applied perspectives. If sports people do exhibit higher risk propensity, then coaches, trainers, and university administrators may need to factor dispositional tendencies into injury prevention strategies, talent identification, and performance psychology interventions. Conversely, if sport participation increases risk taking beyond what is expected from selection effects, then programs aimed at cultivating safe risk management strategies within sports could mitigate potential harms while preserving the performance benefits associated with adaptive risk taking.

This study contributes to the literature by employing a well-characterized student sample, using a multidimensional measure of risk, and testing both categorical and continuous indices of sport participation. The research design allows for clear testing of the primary hypothesis that overall risk taking differs between sports participants and non-participants. Secondary analyses enable more nuanced conclusions about domain-specific differences, dose-response associations, and potential gender effects. While cross-sectional data cannot establish causality, they can highlight robust associations and generate hypotheses for longitudinal research. Finally, the study engages with theoretical frameworks that address selection and socialization processes and assesses implications for sport psychology and public health.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a mid-sized university through campus-wide announcements, email lists, and flyers posted in common areas. Participation was voluntary and open to undergraduate and postgraduate students aged 18 or above. A total of 212 students enrolled and completed the study. Of these, 12 records were excluded from analyses due to incomplete data on the DOSPERT or missing information on sport participation, resulting in a final sample of 200 participants. The sample included 200 university students, of whom 102 self-identified as sports participants according to the study criteria and 98 were classified as non-sports participants. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 30 years, with a mean age of 21.4 years (SD = 2.7). The sample was 56% female and 44% male. Gender distribution was similar across groups, with 55% female in the sports group and 57% female in the non-sports group, reducing the likelihood of gender confounding.

Inclusion criteria required participants to be currently enrolled students and to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria were limited to inability to complete the online questionnaire in English and current injury or medical condition that precluded any sport participation, as these could complicate the classification of sport participation. The study procedures were reviewed and approved by the university ethics committee, and participants were informed that their responses would be confidential and used for research purposes only.

Group assignment was based on self-reported sport participation over the previous six months. Participants were asked to report whether they had participated in organized or informal sport, the type(s) of sport, and the typical number of hours per week spent in sport. Participants who reported at least three hours per week of sport participation over the prior six months or who indicated membership on a university team or formal club were classified as sports participants. Participants who reported less than one hour per week of sport participation were classified as non-sports participants. Individuals reporting between one and three hours per week were not included in the analysis to create a clear contrast between groups, and such borderline cases accounted for the 12 excluded records mentioned above. This approach aimed to minimize misclassification and to establish distinct group boundaries for comparison.

The primary instrument for assessing risk taking was the Domain-Specific Risk-Taking (DOSPERT) scale. The DOSPERT is a self-report questionnaire composed of items that assess the likelihood of engaging in risky activities across multiple domains. For the purposes of the present study, we used the standard five-domain version, which includes subscales for ethical risk taking, financial risk taking (divided into investment and gambling-related items in some versions, but treated here as a single financial subscale), health/safety risk taking, recreational risk taking, and social risk taking. Each item asks participants to rate how likely they would be to engage in the described behavior on a Likert scale from 1 (extremely unlikely) to 5 (extremely likely). Domain scores were computed by averaging the items within each subscale, and an overall DOSPERT score was computed by averaging across all items to reflect generalized risk propensity. The DOSPERT demonstrates adequate internal consistency for both subscales and the total score in student samples. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alpha for the overall scale was .88, and subscale alphas ranged from .74 (ethical) to .82 (recreational), indicating acceptable reliability.

In addition to the DOSPERT, participants provided demographic information including age, gender, year of study, and self-reported academic discipline. They also reported frequency and type of sport participation, whether participation was competitive or purely recreational, and average hours per week spent in sport. These variables were used both to categorize participants and to explore dose-response relationships between sport involvement and risk-taking tendencies.

The survey was administered online using a secure university-hosted platform. Participants completed the measures in a single session that took approximately 15 minutes. To reduce potential bias, the order of items was fixed, beginning with demographic questions, followed by sports participation items, and concluding with the DOSPERT. Attention-check items were embedded to identify inattentive responding; participants who failed two or more attention checks were excluded from analysis. Completion of the survey implied informed consent, and participants were offered entry into a draw for a small voucher as compensation. No deception was used.

Statistical analysis procedures were established a priori. Preliminary analyses included descriptive statistics and checks for normality and homogeneity of variances. Group differences in demographic variables were tested using t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. The primary analysis compared the overall DOSPERT score between sports participants and non-participants using an independent-samples t-test. Effect sizes were computed using Cohen’s d with pooled standard deviation. To examine domain-specific patterns, independent-samples t-tests were conducted for each DOSPERT subscale. Given multiple comparisons across five subscales, a Bonferroni correction was applied to control the familywise error rate, yielding an adjusted alpha of .01 for subscale comparisons (.05/5 = .01). Secondary analyses included Pearson correlations between hours per week of sport participation and DOSPERT scores, and a hierarchical multiple regression predicting overall DOSPERT score from hours of sport participation while controlling for age and gender in the first step.

Assumption checks were performed for t-tests and regression analyses. Normality of residuals was assessed using skewness and kurtosis statistics as well as visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. For regression, multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and residual plots were examined for homoscedasticity and linearity. Where t-test assumptions were violated, nonparametric counterparts were considered; however, given the large enough sample and approximate normality, parametric tests were presumed robust.

Exploratory analyses examined potential gender moderation of the association between sport participation and risk taking. This was tested using a two-way ANOVA with sport group and gender as factors predicting overall DOSPERT score, and by testing the interaction term in a regression model. Additional exploratory contrasts compared competitive versus recreational sports participants where sample sizes permitted. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and significance was assessed at p < .05 unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the key variables are reported first. The overall DOSPERT score across the sample had a mean of 3.15 and a standard deviation of 0.62. For sports participants, the mean overall DOSPERT score was 3.45 (SD = 0.58), whereas for non-sports participants the mean was 2.84 (SD = 0.55). The distributions of scores appeared approximately normal in both groups by visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Skewness and kurtosis values for the overall DOSPERT were within acceptable ranges (skewness = 0.21, kurtosis = -0.34), indicating no severe departures from normality. Levene’s test for equality of variances on the overall DOSPERT score indicated homogeneity of variance (F = 1.12, p = .29), supporting the use of the pooled-variance t-test.

The primary analysis compared overall DOSPERT scores between sports participants and non-participants. An independent-samples t-test revealed that sports participants had significantly higher overall DOSPERT scores than non-sports participants, t(198) = 6.27, p < .001. The effect size for this difference was medium-to-large, with Cohen’s d = 0.89, indicating a robust difference in overall risk-taking propensity between the groups. This finding supported the primary hypothesis that sports people would exhibit elevated risk-taking tendencies relative to non-sports people.

Domain-specific analyses revealed a nuanced pattern of results. Independent-samples t-tests were conducted for each DOSPERT subscale. For the recreational risk subscale, sports participants scored substantially higher (M = 3.78, SD = 0.61) than non-sports participants (M = 2.95, SD = 0.64), t(198) = 9.04, p < .001, with Cohen’s d = 1.27. This large effect suggested that recreational risk taking is particularly elevated among sports participants. For the health/safety subscale, sports participants again scored higher (M = 3.56, SD = 0.69) than non-sports participants (M = 3.05, SD = 0.61), t(198) = 5.21, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.73. This medium-to-large effect is consistent with the idea that engagement in sport correlates with greater acceptance of physical risks.

On the social risk subscale, the difference between groups was smaller but still statistically significant, with sports participants reporting higher social risk taking (M = 3.28, SD = 0.51) than non-sports participants (M = 3.02, SD = 0.47), t(198) = 3.72, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.53. For the financial risk subscale, the difference was modest and did not reach the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of .01; sports participants had slightly higher financial risk scores (M = 2.98, SD = 0.60) compared to non-sports participants (M = 2.83, SD = 0.58), t(198) = 1.93, p = .055, Cohen’s d = 0.25. For the ethical risk subscale, no significant difference emerged between groups (sports M = 2.12, SD = 0.71; non-sports M = 2.08, SD = 0.69), t(198) = 0.48, p = .63, Cohen’s d = 0.06. After applying the Bonferroni correction, the recreational and health/safety subscale differences remained significant, and the social subscale difference was borderline but noteworthy.

Correlation analyses examined the relationship between hours per week of sport participation and DOSPERT scores. Among the full sample, hours of sport participation was positively correlated with overall DOSPERT score, r = .41, p < .001, indicating a moderate association. Similar patterns were observed for subscales; hours per week was strongly correlated with recreational risk taking (r = .54, p < .001) and with health/safety risk taking (r = .39, p < .001). Correlations with social risk taking (r = .21, p = .003) were weaker but significant, whereas correlations with financial (r = .09, p = .18) and ethical risk taking (r = .03, p = .67) were negligible.

A hierarchical multiple regression was conducted to test whether hours per week of sport participation predicted overall DOSPERT score after controlling for age and gender. In the first step, age and gender were entered and together explained a small but statistically significant portion of variance in DOSPERT scores (R^2 = .05, F(2,197) = 5.18, p = .006). Gender was a significant predictor in this step, with males reporting higher DOSPERT scores on average than females (β = .22, p = .002). In the second step, hours per week of sport participation was added to the model and accounted for a significant incremental increase in explained variance (ΔR^2 = .13, ΔF(1,196) = 31.9, p < .001). In the full model, hours per week remained a robust predictor of overall DOSPERT score (β = .36, p < .001), while gender retained a smaller but significant effect (β = .17, p = .01). Age was not a significant predictor in the full model (β = -.05, p = .40). Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated acceptable VIF values (< 1.2), suggesting that predictors were not highly intercorrelated.

Assumption checks for the regression model indicated that residuals were approximately normally distributed and homoscedastic. Visual inspection of scatterplots did not reveal strong departures from linearity. No extreme outliers were detected based on standardized residuals and Cook’s distance values. Therefore, the regression results were considered reliable.

Exploratory analyses examined potential gender moderation of the sports group effect. A two-way ANOVA with sport group (sports vs. non-sports) and gender (male vs. female) predicting overall DOSPERT yielded significant main effects of sport group (F(1,196) = 39.3, p < .001) and gender (F(1,196) = 10.6, p = .001). The interaction between sport group and gender was not significant (F(1,196) = 1.12, p = .29), indicating that the group difference in risk taking was present for both males and females and did not differ appreciably by gender. Follow-up simple effects confirmed that sports participants scored higher than non-sports participants within both male and female subsamples.

Further exploratory contrasts compared competitive sports participants (those reporting university team membership or competitive club participation) and recreational-only sports participants. Competitive participants (n = 48) had slightly higher overall DOSPERT scores (M = 3.57, SD = 0.52) than recreational-only participants (M = 3.34, SD = 0.60), t(100) = 2.05, p = .043, Cohen’s d = 0.40. This suggests that the intensity or level of competitive engagement may be associated with elevated risk propensity, although sample sizes for these contrasts were limited and the effect was smaller than the primary group difference.

Given the cross-sectional design, we considered whether sport participation might reflect reverse causality or confounding. Partial correlations controlling for gender and age indicated that the association between hours per week of sport and overall DOSPERT remained significant (partial r = .35, p < .001), suggesting that the relationship was not solely attributable to demographic variables.

Finally, sensitivity analyses considered whether removing potential outliers or restricting analyses to those aged 18-24 would change the pattern of results. The main findings were robust across these sensitivity checks, with sports participants consistently showing higher overall and domain-specific risk taking relative to non-sports participants.

Discussion

The present study set out to examine whether university students who participate in sport differ from their non-participating peers in self-reported risk-taking propensity. Using the DOSPERT scale to capture both overall and domain-specific tendencies, we observed that sports participants reported significantly higher overall risk-taking scores than non-sports participants. This primary result was robust and accompanied by medium-to-large effect sizes. Domain-specific analyses revealed that the largest differences were in recreational and health/safety risk taking, which is theoretically coherent given the physical and novel aspects of many sporting activities. Social risk taking was also elevated among sports participants, though to a lesser degree, and no meaningful differences were observed in ethical or financial risk domains. Hours per week of sport participation correlated positively with overall DOSPERT scores, and this relationship held after controlling for age and gender. Exploratory contrasts suggested that competitive sports participants might exhibit slightly higher risk propensity than recreational-only participants, although that effect was modest.

These findings contribute to the literature in several ways. First, they provide clear evidence of an association between sport participation and elevated risk-taking propensity in a university student sample. The observed patterns align with the view that sports attract or cultivate individuals who are more inclined to accept risk, particularly in domains relevant to physical activity and recreational engagement. Second, the domain-specificity observed—strong effects for recreational and health/safety domains but not for ethical or financial domains—supports the idea that sport-related risk attitudes are not simply a reflection of global recklessness but are targeted toward areas that are salient in sporting contexts. Third, the positive association between hours per week in sport and DOSPERT scores suggests a dose-response relationship, pointing to the possibility that either stronger selection into sports by high-risk individuals or socialization through increased exposure to sport contexts amplifies risk acceptance.

The current results resonate with prior theoretical and empirical work linking personality traits to sporting outcomes. For instance, Allen and Laborde’s research emphasized the importance of personality characteristics for sporting performance, and our findings extend that perspective by showing that risk-taking propensity is associated with sport participation itself. Such associations can be interpreted within two broad frameworks: selection and socialization. The selection account posits that individuals with higher baseline risk tolerance are more likely to choose sports that involve physical challenges, novelty, and uncertain outcomes. This account is consistent with the strong correlation between self-reported sport hours and DOSPERT scores, as well as with the larger effect sizes observed for recreational and health/safety domains, which likely reflect pre-existing proclivities.

The socialization account proposes that engagement in sport fosters increased comfort with risk through repeated exposure, skill acquisition, and normative shifts within sport communities. For example, participating in a university team may expose individuals to coaching that normalizes calculated risk taking, to peers who valorize daring behaviors, and to structured environments where risk can be managed through equipment and training. Over time, these experiences could recalibrate individuals’ attitudes toward risk, making them more willing to engage in riskier behaviors in relevant domains. While the cross-sectional nature of the present study does not permit causal inference, the positive association between hours of participation and risk propensity is compatible with socialization effects, and longitudinal studies could test whether increases in sport involvement precede increases in risk acceptance.

A combined perspective—where selection and socialization interact—is perhaps most plausible. Initial risk-prone tendencies may lead individuals to enter sport, and subsequent participation may further reinforce or refine those tendencies. This bidirectional view aligns with developmental perspectives in which personality and behavior co-evolve over time. For example, a student with moderate risk tolerance may try out team sports, experience positive reinforcement for risk-taking decisions in competitive situations, and gradually adopt a higher risk-accepting stance relevant to recreational and health/safety domains. Conversely, institutional structures and coaching practices could moderate these trajectories by emphasizing risk management and safety, thereby attenuating any increases in risk propensity attributable to participation.

The domain-specific findings have practical implications. Elevated recreational and health/safety risk taking among sports participants suggests that sport-related behaviors might translate to broader patterns of physical risk acceptance. While this propensity can be adaptive in sport—where calculated risk taking can facilitate performance and skill development—it also raises concerns about injury risk, both within sport and in other settings. Coaches and sports administrators should balance the performance benefits of risk acceptance with the need to teach athletes effective risk assessment and injury prevention strategies. Interventions that promote informed decision-making, situational risk assessment, and adherence to safety protocols could potentially harness the performance advantages of adaptive risk taking while mitigating harm.

The absence of meaningful differences in ethical and financial risk domains indicates that the relationship between sport participation and risk propensity is not indiscriminate. Sports participants are not merely more willing to take any kind of risk; rather, their elevated propensity is concentrated in domains that are ecologically relevant to sport. This specificity suggests that interventions should be targeted. For instance, programs aiming to reduce risky behaviors outside of sport (e.g., irresponsible financial gambles or unethical decisions) may not need to be tailored specifically to athletes more than to peers, whereas injury prevention programs and education on health-related choices may be particularly relevant.

Gender effects emerged as expected, with males reporting somewhat higher overall risk-taking than females. Importantly, the sports-versus-non-sports difference persisted within both genders and the interaction between group and gender was not significant. This suggests that sport participation is associated with increased risk propensity for both males and females, and that gender differences do not fully account for the group effect. These findings underscore that risk-related personality attributes linked to sport participation are not solely a byproduct of gender composition in sporting contexts.

Several limitations of the present study warrant consideration. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding whether risk propensity leads to sport participation or vice versa. Longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to disentangle selection from socialization effects. The reliance on self-report measures introduces potential biases, including social desirability and recall inaccuracies. Although the DOSPERT is a validated instrument with acceptable reliability, future work could complement self-report with behavioral measures of risk taking or observational data from sporting contexts. The sample was drawn from a single university, and while the sample size provided adequate power for primary analyses, the generalizability to other age groups, cultural contexts, or elite athlete populations is uncertain. The operational definition of sports participation—based on self-reported hours per week and membership—was pragmatic but may not capture qualitative differences among sports, such as contact vs. non-contact, team vs. individual, or risk level associated with specific disciplines. Future research should examine whether associations differ by sport type, intensity, or competitive level.

Another limitation relates to potential confounding variables that were not assessed, such as socioeconomic status, prior injury history, and substance use. These factors could influence both sport participation and risk-taking propensities. For instance, individuals from certain backgrounds may have differential access to organized sport and different normative attitudes toward risk. Including a broader set of covariates in future studies would allow for more stringent tests of the robustness of the association between sport participation and risk taking.

Future research directions are abundant. Longitudinal studies tracking students over time as they enter or leave sport programs would be instrumental in testing causal pathways. Experimental interventions that manipulate exposure to sport-like risk management training could evaluate whether structured experiences in sport influence risk attitudes. Studies that include physiological or neurocognitive measures of risk sensitivity, such as response to reward-punishment contingencies or indices of impulsivity, could examine underlying mechanisms. Cross-cultural investigations could explore how cultural norms around sport and risk influence the observed associations. Moreover, research that distinguishes among sport types—extreme sports versus low-contact team sports, for example—could clarify whether high-risk sporting contexts amplify risk propensity more than low-risk contexts.

Theoretical integration across lines of inquiry could be fruitful. For instance, models of personality development that incorporate niche-picking and niche-building processes align with the bidirectional perspective put forward here. Athletes may engage in niche-picking by selecting sports that fit their dispositional tendencies, and through niche-building, they may reshape their own dispositions via reinforcement and practice in sporting environments. Embedding sport participation in such developmental frameworks could link micro-level mechanisms to macro-level patterns of behavior.

Practically, the results suggest that sport programs, particularly at the university level, should consider incorporating education on risk awareness and decision making into training curricula. Doing so could preserve the motivational and performance benefits associated with adaptive risk taking while reducing preventable harms. Tailored approaches that recognize domain specificity are likely to be more effective than generic risk-reduction programs. For instance, interventions focused on injury prevention techniques may be more relevant for athletes than interventions focused primarily on financial decision-making.

Finally, the study’s findings speak to broader questions about the role of dispositional traits in performance contexts. If risk-taking propensity is both a correlate and a contributor to sporting participation and performance, then assessments of risk-related traits might assist in talent identification and individualized coaching. However, caution is warranted because elevated risk acceptance can yield both benefits and costs. Any application of personality assessment in sport should be embedded in ethical guidelines that respect athlete autonomy and prioritize safety.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated that university students who participate in sport report higher overall risk-taking propensity than non-participating peers, with pronounced differences in recreational and health/safety domains. These findings are consistent with theoretical perspectives that emphasize the role of personality in sporting engagement and performance and suggest both selection and socialization pathways. While the cross-sectional design limits causal claims, the results highlight important domain-specific patterns and practical considerations for sport psychology and athlete welfare. Future longitudinal and experimental work is needed to clarify causal mechanisms and to translate these insights into interventions that maximize the benefits of adaptive risk taking while minimizing harm.
