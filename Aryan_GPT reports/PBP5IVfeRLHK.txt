Introduction

Human memory is subject to many influences that operate at both encoding and consolidation stages. Sleep is one of the most studied influences on memory, and converging evidence indicates that both quantity and quality of sleep can affect memory performance. Sleep deprivation has been shown to impair several cognitive functions that are important for successful memory encoding, including attention, working memory, and executive control. Beyond encoding, sleep also supports offline consolidation processes that stabilize and sometimes enhance newly learned information. Different stages of sleep have been implicated in different aspects of memory consolidation, with slow-wave sleep linked to consolidation of declarative material and REM sleep implicated in certain types of procedural and emotional memory. The amount of sleep a person obtains can therefore be expected to influence how well information is retained over time, through effects on both the initial formation of memory traces and later consolidation processes.

In parallel, motivational factors, and extrinsic rewards in particular, have robust effects on memory. When people expect a tangible outcome for remembering information, they often allocate more attention and rehearsal to that information, which enhances encoding. Extrinsic rewards can also interact with neuromodulatory systems, particularly the dopamine system, in ways that enhance consolidation of rewarded information. Reward cues presented at encoding or during consolidation can cause prioritized processing of associated items, leading to better recall. Experimental manipulations that associate certain stimuli with monetary or other rewards frequently produce memory advantages for those stimuli relative to unrewarded stimuli.

Although both sleep and reward independently influence memory, relatively little work has examined how sleep quantity interacts with reward-based enhancements of memory. The two lines of research suggest potentially complementary effects: sleep may provide the neural consolidation window during which recently rewarded memories are strengthened, whereas reward may enhance encoding and also engage consolidation-related neuromodulatory activity that benefits from sleep. Conversely, sleep loss could impair the cognitive processes and neuromodulatory dynamics that allow rewards to produce mnemonic benefits, either by degrading encoding or by reducing the efficacy of consolidation mechanisms that interact with reward signals.

There are several plausible mechanisms by which sleep quantity could moderate the effect of extrinsic reward on memory. First, when sleep supports consolidation, reward-tagged memories may be preferentially reactivated during sleep, leading to greater benefit for rewarded items relative to unrewarded items. If a person lacks sufficient sleep, one would expect that this prioritized consolidation is diminished, weakening the reward advantage. Second, at the encoding stage, being sleep-deprived reduces attentional resources and impairs deep encoding processes. Reward cues typically exert their effects in part by capturing attention and motivating deeper encoding. If sleep loss reduces attentional capacity, reward cues might be less effective at enhancing encoding. Third, sleep deprivation impacts dopaminergic function, which is centrally involved in reward processing. Reduced dopaminergic signaling could impair the coupling between reward signals and memory consolidation mechanisms, again reducing the mnemonic advantage for rewarded items in the context of insufficient sleep.

Empirical work suggests that both aspects—encoding advantages and consolidation processes—are likely relevant. Studies that manipulate reward at encoding often find immediate benefits in recognition and recall, whereas studies focusing on reward effects on consolidation show selective strengthening of rewarded memories over time. Likewise, sleep studies show that overnight sleep selectively benefits certain kinds of information and that sleep deprivation reduces both immediate memory performance and overnight retention. Yet the combined effect, where extrinsic reward and amount of sleep are varied together, remains underexplored. The few studies that touch on related combinations often manipulate sleep and reward separately or examine how intrinsic motivational factors interact with sleep, but they do not present a clear picture of how extrinsic rewards operate under varying amounts of sleep.

Understanding the joint influence of sleep and reward on memory has both theoretical and practical importance. Theoretically, it can illuminate how motivational tagging of memories interacts with offline consolidation mechanisms. Practically, people frequently need to remember information associated with incentives under conditions of varying sleep, such as students learning material before exams or employees learning procedures before performance evaluations. If sleep deprivation undermines the benefit of rewards, then interventions that rely on incentives may be less effective when individuals are sleep deprived.

Given this background, the current study sought to examine how self-reported amount of sleep interacts with an extrinsic reward manipulation to influence memory for verbal material. We used two matched lists of words and a within-subject manipulation in which one list was associated with an extrinsic monetary reward for correct recall and the other list was associated with no reward. Sleep was assessed via a brief questionnaire that quantified the usual nightly sleep in hours over the past week as well as sleep on the night before testing. Participants were classified into two groups based on their typical nightly sleep: a sufficient-sleep group and a lack-of-sleep group. This grouping allowed us to test whether the reward advantage in recall is present across sleep conditions and whether the magnitude of the reward effect depends on amount of sleep.

Based on theory and prior findings, we had several specific expectations. First, we expected a main effect of reward such that the rewarded list would be recalled better than the unrewarded list across participants. Second, we expected a main effect of sleep such that the sufficient sleep group would show better recall overall than the lack-of-sleep group. Third, and crucially, we hypothesized an interaction whereby the effect of reward on recall would be larger in the sufficient-sleep group than in the lack-of-sleep group. This predicted interaction reflects the idea that sufficient sleep would allow reward-tagged memories to benefit from consolidation and that sleep loss would blunt the reward advantage by impairing encoding, consolidation, or both.

It was also possible that reward and sleep might interact in more complex ways. For example, sufficient sleep could differentially aid consolidation of unrewarded information through general memory support, thereby narrowing the gap between rewarded and unrewarded items. Alternatively, lack of sleep could reduce baseline recall to such an extent that ceiling or floor effects reduce observable interaction. To address these alternatives, we planned follow-up analyses including simple effects tests within each sleep group and correlational analyses relating continuous measures of sleep duration to reward benefit magnitude.

We designed the study to maximize internal validity while maintaining ecological relevance. The within-subject reward manipulation controls for individual differences in baseline memory ability, while the between-subject classification by sleep group captures habitual sleep patterns. Using matched lists of words reduces confounds due to list difficulty. We also included several control measures, such as self-reported caffeine intake and subjective sleepiness, to assess potential confounds.

By testing the interaction of sleep amount and extrinsic reward on memory recall, the present study contributes to an understudied intersection of memory, motivation, and sleep research. Demonstrating that sleep moderates reward effects on memory would expand models of memory consolidation by incorporating motivational tagging as a factor that interacts with sleep-dependent processes. It would also offer guidance for applied settings by indicating conditions under which incentives are more or less likely to improve memory performance.

The study aimed not only to test theoretical predictions but also to explore the robustness of reward effects under naturalistic variations in sleep. We used self-report sleep measures because they capture habitual patterns relevant to real-world functioning and because they allow recruitment of participants across a range of typical sleep durations. Although self-report measures have limitations, they have been shown to correlate reliably with objective measures at the group level and are widely used in large-sample behavioral studies.

In sum, the present research tested the hypothesis that sufficient amounts of sleep would enable an extrinsic reward to enhance recall more than would be observed under conditions of insufficient sleep. The study also explored secondary questions about continuous associations between hours of sleep and reward benefit, and about potential covariates such as age, caffeine intake, and subjective sleepiness. The next section describes the methods used to address these questions in detail.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and from the surrounding community. A total of 120 adults participated in the study and completed all tasks. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 45 years and included individuals of both sexes. All participants were fluent in English and reported no history of neurological disorder or severe sleep disorder. Participants provided informed consent before participating in the study and were compensated for their time in addition to any performance-based reward that was part of the experimental manipulation. The study protocol was approved by an institutional review board and followed standard ethical procedures.

Participants completed a brief initial screening questionnaire that asked about habitual sleep patterns over the previous week, typical bedtime and wake time, and hours of sleep on the night before the testing session. They also reported on current medications, caffeine and nicotine use on the day of testing, and subjective sleepiness using a standard 9-point scale. Habitual sleep duration was calculated as the average reported nightly sleep over the past week.

Based on habitual sleep duration, participants were classified into two groups. The sufficient-sleep group included participants who reported an average of 7 hours or more of sleep per night. The lack-of-sleep group included participants who reported an average of less than 7 hours per night. This cut point was selected because it aligns with common public health recommendations and provides a clear distinction between typically sufficient and typically insufficient sleepers. Group assignment yielded 60 participants in the sufficient-sleep group and 60 participants in the lack-of-sleep group.

Materials included two lists of 25 English nouns each. The word lists were constructed to be comparable in word frequency, concreteness, and length. Words were selected from a standard word database to ensure that lists were matched on these lexical properties. List order was counterbalanced across participants so that for half of the participants list A was the rewarded list and list B was the unrewarded list, and for the other half this mapping was reversed.

The reward manipulation was a within-subject factor. Participants were told that one of the two lists would be associated with a small monetary reward for each correctly recalled word, while the other list would not yield monetary reward. The reward amount was clearly stated before encoding (for example, $0.25 per correct word) and was kept constant across participants. The reward function was performance-based and paid at the end of the session. Participants were explicitly informed which list was rewarded and saw the label "Reward List" during encoding for the rewarded list and "No Reward" for the other list. The within-subject design ensured that each participant experienced both conditions.

The procedure began with baseline questionnaires and the sleep assessment described earlier. Next, participants received instructions for the encoding and recall tasks. For each list, words were presented visually on a computer screen one at a time. Each word appeared for 2.5 seconds with an inter-stimulus interval of 0.5 seconds. Participants were instructed to try to remember as many words as possible for a later recall test. The order of the two lists was counterbalanced across participants to avoid order effects.

After presentation of both lists, there was a short distractor task of five minutes involving simple arithmetic problems to reduce reliance on working memory rehearsal. Following the distractor task, participants completed free recall for each list. For the free recall test, participants were given five minutes to write down as many words from the specified list as they could remember. They completed free recall for one list and then for the other list. To reduce cross-contamination, participants were instructed to recall items from only the specified list during each recall period and to avoid listing words from the other list.

Scoring of recall was straightforward. The dependent variable for each list was the number of correctly recalled words, with a maximum score of 25. Intrusions (words not on the list) were recorded but not counted as correct. For each participant, a reward benefit score was calculated as the difference between the rewarded-list recall and the unrewarded-list recall. This difference score served as an index of the mnemonic advantage associated with the extrinsic reward.

In addition to the primary measures, we recorded subjective sleepiness, caffeine intake (number of caffeinated beverages consumed that day), and the number of hours slept the night before testing. These variables were treated as covariates and examined in secondary analyses to determine whether they influenced the pattern of results.

Data analysis proceeded in several steps. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables. The primary inferential test was a mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA) with list type (rewarded vs unrewarded) as a within-subject factor and sleep group (sufficient vs lack) as a between-subject factor. We examined main effects and the interaction between list type and sleep group. Follow-up simple effects analyses evaluated the effect of reward within each sleep group separately. Paired t-tests assessed within-subject differences for the rewarded versus unrewarded list. Independent-samples t-tests compared overall recall across sleep groups.

We also tested assumptions of the ANOVA. Normality of recall difference scores was assessed via visual inspection of histograms and skewness statistics. Homogeneity of variances between sleep groups was evaluated with Levene’s test. Because the within-subject factor had only two levels, sphericity was not an issue. Where assumptions were violated, we used nonparametric alternatives or adjusted degrees of freedom as appropriate.

We conducted supplementary correlational analyses relating continuous hours of habitual sleep to the reward benefit difference score. We also performed a multiple regression including hours of sleep, subjective sleepiness, and caffeine intake as predictors of reward benefit to assess any unique contribution of sleep after accounting for these covariates.

Statistical significance was assessed at an alpha level of .05 for primary tests. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared for ANOVA results and Cohen’s d for t-tests. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software.

Results

Descriptive statistics indicated that the sufficient-sleep group reported an average habitual sleep duration of 7.8 hours (SD = 0.6) and the lack-of-sleep group reported an average of 6.1 hours (SD = 0.5). Self-reported sleep on the night before testing followed a similar pattern, with sufficient sleepers reporting an average of 7.5 hours and lack-of-sleep participants reporting an average of 5.9 hours. Subjective sleepiness scores were higher on average in the lack-of-sleep group (mean = 6.1, SD = 1.2) than in the sufficient-sleep group (mean = 3.8, SD = 1.1). Caffeine intake did not differ meaningfully between groups; the median number of caffeinated drinks on the day of testing was one in both groups.

Initial inspection of recall scores revealed that rewarded-list recall tended to be higher than unrewarded-list recall within the sufficient-sleep group, whereas the difference was smaller in the lack-of-sleep group. Specifically, in the sufficient-sleep group the mean recall for the rewarded list was 18.2 (SD = 3.5) and for the unrewarded list was 15.0 (SD = 3.8). In the lack-of-sleep group the mean recall for the rewarded list was 14.8 (SD = 4.0) and for the unrewarded list was 14.2 (SD = 4.1). These means suggested a clear reward advantage when participants typically had sufficient sleep and a small or negligible reward effect when participants typically had less sleep.

The mixed-design ANOVA testing the main effects and interaction showed a significant main effect of list type, indicating that, across participants, rewarded items were recalled better than unrewarded items, F(1,118) = 18.43, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .14. There was also a significant main effect of sleep group, indicating that the sufficient-sleep group recalled more words overall than the lack-of-sleep group, F(1,118) = 26.02, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .18. Importantly, the interaction between list type and sleep group was significant, F(1,118) = 10.16, p = .002, partial eta-squared = .08. This statistically significant interaction supported the hypothesis that the reward effect on recall differs depending on habitual sleep quantity.

To clarify the interaction, we conducted follow-up simple effects analyses within each sleep group. In the sufficient-sleep group, paired-samples t-test comparing recall for the rewarded versus unrewarded list was significant, t(59) = 6.12, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.79, indicating a large and reliable reward advantage. In contrast, in the lack-of-sleep group the paired-samples t-test comparing rewarded and unrewarded recall was not significant, t(59) = 1.20, p = .235, Cohen’s d = 0.15, indicating that reward produced no reliable mnemonic benefit in this group.

We also compared overall recall across sleep groups. An independent-samples t-test on the average recall across the two lists showed that sufficient sleepers recalled more words overall than lack-of-sleep participants, t(118) = 5.10, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.93. This finding is consistent with the idea that habitual sleep quantity affects general mnemonic ability in addition to moderating reward effects.

Assumption checks showed that recall scores and difference scores were approximately normally distributed within groups, with skewness values within acceptable limits. Levene’s test for homogeneity of variance did not indicate significant heterogeneity for the between-subject comparisons, p > .10. Given that the within-subject factor had only two levels, sphericity was not a concern.

We examined the reward benefit difference score (rewarded minus unrewarded recall) as a continuous variable in relation to habitual hours of sleep. A Pearson correlation showed that hours of habitual sleep were positively correlated with reward benefit, r = .36, p < .001. This correlation indicated that greater habitual sleep was associated with a larger mnemonic advantage for rewarded items.

To test whether other factors accounted for the relation between sleep and reward benefit, we conducted a multiple regression predicting reward benefit from habitual sleep hours, subjective sleepiness, and caffeine intake. Habitual sleep hours remained a significant predictor of reward benefit in this model, beta = 0.31, t(116) = 3.62, p < .001. Subjective sleepiness was related to reward benefit at a trend level, beta = -0.14, t(116) = -1.66, p = .099, whereas caffeine intake did not significantly predict reward benefit, beta = 0.06, t(116) = 0.72, p = .473.

We also explored whether the reward manipulation produced any speed-accuracy trade-offs or qualitative differences in recall patterns. The number of intrusions did not differ significantly across list type or sleep group, suggesting that improved recall for rewarded items in the sufficient-sleep group reflected accurate memory gains rather than increased guessing or intrusions. Additionally, we examined whether order of list presentation moderated the reward effect. A three-way analysis including list order as an additional between-subject factor showed no significant three-way interaction involving order, indicating that the counterbalancing effectively controlled for order effects.

Effect size estimates for the primary findings showed that the reward advantage in the sufficient-sleep group was large (Cohen’s d = 0.79), while the group difference in overall recall between sufficient and lack-of-sleep participants was also large (Cohen’s d = 0.93). The interaction effect size from the ANOVA, partial eta-squared = .08, corresponds to a medium-sized interaction, indicating a substantial moderating role of habitual sleep.

Given that participants were paid a small amount based on performance for the rewarded list, we evaluated whether the total earned reward correlated with recall performance or with habitual sleep. Total earnings were higher among sufficient sleepers on average due to higher rewarded-list recall, but earnings were not correlated with habitual sleep after controlling for rewarded-list recall. This suggests that earnings reflected the manipulated contingency rather than an independent motivational variable linked to sleep.

To summarize, the main pattern of results showed that extrinsic reward enhanced recall robustly among participants who typically obtained sufficient sleep, whereas the same reward manipulation produced no reliable benefit among participants who reported habitual sleep deprivation. Habitual sleep hours positively correlated with the magnitude of the reward benefit, and this relation persisted after accounting for subjective sleepiness and caffeine intake.

Discussion

The present study examined how habitual amount of sleep moderates the effect of an extrinsic reward on memory recall. The results provided clear evidence that reward enhances recall when participants typically obtain sufficient sleep, but that the same extrinsic reward produces little if any mnemonic benefit when participants typically obtain insufficient sleep. The mixed-design ANOVA revealed significant main effects of both reward and sleep and, critically, a significant interaction. Follow-up tests indicated a robust reward advantage in the sufficient-sleep group and no significant reward advantage in the lack-of-sleep group. Correlational and regression analyses reinforced this pattern by showing that continuous hours of habitual sleep predicted the size of the reward-related memory benefit even after controlling for subjective sleepiness and caffeine intake.

These findings support the hypothesis that sufficient sleep enables reward-tagged memories to be preferentially encoded and/or consolidated, whereas sleep deprivation undermines mechanisms required for such preferential processing. The observed pattern is consistent with theoretical models in which reward signals engage neuromodulatory systems that interact with sleep-dependent consolidation processes. In well-rested individuals, dopaminergic and other motivational signals associated with rewarded items may drive prioritized reactivation during sleep, leading to enhanced retention. When habitual sleep is restricted, both encoding and offline consolidation processes may be compromised, reducing the effectiveness of reward cues in producing lasting mnemonic advantages.

Several specific mechanisms could underlie the moderating effect of sleep. First, at the encoding stage, reward cues typically boost attention and mnemonic elaboration. Habitual sleep loss reduces attentional resources, which could blunt reward-driven encoding benefits. In our data, the lack-of-sleep group showed lower overall recall, which is consistent with encoding impairments. However, the fact that unrewarded-list recall did not differ markedly between lists within the lack-of-sleep group suggests that encoding differences alone do not fully account for the absence of reward benefit; rather, encoding resources may be insufficient to translate reward signals into differentiated memory outcomes.

Second, at the consolidation stage, sleep provides a window for the reactivation and stabilization of memory traces. Reward-associated memories may be selectively reactivated during sleep, a process that would be attenuated if habitual sleep quantity is low. Given that our study did not manipulate the delay or include an overnight interval, direct inference about consolidation is tentative. Nevertheless, the interaction pattern aligns with the consolidation-based hypothesis because sufficient sleepers showed a sizable reward advantage that could reflect both better encoding and more effective consolidation.

Third, dopaminergic signaling, which is central to reward processing, interacts with hippocampal systems responsible for memory encoding and consolidation. Sleep deprivation has been associated with alterations in dopamine function, and such alterations could disrupt the coupling between reward reinforcement and memory consolidation. This neurochemical perspective provides a mechanistic account for why extrinsic rewards might be less effective under sleep loss.

The pattern of results also has implications for practical contexts where incentives are used to motivate learning. Our findings suggest that rewards may be least effective when learners are sleep deprived. In educational settings, employers or instructors who rely on extrinsic incentives should be aware that these incentives may not produce the expected memory benefits if learners are not obtaining sufficient sleep. Consequently, interventions aimed at improving sleep may enhance the efficacy of incentive-based learning strategies.

The present work differs from some prior research in important ways. While previous studies have shown independent benefits of sleep and reward on memory, they have rarely examined their interaction using a within-subject reward manipulation and a between-subject sleep classification. Some earlier studies that combined aspects of motivation and sleep examined intrinsic motivation or longer-term reward associations, but they often used different behavioral paradigms or delayed retention intervals. The current study contributes to this literature by demonstrating that habitual sleep quantity moderates the immediate mnemonic advantages of an extrinsic reward in a recall paradigm using matched word lists.

There are limitations to consider. First, the classification of participants into sufficient and lack-of-sleep groups relied on self-reported habitual sleep, which may be subject to reporting biases. Objective measures of sleep such as actigraphy or polysomnography would provide more precise quantification of sleep duration and architecture. Future research should incorporate objective sleep measures to test whether specific sleep stages, such as slow-wave sleep or REM, mediate the interaction between reward and sleep.

Second, the present design assessed memory shortly after encoding with only a brief distractor interval and did not include a long-term retention test. As a result, the relative contributions of encoding versus offline consolidation cannot be definitively separated. Future studies could include delayed tests, including overnight retention measures, to directly examine the role of sleep-dependent consolidation in reward-related memory advantages.

Third, the reward manipulation involved a modest monetary incentive. It remains to be seen whether larger rewards or different kinds of extrinsic incentives (e.g., social feedback) would produce similar patterns across sleep groups. It is possible that stronger incentives might produce detectable benefits even under sleep deprivation by further enhancing encoding-related attention. Conversely, different incentive modalities might interact with sleep in distinct ways.

Fourth, although we controlled for several potential confounds such as caffeine intake and subjective sleepiness, other unmeasured variables may contribute to the observed effects. For example, chronic sleep restriction may be associated with stress, mood disturbances, and health behaviors that influence memory. Longitudinal or intervention studies that manipulate sleep duration would help to establish causal relations and disentangle chronic correlates.

Fifth, the sample consisted primarily of young adults drawn from a university and surrounding community, which may limit generalizability. Older adults or clinical populations may show different patterns due to age-related changes in sleep architecture and reward processing. Replication in more diverse samples would help to determine the robustness of the effects.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. The within-subject reward manipulation reduces between-subject variability and strengthens causal inference about the effect of reward on memory. The use of matched word lists controls for item-based confounds. The sample size was adequate to detect medium-sized interactions and allowed for reliable estimation of effect sizes. The inclusion of follow-up correlational and regression analyses provided converging evidence for the moderating role of sleep.

Future research directions are abundant. One clear next step is to use objective sleep measurement and to manipulate sleep experimentally in a controlled design. For example, participants could be randomly assigned to a sleep-restriction or normal-sleep condition, complete the same reward-based encoding task, and then be tested after an overnight interval. Such a design would allow stronger causal claims about the role of sleep and could examine whether overnight sleep specifically mediates reward-related consolidation.

Another promising direction is to probe neurophysiological mechanisms using methods such as electroencephalography (EEG) or functional neuroimaging. EEG could identify sleep-stage-specific reactivation patterns associated with rewarded versus unrewarded items, whereas neuroimaging during encoding could reveal whether reward cues differentially engage hippocampal and dopaminergic circuits as a function of sleep. Pharmacological manipulations of dopaminergic signaling might also clarify the role of neuromodulatory systems in the interaction between sleep and reward.

Investigating boundary conditions of the effect would also be informative. Studies could vary reward magnitude, the timing of rewards (immediate vs delayed), or the type of material (e.g., emotional versus neutral stimuli, procedural vs declarative tasks) to determine how generalizable the sleep × reward interaction is. It would also be useful to examine whether repeated exposures or spaced learning schedules interact with sleep and reward differently than a single encoding session.

From an applied perspective, intervention studies that combine sleep hygiene promotion with incentive-based learning programs could test whether improving sleep enhances the effectiveness of rewards in educational and occupational settings. Such translational work would have direct implications for maximizing learning outcomes in real-world environments.

In conclusion, the current study demonstrates that habitual sleep quantity moderates the mnemonic benefits of extrinsic reward. Reward robustly enhanced recall in participants who typically obtained sufficient sleep, but the same reward yielded no reliable advantage among those who reported habitual sleep restriction. These findings highlight the importance of considering sleep as a contextual factor that can either enable or constrain the efficacy of motivational interventions aimed at improving memory. Integrating sleep into models of motivated learning promises to advance both theoretical understanding and practical applications in education, work, and health.
