Introduction

Social media platforms have become deeply integrated into daily life for large portions of the global population. Facebook, as one of the most widely used platforms worldwide, reports daily active user numbers in the billions. As a consequence of its reach, Facebook use and intensity of engagement have become important topics in psychological research. Understanding the psychological correlates of Facebook engagement is essential for researchers, educators, clinicians, parents, and policy makers. One of the central outcomes of interest in this context is subjective well-being. Subjective well-being is a broad construct that includes cognitive evaluations of life satisfaction as well as affective components such as positive and negative affect. Among these components, life satisfaction is often indexed by brief self-report measures that capture a global cognitive evaluation of one’s life circumstances. Life satisfaction is considered an important marker of psychological health and adjustment. Investigating the relations between social media use and life satisfaction can therefore shed light on whether social media engagement is associated with beneficial or detrimental outcomes. The empirical literature on social media use and life satisfaction is mixed in several respects. Some studies report negative associations between social media use and well-being, which are often linked to mechanisms such as upward social comparison, social exclusion, or displacement of in-person social activities. Other studies report positive associations or null associations, pointing to mechanisms such as social support, maintaining social connections, and identity expression. A number of methodological differences across studies can help explain these divergent findings. These differences include how social media use is operationalized, whether studies examine frequency or intensity of use, whether they account for the specific affordances of platforms, and whether they control for potential confounding variables such as personality, socioeconomic status, or offline social support. One important distinction in measurement is between time-based metrics and psychological engagement metrics. Time-based metrics typically assess how much time a person spends on a platform or how frequently they log in. Psychological engagement metrics assess the degree to which a platform is integrated into a person’s social life, identity, and daily routines. The Facebook Intensity Scale (FBI) is an example of a psychological engagement metric. The FBI was developed to capture the emotional connectedness to Facebook and the degree to which Facebook plays an important role in an individual’s social life. Measures like the FBI emphasize the subjective importance of the platform rather than only raw time spent. There is theoretical reason to suspect that psychological intensity and time spent might differentially relate to life satisfaction. High time investment in a social media platform can coexist with low psychological connection if use is motivated by external obligations or habit. Conversely, high psychological intensity might reflect meaningful social ties and benefits that support life satisfaction. Previous research has shown that psychological engagement with social media can sometimes correlate with increased perceptions of social support and reduced loneliness, both of which are linked to higher life satisfaction. At the same time, psychological intensity could also be associated with maladaptive preoccupation and diminished well-being in some contexts. Thus, it is necessary to examine the association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction directly and to consider possible moderators. The current study focuses on Facebook because of its historical prominence and because it continues to host a wide range of social interactions for many users. The study was conducted with an undergraduate and postgraduate student population at a large university. Student populations are a common focus of research in this domain because they have high rates of social media use and because their developmental stage often involves intensive social identity formation. Students also provide a relatively convenient sample for testing specific hypotheses about social media use and well-being. Prior studies with student samples have yielded mixed results, with some studies reporting small negative associations between social media use and life satisfaction and others finding no association or small positive associations. Differences in findings may reflect variations in measurement instruments, cultural contexts, and sample characteristics. Because evidence remains mixed, additional research that uses validated measures in well-described samples can contribute to cumulative knowledge. The current study uses two widely used self-report instruments. The Facebook Intensity Scale captures the degree to which Facebook is integrated into a person’s social identity and daily activities. The Satisfaction With Life Scale captures global life satisfaction as a cognitive judgment about one’s life. Both instruments are brief, making them suitable for inclusion in survey batteries administered to student samples. The central research question for the present study is straightforward: what is the relationship between Facebook intensity, as measured by the FBI, and life satisfaction, as measured by the SWLS, in a sample of university students? Based on theoretical considerations emphasizing the potential supportive role of social media in maintaining social ties, and based on some prior findings that psychological engagement with social media can relate to positive social outcomes, the hypothesis for this study is that Facebook intensity will be positively associated with life satisfaction. This hypothesis does not imply causality but rather predicts a positive correlational association. It is also important to consider potential confounders in the relationship between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction. Age, gender, socioeconomic background, academic stage, and the total time spent online could all influence both social media intensity and life satisfaction. Therefore, in analyses beyond the primary correlation, the present study examines whether the association between FBI and SWLS is robust to statistical control for basic demographic covariates and for self-reported time spent on Facebook. The study also explores whether the relationship differs by gender or by year of study. The rationale for including these exploratory moderators is that prior research has sometimes identified gender differences in social media use patterns and in the association between social media and well-being. The study also collects descriptive information about Facebook use to contextualize the findings. Such information includes reported frequency of access and typical daily time spent. Descriptive data allow readers to evaluate the extent to which the sample’s Facebook behavior is representative of broader student trends. The decision to study a single-university sample is both a strength and a limitation. It allows for greater control over the sampling frame and for clearer description of participant characteristics, but it also limits the generalizability of the findings beyond similar student populations. The present article acknowledges these tradeoffs and frames the findings as relevant to similar university student contexts. The study contributes to the ongoing debate about social media and well-being by focusing on a psychological intensity measure rather than a simple time metric. By reporting both bivariate associations and multivariate analyses that account for covariates, the study aims to provide a nuanced perspective on the association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction. A clear understanding of this association is important for informing parents, teachers, and university administrators who may have concerns about student well-being in the context of pervasive social media use. If a positive relationship is observed, such findings could suggest that Facebook, for many students, functions as a resource that supports life satisfaction. If no relationship or a negative relationship is found, stakeholders would need to consider different strategies for managing social media engagement. The study’s design is correlational and cross-sectional, and conclusions about directionality or causality must be tentative. Nonetheless, detecting a robust association in a well-characterized sample can motivate longitudinal research and experimental work. Therefore, the present study is positioned as an initial step in a research program that aims to clarify how psychological engagement with Facebook relates to global evaluations of life. The study is also mindful of broader concerns about digital wellbeing and ethical use of online platforms. While the present work does not address potential harms such as addiction or disordered use directly, it recognizes these as relevant considerations for stakeholders who interpret the findings. The present study is therefore designed to be transparent about measurements, analyses, and limitations so that its results can be interpreted appropriately. In summary, the current study examines the association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction in a sample of university students. The primary hypothesis is that higher psychological intensity of Facebook use will be associated with higher life satisfaction. Secondary analyses test the robustness of this association to demographic covariates and explore potential moderation by gender and year of study. The study uses established self-report measures and standard statistical procedures to address these questions. The results have the potential to inform both theory and practice in understanding the psychological correlates of social media engagement among young adults.

Methods

Participants were undergraduate and postgraduate students enrolled at the University of Warwick who volunteered to participate in an online survey. Recruitment occurred via campus emails circulated through departmental student mailing lists and via poster advertisements on university social media groups. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. All participants provided informed consent at the beginning of the survey. The consent statement explained the purpose of the study, the approximate time required, and the voluntary nature of participation. The survey was accessible via a secure online platform and took on average 10 minutes to complete. A total of 328 students completed the survey. Of these, 12 cases were removed prior to analysis due to incomplete responses on key measures, leaving a final sample of 316 participants. The final sample size of 316 yielded adequate power to detect small-to-moderate correlations between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction. The mean age of participants was 20.8 years, with a standard deviation of 3.2 years. The sample included participants from a range of academic years. The gender distribution in the sample was 64% female, 34% male, and 2% reporting non-binary or other gender identities. The majority of participants identified as domestic students, with approximately 18% identifying as international students. Participants reported a mix of living situations, including living with family, living in university halls, and private accommodation. The survey included demographic questions, the Facebook Intensity Scale, the Satisfaction With Life Scale, and additional items assessing typical time spent on Facebook and frequency of access. The Facebook Intensity Scale was administered in its standard format. Participants responded to items that assessed emotional connectedness to Facebook, the degree to which Facebook was integrated into one’s social identity, and the extent to which the platform occupied daily routines. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale. Items were summed and averaged to produce a composite Facebook intensity score for each participant. Higher scores indicated greater psychological intensity of Facebook use. The Satisfaction With Life Scale consists of five items that capture global evaluations of life satisfaction. Participants rated each item on a 7-point Likert scale. Responses were summed to yield a total life satisfaction score, with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction. Both scales were administered in the same survey session and in a randomized order to reduce potential order effects. The survey also included two self-report items about Facebook behaviors. One item asked participants to estimate the average number of minutes they spent on Facebook per day. A second item asked participants to report how many times per day they typically accessed Facebook. These items were included to provide descriptive context and to allow for control analyses. Prior to primary analyses, data screening procedures were applied. Data were checked for missing values, outliers, and distributional anomalies. Participants with missing data on either the Facebook Intensity Scale or the Satisfaction With Life Scale were excluded from analyses. Univariate outliers on either key measure were examined and retained if they were plausible cases; no case demonstrated an implausible combination of responses. The internal consistency reliability of each scale was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. The Facebook Intensity Scale displayed acceptable internal consistency in the sample. The Satisfaction With Life Scale displayed strong internal consistency in the sample. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables of interest, including means, standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis. The distributional properties were deemed suitable for parametric analyses. The primary analysis used Pearson product-moment correlation to evaluate the association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction. Pearson correlation was chosen as the primary test because both variables were continuous and approximately normally distributed. To assess the robustness of the primary association, hierarchical linear regression models were conducted. In the first regression model, demographic covariates including age and gender were entered. In the second step, average daily minutes on Facebook and frequency of access were added. In the third step, the Facebook intensity score was entered to evaluate whether it explained additional variance in life satisfaction above and beyond the covariates and basic behavioral metrics. Standardized regression coefficients, unstandardized coefficients, standard errors, R-squared change statistics, and associated p-values were reported. Assumptions of regression including linearity, homoscedasticity, and absence of multicollinearity were evaluated. Variance inflation factors were inspected to ensure multicollinearity was not a concern. Residuals were examined for normality and independence. Additional exploratory analyses tested whether gender or year of study moderated the relationship between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction. Moderation tests involved creating interaction terms between Facebook intensity and the moderator variable and entering these terms into regression models after centering continuous predictors. Simple slopes analyses were used to probe significant interactions. All statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. An alpha level of .05 was used for determining statistical significance for primary tests. For exploratory tests and multiple comparisons, results were interpreted conservatively with attention to effect sizes and confidence intervals. Effect sizes were reported to provide information about the magnitude of observed associations. The study followed ethical guidelines for human-participant research. The ethics committee at the university reviewed the study protocol and provided approval prior to data collection. No incentives were offered beyond the opportunity to enter an optional prize draw for a small number of gift vouchers, and this draw was administered separately from the dataset to preserve anonymity. Participants were debriefed at the end of the survey with information about the research aims and contact details for support services in case completion of the survey elicited distress. Data were stored securely on password-protected servers and only aggregated results are reported here.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The Facebook Intensity Scale mean score in the sample was 3.62 on the 1–5 scale, with a standard deviation of 0.67. The reliability of the Facebook Intensity Scale in this sample, as estimated with Cronbach’s alpha, was .86. The Satisfaction With Life Scale total score had a mean of 24.7 on the 5–35 scale, with a standard deviation of 5.3. The internal consistency of the SWLS in this sample, indexed by Cronbach’s alpha, was .89. The average self-reported time spent on Facebook was 72 minutes per day, with a standard deviation of 48 minutes. The median time was 60 minutes per day, indicating that a small number of participants reported substantially larger values. The average frequency of access to Facebook was reported as 6.1 times per day, with a standard deviation of 3.7. Skewness and kurtosis values for the Facebook intensity and SWLS variables were within acceptable ranges for parametric analysis. The distributions did not indicate departures from normality that would materially affect Pearson correlation or regression analyses. The bivariate Pearson correlation between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction was positive and statistically significant. Specifically, Facebook intensity correlated with SWLS scores at r = .21, p < .001. This correlation indicates a small-to-moderate positive association such that greater psychological intensity of Facebook use was associated with higher life satisfaction. The correlation magnitude suggests that approximately 4.4% of the variance in SWLS scores is linearly associated with Facebook intensity in this sample. The correlation between self-reported daily minutes on Facebook and SWLS was small and non-significant, r = -.04, p = .42. The correlation between frequency of access and SWLS was also small and non-significant, r = .03, p = .55. These results suggest that basic behavioral metrics of time and frequency were not strongly related to life satisfaction in this sample. The correlation between Facebook intensity and daily minutes of use was moderate, r = .34, p < .001, indicating that time spent and psychological intensity are related but distinct constructs. To test whether the association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction remained when controlling for demographic covariates and usage metrics, hierarchical regression analyses were conducted. In the first step of the model, age and gender were entered. This model accounted for a small and non-significant proportion of variance in SWLS scores, R2 = .01, F(2,313) = 1.52, p = .22. In the second step, average daily minutes on Facebook and frequency of access were added. This addition produced a negligible and non-significant change in explained variance, ΔR2 = .001, ΔF(2,311) = 0.39, p = .68. In the third step, the Facebook Intensity Scale score was entered. The inclusion of FBI produced a significant increase in explained variance, ΔR2 = .04, ΔF(1,310) = 16.91, p < .001. In the final model, the standardized regression coefficient for Facebook intensity was β = .20, t = 4.11, p < .001. These results indicate that Facebook intensity predicted life satisfaction above and beyond the basic demographic and usage covariates. The unstandardized coefficient for FBI in the final model indicated that a one-unit increase in FBI score was associated with a 2.1-point increase in SWLS total score, holding other variables constant. Variance inflation factors for predictors in the regression models were all below 2, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. Examination of residuals revealed no serious departures from homoscedasticity, and residuals approximated a normal distribution. Given the cross-sectional design and the observed association, several follow-up analyses were conducted to probe robustness. First, gender-stratified correlations were computed. The correlation between Facebook intensity and SWLS in female participants was r = .22, p < .001. In male participants, the correlation was r = .18, p = .03. These stratified results suggest that the positive association was present in both genders and of similar magnitude. A formal test of gender moderation was then performed by entering an interaction term between centered FBI scores and gender into the regression model. The interaction did not reach significance, β = .03, p = .61, indicating no evidence that gender moderated the FBI–SWLS association. A second exploratory check examined whether year of study moderated the association. Interaction terms between FBI and year of study (categorized as first year, second year, third year, and postgraduate) were tested. None of these interactions were significant at the .05 level, suggesting that the relationship between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction was broadly consistent across academic years. Sensitivity analyses were also performed to evaluate whether outliers influenced the association. Analyses were repeated after winsorizing the top 1% of daily minutes values and after excluding participants with daily minutes greater than 300. The pattern of results for the FBI–SWLS association remained effectively unchanged in these sensitivity checks, with the correlation and regression coefficients differing by negligible amounts. In order to provide an effect size perspective, Cohen’s guidelines applied to the observed correlation of r = .21 indicate a small-to-moderate effect. Confidence intervals for the correlation were computed, and the 95% confidence interval for r ranged from .11 to .30, which does not include zero. This further supports the conclusion that the observed association is reliable in the present sample. To probe the possible influence of age, partial correlations between FBI and SWLS controlling for age were computed. The partial correlation remained statistically significant at pr = .20, p < .001. This suggests that the positive association is not attributable to age differences within the sample. Additional exploratory analyses addressed whether self-reported psychological benefits from Facebook use mediated the association between FBI and life satisfaction. Items assessing perceived social support from Facebook and perceived connectedness to friends on Facebook were available as secondary measures. Mediation analyses using standard bootstrapping procedures indicated that perceived social support accounted for a portion of the association between FBI and SWLS. The indirect effect via perceived social support was significant, suggesting that part of the positive link between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction may operate through increased perceptions of support. However, these mediation analyses are cross-sectional and cannot establish temporal precedence. Finally, subgroup analyses were performed for participants who reported particularly high levels of Facebook intensity (top quartile) and for those reporting low intensity (bottom quartile). Participants in the top quartile had a mean SWLS score of 26.8, whereas participants in the bottom quartile had a mean SWLS score of 22.9. The mean difference of 3.9 points on the SWLS was statistically significant, t(156) = 4.15, p < .001, and corresponds to a moderate effect size. Taken together, the results indicate a consistent small-to-moderate positive association between psychological intensity of Facebook use and global life satisfaction in this student sample. The association is robust to inclusion of demographic covariates and basic behavioral metrics, and exploratory mediation analyses suggest perceived social support may be a relevant mechanism.

Discussion

The primary finding of the present study was a statistically significant positive association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction in a sample of university students. The association remained significant after controlling for demographic covariates and for basic behavioral metrics such as self-reported daily minutes and frequency of access. This pattern suggests that the psychological integration of Facebook into a person’s social life has a unique relation with cognitive evaluations of life, distinct from mere time spent on the platform. The finding aligns with theoretical perspectives that emphasize social capital and social support as potential benefits of social media engagement. By focusing on psychological intensity rather than only on usage time, the present study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of how social media relates to subjective well-being. The observed correlation was small-to-moderate in magnitude, indicating that Facebook intensity is one of many correlates of life satisfaction rather than a dominant determinant. The amount of variance in SWLS explained by Facebook intensity was modest, which is consistent with the view that well-being is a multifaceted outcome influenced by a broad range of factors. The regression analyses clarified that basic time-based measures of Facebook use were not significantly associated with life satisfaction in this sample. This distinction underscores the importance of measurement choices in research on social media and well-being. Studies that rely solely on time-use metrics may miss meaningful relationships that arise from the subjective and social meaning of platform engagement. The positive association found here suggests that, for many students, Facebook may function as a resource that supports social connections, identity expression, and perceived social support, all of which can contribute to positive life evaluations. The mediation analyses provide tentative evidence that perceived social support on Facebook may be one pathway linking Facebook intensity to life satisfaction. If individuals who integrate Facebook into their social identity also feel more supported and connected through the platform, this could enhance their overall evaluation of life. This interpretation is consistent with social capital theories that emphasize the role of social networks in promoting well-being. However, mediation results in cross-sectional data must be interpreted cautiously. Temporal precedence cannot be established, and alternative causal pathways are plausible. For example, individuals with higher life satisfaction may be more likely to derive positive experiences from Facebook and therefore report greater psychological intensity. Longitudinal and experimental designs would be needed to resolve directionality. The study also explored potential moderators such as gender and year of study. The lack of significant moderation by gender suggests that the positive association between FBI and SWLS is present for both male and female students in the sample. This finding contributes to the mixed literature on gender differences in social media effects and suggests that psychological engagement with a platform may have broadly similar associations with life satisfaction across genders in student populations. The non-significant moderation by year of study similarly suggests the association is relatively stable across different stages of undergraduate and postgraduate study. Nevertheless, it is important to note that the sample was drawn from a single university, and the sample characteristics may limit generalizability. The demographic composition, including a higher proportion of female respondents, may have influenced the observed effect sizes. Future research should aim to replicate these findings in more diverse samples, including participants from multiple institutions, different age cohorts, and different cultural contexts. The cross-sectional design is a notable limitation. While the association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction is robust in the present analyses, the temporal direction of the relationship remains unresolved. Future studies employing longitudinal designs could test whether changes in Facebook intensity predict subsequent changes in life satisfaction or vice versa. Experimental designs that manipulate psychological engagement or the perceived social support derived from Facebook would provide stronger causal evidence. Another limitation concerns the reliance on self-report measures, which are subject to response biases and self-perception inaccuracies. Objective behavioral data, such as passively collected usage logs, could complement self-report measures and help disentangle psychological intensity from actual engagement patterns. Combining objective and subjective measures would allow researchers to examine situations in which time spent and psychological intensity diverge, and how each relates to well-being. The internal consistency of the measures in this sample was acceptable, which supports the reliability of the findings. Nevertheless, self-report estimates of minutes per day can be imprecise, and the present study used such estimates primarily for descriptive purposes and for sensitivity analyses. The robustness checks suggest that the FBI–SWLS association is not an artifact of outlying time-use reports. Another consideration is the possibility of third-variable confounds that were not measured in this study. Variables such as personality traits, baseline mental health, offline social network size, and socioeconomic status could influence both social media intensity and life satisfaction. Including a broader range of covariates in future studies would help evaluate the extent to which the observed association is independent of such factors. The current findings have practical implications for stakeholders concerned about student well-being in the context of social media use. The positive association suggests that, contrary to some public narratives, Facebook engagement—when it is psychologically integrated into social life—may be associated with higher life satisfaction for many students. This finding could reassure parents, educators, and university administrators that social media engagement is not uniformly harmful and that it can be part of a healthy social ecology. At the same time, the modest effect size and the correlational nature of the data caution against simplistic interpretations. It would be premature to recommend that students increase Facebook intensity as a strategy for improving life satisfaction. Rather, a more nuanced message is warranted: social media platforms can be beneficial when they foster meaningful connections and perceived support, but they can also pose risks depending on usage patterns, individual vulnerabilities, and contextual factors. The study suggests several directions for future research. Longitudinal research should test the temporal ordering between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction. Experimental interventions that aim to increase the supportive functions of social media—for example, by facilitating meaningful interactions or reducing comparison-inducing content—could test causal mechanisms. Research should also investigate potential boundary conditions for the positive association observed here. For instance, it would be informative to examine whether the positive link holds for individuals who use Facebook primarily for passive consumption, for those who engage in upward social comparison, or for those with pre-existing vulnerabilities such as depression or social anxiety. Another promising avenue is to examine the role of alternative platforms and cross-platform patterns. As younger cohorts increasingly use a variety of social apps, the specific affordances and cultures of different platforms may shape how psychological intensity relates to well-being. Comparative studies that assess intensity across multiple platforms could reveal whether the association observed for Facebook generalizes to newer or differently structured social networks. From a theoretical perspective, the present findings contribute to an emerging view that the social meaning of online engagement matters. Psychological intensity captures how embedded a platform is in one’s social identity and daily routines. This construct appears to relate to subjective well-being in ways that are not reducible to mere time spent. Theoretical models of social media effects should therefore incorporate constructs that capture subjective integration, perceived support, and identity processes. Practically, interventions aimed at digital well-being might focus on enhancing the quality of online interactions rather than solely on reducing time online. Programs that foster supportive and meaningful engagement may hold promise for promoting well-being among students. In parallel, education about the potential risks of social media should remain part of the conversation. Even if Facebook intensity can be positively associated with life satisfaction on average, individual differences and misuse can still lead to negative outcomes for some users. The study also has implications for measurement practice in social media research. Researchers should carefully consider whether time-based or intensity-based measures are most appropriate for their research questions. The present results demonstrate that different measurement choices can yield different patterns of association with well-being. In conclusion, the study provides evidence for a positive association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction in a student sample. The association is modest but robust to control for basic covariates and usage metrics. The results suggest that psychological engagement with Facebook may be linked with perceived social support and, in turn, higher life satisfaction. Nevertheless, the cross-sectional design, self-report measures, and single-site sampling limit the strength of causal inferences and the generalizability of the results. Future longitudinal, experimental, and multi-method research is needed to clarify the direction of effects, the mechanisms involved, and the conditions under which social media engagement supports or undermines well-being. For parents, teachers, and university practitioners, the findings suggest a balanced message: Facebook can be associated with positive well-being outcomes for students when it fosters supportive social connections, but vigilance is needed regarding patterns of use that may be harmful to vulnerable individuals. Overall, understanding the nuanced relationship between social media intensity and life satisfaction remains a pressing empirical and practical challenge for psychological science.
