Introduction

Social connection is a central feature of human life, and the experience of being accepted or excluded by others shapes cognition, emotion, and behavior in fundamental ways. One influential perspective in social psychology emphasizes that social experiences are not processed solely at an abstract cognitive level but are often instantiated through bodily and perceptual systems, an idea broadly captured within embodied cognition frameworks. Within this broader literature, the metaphorical link between social warmth and physical warmth has attracted considerable attention, leading researchers to examine whether social phenomena can influence perceptions of physical temperature and vice versa. For example, people who hold a warm object have been reported to judge others as psychologically warmer, and conversely, feelings of social rejection have been argued to produce sensations that mimic physical cold. These findings lend support to the notion that social and physical domains are intertwined in the human cognitive architecture, and they suggest possible mechanisms through which social experiences could regulate physiological states.

A particularly striking set of findings in this area comes from research proposing that being socially excluded can literally make people feel colder, insofar as their estimates of ambient temperature are lowered after exclusion experiences. This research has been interpreted as evidence that social disconnection engages neural and bodily systems that overlap with those involved in thermal perception, thereby producing embodied sensations consistent with the metaphor of social coldness. The implications of such effects are broad because they suggest that social experiences can be felt not only in terms of mood or cognition but also through basic sensory judgments that could influence behavior in everyday environments. If social exclusion reliably reduces perceived ambient temperature, then social processes could have subtle impacts on comfort, choice of clothing, or the use of heating in shared spaces.

Despite the theoretical appeal and empirical intrigue of such effects, the robustness and generality of social-to-thermal influences have been the subject of debate. Some studies have successfully demonstrated bidirectional links between interpersonal warmth and physical temperature, while other attempts at replication or extension have produced mixed results. Differences in sample characteristics, experimental procedures, the timing of measures, and the operationalization of exclusion and temperature judgments may all contribute to inconsistent findings. In addition, the mechanisms that might underlie a social-to-thermal effect are not fully established; possibilities include affective pathways in which exclusion produces negative affect that colors perception, cognitive metaphors that bias interpretation of sensory inputs, or physiological changes in thermoregulatory systems that alter sensory thresholds. Parsing these alternatives requires carefully controlled experiments and consideration of individual differences that might moderate the effect.

One important candidate moderator is personality, particularly traits that influence social motivation and sensitivity to social context. Extraversion is a broad trait reflecting tendencies toward sociability, positive affect, and reward sensitivity, and it has been linked to different patterns of social perception and reactivity. Extraverts, relative to introverts, typically seek social engagement, derive greater reward from social stimuli, and may experience social inclusion more intensely. At the same time, extraversion might confer resilience to social stressors, or alternatively, it might amplify the impact of social cues because social experiences are more salient to extraverts. Given these divergent possibilities, examining extraversion as a moderator of social-to-thermal effects is theoretically informative. If extraverts were less affected by exclusion in terms of temperature estimates, this might suggest a buffer effect whereby trait sociability protects against embodied sensations of cold. If extraverts were more affected, this might indicate heightened sensitivity to social cues manifesting in greater embodied responses.

Beyond extraversion, methodological considerations are central to evaluating the reliability of social-to-thermal effects. The original paradigms that reported that social exclusion lowered perceived ambient temperature often relied on recall manipulations in which participants were asked to write about a time they felt excluded or included. Variants of the manipulation have included more direct experimental forms such as simulated exclusionary tasks, but each approach has specific trade-offs. Recall manipulations can elicit vivid autobiographical emotional experience but may vary in intensity and duration across participants. Simulated exclusion tasks can standardize the social provocation but may be perceived as less personally consequential. The choice of temperature measure also matters; self-reported estimates of ambient temperature are subjective and may be influenced by demand characteristics, while objective thermometry offers a precise metric but may not capture conscious perception in the same way. Moreover, timing is crucial because the temporal dynamics of embodied effects may be transient; an effect observed immediately after an exclusionary event might dissipate with delay.

Another methodological issue concerns the sample characteristics and study context. Many reports of social-to-thermal effects come from samples of convenience, including undergraduate students in particular cultural contexts, and the ambient physical environment of the laboratory can vary. Cultural norms about warmth and social behavior may shape the salience of thermal metaphors and the likelihood that social experiences bleed into perceptual judgments. Gender differences in thermal perception and social reactivity might also affect observed outcomes. Given these contingencies, replication attempts in diverse samples and with careful measurement are necessary to establish robustness.

The present study was designed within this context of both theoretical promise and empirical uncertainty. The study had two primary aims. First, it sought to replicate the finding that recalling a time of social exclusion produces lower ambient temperature estimates relative to recalling social inclusion, a pattern that, if replicated, would provide further evidence for embodied links between social and thermal domains. Second, it aimed to assess whether individual differences in extraversion moderate any effect of social exclusion on temperature estimates. The latter aim was motivated by the idea that trait sociability and affective style could influence the degree to which social experiences are embodied in sensory judgments.

Several specific hypotheses were formulated. The primary hypothesis was that participants asked to recall a recent episode of social exclusion would report lower estimates of the room temperature than participants asked to recall a recent episode of social inclusion. The secondary hypothesis was that degree of extraversion would be associated with temperature estimates such that extraversion would interact with social condition; this interaction could take the form of extraverts showing either attenuated or amplified effects of exclusion compared with introverts, and the present study aimed to test for a reliable interaction without assuming the direction a priori. Exploratory analyses were also planned to examine whether gender, actual ambient temperature, or manipulation checks of perceived exclusion might account for the pattern of results.

In designing the study, several decisions were made to enhance internal validity while preserving ecological relevance. A between-subjects design was chosen for the social condition to avoid carryover effects between inclusion and exclusion manipulations. Standardized instruments for measuring extraversion were selected to ensure reliable assessment of trait differences. The temperature estimate measure was framed within a maintenance questionnaire to reduce demand characteristics and to capture a naturalistic judgment about the immediate environment. A manipulation check item assessing state feelings of exclusion was included to verify that the recall task produced differences in subjective social experience.

Given prior evidence and these methodological choices, the present study set out to conduct a straightforward test of the social-exclusion-as-cold hypothesis and to explore whether extraversion might shape the effect. The sample size was determined by practical considerations common in undergraduate research contexts, and the study proceeded under the expectation that any replication of the original effect would manifest as a modest but reliable mean difference between conditions. At the same time, the inclusion of personality assessment and manipulation checks provided the opportunity to investigate boundary conditions and to consider alternative explanations in the event of a null result.

The results of the study ultimately did not replicate the initial findings reported in prior work, and they did not support an interaction between extraversion and social condition. These outcomes raise questions about the robustness of social-to-thermal effects and about the conditions under which embodied metaphorical mappings may influence perception. To interpret these findings, it is necessary to consider potential methodological explanations, the theoretical implications for embodied cognition frameworks, and the directions for future research that could clarify the processes at play. The remainder of this article presents the methods and results in detail, followed by an extended discussion of these issues.

Methods

Participants were 67 undergraduate students drawn from a psychology subject pool at a mid-sized university. The sample comprised both women and men, and participants ranged in age from 18 to 29 years. Participants were recruited in exchange for partial course credit or entry into a small prize draw for participating in research studies. All participants provided informed consent prior to beginning the study, and the study procedures were approved by the university's institutional review board and conformed to standard ethical guidelines for research with human participants.

The study used a between-subjects design in which participants were randomly assigned to one of two writing conditions: social exclusion recall or social inclusion recall. Randomization occurred upon arrival and was implemented by the experimenter using sealed envelopes containing the condition assignment. The main dependent variable was the participant's estimate of the ambient room temperature, collected as part of a maintenance questionnaire administered after the recall manipulation. A secondary measure was participants' extraversion, assessed via standardized personality questionnaires administered prior to the recall manipulation to avoid contaminating trait measures with induced state effects.

Personality assessment included two complementary instruments aimed at measuring extraversion reliably. The primary instrument was a short form of the International Personality Item Pool (IPIP) extraversion scale consisting of 20 items. Respondents indicated the extent to which each item described them on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Item responses were summed to create an IPIP extraversion score, with higher scores indicating greater trait extraversion. Internal consistency of the IPIP extraversion scale in the present sample was calculated and reported below.

In addition to the IPIP measure, participants completed the extraversion domain from a brief version of the NEO inventory, referred to here as the NEO extraversion domain. The NEO extraversion domain included 12 items assessing sociability, assertiveness, and positive affectivity, and these items were similarly scored on a Likert scale and summed to generate a domain score. The two extraversion measures were correlated and used to create a composite extraversion index by z-scoring each measure and averaging the z-scores, yielding a unitary extraversion variable for use in the main analyses.

All participants completed the personality questionnaires on arrival in the lab. After finishing the personality measures, participants were given a brief neutral filler task intended to mask the upcoming writing exercise and to standardize timing across participants. The writing manipulation then proceeded in which participants were asked either to recall and write in detail about a personal experience in which they had felt socially excluded or to recall and write about a time when they had felt socially included. Instructions emphasized that participants should write about the event in as much detail as possible, including how they felt physically and emotionally at the time, and that they should spend at least five minutes writing. The experimenter collected the written responses after the allotted time.

Following the writing task, participants were given the maintenance questionnaire, which included several filler items about facilities and environment and one critical item asking participants to estimate the current room temperature in degrees Celsius. The item was phrased neutrally to reduce demand effects, for example: "Please indicate what you think the current room temperature is in degrees Celsius." Participants were free to provide an approximate value if they were unsure. The maintenance questionnaire also included a manipulation check item asking participants to rate how excluded or included they felt during the recall task on a 7-point scale ranging from not at all to extremely. Additional items probed current mood and arousal as exploratory covariates.

The laboratory environment was a standard testing room in the psychology department with central heating. The ambient temperature of the room was recorded by the experimenter using a digital thermometer immediately before the first participant and periodically throughout data collection. The thermometer readings were recorded to allow comparison between subjective estimates and objective ambient temperature, and to check whether variations in true room temperature might contribute to variability in participants' estimates.

All questionnaires and the writing task were administered on paper and completed in the laboratory while participants were seated alone. The experimenter provided scripted instructions and remained outside the testing room while participants worked so that the experience was individual and minimally influenced by experimenter presence. Testing sessions took place over multiple days during a standard university schedule, and the time of day was recorded for each participant to allow investigation of any time-of-day effects on temperature perception.

Scoring and data preparation followed preregistered procedures in which improbable responses were screened, and cases with missing critical data were excluded. Specifically, participants who failed to provide a temperature estimate or who left the extraversion scales largely blank were removed from analyses. No other exclusions were made. Extraversion scores were standardized and used as continuous predictors in the main analyses, and descriptive statistics for all measures were calculated.

The main statistical analyses tested three primary effects: the main effect of social condition on temperature estimates, the main effect of trait extraversion on temperature estimates, and the interaction between social condition and extraversion predicting temperature estimates. An independent-samples t-test compared the mean temperature estimates of the exclusion and inclusion groups. Pearson correlation assessed the simple association between trait extraversion and temperature estimate. A multiple linear regression analysis was conducted in which temperature estimate was regressed on social condition (coded 0 for inclusion, 1 for exclusion), standardized extraversion, and the interaction term of condition by extraversion. Regression coefficients, standard errors, t-values, and p-values were reported for each predictor. Effect sizes were computed using Cohen's d for the t-test and partial r-squared for the regression interaction.

Exploratory analyses included an ANOVA using a median split of extraversion to create "introvert" and "extravert" groups and testing for a condition by extraversion categorical interaction, though the primary analyses relied on the continuous measure of extraversion to preserve statistical power and measurement precision. Additional exploratory models included gender and objective room temperature as covariates to determine whether these factors explained variance in subjective estimates. All statistical tests were two-tailed with an alpha level of .05, and 95% confidence intervals were reported for key effects. Analyses were conducted using standard statistical software, and assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were checked and deemed acceptable for the planned analyses.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the sample and the measures are presented first. The final sample included 67 participants after excluding two participants who failed to provide a temperature estimate. The sample comprised 42 women and 25 men, and the mean age was 20.3 years with a standard deviation of 2.1 years. The objective room temperature recorded during testing sessions had a mean of 21.9 degrees Celsius and a standard deviation of 0.8 degrees Celsius, reflecting modest variability across testing days. The subjective temperature estimates provided by participants had a mean of 21.98 degrees Celsius with a standard deviation of 1.9 degrees Celsius, indicating some individual variation but reasonably tight clustering around the true room temperature.

The IPIP extraversion scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in this sample, with Cronbach's alpha of .84. The NEO extraversion domain also showed acceptable reliability, with alpha equal to .79. The two measures were significantly correlated, r = .62, p < .001, and thus it was reasonable to create a composite extraversion index by averaging the standardized scores from each instrument. The composite extraversion score had a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 by construction, and the distribution did not show evidence of extreme skew or kurtosis.

Manipulation check analyses examined whether the writing task elicited differences in subjective feelings of inclusion and exclusion. Participants in the exclusion recall condition reported higher feelings of exclusion in the manipulation check item (M = 5.02, SD = 1.12, on a 7-point scale) than participants in the inclusion recall condition (M = 2.18, SD = 1.35). An independent-samples t-test confirmed that this difference was statistically significant, t(65) = 12.87, p < .001, Cohen's d = 3.15, indicating a strong manipulation effect on subjective feelings of exclusion. Thus, the recall manipulation operated as intended in producing a felt difference between conditions.

The primary test of the social-exclusion-as-cold hypothesis compared subjective room temperature estimates between the exclusion and inclusion conditions. Participants in the exclusion condition provided a mean temperature estimate of 21.86 degrees Celsius (SD = 1.97), whereas participants in the inclusion condition provided a mean estimate of 22.10 degrees Celsius (SD = 1.82). An independent-samples t-test indicated that this difference was small and not statistically significant, t(65) = -0.52, p = .60, Cohen's d = -0.13. The 95% confidence interval for the mean difference ranged from -0.93 to 0.50 degrees Celsius, which includes zero and suggests that if an effect exists it is of small magnitude.

The relationship between trait extraversion and subjective temperature estimate was also examined. Pearson correlation between composite extraversion and temperature estimate was r = .06, p = .64, indicating no evidence of a linear association in the sample. The absence of a simple correlation implies that extraversion alone did not predict variation in estimates of ambient temperature across participants.

To test for a moderation effect, a multiple linear regression analysis was conducted predicting subjective temperature estimate from social condition (0 = inclusion, 1 = exclusion), standardized composite extraversion, and the interaction term between condition and extraversion. The overall regression model accounted for a non-significant portion of variance, R^2 = .01, F(3,63) = 0.18, p = .91. The coefficient for social condition was b = -0.24, SE = 0.46, t = -0.52, p = .60, indicating no main effect of exclusion on temperature estimate when controlling for extraversion. The coefficient for extraversion was b = 0.11, SE = 0.20, t = 0.55, p = .59, indicating no main effect of extraversion in the regression model. The interaction term had a coefficient of b = 0.05, SE = 0.38, t = 0.13, p = .90, indicating no evidence that the effect of condition on temperature estimate varied by level of extraversion.

Given the non-significant interaction in the continuous regression, an exploratory ANOVA was conducted using a median split to create low and high extraversion groups and assessing a 2 (condition) by 2 (extraversion group) factorial design. Mean temperature estimates for the four cells were as follows: inclusion-low extraversion M = 21.98, inclusion-high extraversion M = 22.20, exclusion-low extraversion M = 21.74, and exclusion-high extraversion M = 21.98. The ANOVA revealed neither a significant main effect of condition, F(1,63) = 0.27, p = .61, nor a main effect of extraversion group, F(1,63) = 0.09, p = .77, nor a significant interaction, F(1,63) = 0.01, p = .94. These exploratory categorical analyses thus mirrored the continuous analyses in finding no support for the hypothesized effects.

Because objective room temperature was recorded during testing, additional regression models were run including the measured room temperature as a covariate to account for potential physical variability. When objective temperature was included as a covariate, the pattern of results remained unchanged: objective temperature strongly predicted subjective estimate (b = 0.82, SE = 0.12, t = 6.83, p < .001), but neither social condition (b = -0.21, SE = 0.42, t = -0.50, p = .62) nor extraversion (b = 0.09, SE = 0.18, t = 0.50, p = .62) nor their interaction (b = 0.03, SE = 0.33, t = 0.09, p = .93) accounted for additional variance. This indicates that participants' subjective estimates tracked objective temperature well, and that variability in true room temperature did not mask an effect of social condition.

Given prior suggestions that gender might moderate social reactivity, gender was entered as an additional covariate in supplementary analyses. Gender did not reliably predict subjective temperature estimate in these models (b = 0.12, SE = 0.28, t = 0.43, p = .67), and adding gender did not change the non-significant effects of condition, extraversion, or their interaction. Similarly, time of day was tested as a covariate and was unrelated to temperature estimates, t(65) = 0.36, p = .72, and did not alter the pattern of results.

Because the manipulation check showed a robust difference in subjective feelings of exclusion between conditions, the absence of an effect on temperature estimates is unlikely to be due to failure of the manipulation. To further probe whether the strength of the felt exclusion experience predicted temperature estimate within the exclusion group, a post hoc correlation was computed between exclusion manipulation check scores and subjective temperature estimate among participants in the exclusion condition. The correlation was r = -0.07, p = .70, indicating no evidence that participants who reported stronger feelings of exclusion judged the room as colder.

Effect size estimates for the primary contrasts were small, and 95% confidence intervals included zero, as noted above. Given the sample size of 67 and the observed standard deviations, the study had limited power to detect small effects; post hoc power calculations suggested that the present study had approximately 50% power to detect a medium effect (d = .5) and substantially lower power to detect small effects (d = .2). Therefore, while the study was able to detect large manipulation differences in subjective exclusion, it may have been underpowered to detect small social-to-thermal effects.

In summary, the manipulation check confirmed that the recall exercise successfully induced differing subjective feelings of exclusion and inclusion, but there was no evidence that social exclusion influenced subjective estimates of ambient temperature. Trait extraversion was unrelated to temperature estimates, and there was no interaction between extraversion and social condition. Objective room temperature predicted subjective estimates strongly, and including it as a covariate did not reveal hidden effects of social condition. These results indicate a failure to replicate the original reported effect within the present sample and procedures.

Discussion

The present study aimed to replicate a striking finding from the social embodiment literature that social exclusion can produce a sensation of coldness reflected in lower estimates of ambient temperature. The study also tested whether individual differences in extraversion moderate any such social-to-thermal effect. The data collected from 67 undergraduate participants do not support either the primary hypothesis that recalling exclusion leads to lower temperature estimates or the secondary hypothesis that extraversion moderates this effect. These outcomes prompt consideration of multiple possible explanations, implications for theory, and directions for future research.

First, it is important to emphasize that the recall manipulation successfully elicited differences in subjective feelings of exclusion, as evidenced by large and statistically significant differences on the manipulation check item. This confirms that the psychological state the literature associates with social exclusion was induced in the present study. Thus, the absence of an effect on temperature estimates cannot be readily attributed to a failure to manipulate social exclusion. Instead, the null results suggest that the link between induced exclusion and perceived ambient temperature may be weaker or more context-dependent than some prior reports imply.

One class of explanation concerns statistical power and effect size. The present sample of 67 participants provided adequate sensitivity to detect medium-sized effects but limited power to detect small effects. If the true effect of social exclusion on subjective temperature estimates is small, the study may have been underpowered to detect it. The confidence intervals around the mean differences include values that would correspond to small effects in either direction, so the data do not definitively rule out the presence of a small effect. However, the observed effect size in the present sample was close to zero, and the pattern of results across multiple analyses consistently failed to show even a trend toward exclusion producing lower temperature estimates. This consistency suggests that if an effect exists it is likely small under the present conditions.

Another explanation relates to procedural differences between studies. The literature on social-to-thermal effects includes a variety of operationalizations of both exclusion and temperature, and seemingly minor differences may influence outcomes. The present study used a recall task and a subjective temperature estimate framed within a maintenance questionnaire, whereas other studies may have used different recall prompts, simulated exclusion tasks, or different ways of eliciting temperature judgments. For example, some paradigms ask participants to hold a warm or cold object or to report their own bodily sensations of warmth, rather than estimating ambient room temperature. It is conceivable that embodied effects manifest more readily in judgments tied to one's body (e.g., feeling chilled) than in assessments of a room environment. Therefore, the measure of temperature used here—a cognitive estimate of room temperature—may be less sensitive to social manipulation than other thermal measures.

Contextual factors in the testing environment may also attenuate effects. In the present study the objective room temperature was relatively stable and close to comfortable levels, and participants’ subjective estimates closely tracked the measured temperature. When environmental cues are salient and participants are confident in their judgments, there may be less room for social states to influence estimates. In other words, when perceptual inputs are clear and reliable, top-down influences from social metaphors may exert minimal impact. By contrast, under more ambiguous sensory conditions—such as when ambient temperature is less easily discriminable or when participants are distracted—the influence of social state on judgment might be greater. Future research could manipulate sensory ambiguity to test whether social-to-thermal effects emerge under conditions of increased perceptual uncertainty.

Measurement issues may also be relevant. The subjective estimate item required participants to supply a numerical temperature value, which may have prompted participants to rely on recall of typical room temperature or on knowledge of thermostat settings rather than on momentary perceptual experience. A different assessment approach, such as asking participants to rate whether they felt cold or warm on a Likert scale, or using an implicit or behavioral measure related to warmth (for example, choice of a blanket, or willingness to hold a warm object), might be more sensitive to social influences. Indeed, there is evidence in the broader literature that some social-to-thermal effects are evident in behavioral choices, such as selecting warm beverages after social exclusion, which may not be captured by explicit temperature estimation.

Theoretical implications of the null findings merit careful consideration. One interpretation is that embodied metaphors linking social and physical warmth may not reliably translate into perceptual changes in ambient temperature. The mapping between social warmth and physical warmth might be more conceptual or inferential than truly sensory, meaning that social experiences influence judgments about others or about interpersonal warmth without necessarily altering low-level sensory perception. Alternatively, the metaphor may operate under specific boundary conditions—such as when the social experience is interpersonal and directly embodied (e.g., physical contact) or when the context primes thermal metaphors explicitly.

Personality moderation hypotheses, such as the possibility that extraversion would buffer or amplify social-to-thermal effects, were not supported by the present data. The absence of an interaction suggests that trait extraversion, at least as measured by the IPIP and NEO scales and in this undergraduate sample, does not modulate the extent to which social exclusion influences room temperature estimates. This finding narrows the scope of personality factors implicated in embodied social effects, but it does not rule out other personality traits or motivational states that might matter. For instance, attachment style, social anxiety, or need to belong could plausibly influence embodied responses to exclusion and merit investigation.

The present results also invite reflection on the boundary between cognitive and perceptual processes in embodied cognition. If social experiences alter conceptual associations without changing sensory thresholds, experimental designs that rely on subjective judgments that are closer to conceptual reasoning may detect effects that direct sensory measures do not. Conversely, if social experiences can alter sensory perception under specific circumstances—such as during acute threat or extreme social pain—studies of everyday social exclusion may not capture those conditions. Clarifying whether embodied effects operate primarily at the level of sensation, perception, or conceptual appraisal is an important theoretical task for future work.

Methodological improvements for subsequent research include increasing sample size to ensure adequate power to detect small effects, pre-registering hypotheses and analysis plans to reduce researcher degrees of freedom, and diversifying measurement approaches. Using converging methods—self-report, behavioral indices, physiological measures like skin temperature or thermography, and neural measures—would provide a richer test of whether social states affect thermal perception at multiple levels. Additionally, employing more standardized or ecologically valid exclusion manipulations, such as Cyberball or controlled social interactions, could improve comparability across studies.

Cross-cultural investigations may also be revealing. Cultural differences in the salience of thermal metaphors and in social norms regarding inclusion and exclusion could influence the degree to which social experiences map onto thermal perception. For instance, cultures that frequently use thermal language to describe social relationships might show stronger social-to-thermal effects. Longitudinal or field studies could examine whether chronic social isolation influences thermoregulatory behavior or subjective thermal comfort in real-world settings, extending the laboratory findings to more applied contexts.

Alternative theoretical accounts should be considered as well. For example, affective explanations posit that negative affect produced by exclusion may generalize to a negative hedonic evaluation of the environment, which could be confounded with temperature judgments. If this is the case, controlling for mood and affect should attenuate any observed social-to-thermal effects. In the present study mood measures did not reveal relations with temperature estimates, but more sensitive mood assessment or experimental mood manipulations might clarify these dynamics.

Finally, practical implications of the broader research program are worth noting. If social experiences can influence perceived temperature or thermal comfort, this could have consequences for shared environments such as offices, classrooms, and public spaces, where social climate might interact with physical conditions to shape occupant comfort and behavior. The current null findings caution against assuming strong effects in typical laboratory settings, but they do not negate the possibility that social factors play a role under certain conditions. Understanding when and how social context shapes sensory experience could inform design and policy decisions regarding social inclusion and environmental management.

In conclusion, the present study did not find evidence that recalling social exclusion leads to lower room temperature estimates nor that extraversion moderates such an effect. The recall manipulation succeeded in altering reported feelings of exclusion, indicating that the psychological state was induced, but this did not translate into altered temperature judgments. These results suggest that social-to-thermal influences may be context-dependent, small in magnitude, or contingent on specific measurement approaches. Future research with larger samples, varied measures, and careful attention to boundary conditions is needed to delineate the robustness and mechanisms of embodied links between social and physical warmth.
