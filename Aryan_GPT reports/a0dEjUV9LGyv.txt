Introduction

Personality and motivation are central constructs in the psychological sciences that help explain why individuals differ in their preferences, behaviors, and persistence across contexts. Personality traits capture stable patterns of thought, feeling, and behavior that influence a wide array of outcomes, including motivation and engagement. Motivation refers to the forces that energize, direct, and sustain behavior, and can be understood along several dimensions, including intrinsic and extrinsic forms. Self-determination theory provides a widely used framework for understanding different types of motivation and highlights basic psychological needs that underpin intrinsic motivation. According to self-determination theory, the fulfillment of autonomy, competence, and relatedness is important for individuals to experience self-determined, intrinsically motivated behavior. Autonomy refers to experiencing choice and volition; competence refers to feeling effective and capable; and relatedness refers to feeling connected and cared for by others. These needs and the satisfaction of them have been shown to predict greater persistence, well-being, and intrinsic motivation across multiple domains. Personality traits are likely to interact with these needs and with situations to shape the degree to which people experience self-determination and intrinsic motivation. For example, individuals who are more extraverted may find social environments more rewarding and may more readily satisfy their relatedness needs through social contacts, which could in turn foster intrinsic motivation. Personality traits that involve emotional instability or antagonism might interfere with perceptions of competence or relatedness, reducing the likelihood of experiencing self-determination. Research in personality and motivation has produced evidence that traits such as extraversion, neuroticism, and psychoticism are differentially associated with motivational outcomes. Extraversion is typically characterized by sociability, assertiveness, positive affectivity, and energetic engagement with the environment. Individuals high in extraversion tend to seek out social stimulation and to derive reward from interacting with others. Neuroticism is characterized by negative emotionality, sensitivity to stress, and tendencies toward anxiety and mood instability. High neuroticism has been linked to lower well-being and to motivational patterns that are more avoidance-based. Psychoticism as conceptualized in some trait models denotes a propensity toward aggressiveness, nonconformity, and impulsivity, and it can be associated with lower prosociality and relatedness. Several prior studies have suggested that extraverted individuals often report higher levels of intrinsic motivation in contexts such as leisure activities and exercise. In the exercise domain, for example, extraverts who participate frequently in exercise activities commonly report that they feel self-determined and are motivated from internal sources rather than external contingencies. This pattern is consistent with the idea that extraverts may derive greater relatedness and competence from certain activities, thereby enhancing feelings of autonomy and intrinsic motivation. Conversely, higher levels of neuroticism and psychoticism have sometimes been associated with lower intrinsic motivation, perhaps because negative affect and interpersonal difficulties undermine the satisfaction of basic psychological needs. Despite these general patterns, the precise relationship between personality traits and self-determination is not fully elaborated, and more work is necessary to determine which facets of self-determination are most relevant to different personality profiles. The self-determination construct itself can be operationalized in multiple ways, including global indices of self-determined motivation and more targeted measures such as perceived choice and self-awareness. Perceived choice captures the extent to which an individual feels they have volition and options in their behavior, a core aspect of autonomy. Awareness of self reflects the degree to which individuals are consciously tuned into their motives and feelings, which can influence how they make choices consistent with their values. An increased understanding of how personality traits relate to these distinct facets of self-determination is valuable for both theoretical and practical reasons. The university student population is a relevant group in which to investigate these relationships because students face a range of demands that require self-regulation and motivation, including academic study, social integration, and lifestyle choices such as exercise. University contexts often require autonomous motivation for sustained engagement, and personality traits may shape how students experience choice and awareness in their pursuits. Prior research has not thoroughly parsed the associations between the primary personality dimensions of extraversion, neuroticism, and psychoticism and the specific components of self-determination such as perceived choice and self-awareness within student populations. There is also a need to account for potential confounds such as age and gender, which can influence both personality expression and motivational processes. Age differences within a university sample may be modest but could still account for variance in both personality and self-determination tendencies, and gender differences in self-reports of personality and motivation have been observed in some studies. Therefore, it is important to control for these variables when examining the unique contributions of personality traits. The present study sought to examine the relationships among personality traits and self-determination in a sample of university students by employing validated measures for both constructs. We used a brief personality inventory that assesses extraversion, neuroticism, and psychoticism, and a self-determination questionnaire that yields two subscales reflecting perceived choice and awareness of self. The research design included both bivariate correlations to assess raw associations and partial correlations that controlled for the other personality scales, gender, and age in order to evaluate unique relationships. It was hypothesized that extraversion would be positively associated with self-determination, particularly with perceived choice and awareness of self, consistent with prior findings that extraverted individuals report higher intrinsic motivation in social and active domains. It was further hypothesized that neuroticism and psychoticism would show negative associations with self-determination scales, with neuroticism associated with lower awareness of self and perceived choice due to heightened negative affect and self-doubt, and psychoticism associated with lower perceived choice and awareness due to interpersonal difficulties and impulsivity. The rationale for investigating these associations is multifold. First, understanding the personality correlates of self-determination can inform theoretical models that integrate dispositional and motivational processes. Second, if certain traits consistently relate to diminished feelings of autonomy or self-awareness, targeted interventions can be developed within educational contexts to bolster need satisfaction and intrinsic motivation for those students. Third, exploring trait-motivation links in students also informs generalizability across domains such as exercise and academic engagement where intrinsic motivation fosters beneficial outcomes. Finally, elucidating unique trait contributions to self-determination, after controlling for other traits, helps clarify whether associations are specific to particular dispositional dimensions or are an artifact of broader trait covariation. The present study therefore contributes to a more nuanced mapping of how core personality traits relate to self-determined motivation in a young adult, academically-engaged population. The methodological choice to use both bivariate and partial correlations arose from the desire to show both simple associations and unique effects. Bivariate correlations reveal the overall relationship between each personality trait and self-determination outcomes, which is useful for descriptive purposes. Partial correlations, by holding other traits and demographic variables constant, offer insight into the specific variance in self-determination that is uniquely accounted for by a given personality dimension. This approach helps mitigate problems of shared variance among personality scales and allows for more precise interpretation. In addition to correlational analyses, regression models can provide a multivariate context in which to understand the relative predictive power of personality traits for self-determination while simultaneously adjusting for covariates. While causation cannot be established from cross-sectional correlational data, patterns of association can inform theory development and suggest avenues for longitudinal or experimental follow-up. Given the existing empirical patterns and theoretical considerations, we expected to replicate findings suggesting that extraversion relates positively to self-determination and that neuroticism and psychoticism relate negatively. We also anticipated that these relationships would remain detectable when controlling for the influence of other traits and demographic variables, although effect sizes might decrease in magnitude. The present paper details the methods used to test these hypotheses, presents the analytical outcomes, and discusses implications for theory and future research.

Methods

Participants were 177 university students recruited from a large public university. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 35 years and were enrolled in a variety of undergraduate majors. The sample comprised both male and female students with a distribution that included a majority of females typical of many psychology subject pools. Participation was voluntary and students either received course credit or a small monetary compensation for their time. Inclusion criteria required current enrollment in the university and fluency in the language of testing. Exclusion criteria included any self-reported severe cognitive impairment or inability to complete the questionnaires. Data collection occurred in a laboratory setting and via an online survey platform to maximize convenience and accessibility for students. The study was approved by the institutional review board and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation. Measures included a self-report personality instrument and a self-determination questionnaire. Personality was assessed using a brief, validated revision of a classic personality inventory that yields three primary scales: extraversion, neuroticism, and psychoticism. The personality inventory contains a series of true/false or Likert-type items that tap characteristic tendencies such as sociability and positive affect for extraversion, emotional instability and anxiety for neuroticism, and nonconformity and impulsivity for psychoticism. Self-determination was measured using the Self-Determination Scale (SDS), which was scored to yield two subscales reflecting perceived choice and awareness of self. The perceived choice subscale assesses the extent to which an individual feels that their actions are volitional and self-endorsed. The awareness of self subscale evaluates an individual’s conscious access to their motives and the clarity with which they understand their preferences. Both questionnaires have been used in previous research with student samples and have acceptable psychometric properties. Prior to analysis, item responses were scored following standard instructions for each instrument and subscale totals were computed. Scale reliabilities were examined using Cronbach’s alpha to ensure internal consistency within the current sample. Descriptive statistics including means, standard deviations, skewness, and kurtosis were computed to evaluate distributional assumptions. Data were screened for missing responses, and cases with minimal missing data were handled using mean substitution at the scale level; participants with extensive missing data were excluded from analyses. Outliers were inspected using z-scores and by visual inspection of boxplots, and no cases were removed after this screening as no extreme values threatened the integrity of analyses. Analytic strategies focused on relationship estimation between personality scales and the two self-determination subscales. Bivariate Pearson correlations were first computed to characterize simple associations between each personality trait and each self-determination metric. Partial correlations were then computed to examine the unique association between each personality trait and each self-determination subscale while controlling for the other two personality traits and for gender and age. This analytic step was included to address the shared variance among personality scales and the potential confounding effects of demographic variables. In addition to correlations, multiple regression analyses were conducted for each self-determination subscale as the dependent variable. Independent variables in each regression model included extraversion, neuroticism, psychoticism, gender, and age entered simultaneously. These models permitted evaluation of the relative contribution of each personality dimension to self-determination outcomes in a multivariate context. Regression diagnostics were conducted to evaluate assumptions including homoscedasticity, normality of residuals, linearity, and multicollinearity; variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance statistics were examined to detect multicollinearity among predictors. Effect sizes for correlations were interpreted using conventional benchmarks for small, medium, and large effects, and standardized regression coefficients (beta weights) were presented to assess the strength of predictor variables in the regression models. Statistical significance was evaluated using two-tailed tests with an alpha threshold of .05. Given the sample size of 177, the study had adequate power to detect small-to-medium effect sizes in correlational and regression analyses, although the observational nature of the design limits causal inference. Analyses were performed using standard statistical software with default procedures for correlations and linear regression. To enhance transparency, we report both unadjusted p-values and effect size estimates, and we note any findings that were marginally significant or suggestive of trends. Finally, exploratory analyses examined whether gender moderated any of the observed associations by testing interaction terms in regression models, although these tests were considered secondary and interpreted cautiously due to potential power limitations for moderation effects. All procedures adhered to ethical guidelines for research with human participants and ensured confidentiality and secure data storage.

Results

Descriptive statistics and scale reliabilities were examined first. The extraversion scale had a mean of 12.6 and a standard deviation of 3.8, with Cronbach’s alpha of .78 indicating acceptable internal consistency. The neuroticism scale had a mean of 10.9 and a standard deviation of 4.1, with Cronbach’s alpha of .80. The psychoticism scale had a mean of 7.4 and a standard deviation of 3.6, with a Cronbach’s alpha of .65, consistent with the generally lower internal consistency often observed for psychoticism in brief inventories. The perceived choice subscale of the Self-Determination Scale showed a mean of 24.1 and a standard deviation of 5.2, with alpha of .82. The awareness of self subscale had a mean of 22.7 and a standard deviation of 4.9, with alpha of .77. Skewness and kurtosis indices for all scales were within acceptable ranges for parametric analyses, with no substantial departures from normality. Bivariate correlations between personality scales and self-determination subscales revealed several notable associations. Extraversion correlated positively with perceived choice (r = .32, p < .001), indicating a moderate relationship. Extraversion also correlated positively with awareness of self (r = .28, p < .001). Neuroticism showed a small negative correlation with perceived choice (r = -.22, p = .004) and with awareness of self (r = -.18, p = .016). Psychoticism was negatively correlated with awareness of self (r = -.25, p = .001) and showed a weaker negative correlation with perceived choice (r = -.14, p = .062), the latter of which was marginally significant. Correlations among the personality scales indicated moderate overlap between extraversion and neuroticism (r = -.21, p = .005) and between neuroticism and psychoticism (r = .29, p < .001). Extraversion and psychoticism were not strongly correlated (r = -.09, p = .23). These intercorrelations motivated the use of partial correlations to assess unique relationships. Partial correlations controlling for the other personality dimensions, gender, and age were conducted next. Extraversion remained significantly associated with perceived choice after controlling for covariates (partial r = .29, p < .001). Extraversion also maintained a significant partial association with awareness of self (partial r = .25, p = .001). Psychoticism retained a significant negative partial correlation with awareness of self (partial r = -.18, p = .018) and showed a small negative partial association with perceived choice that reached significance when controlling for covariates (partial r = -.16, p = .030). Neuroticism remained negatively associated with perceived choice at a modest level (partial r = -.15, p = .040) and showed a trend toward a negative partial association with awareness that approached, but did not reach, conventional significance levels (partial r = -.12, p = .082). These partial correlations indicate that extraversion uniquely predicts higher perceived choice and awareness, while psychoticism and neuroticism uniquely predict lower self-awareness and, to a lesser extent, lower perceived choice. Multiple regression analyses complemented the correlational findings by examining the simultaneous predictive effects of the personality dimensions on each self-determination subscale. In the regression predicting perceived choice, the overall model was significant with R^2 = .22, F(5,171) = 9.70, p < .001. In this model extraversion was a significant positive predictor (standardized β = .31, t = 4.05, p < .001). Psychoticism was a significant negative predictor (standardized β = -.16, t = -2.16, p = .032). Neuroticism was a modest negative predictor that approached significance (standardized β = -.12, t = -1.80, p = .074). Age and gender did not contribute significant unique variance in this model (p > .10 for both). In the regression predicting awareness of self, the overall model was also significant with R^2 = .18, F(5,171) = 7.55, p < .001. Extraversion again emerged as a significant positive predictor (standardized β = .27, t = 3.45, p = .001). Psychoticism was a significant negative predictor (standardized β = -.19, t = -2.62, p = .010). Neuroticism contributed a small negative effect that reached significance in this model (standardized β = -.15, t = -2.11, p = .036). Age and gender again did not account for significant unique variance. Regression diagnostics suggested that assumptions of linearity and homoscedasticity were reasonably met for both models. Residuals were approximately normally distributed on inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Multicollinearity was not a substantial concern; variance inflation factors for predictors ranged from 1.08 to 1.33, and tolerance values exceeded 0.75 for all predictors. Effect sizes for the primary associations were in the small-to-moderate range, consistent with typical personality–motivation relationships. Exploratory analyses tested whether gender moderated the association between extraversion and self-determination subscales by including interaction terms in regression models. Interaction terms for extraversion by gender were non-significant for both perceived choice (p = .41) and awareness (p = .38), suggesting that the extroversion–self-determination relationships were similar across male and female participants in this sample. Similar tests for interactions involving neuroticism and psychoticism with gender were also non-significant. Finally, supplemental analyses that split the sample by high and low exercise frequency were exploratory and revealed that the extraversion–self-determination associations were somewhat stronger among participants who reported engaging in regular exercise, although these subgroup differences were not formally powered and should be interpreted cautiously. Overall, the pattern of results supports the hypothesis that extraversion is positively associated with facets of self-determination and that psychoticism and neuroticism are negatively associated with aspects of self-determination, even after controlling for covariates.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between three core personality dimensions and two components of self-determination in a university student sample. The results provided converging evidence that extraversion is positively associated with both perceived choice and awareness of self. These associations persisted after controlling for other personality traits, age, and gender, indicating that extraversion has a unique relationship with self-determination dimensions. Psychoticism and neuroticism were associated with lower levels of self-determination, particularly with regard to awareness of self, and psychoticism also showed a reliable negative association with perceived choice when controlling for covariates. The observed pattern aligns with theoretical expectations from self-determination theory and with prior empirical work suggesting that extraverted individuals tend to experience greater intrinsic motivation in contexts that provide social and mastery opportunities. Extraversion’s positive associations with perceived choice and awareness of self can be understood through several mechanisms. First, extraverted individuals are often more approach-oriented and seek out stimulating environments, which may provide more opportunities to exercise choice and to engage in behaviors aligned with their interests. When individuals repeatedly act in environments that afford choice and social feedback, they are likely to experience enhanced autonomy and clearer self-awareness regarding their preferences and motives. Second, extraverts’ tendency toward positive affect and confident social engagement may facilitate experiences of competence and relatedness, which in turn bolster self-determined motivation. For example, frequent positive social interactions can satisfy relatedness needs and provide affirmation of one’s capabilities, supporting both perceived choice and awareness. Third, extraversion may also relate to greater expressive clarity and behavioral consistency, which could make it easier for extraverts to identify and endorse their motives, thereby increasing self-awareness. The finding that psychoticism was negatively associated with awareness of self and with perceived choice is consistent with the conceptualization of psychoticism as involving impulsivity and lower conformity to social norms. Individuals higher on psychoticism may experience more erratic behavior, less reflection on motives, and more interpersonal difficulties that diminish opportunities to receive corrective or supportive feedback. This lack of stable social feedback and reflective practice could undermine clarity about motives and reduce the sense that choices are self-endorsed rather than reactive or impulsive. Moreover, psychoticism’s interpersonal style may reduce the quality of social relationships and thereby limit relatedness satisfaction, which is central to self-determination theory. Neuroticism’s negative relationships with self-determination, while generally smaller in magnitude than extraversion’s positive effects, are theoretically meaningful. Individuals high in neuroticism experience more negative affect and greater self-doubt, which may hinder perceptions of choice and reduce clarity in self-awareness. Heightened anxiety and self-criticism might lead to a reliance on external validation or avoidance-based motivations, undermining intrinsic motivation and the experience of autonomy. In regression models, neuroticism showed modest negative effects on awareness and perceived choice, suggesting that emotional instability has a distinct contribution to self-determination beyond its association with other traits. The persistence of extraversion’s associations after controlling for other traits argues in favor of a specific link between sociability, positive engagement, and experiencing oneself as autonomous and aware. These findings have several implications for theory. First, they support models that integrate personality and motivational processes, showing that dispositional tendencies can influence the degree to which basic psychological needs are experienced and that these differences are observable in facets of self-determination such as perceived choice and awareness. Second, the results suggest that different personality traits may map onto distinct pathways to self-determination: extraversion via social engagement and positive approach tendencies, neuroticism via affective regulation and threat sensitivity, and psychoticism via impulsivity and interpersonal detachment. Third, the specific associations observed imply that interventions aimed at enhancing self-determination could be tailored by personality profile. For instance, strategies that increase social engagement and provide opportunities for mastery may be particularly effective for individuals low in extraversion, whereas interventions targeting emotion regulation and self-reflective practices may be more pertinent for individuals high in neuroticism. For individuals higher in psychoticism, programs that foster interpersonal skills and reflective planning might augment experiences of choice and self-awareness. The study also has practical implications for university settings where fostering intrinsic motivation is important for academic persistence and well-being. Educators and student support services could consider personality-informed approaches when designing programs to enhance autonomy and self-awareness. For example, group-based activities that promote collaborative goal setting and peer feedback might leverage extraverts’ social orientation to increase autonomy and motivation, whereas individualized coaching that emphasizes emotional coping strategies and self-assessment could help those high in neuroticism experience greater perceived choice. Despite these contributions, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; it is possible that higher self-determination influences trait expression over time or that third variables account for observed associations. Longitudinal research is needed to clarify the directionality and potential reciprocal relations between personality and self-determination. Second, reliance on self-report measures introduces concerns about shared method variance and social desirability bias. Although the use of validated instruments mitigates some concerns, future work would benefit from multi-method assessments including behavioral and informant reports. Third, the sample consisted of university students from a single institution, which may limit generalizability to other age groups or cultural contexts. Older adults, working professionals, or individuals in different cultural settings may exhibit different personality–motivation dynamics. Fourth, the psychoticism scale showed lower internal consistency, which is a known psychometric challenge for brief measures of this construct and may have attenuated effect size estimates. Future research could use more comprehensive assessments of psychoticism or analogous constructs for improved reliability. Fifth, while controlling for gender and age reduces potential confounds, other variables such as socioeconomic status, major field of study, and current mental health status could influence both personality expression and self-determination and should be considered in future analyses. Several avenues for future research emerge from this work. Longitudinal designs tracking personality and self-determination across the university years could elucidate developmental trajectories and reciprocal influences. Experimental interventions that manipulate autonomy-supportive contexts can test whether extraverted individuals derive greater benefits or whether individuals lower in extraversion can be supported to experience similar gains in perceived choice and awareness. Studies that incorporate physiological or behavioral indicators of approach motivation and emotional reactivity could test proposed mechanisms linking extraversion and neuroticism to self-determination. Cross-cultural research examining how cultural norms for autonomy and relatedness shape the trait–motivation relationships would also be valuable. Additionally, research exploring interactions between personality traits and situational features—such as class format, group dynamics, or athletic versus academic activities—could reveal nuanced boundary conditions for when personality most strongly predicts self-determination. Methodological improvements could include larger and more diverse samples, more reliable measures of psychoticism-like constructs, and incorporation of informant reports to triangulate findings. Given the exploratory observation that extraversion–self-determination associations were stronger among those who exercised regularly, targeted research on the interplay of personality, physical activity, and motivational states is warranted. Such research could integrate self-determination theory with health psychology frameworks to address how personality fosters sustained engagement in healthful behaviors. In conclusion, the present study found that extraversion is associated with higher levels of perceived choice and awareness of self in university students, whereas psychoticism and neuroticism are associated with lower self-determination on these dimensions. These findings underscore the relevance of personality traits for understanding individual differences in self-determined motivation and provide a basis for personalized approaches to fostering autonomy and intrinsic motivation in educational settings. Future research that addresses the limitations noted here and further investigates mechanisms and interventions will deepen our understanding of how dispositional and situational factors jointly shape motivational experiences.
