Introduction

Every day, people make decisions under uncertainty across a wide variety of domains. Sometimes these decisions are made on the basis of a description of possible outcomes and their probabilities, such as when a person reads a weather forecast before deciding whether to take an umbrella. At other times, decisions are made primarily from personal experience, such as when a person repeatedly attends a train service that is sometimes late and must decide whether to leave earlier the next day. The distinction between decisions made from description and decisions made from experience is consequential because the two information formats can lead to systematic differences in choices. One prominent empirical regularity is the observation that rare events tend to be overweighted when probabilities are supplied in a description and underweighted when probabilities must be inferred from sampling experience. This pattern, often described as the description–experience gap, has been reported in multiple studies and has stimulated a large literature on the cognitive processes that underlie probabilistic choice. The basic pattern is that when people are given explicit probability information such as "1% chance of a very bad outcome," they often treat that small probability as more important than would be predicted by objective expected value alone. By contrast, when people must learn about the same low-probability event through repeated sampling or experience, they often underweight it, acting as if the event is even less likely than it truly is. A central explanation for the underweighting in experience-based choice has been sampling error: because rare events occur infrequently, they may simply not be observed by many decision-makers during the sampling phase, and so their influence on decisions is diminished. Another line of explanation emphasizes memory and representativeness: people may rely on a small set of salient memories rather than on a statistically representative account of the distribution of outcomes. These ideas suggest that the number of samples, stopping strategies for sampling, and how samples are encoded and retrieved are all crucial determinants of whether experience leads to underweighting or to accurate weighting of rare outcomes. The overweighting in description, in contrast, has been linked to cognitive operations that make rare outcomes salient and multiply their subjective weight, possibly because explicit numeric probabilities are mapped onto nonlinear probability weighting functions that amplify small probabilities in decision weight. Prospect-theory inspired accounts have formalized this observation by positing a probability weighting function that is inverse S-shaped for gains and losses under descriptive information. Together, these descriptive and experiential findings create a compelling picture in which the same objective gamble can be treated differently purely because of whether its probabilities are learned by description or by experience. However, there are important unresolved questions about the generality of the description–experience gap and about moderating factors that determine when and how the gap emerges. One open question concerns the role of the medium by which experience is sampled. Most experimental demonstrations of the description–experience gap have used computerized sampling where trials are generated and presented on a screen, and subjects click to obtain samples. Computerized presentation has many practical advantages but may also shape attention, the perceived realism of outcomes, and the cognitive strategies that participants adopt. Physical sampling procedures—where participants physically manipulate tokens, counters, or cards during sampling—are comparatively rare in this literature. Physical sampling could influence behavior for several reasons. First, tactile interaction might increase engagement and memory for sampled outcomes, thereby making rare events more memorable and reducing underweighting. Second, physical counters often leave a visible record of previous samples, directly supporting a more representative encoding of the distribution; in contrast, computerized single-trial displays may not afford such cumulative representation unless specifically designed to do so. Third, the act of physically drawing counters may make participants adopt a different stopping rule for sampling, perhaps leading to larger sample sizes and thus improving the chances that a rare event is observed. These potential mechanisms suggest that altering the sampling medium from computer displays to physical counters could attenuate or even eliminate the description–experience gap. Another factor that could moderate choices about rare events is the decision-maker's dispositional affect or outlook, such as optimism. Optimism, broadly defined as a generalized tendency to expect positive outcomes, could plausibly influence how people respond to low-probability events. For instance, optimistic individuals might downweight low-probability negative events more than less optimistic individuals because their general expectation of favorable outcomes biases them against anticipating bad luck. Alternatively, optimism might promote risk-taking when the gamble involves a low chance of a large win, because optimistic individuals expect to be among the lucky few. Empirical support for links between optimism and risky decision-making is equivocal, with some studies reporting associations and others finding no effect. Despite this, it remains theoretically plausible that trait optimism could moderate the description–experience gap by shaping the encoding, attention, or updating of probabilistic information. The current study therefore considered optimism as an individual difference that could relate to risky choice in description and experience conditions. Beyond these theoretical considerations, there is a practical motivation to examine whether the description–experience gap replicates under modifications of standard paradigms. Replication is necessary not only to confirm robustness but also to delineate boundary conditions. If a modest change such as the modality of sampling eliminates the gap, that change tells us something important about the cognitive processes that produce the gap in the first place. Specifically, if physical counters lead to data that are more representative during sampling, and if this change eliminates underweighting of rare events, then sampling processes are implicated as the proximal cause of the experience-based underweighting. Conversely, if physical counters do not alter the gap, then explanations emphasizing deeper cognitive transformations, such as probability weighting functions that differ when information is described versus learned, gain support. A second practical reason to test a modified sampling procedure is to explore ecological validity. People often encounter risky environments in ways that are more akin to physical sampling than to rapid computer trials. For example, small business owners may experience rare equipment failures over months in a tangible way that leaves a record in repair logs, and consumers may keep physical receipts that serve as external memory. If the description–experience gap depends on an artificial aspect of the lab environment, then its relevance to real-world behavior is limited. The current study therefore adopted a physical sampling method to test whether the description–experience phenomenon is robust to such a change. In designing the experiment, it was important to maintain key features of earlier description–experience paradigms so that results would be comparable. The experiment included a between-subjects manipulation in which participants either received a full description of a risky option's outcome distribution (description condition) or learned the distribution by drawing physical counters from an opaque container (experience condition). The risky option in both conditions involved a choice between a safe, certain outcome and a risky, probabilistic outcome with a rare event that offered a relatively large payoff. The probability and payoff parameters were chosen to be in the range commonly used in prior studies showing the description–experience gap, so that any failure to replicate could more plausibly be attributed to the sampling medium or other design differences rather than to an extreme choice of parameters. The study also included an optimism questionnaire administered after the decision phase to assess individual differences in generalized positive expectations. This allowed us to test whether optimism correlated with the tendency to choose the risky option and whether optimism moderated any difference between description and experience conditions. The study's primary hypotheses were thus threefold. First, we hypothesized a replication of the classic description–experience gap: participants in the description condition would be more likely to choose the risky option than those in the experience condition, reflecting overweighting of the rare event in description and underweighting in experience. Second, we hypothesized that the use of physical counters might reduce the size of the experience-based underweighting relative to what has been observed in computerized versions, potentially attenuating the gap. This second hypothesis was directional in the sense that it anticipated a smaller gap rather than a reversal, but the magnitude of attenuation was uncertain. Third, we hypothesized that greater optimism would be associated with increased choice of risky options when the gamble had a small chance of a large positive outcome, and that this relationship might be stronger in one modality (description or experience) if optimism affects the encoding or weighting of probabilistic information differentially across formats. The study therefore provided both a conceptual replication of prior work and an empirical test of potential boundary conditions related to sampling modality and individual differences. By combining a physical sampling procedure with a standard decision task and an optimism measure, the experiment offered an opportunity to refine theoretical accounts of the description–experience gap and to identify conditions under which the gap is robust. The following Methods, Results, and Discussion sections present the details of the study, the analytic outcomes, and the interpretation of results in light of these hypotheses and the broader literature.

Methods

Participants were recruited from an undergraduate subject pool and from a general community participant list. In total, 120 individuals volunteered to take part in the experiment. Of these, seven participants were excluded prior to analysis because they failed an attention check embedded in the instructions or because they did not complete the optimism questionnaire; one additional participant was excluded because of a prior familiarity with the description–experience literature that may have influenced their behavior. The final analyzed sample therefore consisted of 112 participants. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 56 years, with a mean age of 23.9 years and a standard deviation of 6.1 years. There were 68 female and 44 male participants in the final sample. Participants provided written informed consent prior to participation and were compensated with course credit or a small monetary payment equivalent to standard subject pool rates. The study employed a between-subjects experimental design with two conditions: description and experience. Participants were randomly assigned to one of the two conditions using a random number generator at the time of recruitment, yielding 57 participants in the description condition and 55 in the experience condition after exclusions. The core decision task required each participant to choose between two options on a single forced-choice trial. Option A was a safe, certain outcome that yielded a moderate payoff with probability 1.0. Option B was a risky gamble that offered a large payoff if a rare event occurred and a zero payoff otherwise. The exact payoff schedule was explained in both conditions, and the gamble parameters were chosen to match commonly used values from prior studies that investigated rare-event perception. Specifically, Option A provided a guaranteed 3 points, and Option B provided 0 points with probability 0.90 and 31 points with probability 0.10. The expected value of Option B (3.1 points) slightly exceeded that of Option A (3 points), creating a context in which choosing the risky option could be justified by expected value but might depend on subjective probability weighting. The choice between a certain small gain and a probabilistic large gain with a rare event is a standard format for producing description–experience differences. The description condition presented participants with a written summary that explicitly stated the probabilities and payoffs for each option. The description was displayed on paper with a boldface statement that Option B produced 31 points on 10 out of 100 draws and 0 points on 90 out of 100 draws. Participants were instructed to read the description carefully and to imagine making a single choice between the options. They were told that their decision would determine a bonus that would be converted to a small monetary reward at the end of the experiment according to a fixed exchange rate. The description condition thus provided full explicit probability information in a conventional format. The experience condition required participants to learn about the distribution underlying Option B through physical sampling. An opaque cloth bag contained 100 small counters, each marked either 0 or 31. Ninety counters were marked 0 and ten counters were marked 31, providing an exact correspondence to the 90/10 probability structure used in the description condition. Participants in the experience condition were instructed that they could draw counters from the bag with replacement for up to 20 draws. Each draw involved reaching into the bag, selecting a counter, recording the result on a tally sheet provided by the experimenter, and then returning the counter to the bag while shaking it to restore the distribution. The experimenter emphasized that the draws were independent and that the bag contained the same composition throughout. The decision procedure in the experience condition was thus designed to mimic common computer-based sampling procedures but using tangible, physical counters that left a written trace on participants' tally sheets. The maximum number of draws (20) was chosen to balance participant time and the desire to provide enough opportunities for observing rare events. Participants were free to stop sampling earlier than 20 draws if they felt they had sufficient information to make a decision; the number of draws taken was recorded automatically from the tally sheets. After completing the sampling phase, experience-condition participants were asked to make the same one-time choice between Option A and Option B that description-condition participants made. To incentivize truthful choice and engagement, one participant from each session was randomly selected at the end of the experiment to have their decision realized for a small monetary bonus. Realization involved translating points into cents at a fixed exchange rate; if Option B had been chosen and a rare outcome was to be realized, the experimenter used a separate draw from a fresh bag of counters to produce the realized payoff. This incentive scheme is standard and was communicated to participants prior to their decision. In addition to the primary decision task, participants completed an optimism questionnaire after the choice phase. The optimism questionnaire consisted of six items designed to capture generalized positive expectations about the future. Each item was presented with a five-point Likert scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree. The items were presented in mixed key so that higher scores corresponded to greater optimism after reverse scoring where necessary. The optimism measure was intended to be brief to minimize participant fatigue while providing adequate variability for correlational analysis. The internal consistency of the optimism scale was assessed using Cronbach's alpha in the sample. In addition to the optimism measure, participants completed manipulation checks. Description-condition participants were asked to restate the probability of the rare event in their own words to confirm comprehension. Experience-condition participants were asked to report how many rare events they had observed during sampling and to estimate the probability of the rare outcome as a percentage. These manipulation checks served both to ensure that participants had processed the information and to provide data on subjective probability estimates for exploratory analysis. The primary outcome measure was the binary choice between Option A (safe) and Option B (risky). Secondary measures included the number of draws taken in the experience condition, subjective probability estimates derived from the manipulation check responses, and the optimism scale score. Statistical analyses were pre-registered prior to data collection, and the analytic plan followed three primary steps. First, we compared the proportion of participants choosing the risky option between the description and experience conditions using a chi-square test of independence and computed the associated effect size (Cramer's V) and 95% confidence interval for the difference in proportions. Second, we examined whether optimism predicted risky choice using logistic regression, with choice as the dependent variable and optimism score as the predictor, and tested for interaction between condition and optimism by including a condition-by-optimism interaction term. Third, we conducted exploratory analyses to investigate whether the number of rare events observed during sampling was associated with subsequent risky choice, using point-biserial correlations and logistic regression in the experience condition. We also computed Bayesian equivalents for the primary tests to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis as appropriate. All analyses were conducted with standard statistical software and with an alpha threshold of 0.05 for frequentist tests. Effect sizes and confidence intervals were reported to aid interpretation beyond p-values.

Results

The final analyzed sample consisted of 112 participants, with 57 participants in the description condition and 55 in the experience condition. Descriptive statistics for age and gender indicated that the two conditions were comparable on observed demographics, with no significant differences in mean age (description mean = 24.1 years, experience mean = 23.6 years; t(110) = 0.45, p = 0.65) or in gender composition (χ2(1) = 0.12, p = 0.73). All participants in the description condition correctly restated the provided probabilities on the manipulation check, indicating high comprehension of the descriptive information. In the experience condition, the number of draws taken by participants varied considerably, demonstrating that the optional sampling procedure elicited individual differences in sampling behavior. The mean number of draws in the experience condition was 12.7 with a standard deviation of 5.1. The distribution of draws taken was slightly right-skewed, with a median of 13 draws and a range from 2 to the maximum of 20 draws. Participants observed a mean of 1.17 rare events (31-point counters) during sampling, with a standard deviation of 1.05 rare events observed. Twenty-two participants in the experience condition observed no rare events during their sampling, 18 observed one rare event, 10 observed two rare events, and the remaining five observed three or more rare events. The primary comparison tested whether the proportion of participants choosing the risky Option B differed across the description and experience conditions. In the description condition, 33 of 57 participants chose the risky option, representing 57.9% of that group. In the experience condition, 29 of 55 participants chose the risky option, representing 52.7% of that group. The absolute difference in proportions was 5.2 percentage points, with a 95% confidence interval for the difference spanning approximately -12.9 to 23.3 percentage points. A chi-square test of independence yielded χ2(1) = 0.30, p = 0.58, indicating no significant difference in risky choice between conditions. The corresponding Cramer's V was 0.05, reflecting a very small effect size. Given the non-significant frequentist result, we computed a Bayesian contingency-table analysis to quantify the relative evidence for the null and alternative hypotheses. The Bayes factor in favor of the null hypothesis (no difference) over a medium-effect alternative was approximately BF01 = 3.4, suggesting moderate evidence for the null over the hypothesized difference of substantive magnitude. Because many studies in this domain have reported larger description–experience effects, we also examined whether the observed sample size provided sufficient power to detect an effect of the size commonly reported in the literature. A post hoc power calculation indicated that with the observed sample size, the study had approximately 80% power to detect a difference in proportions of approximately 25 percentage points between conditions, but lower power to detect smaller differences. To examine the role of optimism, we first assessed the internal consistency of the optimism questionnaire. Cronbach's alpha for the six-item optimism scale in the current sample was 0.79, indicating acceptable reliability. The mean optimism score across the full sample was 3.21 on the five-point scale with a standard deviation of 0.62. There was no significant difference in mean optimism between the two conditions (description mean = 3.24, experience mean = 3.18; t(110) = 0.53, p = 0.60, Cohen's d = 0.10). We then tested whether optimism predicted the binary risky choice by fitting a logistic regression model with risky choice as the dependent variable and optimism score as the predictor. The logistic regression coefficient for optimism was b = 0.17 (SE = 0.26), corresponding to an odds ratio of 1.19, and the effect was not statistically significant (Wald z = 0.65, p = 0.52). The 95% confidence interval for the odds ratio ranged from approximately 0.73 to 1.94, indicating that the data were compatible with both modest decreases and increases in the odds of choosing the risky option per unit increase in optimism, but were centered near null. To test whether the relationship between optimism and choice depended on condition, we estimated a second logistic regression including condition, optimism, and their interaction. The interaction term was non-significant (b = -0.09, SE = 0.39, p = 0.82), and the overall model did not improve fit relative to a model without the interaction (likelihood-ratio test χ2(1) = 0.03, p = 0.86). Point-biserial correlations between optimism and risky choice within each condition were small and non-significant (description r_pb = 0.07, p = 0.60; experience r_pb = -0.03, p = 0.83). Given the variability in sampling behavior in the experience condition, we explored whether the number of draws or the number of observed rare events predicted subsequent risky choice. A point-biserial correlation between number of draws and risky choice in the experience condition was r_pb = 0.10 (p = 0.46), indicating no significant association between drawing more samples and choosing the risky gamble. Similarly, the number of observed rare events had a correlation with risky choice of r_pb = 0.12 (p = 0.38), again non-significant. Logistic regression in the experience condition with number of draws and number of rare events as simultaneous predictors showed that neither predictor reached significance (number of draws b = 0.04, SE = 0.06, p = 0.50; number of rare events b = 0.21, SE = 0.31, p = 0.50). We also asked whether subjective probability estimates reported in the manipulation check predicted choices. In the experience condition, participants provided percentage estimates for the likelihood of drawing a 31-point counter. The mean subjective estimate was 11.2% with a standard deviation of 6.4%. The correlation between subjective estimate and risky choice was r_pb = 0.09 (p = 0.49), indicating no significant relation. When subjective probability estimates were entered into a logistic regression predicting risky choice while controlling for the actual number of rare events observed, the estimate remained non-significant (b = 0.02, SE = 0.03, p = 0.46). Exploratory analyses examined whether participants who observed no rare events differed from those who observed one or more rare events in their tendency to choose the risky option. Among the twenty-two participants who observed no rare events, 11 chose the risky option (50%). Among the remaining thirty-three participants who observed one or more rare events, 18 chose the risky option (54.5%). The difference in proportions was small and not statistically significant (Fisher's exact test p = 0.78). Given the null findings for both the primary condition comparison and the optimism correlations, we conducted sensitivity analyses to assess whether excluding participants with extreme sampling behavior or low comprehension altered the results. Excluding the five participants who took fewer than four draws in the experience condition did not meaningfully change the outcomes; the difference in proportions between conditions remained small (difference = 6.8 percentage points) and non-significant (χ2(1) = 0.37, p = 0.54). Similarly, excluding participants with more than three rare events observed did not alter the primary conclusion. The Bayesian logistic regression that included condition and optimism also provided evidence favoring the null hypothesis for both predictors, with BF01 values of approximately 3.0 for condition and 4.2 for optimism, indicating moderate support for the absence of effects. In summary, the primary analyses revealed no significant difference between description and experience conditions in the proportion of participants choosing the risky option, and no evidence that optimism predicted risky choice or interacted with condition. Exploratory analyses of sampling behavior and subjective probability estimates likewise failed to reveal significant associations with choice.

Discussion

The present study set out to replicate the well-documented description–experience gap in risky choice using a physical sampling procedure and to examine whether trait optimism related to choices involving rare events. The main findings were clear and consistent across multiple analyses: there was no significant difference in risky choice between participants who received a full description of the probability distribution and those who learned the distribution through physical sampling. In addition, individual differences in optimism did not predict the tendency to choose the risky option, nor did optimism interact with information format. Exploratory analyses examining sampling behavior within the experience condition likewise failed to reveal robust associations between the number of draws, the number of observed rare events, subjective probability estimates, and subsequent choice. These null findings warrant careful consideration because they diverge from many prior studies that reported overweighting of rare events in description and underweighting in experience. The results raise two classes of interpretation. One interpretation is that the description–experience gap is sensitive to procedural features, and that the physical sampling procedure used here eliminated or attenuated the effect. An alternative interpretation is that the gap may be less robust than previously thought under a broader set of methodological variations, and that reporting biases in the literature have inflated expectations of replication. Both interpretations have implications for theory and for future research design. Considering the possibility that physical sampling altered the cognitive processes underlying experience-based choice, several mechanisms could plausibly explain why the gap did not emerge. First, physical counters might have provided a tangible record during sampling that enhanced memory for rare events. Because each sample was recorded on a tally sheet and counters were visible during handling, participants may have been better able to form an accurate mental representation of the distribution. This could increase the likelihood that rare events influenced later choice decisions, thereby eliminating the underweighting typically observed when samples are presented one at a time on a computer screen and are not aggregated externally. Second, the tactile and visual feedback of drawing counters could have increased engagement and attention during sampling relative to passive computer displays, leading participants to sample more carefully and to be more sensitive to the outcomes they observed. Although the mean number of draws was only modestly above 12 in our sample, variability in draws did not predict choice, suggesting that mere quantity of sampling is not the whole story; rather, the qualitative experience of interacting with physical tokens and externally recording results may have improved representativeness for a given number of draws. Third, the physical nature of sampling might have changed participants' stopping rules. When counters are physically present and a tally sheet accumulates visible marks, participants may feel less uncertainty and thus stop sampling at a point that yields a more accurate internalized estimate of the distribution. In contrast, computer-based sampling that does not leave a durable trace may encourage participants to draw fewer or noisier inferences from the sampled trials. Any of these mechanisms could account for the attenuation of underweighting in the experience condition. A second set of factors relates to the parameters chosen for the gamble and the sampling regime. The present study used a 90/10 split with up to 20 draws allowed. While these values are similar to those used in prior work, small differences in probabilities, payoff ratios, or draw limits can influence the probability of observing rare events and thereby alter behavior. For example, had the rare event probability been 1% rather than 10%, the likelihood of observing the rare outcome within 20 draws would have been substantially lower, possibly producing stronger underweighting. Similarly, if participants had been required to sample with replacement without a visible tally sheet or had been given fewer draws, the experience condition may have looked more like previous computer-based implementations that observed underweighting. Thus, it is possible that the lack of a description–experience gap reflects the particular combination of a relatively high rare-event probability, a generous maximum number of draws, and the physical recording of each draw. A related point is that the expected value difference between the two options in our design was small (Option B EV = 3.1 vs. Option A EV = 3.0). Although this subtlety matches some prior designs and ensures that choices are not dominated purely by EV, it also means that participants' preferences may have been sensitive to small subjective shifts in perceived probability or to idiosyncratic risk attitudes. If participants in both conditions were roughly ambivalent about the small EV advantage of the risky option, then the description–experience manipulation might have had limited room to move behavior in one direction or the other. Concerning optimism, the lack of a relationship with risky choice suggests that generalized positive expectations, at least as measured by our brief optimism instrument, do not strongly influence single-shot choices between a certain small gain and a risky gamble with a rare large gain. There are several possible explanations for this null association. One is that optimism is more relevant to repeated or outcome-dependent decision contexts rather than to single, explicitly framed gambles. Optimism may influence persistence, subjective well-being, or interpretations of ambiguous future scenarios, but these tendencies may not translate into altered weighting of explicit numeric probabilities in a one-off laboratory choice. Another possibility is that our optimism measure, while reliable, may not have tapped the specific form of cognitive bias that would influence probabilistic weighting. For example, a measure that explicitly captures expectancy for being "lucky" or for believing that personal outcomes will deviate from population averages might correlate more strongly with choice under risk. It is also possible that optimism interacts with affective responses to observed outcomes in repeated-choice tasks, shaping learning and updating from feedback, rather than influencing the initial valuation of described probabilities. The study's null findings also invite reflection about statistical power and evidence interpretation. Although the sample size afforded reasonable power to detect relatively large effects, it was less well powered to detect small effects. Our Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence favoring the null over a medium-sized effect, suggesting that the absence of a sizable description–experience gap in our data is not simply an artifact of low power. However, the data are compatible with modest effects that our sample could not detect, and larger samples would be required to rule out small but theoretically meaningful differences. Beyond issues of statistical power, methodological limitations of the present study are important to acknowledge. First, the participant sample consisted primarily of university-affiliated individuals and community volunteers, which may limit generalizability. Different populations, such as older adults, professionals with high exposure to low-probability events, or individuals from cultures with different attitudes toward risk, might show different patterns. Second, the incentive structure involved converting points to a small monetary bonus for one randomly selected participant rather than paying all participants for their choices. Although this is a common approach that preserves incentives while controlling costs, it may not produce the same level of motivational engagement as paying every participant for the outcome associated with their decision. If motivation affects the degree to which participants attend to probabilities or sample carefully, then the incentive structure could influence the presence of the description–experience gap. Third, the optimism questionnaire was brief by design, and while its internal consistency was acceptable, a more comprehensive measure of dispositional optimism or additional personality constructs (such as risk propensity or numeracy) could provide a richer picture of individual differences that interact with information format. Fourth, the physical counters and tally sheets, while designed to increase ecological validity and to provide a visible sample record, may have introduced unintended cues—for example, the sight of many zero-marked counters could have induced a framing effect or produced anchoring that affected subsequent probability estimates. Future work should consider variations that isolate these possibilities, such as using physical counters without a visible tally for comparison. Theoretical implications of the present null findings are worth emphasizing. If physical sampling attenuates or eliminates the description–experience gap, as our data suggest, then process-based explanations that emphasize sampling error and representativeness gain support. Specifically, the idea that experience-based underweighting arises because rare events are unlikely to be observed in small samples could be complemented by the notion that the externalization and salience of samples determine how representative the learned distribution is. These ideas connect to broader theories of memory and judgment that posit that external aids and encoding formats shape the retrieval processes that drive choice. Conversely, if future replications demonstrate that the description–experience gap can be reliably eliminated by modest changes in the sampling procedure, then models that ascribe the gap to intrinsic differences in how described probabilities are cognitively transformed (for example via a consistent, domain-general probability weighting function applied to descriptions) may need to incorporate procedural moderators such as memory aids and sampling media. The present results therefore encourage theoretical pluralism, suggesting that both sampling mechanisms and representational transforms likely play roles, with their relative influence depending on the specifics of the situation. Several concrete directions for future research arise from the current study. One priority is to conduct a systematic comparison of physical versus computerized sampling in a within-subjects design, so that each participant experiences both formats and acts as their own control. Such a design would increase statistical power to detect format effects and would allow direct assessment of how individuals change their sampling strategies between modalities. Another direction is to manipulate the visibility of sample records explicitly; for example, compare physical counters plus tally, physical counters without tally, computer sampling with a running histogram, and computer sampling without aggregate displays. These manipulations would help disentangle whether it is the tactile nature of counters, the visual persistence of sample records, or the mere availability of aggregated information that matters. It would also be valuable to vary rare-event probability and payoff magnitudes systematically to map out the boundary conditions under which the gap emerges or disappears. A third avenue is to incorporate richer individual-difference measures such as full pessimism–optimism inventories, numeracy, working memory capacity, need for cognition, and risk attitudes. Collecting these measures would illuminate whether specific traits moderate the translation from sampled experience to choice and would test hypotheses about whether optimism interacts with feedback-driven learning processes rather than initial probability weighting. Fourth, process-tracing methodologies, including eye-tracking, think-aloud protocols, or neurophysiological measures, could reveal the attentional and cognitive operations that differ across sampling modalities and that predict whether rare events are encoded and retrieved. Finally, formal modeling efforts that fit alternative process models to choice and sampling data—such as reinforcement-learning models, Bayesian updating models that incorporate sampling noise, and drift-diffusion models of decision accumulation—could adjudicate between competing accounts by showing which model best captures observed behavior under different sampling conditions. In conclusion, the current study failed to replicate the canonical description–experience gap when experience was acquired through physical counters and while using a brief optimism questionnaire. The absence of a significant difference between description and experience conditions, coupled with the lack of association between optimism and choice, points to the importance of procedural details in shaping how people learn from and act upon probabilistic information. Rather than invalidating prior findings, these results prompt a more nuanced view that recognizes the description–experience phenomenon as contingent on the nature of sampling and representation. Understanding those contingencies will be essential for both theoretical advances and practical applications in domains where rare events matter.
