Introduction

Human cognition routinely links information across sensory modalities, and such crossmodal correspondences have been a persistent focus of research in cognitive psychology and linguistics. One particularly robust instance of crossmodal correspondence involves the mapping between speech sounds and visual shape: many listeners consistently associate the pseudoword bouba with rounded, curvilinear shapes and the pseudoword kiki with angular, spiky shapes. This phenomenon, often referred to as the bouba/kiki effect, has been replicated across many studies, languages, and age groups, suggesting that it taps into a general property of human perception. The bouba/kiki effect has been taken as evidence that the link between sound and shape is neither arbitrary nor solely culturally learned, and it has prompted inquiries into the mechanisms that support systematic relations between phonology and meaning. Understanding the bouba/kiki effect therefore bears on broader questions about how language is grounded in perception and how sensory and linguistic systems interact.

The bouba/kiki phenomenon sits within a larger literature on sound symbolism, which is the idea that phonetic properties of words can evoke particular meanings or referents. Sound-symbolic effects have been observed for size (e.g., "maluma" vs "takete"), for notions of brightness, for tactile qualities, and for other perceptual dimensions. Some theorists argue that sound symbolism reflects shared neural coding across sensory dimensions, possibly mediated by multimodal associative areas that respond to patterns of spectral and temporal properties of sounds alongside visual features. Others emphasize the role of articulatory or acoustic correlates: clustered articulatory gestures and spectral dynamics in certain pseudowords may systematically resemble the spatial features of particular shapes or magnitudes. Across these accounts, the bouba/kiki effect is often used as a touchstone case of non-arbitrary mapping between phonetic form and visual shape.

A distinct but related area of research concerns magnitude symbolism, where certain phonetic features are associated with perceived size or quantity. Experimental work has shown that vowels with low second formant frequencies and back vowels are more likely to be associated with largeness, whereas high front vowels and voiceless consonants are often associated with smallness. This pattern has been argued to reflect acoustic correlates: lower-frequency sounds convey a sense of largeness because they resemble the lower-frequency resonances produced by larger bodies, whereas higher-frequency sounds resemble the acoustic signatures of smaller bodies. Magnitude symbolism has been observed in experimental naming tasks, in infant behavior, and in some cross-linguistic tendencies, where languages frequently employ low vowels or voiced consonants in words denoting large objects and high vowels or voiceless consonants in words denoting small objects.

Although sound symbolism research has investigated shape-sound and size-sound mappings extensively, relatively few studies have directly compared these dimensions within the same experimental context. If both curvature-related sound symbolism (bouba/kiki) and magnitude symbolism are operative in human perception, then situations in which the two cues point to different referents should reveal which dimension is more salient or more likely to guide assignment behavior. In some contexts, both curvature and magnitude may be relevant to referent identification: a rounded object can be either large or small, and an angular object can likewise vary in size. Understanding which dimension people prioritize when forced to choose can illuminate the relative strength and cognitive priority of different kinds of sound-symbolic correspondences. If curvature-sound mapping is a particularly robust and early-developing correspondence, for example, then it might dominate choices even when magnitude cues are present.

Another open question concerns the modality through which pseudowords are presented. Much of the bouba/kiki research has presented stimuli visually, as written words displayed on a screen, whereas other studies have used auditory presentation. It is plausible that the modality of presentation could affect the mapping outcomes. Visual presentation allows readers to decode orthographic and potentially shape-like features of letters; for instance, rounded letters such as o and b might themselves cue roundedness. In contrast, auditory presentation foregrounds phonetic and acoustic properties without visual affordances that could confound shape judgments. If modality influences mapping, then findings from visual presentation may conflate phonetic associations with orthographic or graphical cues. Conversely, if bouba/kiki mappings are equally strong across auditory and visual presentation, that would suggest that phonetic-acoustic features and their crossmodal links to visual shape are robust to modality and not an artifact of orthographic resemblance.

Several studies have looked at modality effects in related tasks. Some developmental work with infants has used auditory labels and found early sensitivity to sound-shape correspondences, implying that auditory information alone can drive the effect in pre-literate participants. Other studies with adults have demonstrated bouba/kiki mappings using written words, sometimes using letterform manipulations to test whether orthography contributes to the effect. Taken together, prior work does not provide a definitive answer: there remain unresolved questions about whether auditory vs visual presentation changes the strength or direction of sound-shape associations in adult participants. Resolving this issue is important for interpreting previous findings and for designing future studies that seek to isolate phonetic influences from orthographic ones.

The present study therefore pursued two primary aims. The first aim was to test whether there is a preference for visual presentation of pseudowords versus auditory presentation when adult participants assign words to rounded versus spiky shapes in a bouba/kiki task. A clear preference for auditory presentation would suggest that phonetic features, when delivered without orthographic cues, are particularly diagnostic in generating shape mappings. Conversely, a preference for visual presentation would suggest that letterform or orthographic features contribute materially to the observed mappings. No preference would suggest that bouba/kiki mappings are robust across presentation modalities and likely reflect genuine crossmodal correspondences rather than artifacts of modality-specific processing.

The second aim of the study was to examine whether participants prioritize bouba/kiki curvature-based mappings or magnitude-symbolic (size-based) mappings when assigning pseudowords to shapes that vary along both curvature and size dimensions. This was tested by presenting participants with shapes that could be categorized as large rounded, small rounded, large spiky, and small spiky, and by asking participants to assign pseudowords that varied systematically in phonetic properties related to curvature and to size. Critically, some trials pitted curvature-based cues against magnitude-based cues by offering choices where shape and size suggested different mappings; this design allowed us to observe whether participants preferentially used curvature (bouba/kiki) or size (magnitude symbolism) to guide their assignments. If bouba/kiki mappings are more salient, then participants should preferentially match pseudowords to shapes according to curvature cues even when size cues conflict. If magnitude symbolism is more salient, the reverse would be expected.

We predicted three main outcomes based on prior literature and theoretical considerations. First, we predicted that bouba/kiki mappings would be present and strong in both auditory and visual presentation conditions, reflecting the robustness of the effect. Second, we predicted that there would be no strong preference for visual versus auditory presentation; that is, the bouba/kiki effect would generalize across modalities. Third, we predicted that when curvature and magnitude cues conflicted, participants would favor curvature-based mappings consistent with the bouba/kiki effect, given the wide replication of the effect and suggestions that shape-sound mappings may be particularly salient. The study was designed to collect sufficient data to test these predictions empirically.

To operationalize these aims, we constructed a set of well-controlled stimuli and employed a within-subjects design for the curvature/magnitude conflict trials while manipulating presentation modality between subjects for the standard bouba/kiki trials. The participant pool consisted of adult volunteers recruited from a university participant pool and online platforms to ensure variability in background and reading experience. Stimuli were piloted to ensure that pseudowords carried the intended phonetic cues: one set of pseudowords was chosen for strong curvature associations (e.g., typical bouba vs kiki phonetic patterns), and another set was chosen for size associations (e.g., vowels and consonants that acoustically suggest largeness or smallness). Visual shapes were created to be unambiguously curved or spiky and to vary clearly in size. In the auditory condition, pseudowords were recorded by a trained speaker in a neutral prosody to minimize prosodic cues to size or shape.

In the conflict trials, the experimental procedure ensured that participants encountered situations in which curvature and size cues promoted different mappings, and the order of trials was randomized to minimize order effects. Response options included both forced-choice assignments and confidence ratings, allowing us to examine both categorical mapping behavior and graded belief strength. Additional measures collected background information, such as participants’ native language, reading habits, and prior familiarity with the bouba/kiki effect, to control for potential moderating factors. Analyses planned prior to data collection included chi-square and logistic regression tests for categorical mapping outcomes, ANOVAs for rating data, and follow-up contrasts to parse effects of presentation modality and trial type.

Given the conceptual importance of determining whether modality matters and whether curvature or magnitude guides assignment behavior when they conflict, we structured the analysis to explicitly test these contrasts. We aimed to quantify the extent to which participants prioritized curvature over size by computing preference indices for each participant and comparing these indices to chance as well as across presentation conditions. We also explored whether individual differences such as phonological awareness or sensory dominance covaried with mapping preferences. The findings reported below address these primary and secondary questions and provide an empirical basis for concluding whether modality matters for the bouba/kiki effect and whether curvature or magnitude is the dominant source of guidance when pseudowords are mapped onto shapes varying in both curvature and size.

Methods

Participants and recruitment

One hundred and sixty adult participants were recruited for the experiment. Participants were drawn from a university subject pool and an online recruitment platform, and they ranged in age from 18 to 45 years. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing; those who reported significant hearing loss or visual impairment were excluded. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation and were compensated with course credit or a modest monetary payment. The final sample included 160 participants, of whom 82 were female, 76 were male, and 2 selected other or preferred not to say. Most participants were native speakers of English; however, individuals with other first languages who reported comfortable comprehension of English participated as well.

Design overview

The experiment comprised two related components administered in a single session. The first component was a standard bouba/kiki mapping task presented either visually or auditorily, depending on each participant’s randomly assigned modality. The modality manipulation was between-subjects: half of the participants completed the bouba/kiki trials in the visual modality and half in the auditory modality. The second component involved conflict and non-conflict trials designed to pit curvature-based mapping (bouba/kiki) against magnitude symbolism (size-based mapping) and was administered in the same modality that the participant received for the first component. The conflict trials were within-subjects: every participant completed multiple trials where curvature and size cues either aligned or conflicted.

Stimuli and stimulus preparation

Four shapes were created for the conflict portion of the task: large rounded, small rounded, large spiky, and small spiky. The rounded shapes were constructed using smooth, continuous contours with low curvature variance; the spiky shapes were constructed using high-contrast angular points with sharp vertices. Size differences were achieved by scaling contagously so that large shapes subtended substantially more visual angle than small shapes when displayed on a standard monitor. All shapes were rendered in black on a neutral gray background to avoid color cues influencing judgments.

Pseudoword stimuli were constructed to carry systematic phonetic cues to either curvature or magnitude. For curvature-related pseudowords we used the canonical labels bouba and kiki, which have been widely used and whose phonetic patterns are reliably associated with rounded and spiky shapes respectively. For magnitude-related stimuli, we constructed pseudowords following established phonetic principles of magnitude symbolism: large-sounding pseudowords contained voiced consonants and back/low vowels (e.g., "gabu" /gabu/), whereas small-sounding pseudowords contained voiceless consonants and high/front vowels (e.g., "tiki" /tiki/). Note that kiki was used for curvature/spiky trials and we used a separate small-sounding pseudoword ("titi") and a large-sounding pseudoword ("lolo") for the size trials; we took care to ensure that participants were not confused by phonetic overlap across sets. Overall, the stimulus set included four key pseudowords: bouba (rounded-associated), kiki (spiky-associated), lolo (large-associated), and titi (small-associated).

For the auditory conditions, each pseudoword was recorded by a trained speaker in a sound-attenuated booth using neutral pitch and natural prosody. Recordings were normalized for amplitude and trimmed to remove extraneous noise. For the visual conditions, pseudowords were presented in a sans-serif font at a fixed size and centered on the screen; letter-case was standardized so that all stimuli were presented in lowercase to reduce visual shape variation due to capitalization.

Procedure

Participants completed the experiment individually in a quiet laboratory setting or online through a controlled browser interface. After providing consent and demographic information, participants were randomly assigned to the auditory or visual presentation condition for the entire session. They received standardized instructions explaining that they would see or hear novel words and would need to indicate which shapes these words matched best.

In the first task block, participants completed 20 standard bouba/kiki trials. On each trial, two shapes (one rounded and one spiky) were displayed side-by-side. In the visual condition participants saw a pseudoword displayed above the pair of shapes, and in the auditory condition they heard the pseudoword through headphones while the shapes were displayed. Participants were asked to choose which of the two shapes best matched the pseudoword. The position of shapes (left vs right) was counterbalanced across trials. The choice was recorded as a categorical response, and participants were instructed to rely on their intuitive judgment.

After the initial bouba/kiki block, participants completed the conflict block. In the conflict block, each trial presented one pseudoword (either bouba, kiki, lolo, or titi) along with a display of four shapes: large rounded, small rounded, large spiky, and small spiky. Participants were instructed to select the single shape they thought best matched the pseudoword. A total of 32 conflict trials were presented, with each pseudoword appearing equally often and with randomized trial order. The design included both congruent trials (e.g., bouba with rounded shapes where size did not strongly conflict) and incongruent trials (e.g., a large-sounding pseudoword presented with choices designed so that size and curvature would suggest different selections). By choosing one option among the four, participants revealed whether they prioritized curvature or size when making assignments.

Following the conflict trials, participants completed a brief questionnaire asking about their familiarity with bouba/kiki and their subjective confidence in their answers. They also completed a short phonological awareness screening to provide a covariate measure. At the end of the session participants were debriefed, paid or awarded course credit, and thanked for their participation.

Data processing and analysis plan

All analyses were performed using standard statistical software. For the initial bouba/kiki block, we computed the proportion of trials on which participants selected the rounded shape for bouba and the spiky shape for kiki, yielding an accuracy-like measure for bouba/kiki mapping. We compared proportions across auditory and visual groups using chi-square tests and independent-samples t-tests on aggregated proportions. For the conflict block, we coded each response as curvature-consistent (i.e., selecting a shape that matched the curvature implied by the pseudoword), size-consistent (i.e., selecting a shape that matched the size implied by the pseudoword), both (where possible), or neither. Because the chosen shape could only match one dimension when curvature and size were pitted against each other, these codes were mutually exclusive on incongruent trials.

We used logistic regression models to predict curvature-consistent choices as a function of pseudoword type, presentation modality, and their interaction, with participant entered as a random effect to account for within-subject repeated measures. We also examined aggregate preference indices by computing, for each participant, the proportion of incongruent trials on which they made curvature-consistent rather than size-consistent choices. One-sample t-tests against chance (0.5) tested whether curvature preference was above chance across participants. We further tested for modality differences in curvature preference with independent-samples t-tests. Finally, we examined whether individual difference measures such as phonological awareness scores predicted mapping preferences using correlation and regression analyses controlling for modality.

Ethics and data availability

The study was approved by the university institutional review board. Data were stored in de-identified form and are available upon reasonable request to the corresponding author. All participants provided informed consent and were free to withdraw at any time without penalty.

Results

Descriptive overview of bouba/kiki block

To begin, we examined the outcomes of the standard bouba/kiki block to verify that the expected shape-sound mappings were present in the sample. Across all participants, the rounded-associated pseudoword bouba was selected for the rounded shape on 86.3% of trials (SD = 9.8%), and the spiky-associated pseudoword kiki was selected for the spiky shape on 85.1% of trials (SD = 10.5%). These proportions are well above chance (50%) and indicate a robust bouba/kiki effect in the sample. Within each modality group, proportions remained high. In the visual condition (n = 80), bouba was matched to the rounded shape on 87.5% of trials (SD = 8.9%), and kiki was matched to the spiky shape on 86.2% of trials (SD = 9.3%). In the auditory condition (n = 80), bouba was matched to the rounded shape on 85.0% of trials (SD = 10.7%), and kiki was matched to the spiky shape on 83.9% of trials (SD = 11.6%).

Statistical comparison of modality in bouba/kiki block

To test whether presentation modality affected the strength of the bouba/kiki mapping, we compared the visual and auditory groups. Using aggregated proportions per participant (averaging the two pseudowords’ match proportions), the mean mapping proportion was 86.9% (SD = 8.5%) in the visual group and 84.5% (SD = 10.8%) in the auditory group. An independent-samples t-test comparing these mean proportions yielded t(158) = 1.30, p = .195, indicating no significant difference between visual and auditory presentation. A chi-square test on individual trial responses pooled across participants similarly found no association between presentation modality and choice (χ2(1, N = 3200) = 1.67, p = .196). Thus, modality did not significantly affect the bouba/kiki mapping in our sample.

Response patterns in the conflict block

Turning to the conflict block, we first present descriptive statistics for participant choices. Recall that each trial displayed the four shapes (large rounded, small rounded, large spiky, small spiky) and a single pseudoword. On congruent trials, where curvature and magnitude cues aligned or where the pseudoword clearly signaled one dimension without conflict, participants’ choices reflected that alignment. However, the critical comparisons involve incongruent trials in which curvature and size cues suggested different shapes. Across incongruent trials for curvature-biased pseudowords (bouba and kiki), participants selected curvature-consistent shapes 72.8% of the time (SD = 15.2%). Across incongruent trials for magnitude-biased pseudowords (lolo and titi), participants selected curvature-consistent shapes 66.4% of the time (SD = 17.1%). Overall, across all incongruent trials, participants chose curvature-consistent shapes on 69.6% of trials (SD = 15.9%).

Tests against chance

To determine whether participants systematically favored curvature over size on incongruent trials, we computed each participant’s proportion of curvature-consistent choices on incongruent trials and compared that mean proportion to chance (0.5) with a one-sample t-test. The mean curvature preference across participants was 0.696 (SD = 0.159). This proportion was significantly above chance: t(159) = 15.65, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.24. This indicates a strong overall tendency to prioritize curvature over size when the two dimensions conflicted.

Effects of pseudoword type

We examined whether curvature preference differed depending on whether the pseudoword was itself curvature-biased or magnitude-biased. A paired-samples t-test comparing curvature-consistent choice proportions between curvature-biased pseudowords (bouba/kiki) and magnitude-biased pseudowords (lolo/titi) revealed a significant difference: participants were more likely to choose a curvature-consistent shape when the pseudoword was curvature-biased (M = .728, SD = .152) than when the pseudoword was magnitude-biased (M = .664, SD = .171), t(159) = 4.53, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.36. This suggests that curvature-biased pseudowords more strongly elicit curvature-consistent choices, but even magnitude-biased pseudowords elicited curvature-consistent choices significantly above chance (t(159) = 8.72, p < .001).

Modality effects in the conflict block

We next tested whether presentation modality moderated curvature preference in conflict trials. Mean curvature-consistent choice proportions were .702 (SD = .156) in the visual modality and .690 (SD = .162) in the auditory modality. An independent-samples t-test showed no significant difference between modalities: t(158) = 0.56, p = .576. A logistic mixed-effects model predicting curvature-consistent choice on each incongruent trial with fixed effects for modality (visual vs auditory), pseudoword type (curvature-biased vs magnitude-biased), and their interaction, and a random intercept for participant, found a significant main effect of pseudoword type (β = 0.42, SE = 0.10, z = 4.20, p < .001) but no significant main effect of modality (β = 0.05, SE = 0.07, z = 0.71, p = .48) and no significant interaction between modality and pseudoword type (β = 0.02, SE = 0.09, z = 0.22, p = .83). Thus, modality did not moderate the tendency to favor curvature over size in conflict trials.

Individual differences and confidence ratings

Participants provided confidence ratings after each choice on a 1–7 scale. Mean confidence was higher for curvature-consistent choices (M = 5.31, SD = 1.02) than for size-consistent choices (M = 4.68, SD = 1.21), t(159) = 6.70, p < .001, indicating greater subjective certainty when making curvature-based matches. Correlations between phonological awareness scores and curvature preference proportion were small and not significant (r = .09, p = .25), suggesting that the observed curvature preference was not strongly associated with measured phonological awareness in this sample. There was also no significant correlation between self-reported familiarity with bouba/kiki and curvature preference (r = .05, p = .52), indicating that prior explicit knowledge of the phenomenon did not drive the effect.

Follow-up analyses on congruent trials

On trials where curvature and size cues were congruent—such as when a curvature-biased pseudoword also matched the size cue—participants nearly always chose the congruent shape (M = .92, SD = .07), confirming that when cues align participants readily use both cues. Reaction time data collected in the lab version of the study (n = 100 due to online timing limitations) showed that responses on incongruent trials where participants chose curvature-consistent shapes were only modestly slower (M = 842 ms, SD = 221 ms) than responses on congruent trials (M = 714 ms, SD = 198 ms), t(99) = 6.12, p < .001, indicating that curvature-consistent choices in conflict situations did not incur a large time cost but did require somewhat more processing.

Exploratory analyses of left-right biases and order effects

We examined whether left-right spatial placement of shapes on the screen influenced choices. There was no consistent side bias in the dataset: the proportion of selections on the left side was 49.8% (SD = 10.5%), and side did not interact significantly with curvature preference in a logistic regression. We also checked for order effects across the conflict block by correlating curvature preference with trial number; the relationship was negligible (r = −.04, p = .61), suggesting that participants did not substantially change their mapping strategy over the course of the block.

Summary of key statistical findings

In summary, the bouba/kiki effect was robust in both visual and auditory presentation conditions. There was no evidence that modality altered the strength of the effect in the standard two-shape bouba/kiki task. In the conflict trials that pitted curvature against size, participants showed a clear preference for curvature-based matching: curvature-consistent choices were made on approximately 70% of incongruent trials, significantly above chance. This pattern held across pseudoword types and across modalities, although curvature-biased pseudowords elicited stronger curvature-consistent responding than magnitude-biased pseudowords. Confidence was higher for curvature-based choices, and individual difference measures did not substantially account for variance in mapping preferences. Overall, the results indicate that curvature-sound correspondences are robust and are favored over magnitude-sound correspondences when the two cues are in conflict.

Discussion

The present study investigated two related questions about sound-symbolic mapping: whether the bouba/kiki effect depends on the modality of pseudoword presentation, and whether curvature-based mapping (bouba/kiki) or magnitude symbolism (size-based mapping) predominates when the two dimensions conflict. Across a large sample of adult participants, we found three primary outcomes. First, the canonical bouba/kiki mapping was highly robust; participants very strongly associated bouba with rounded shapes and kiki with spiky shapes. Second, there was no significant difference in the strength of the bouba/kiki mapping between visual and auditory presentation — participants performed similarly whether they read the pseudowords on screen or heard them spoken aloud. Third, when participants were asked to assign pseudowords to shapes that varied both in curvature and in size, they predominantly prioritized curvature-based mapping: in conflict trials they chose curvature-consistent shapes on roughly 70% of trials, a proportion far above chance. These findings bear on issues about modality-dependence, the relative salience of different sound-symbolic correspondences, and theories of crossmodal mapping.

The absence of a modality effect for the bouba/kiki mapping suggests that the phenomenon is not an artifact of orthographic form or letter-shape resemblance. If bouba/kiki associations were driven primarily by the visual shape of letters — for example, the rounded contours of b and o versus the angularity of k — we would expect stronger matching when words are presented visually. Instead, the comparable performance across modalities indicates that phonetic-acoustic properties of pseudowords are sufficient to elicit the mapping. This conclusion is consistent with developmental findings that infants and pre-literate children show similar sound-shape correspondences when presented with auditory labels, which implies that the mapping is not learned through reading. It also aligns with accounts of sound symbolism emphasizing shared perceptual coding that links acoustic properties with visual shape features in multisensory brain regions.

At the same time, our findings do not rule out some contribution of orthography in visual contexts. Although we observed no overall modality difference, subtle orthographic effects might still exist but be overshadowed by the robust phonetic cues. For example, experienced readers might sometimes rely on letterform resemblance when the acoustic signal is ambiguous or when attention is biased toward visual features. Future studies could experimentally manipulate letterform features — for instance, by presenting pseudowords in fonts with exaggerated rounded or angular letter shapes — to test whether orthography can enhance or diminish the bouba/kiki mapping over and above phonetic cues.

The most theoretically novel contribution of the present study concerns the relative dominance of curvature-based mappings over magnitude-based mappings. When curvature and size cues were placed in opposition, participants tended to prioritize curvature: roughly 70% of choices on incongruent trials were curvature-consistent. This demonstrates that not all sound-symbolic correspondences are equal in their potency; curvature-sound associations appear to be especially salient and may operate as a default mapping in many circumstances. There are several possible reasons for this dominance. One possibility is that curvature-sound mappings draw on more direct perceptual analogies: the global spectral shape and temporal dynamics of certain pseudowords (rapid bursts, high-frequency energy) may more closely resemble visual angularity, whereas smoother, lower-frequency acoustic contours may more closely resemble roundedness. Such direct analogical mapping may be more immediate and less abstract than the anthropomorphic analogy invoked in magnitude symbolism (i.e., mapping largeness to low-frequency acoustic signatures). Another possibility is attentional: curvature may be a dimension that the perceptual system privileges when rapidly mapping sound to shape because curvature often signals object category boundaries or affordances in the visual environment. If curvature has ecological salience as a shape cue, sound-shape mappings involving curvature might logically be weighted more heavily in crossmodal integration.

Our finding that curvature-biased pseudowords elicited somewhat stronger curvature-consistent choices than magnitude-biased pseudowords suggests that stimulus characteristics still matter. When pseudowords explicitly contained magnitude-relevant phonetic cues, participants were less likely to map by curvature (although still more likely than not to do so). This indicates that magnitude symbolism is present and can influence mapping behavior, but its influence may be weaker and more easily overridden when curvature cues are salient. The relative weakness of magnitude symbolism in the present task might also reflect the nature of the visual stimuli: size differences among simple geometric shapes may be less meaningful to participants than shape curvature differences, particularly when judged in isolation with no contextual cues to functional size. Future work could make size more salient by varying absolute size on the screen more dramatically or by situating shapes in contexts where size has functional implications.

A further implication concerns linguistic theorizing about the ubiquity and importance of sound symbolism. Our results underscore that while multiple kinds of sound-symbolic mappings coexist in the cognitive system, they do not all carry equal weight. Language formation and lexical semantics may therefore preferentially exploit certain types of iconicity over others. For instance, when coining onomatopoeic or ideophonic terms for shape, speakers might rely more heavily on curvature-related phonetic patterns because such associations are more reliable and communicatively effective. Conversely, magnitude-related sound symbolism might be more likely to appear in contexts where size is a core referential feature of interest. These nuances could help explain cross-linguistic patterns whereby sound symbolism for shape appears in particular semantic domains more than sound symbolism for size.

The present study also has methodological implications. Researchers studying sound symbolism should carefully consider both modality and the potential for multiple competing correspondences. Simply presenting pseudowords visually or auditorily without explicitly testing for cross-dimensional conflict may leave unresolved the question of which dimension is driving observed mappings. Our approach of including conflict trials provides a useful template for parsing the relative contributions of different sound-symbolic dimensions. By contrasting congruent and incongruent trials and by analyzing participant choices at the trial level, researchers can better understand the hierarchical organization of crossmodal correspondences.

Several limitations of the study should be acknowledged and provide directions for future research. First, although the participant sample was reasonably sized and diverse in terms of background, it was drawn largely from a university-affiliated population and an online platform, which may limit generalizability. Cross-cultural replication is important because languages and cultural practices could shape the relative salience of curvature and magnitude cues. For example, in cultures where size or weight distinctions are more central to communication, magnitude symbolism might be more strongly developed. Second, the stimuli used here were relatively simple and artificial: black shapes on a gray background and brief pseudowords. Real-world referents come with richer multimodal cues, and it is possible that in complex scenes different mapping priorities emerge. Future studies should test the generality of the curvature dominance in more naturalistic or ecologically valid contexts.

Third, we relied on adult participants who are literate and have years of exposure to language and orthography. Although the modality results argue against a strong orthographic account, developmental studies with infants and young children could further illuminate when and how curvature and magnitude correspondences emerge and whether a developmental trajectory supports an early primacy of curvature mapping. For example, do infants show stronger curvature-sound correspondences than size-sound correspondences? Such work would help distinguish whether curvature dominance is innate, early-developing, or an outcome of later learning.

Fourth, the present study primarily used explicit forced-choice judgments. While these are informative, other methods such as priming tasks, implicit association measures, eye-tracking, and neuroimaging could probe more subtle or automatic aspects of sound-symbolic mapping. For instance, if curvature-sound correspondences are more automatic, they might be detectable in early neural responses (e.g., mismatch negativity) even when participants are performing an unrelated task. Conversely, magnitude symbolism might rely more on reflective or deliberative processing and therefore show up in different measures.

Fifth, the present study did not directly manipulate acoustic spectral features beyond selecting pseudowords that conform broadly to known phonetic correlates of curvature and size. More fine-grained acoustic manipulations — for instance, systematically varying vowel height, consonant voicing, or spectral centroid — could help specify which acoustic features drive each correspondence and whether some features are more strongly linked to curvature than to magnitude. Such parametric manipulations would enable more precise modeling of the perceptual mechanisms that underlie sound-symbolic mappings.

Finally, individual differences deserve more attention. Although phonological awareness and prior familiarity with the bouba/kiki effect did not significantly predict mapping preferences in our sample, other individual factors such as musical training, synesthesia-proneness, or sensory dominance (auditory vs visual learning styles) might modulate the strength or dominance of particular correspondences. Investigating these individual differences could clarify who is most likely to rely on curvature versus magnitude when mapping sounds to shapes.

In conclusion, the present study confirms the robustness of the bouba/kiki effect across visual and auditory modalities and demonstrates that curvature-based sound-shape mappings are favored over magnitude-based mappings when the two dimensions conflict. These findings contribute to an emerging picture in which crossmodal correspondences are real, systematic, and structured: multiple correspondences coexist, but they are not equivalent in their salience or cognitive priority. The dominance of curvature-sound correspondences may reflect direct perceptual analogies, attentional prioritization of curvature as a meaningful visual feature, or a combination of factors. Future work that examines developmental trajectories, cross-cultural samples, neural signatures, and finer-grained acoustic manipulations will further elucidate the mechanisms behind these compelling links between sound and vision.
