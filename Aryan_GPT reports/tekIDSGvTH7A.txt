Introduction

Behavioral priming has been a central topic in social cognition for several decades. The basic claim of behavioral priming is that incidental exposure to a concept can influence downstream behavior in ways that align with that concept. Such influences are often described as automatic because they are said to occur without conscious intent. Early demonstrations of behavioral priming reported striking effects on simple, observable behaviors. One seminal study reported that participants who completed tasks containing words related to old age walked more slowly afterward than those who completed neutral tasks. That finding became widely discussed because it suggested that abstract activation of a stereotype could translate directly into bodily movement. The original result was interpreted as evidence for the pervasiveness and potency of automatic processes in social behavior. Over time, the simple narrative that primes reliably produce behavioral change has become more nuanced. A growing body of research has failed to replicate some of the classic behavioral priming effects. These replication difficulties have led to debates about boundary conditions, effect sizes, and methodological specifics that might determine whether priming effects appear. One central question that has emerged is whether individual differences moderate the influence of primes on behavior. If primes do not exert uniform effects across people, then some failures to replicate could be explained by heterogeneity in participant characteristics. Individual difference factors that are plausible moderators include motivation, personality traits, implicit motives, and social value orientations. Social value orientation, or SVO, describes the degree to which people place weight on others' outcomes relative to their own. SVO is typically operationalized along a spectrum ranging from competitive or individualistic orientations to prosocial or cooperative orientations. Individuals who are more prosocial by SVO might process social information differently. The literature on SVO shows that it predicts a variety of social choices and interaction patterns. It is therefore plausible that SVO could moderate responses to social or social stereotype primes. Despite this plausibility, relatively little research has explicitly examined whether SVO consistency influences basic priming effects on overt behavior. Theoretically, SVO could impact priming effects in at least two ways. One possibility is that SVO acts as a gating variable, such that primes only influence behavior when they do not conflict with relatively stable social goals indexed by SVO. Under this account, a prime that activates an elderly stereotype might slow the walking speed of participants whose SVO is aligned with social sensitivity, but not affect participants with a self-focused SVO. A different possibility is that SVO influences the processing of prime materials at encoding, thereby altering the strength of the prime activation. For example, prosocial individuals might attend more to social words in a sentence-unscrambling task, strengthening the prime. Conversely, competitive individuals might disregard social content, weakening the prime. Despite the theoretical plausibility of such moderation, extant empirical work remains sparse. Past replication attempts of the classic elderly priming effect have varied greatly in design. Some replications used identical materials and procedures, while others implemented modified tasks that were nonetheless intended to capture the same conceptual manipulation. Differences in the temporal spacing of the prime and behavioral measure, the social context of testing, and participant expectations have all been suggested as sources of variability. More broadly, the literature on social priming has highlighted that subtle differences in experimental context and participant population can have outsized effects on outcomes. Given this variability, testing for individual moderators such as SVO is a necessary step toward mapping the boundary conditions of priming. The present study was designed as a direct and conservative replication of the classic elderly priming demonstration with a clear test of moderation by SVO. The replication aspect of the study followed the basic format of the original demonstration. Participants completed a sentence-unscrambling task that contained elderly-related words for the priming condition or neutral words for the control condition. Immediately following the task, participants were asked to walk down a short corridor while their walking time was measured surreptitiously. The walking time after the prime served as the behavioral dependent variable. The added component in the present work was the measurement and categorization of participants’ SVO. Rather than treating SVO as a continuous covariate only, the study classified participants as high or low in SVO consistency to test for interactions with the prime manipulation. The choice to categorize SVO was motivated by practical considerations and by prior research that often uses categorical splits to identify groups that differ in social orientations. Theories that posit interactions between stable preferences and situational cues predict that categorical differences might be especially informative. If the behavioral priming effect depends on alignment between the prime content and a participant’s enduring social goals, then we would expect a prime-by-SVO interaction. Specifically, if elderly primes activate prosocial or socially attuned scripts, then high-SVO-consistency individuals might be more susceptible to the prime and consequently show greater slowing. Conversely, low-SVO individuals might be less influenced or even show non-significant changes. Alternatively, if SVO does not interact with prime activation, then no moderation should be observed and main effects would be the only predictors of behavior. The literature leaves open which of these patterns is more likely. There are empirical reasons to be cautious in expecting large interactions. First, individual differences often account for only modest amounts of variance in behavior in experimental contexts, especially when the experimental manipulation itself has limited effect size. Second, practical measurement issues with SVO classification can attenuate interactions, such as noisy self-report measures or arbitrary median splits. Despite these concerns, the current study proceeded with clear and pre-specified analyses to test both the main effect of the prime and its interaction with SVO consistency. The study also included several methodological safeguards designed to maximize the likelihood of detecting an effect if one existed. Procedures were standardized across participants. The experimenter’s behavior during the critical walking measure was scripted to minimize unintended social cues. The walking corridor was unobstructed and free from visual or auditory distractions. The stimulus materials for the sentence-unscrambling task were checked for word frequency and length to ensure that elderly-related words were not systematically different from neutral words in these basic lexical properties. SVO was measured using a standard choice-based questionnaire that asks participants to indicate allocations of resources between self and other. Responses were scored and then used to create a high-consistency and low-consistency grouping. Prior to data collection, a power analysis was conducted to determine an appropriate sample size. The power analysis was based on conservative estimates of the original effect and on modest estimates of interaction effect sizes. The resulting sample size balanced practical constraints with the aim of achieving adequate power for detecting medium-sized interactions. The current article reports the full procedure, the complete analysis plan, and all outcomes. The central hypotheses were simple. Hypothesis 1 predicted that participants in the elderly prime condition would have longer walking times than those in the neutral condition. Hypothesis 2 predicted that SVO consistency would moderate this effect, such that individuals with high SVO consistency would show a stronger prime effect than those with low SVO consistency. The study also included exploratory analyses to examine whether the effects varied by participant gender, self-reported familiarity with elderly individuals, and baseline walking tendencies measured at the start of the session. The inclusion of exploratory analyses was intended to provide additional context for interpreting the main outcomes. In sum, this study tested whether an elderly stereotype prime would produce a measurable change in walking speed and whether that change would be moderated by participants’ social value orientation. The study aimed to contribute to the ongoing discussion about replicability and boundary conditions in behavioral priming by explicitly testing a plausible individual difference moderator. The following sections describe the methods and results in full detail and then place the outcomes in a broader theoretical context.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and from community outreach lists. A total of 120 individuals participated in the study. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 45 years. There were both male and female participants and a small number of individuals who identified as non-binary. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and were fluent in the language used in the tasks. Participants received course credit or a small monetary stipend for participation. The study was approved by the institutional human subjects review board and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation. The design was a 2 × 2 between-subjects factorial design. The first factor was the prime condition with two levels: elderly prime and neutral prime. The second factor was SVO consistency with two levels: high consistency and low consistency. Participants were randomly assigned to the prime condition. SVO consistency was measured and then used to categorize participants for analysis. The primary dependent variable was the time in seconds taken to walk from the experimental room door to a predefined point down the corridor after completing the sentence-unscrambling task. The walking path was 6 meters long and unobstructed. The endpoint was marked by a subtle visual cue visible only to the experimenter. The walking time was measured using a concealed stopwatch in the experimenter’s pocket to mirror procedures used in classic demonstrations. The sentence-unscrambling task consisted of 30 items. In each item, participants were presented with four or five words and instructed to form a coherent four-word sentence by choosing and ordering four of the words. In the elderly prime condition, 12 of the items contained words associated with the elderly stereotype (for example: "Florida", "wrinkle", "retire", "bingo") embedded in otherwise neutral sentences. In the neutral condition, the task was identical in format but contained neutral words matched for length and frequency. The sentence items were presented on a computer and participants typed their chosen sentences. Participants were told that the task assessed verbal fluency and that their sentences would be used to evaluate sentence construction. The SVO measure was a standard giving-choice questionnaire. Participants completed nine allocation decisions in which they selected a point allocation between self and an anonymous other. The choices were presented on the same computer in a separate block following the sentence task. Responses generated scores on a continuous SVO angle metric. To create categorical SVO consistency groups, participants were classified into high-consistency and low-consistency groups based on a tertile split around the most prosocial responses. Participants in the top tertile in prosocial allocations were classified as high-consistency and those in the bottom tertile were classified as low-consistency. Participants in the middle tertile were retained for descriptive analyses but excluded from the primary 2 × 2 ANOVA to increase contrast between groups. This approach yielded a final analytic sample of 96 participants. The full dataset including those in the middle tertile is reported in supplementary analyses described below. The procedure began with participants arriving at the laboratory and completing informed consent. They then completed a brief demographic questionnaire that included age, gender, and questions about familiarity with older adults. After demographics, participants completed the sentence-unscrambling task. Following the unscrambling task, the experimenter gave participants a short cover instruction asking them to go to an adjacent room to complete the next part of the study because the experimenter needed to retrieve materials. The experimenter stood up, opened the laboratory door, and casually said "Please walk down that hall to the next room" and then timed the participant’s walk with the concealed stopwatch. No further instructions were given, and the experimenter maintained a neutral affect and gaze to avoid providing additional social cues. Once the participant reached the endpoint, the experimenter thanked the participant and asked them to return to the computer to complete the SVO measure. After completing the SVO block, participants completed several filler questionnaires unrelated to the main hypotheses. At the end of the session, all participants were debriefed about the purpose of the study and were asked whether they had suspected any relation between the tasks and the walking measure. A small number of participants reported suspicion; these cases were retained for primary analyses but examined in sensitivity analyses. Data exclusion criteria were pre-registered before data collection commenced. Participants were excluded if they failed to follow basic task instructions, if they experienced technical difficulties during the sentence task, or if they deliberately paused in the corridor for reasons unrelated to walking speed (for example, stopping to tie a shoe). Two participants were excluded for task noncompliance and two more for technical issues, resulting in the 96-participant analytic sample mentioned above. The statistical analysis plan specified an omnibus 2 × 2 ANOVA with prime condition and SVO consistency as between-subjects factors, and walking time as the dependent variable. Planned comparisons included simple effects tests of the prime within each SVO grouping. All tests were two-tailed. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared for ANOVAs and Cohen’s d for t-tests. Assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were to be checked via Shapiro-Wilk tests and Levene’s test, respectively. If assumptions were violated, appropriate nonparametric alternatives would be used. In addition to frequentist analyses, the plan included Bayesian t-tests to quantify evidence for the null versus the alternative hypotheses for key comparisons. Exploratory analyses were planned to examine gender and age as possible moderators and to analyze the full sample using SVO as a continuous predictor in regression models. Data collection continued until the pre-specified sample size target was reached. This target was based on the power analysis which aimed for 80% power to detect a medium-sized prime-by-SVO interaction at alpha = .05. All materials, data processing scripts, and anonymized data files were stored in a public repository upon completion of the study. The experimenters followed a script for all interactions with participants, and experimenter behavior was kept constant across conditions. The walking corridor temperature, lighting, and noise levels were monitored and did not vary across sessions. The experimenter’s clothing was neutral and constant to reduce visual cues. Participants were tested individually in a quiet testing room. The timeline of tasks for each participant was identical. The primary dependent measure was the walking time in seconds, and raw times were used for all analyses except when log-transformation was required due to skew. The debriefing included an explanation of the purpose of the priming manipulation and reassurances that the tasks were harmless. Participants were given the opportunity to ask questions and withdraw their data if they wished. No participants withdrew their data. The pre-registration document also described sensitivity analyses, which we report in the results below.

Results

The analytic sample included 96 participants after exclusions and tertile grouping. There were 48 participants in the elderly prime condition and 48 in the neutral prime condition. Within each prime condition, there were 24 participants categorized as high-SVO-consistency and 24 as low-SVO-consistency, yielding four cell sizes of equal numbers. The sample’s mean age was 22.7 years with a standard deviation of 4.1 years. The gender distribution was 56% female, 42% male, and 2% non-binary. The mean baseline familiarity with older adults, rated on a five-point scale, was 3.1 with a standard deviation of 1.0. Descriptive statistics for walking times are presented here for clarity. Mean walking time in the elderly prime condition across SVO groups was 4.82 seconds with a standard deviation of 0.94. Mean walking time in the neutral prime condition across SVO groups was 4.76 seconds with a standard deviation of 0.90. Within the high-SVO group, mean walking time after elderly prime was 4.88 seconds (SD = 0.96). Within the high-SVO group after neutral prime, mean walking time was 4.79 seconds (SD = 0.91). Within the low-SVO group after elderly prime, mean walking time was 4.76 seconds (SD = 0.92). Within the low-SVO group after neutral prime, mean walking time was 4.73 seconds (SD = 0.89). These descriptive patterns do not suggest notable differences between prime or SVO conditions. Examination of the distributions indicated approximately normal distributions for walking time within cells. Shapiro-Wilk tests were non-significant for each cell, indicating no strong departures from normality. Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances across the four cells was non-significant, supporting the ANOVA assumption of equal variances. The 2 × 2 ANOVA of walking time with prime condition and SVO consistency as between-subjects factors revealed no significant main effect of prime condition. The F statistic for the main effect of prime was F(1,92) = 0.16, p = .69, partial eta squared = 0.002. The main effect of SVO consistency was also non-significant with F(1,92) = 0.09, p = .76, partial eta squared = 0.001. Crucially, the two-way interaction between prime condition and SVO consistency was non-significant as well, F(1,92) = 0.03, p = .86, partial eta squared < 0.001. Simple effects analyses corroborated these findings. Within the high-SVO group, a t-test comparing elderly prime and neutral prime walking times yielded t(46) = 0.32, p = .75, Cohen’s d = 0.09. Within the low-SVO group, a t-test yielded t(46) = 0.14, p = .89, Cohen’s d = 0.04. These effect sizes are very small and uninformative about meaningful differences. The Bayesian t-tests provided additional evidence consistent with the null. For the comparison of elderly prime versus neutral prime across the full sample, the Bayes factor BF01 = 6.2 in favor of the null hypothesis, indicating the data were about six times more likely under the null than under a model positing a difference. For the key interaction, a Bayesian ANOVA favored the null model over the model with the interaction by a factor of approximately 5.5. Exploratory regression analyses treating SVO as a continuous predictor yielded similar null results. In a linear regression predicting walking time from prime condition (dummy coded), continuous SVO, and their interaction, the interaction term had a standardized coefficient near zero, b = 0.02, t(92) = 0.11, p = .91. Age and gender were included as covariates in a follow-up model to examine potential confounds. Neither age nor gender significantly predicted walking time, and their inclusion did not change the conclusions about the prime or interaction. Sensitivity analyses examined the full sample including the middle tertile participants and treated SVO continuously. These analyses again produced non-significant main effects and interactions. The raw means for the full sample were nearly identical to the tertile-based analytic sample. Checks for outliers indicated that no individual walking times exceeded three standard deviations from the mean. When log-transforming walking times to address potential skew, the ANOVA results remained non-significant. The number of participants who reported suspicion during debriefing was small (n = 6). Excluding these participants had negligible effects on the results. The effect sizes in those sensitivity analyses remained trivial. The power analysis conducted prior to data collection had indicated sufficient power to detect a medium-sized interaction. Given the observed effect sizes, post-hoc observed power for the interaction was low because the effect sizes were near zero. However, post-hoc power calculations are limited in interpretive value for null effects. Additional exploratory analyses tested whether familiarity with older adults moderated any priming effects, and no significant moderation was observed. Likewise, gender did not interact with prime condition in any meaningful way. Correlational analyses between SVO angle scores and walking times showed no meaningful relationship, r = .02, p = .84. Finally, we examined trial-level data from the sentence-unscrambling task to ensure that elderly-related words were processed at rates similar to neutral words. Response times and error rates on the sentence task did not differ significantly between the elderly-related items and matched neutral items, suggesting that the materials were approximately equivalent in task difficulty. Taken together, these results indicate a consistent pattern of null findings across multiple analytic approaches and robustness checks. No evidence was found in support of the hypothesis that elderly stereotype primes slow walking speed in this sample. Nor was evidence found that SVO consistency moderates any prime-related behavioral change. The Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence in favor of the null over the alternative models for the primary comparisons. The remaining sections consider implications and caveats of these outcomes.

Discussion

The present study aimed to replicate a classic behavioral priming effect and to test whether individual differences in social value orientation (SVO) moderate the prime-behavior link. The main outcome was clear: no reliable priming effect on walking time was observed. In addition, SVO did not moderate the effect. The null findings were consistent across several analytic approaches, including standard ANOVA, regression with continuous SVO scores, and Bayesian analyses that quantified evidence in favor of the null. The Bayesian results were particularly informative because they suggested that the data were more likely under the null than under substantive alternative models. The lack of a prime effect aligns with several recent reports of difficulties in reproducing classic social priming results. The present findings therefore contribute to a growing literature that calls for careful attention to boundary conditions and methodological specifications in behavioral priming research. At the same time, the lack of support for an SVO moderation also speaks to theoretical models that propose individual orientations as consistent moderators of situational influence. The null interaction suggests that SVO, as measured and categorized in the present study, did not function as a gating variable for the elderly prime. This result temper expectations that stable social preferences will necessarily amplify or attenuate subtle situational activations in straightforward ways. Several possible explanations for the null findings deserve consideration. One category of explanation concerns the potency and content of the prime itself. Although the sentence-unscrambling materials were constructed to include elderly-related words, it is possible that the prime did not sufficiently activate the elderly stereotype in the current sample and setting. Word-based priming often relies on repeated and salient exposure, and the strength of the activation may be sensitive to small variations in item selection or experimental context. Moreover, contemporary participants may differ from earlier participant pools in terms of baseline exposure to elderly-related concepts, which could alter susceptibility to such primes. Another possibility is that the behavioral measure—walking time along a short, well-lit corridor—was not sensitive enough to capture subtle motor changes that primes might produce. Although the procedure mirrored that of early demonstrations, minor differences in the physical layout, experimenter cues, or length of the walking path could reduce the variability or the detectability of small effects. The present study attempted to standardize these elements, but some unmeasured procedural nuances may still matter. A third cluster of explanations involves measurement and categorization of SVO. The tertile split used to create high- and low-consistency groups provided maximal contrast but may also have introduced artificial dichotomization that obscures subtler interactive patterns. While analyses using continuous SVO scores similarly failed to find effects, measurement error in the SVO task could still attenuate interaction effects. The SVO questionnaire relies on hypothetical allocations and may not fully capture the real-time social motivations that would interact with stereotype activation to influence behavior. It is also possible that other individual differences, such as chronic concerns about appearing stereotypically old, explicit attitudes toward aging, or chronic mood states, are stronger moderators than SVO. The absence of an interaction therefore cannot be taken as definitive evidence that individual differences do not matter; rather, it suggests that SVO in this operationalization was not a key moderator in this particular experimental context. Beyond these proximal explanations, broader theoretical implications arise. If primes do not reliably induce behavioral changes across typical laboratory contexts, then models that posit broadly automatic translation from activated concepts to overt behavior require refinement. One refinement is to emphasize that such translations likely occur under constrained conditions—when activation is strong, when the behavior is highly scripted and directly linked to the concept, and when the social context permits the behavioral expression. Another refinement is to integrate individual differences more tightly into theoretical models, but in a way that specifies mechanisms. For example, rather than treating SVO as a global moderator, future models might specify that prosocial orientations facilitate encoding of social semantic content, which in turn strengthens prime activation only when the prime is conceptually congruent with a behavioral affordance present in the environment. The present null findings also underscore the importance of methodological transparency and pre-registration. The study followed a pre-registered plan, used standardized procedures, and reported all planned and exploratory analyses. Such practices reduce the risk of selective reporting and enhance interpretability of null outcomes. The use of Bayesian statistics also provided a principled way to interpret evidence in favor of the null. Future work should continue to employ these practices while also exploring variations in prime strength, timing, and behavioral measures. Several concrete directions for future research emerge from the present work. First, researchers could vary prime intensity in a parametric fashion to test whether there is a dose-response relationship between activation strength and behavioral change. Such manipulations could include increasing the proportion of prime-related items, using multimodal primes (visual and linguistic), or employing immersive tasks that induce a richer contextual activation. Second, researchers could broaden the range of individual difference measures to include constructs more directly tied to the targeted stereotype, such as attitudes toward aging, intergenerational contact, and self-perceptions of aging. Combining these measures with physiological indicators of activation could clarify whether primes are being encoded even when behavioral effects are absent. Third, researchers could use more sensitive behavioral measures or aggregate across multiple behaviors to increase reliability. For instance, motion-tracking devices could capture subtle changes in gait beyond gross walking time, and pooling across several behavioral tasks might reveal cumulative effects that are not apparent in a single measure. Fourth, multi-lab collaborative projects that standardize materials and procedures across sites could provide the sample sizes and diversity needed to detect small effects and to test moderators rigorously. Such efforts would help to delineate whether null results like those obtained here reflect genuine absence of effect or insufficient sensitivity. Methodological and conceptual improvements should also clarify the temporal dynamics of priming. For example, short delays between prime and behavior have sometimes been found necessary for effects to manifest, while longer delays lead to decay. Systematic manipulation of delay intervals could help to identify the temporal window in which primes exert influence. The present study used an immediate walking measure; varying delay length may illuminate whether timing matters for interactions with stable SVO traits. Another important consideration concerns ecological validity. Laboratory primes and brief behavioral measures may not capture the complexity of real-world social influence. Field experiments that embed primes in naturalistic settings might yield more robust and meaningful effects, albeit with increased challenges in control and measurement. Ethical and practical concerns must be balanced when moving into field contexts. The null findings also encourage theorists to revisit assumptions about automaticity. Models that posit direct, unconscious translation from concept activation to behavior may overstate the ease with which abstract constructs become embodied actions. A more nuanced view is that automatic processes interact dynamically with goals, affordances, norms, and situational constraints to produce behavior. This perspective is compatible with the present results and with broader replication challenges in the field. Theoretically, the question shifts from whether priming can produce behavior change to when and for whom it does so in meaningful ways. The present study contributes to that conversation by explicitly testing an individual difference moderator and finding no support for the hypothesized interaction. Limitations of the study should be acknowledged. The sample consisted largely of university-age participants, which may limit generalizability. The SVO measure, while standard, may not capture all aspects of social orientation relevant to stereotype processing. The corridor walking measure, although historically grounded in prior research, might have lacked sensitivity compared to modern motion capture techniques. Moreover, even though the study utilized pre-registration and reasonable power for medium-sized effects, it may still have been underpowered to detect very small interactions. Despite these limitations, the results are informative. They add to mounting empirical evidence that classic social priming effects are not robustly replicable without careful attention to boundary conditions and that single, simple manipulations may not have large or reliable behavioral consequences across diverse participant samples. The present work therefore recommends a tempered view of behavioral priming effects and advocates for research strategies that systematically test moderators, increase measurement sensitivity, and employ larger, more diverse samples. In conclusion, the current study did not replicate a classic elderly priming effect on walking time and did not find that SVO consistency moderated the outcome. These null results suggest that the prime-behavior link may be weaker and more context-dependent than initially proposed. Future research should pursue systematic variations in prime intensity, examine a broader array of individual differences with theoretically grounded mechanisms, and use more sensitive behavioral and physiological measures. Such efforts will help to clarify the conditions under which situational cues can and cannot shape behavior, and will advance understanding of the interplay between stable dispositions and transient activations in social life.
