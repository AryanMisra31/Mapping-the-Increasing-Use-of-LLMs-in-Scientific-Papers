Introduction

Sexism, broadly understood as prejudicial beliefs, attitudes, or behaviors directed toward individuals on the basis of their gender, remains a salient social problem with persistent implications for equality, wellbeing, and access to resources. Research into the antecedents and correlates of sexist attitudes has spanned psychological, sociological, and political perspectives, reflecting the multifaceted nature of the phenomenon. Among the array of potential predictors of sexist attitudes, socioeconomic background occupies an important position in theoretical discussions because it organizes access to resources, exposure to diverse perspectives, and the life experiences that shape ideological orientation. Socioeconomic background is often operationalized in varied ways in the literature, including measures of income, educational attainment, parental SES, occupational prestige, and subjective social status, each tapping slightly different dimensions of social stratification. The question of whether and how socioeconomic background relates to sexism is of interest both for theory—because associations would speak to mechanisms such as resource threat, socialization, and ideological maintenance—and for practice—because identifying malleable social factors can inform interventions aimed at reducing sexist attitudes. Several strands of prior work suggest plausible pathways linking SES and sexism. One influential line of research proposes that higher education and higher socioeconomic status expose individuals to diverse viewpoints and promote critical thinking, thereby reducing exposure to rigid gender stereotypes and lowering acceptance of sexist beliefs. This view is consistent with socialization frameworks that emphasize the effect of educational environments and cultural capital on cognitive styles and intergroup attitudes. Another theoretical account draws on Social Dominance Orientation and system-justifying theories, positing that individuals with lower access to resources may endorse certain traditional gender roles as a compensatory means of securing social stability, while individuals with greater resources may have less motivation to endorse hierarchical group-based ideologies. Conversely, alternative theories suggest that higher socioeconomic status may be associated with higher levels of certain forms of sexism if high-status groups use ideological beliefs to justify unequal systems from which they benefit. These alternative perspectives underscore that the relationship between SES and sexism is not clearly unidirectional in theory and may depend on context, the particular aspect of SES being measured, and the dimension of sexism under study. Empirical literature addressing SES and sexism, though not as extensive as would be ideal, offers mixed evidence that complicates simple conclusions. Some studies find that lower SES is associated with higher levels of traditional gender-role endorsement and hostile forms of sexism, while other studies find either null associations or small effects that are sensitive to how SES is measured and which demographic controls are included. For example, studies that use educational attainment as the primary SES indicator often find a stronger inverse association with sexist attitudes than do studies that rely solely on income measures, suggesting that education may afford particular cognitive and social experiences relevant to prejudice reduction. Additionally, gender itself moderates many associations: men and women may display different patterns of association between their SES and their endorsement of sexist beliefs, and the social meanings of socioeconomic position can vary by gender. Intersectional perspectives further complicate the landscape by noting that race, ethnicity, and cultural background can shape both the distribution of SES and the normative content of gender attitudes, meaning that patterns observed in predominantly White or Western samples may not generalize to other groups. Methodological features of past work also warrant careful consideration. Several studies in this domain have relied on convenience samples, often limited to college students, which constrains variability in SES and reduces generalizability. Others have used cross-sectional designs, limiting causal inference. Measurement heterogeneity in both SES and sexism further complicates synthesis because different instruments capture distinct conceptual nuances—some sexism measures emphasize hostile sexist stereotypes, others capture benevolent paternalistic attitudes, while still others assess modern or subtle forms of sexism. Despite these complexities, there is a conceptual rationale for expecting a link between socioeconomic background and sexism that merits further empirical attention. Socioeconomic conditions shape everyday social interactions, access to educational and occupational settings that might broaden perspectives, and the stressors associated with relative deprivation that could exacerbate rigid ideological stances. Furthermore, the cultural milieu that accompanies different socioeconomic strata can socialize individuals into particular gendered norms and expectations that sustain sexist beliefs. Given the theoretical plausibility and mixed empirical findings, an additional direct test focusing specifically on the relationship between a straightforward income-based indicator of socioeconomic background and a standardized measure of sexism has value. The abstract that motivated the present reconstruction indicated that a sample of one hundred participants provided basic demographic information and reported annual household income on a scale, and then completed a questionnaire measuring sexism, producing a negative correlation between SES and sexism. This simple empirical claim aligns with the body of literature suggesting an inverse association, at least in some contexts. Nevertheless, because the prior literature is heterogeneous with respect to operationalization and sample characteristics, a careful and transparent description of methods and analyses is important to understand the degree to which conclusions can be generalized. The present illustrative report therefore sets out to construct a coherent empirical narrative consistent with the abstract’s parameters and to place the findings in a broader theoretical and empirical context. Doing so involves explicating the measurement choices, describing analytic strategies, and considering alternative explanations and boundary conditions that could explain an observed negative association between income-based SES and measured sexism. Addressing potential confounds is particularly important in this area: demographic variables such as age, gender, and education are plausibly related to both income and sexism and therefore require consideration in multivariate analyses. The present simulation also attends to psychometric considerations in the measurement of sexism; high internal consistency and construct validity of a sexism questionnaire enhances the credibility of any observed associations. Finally, the report aims to integrate the hypothetical results with extant theory by exploring mechanisms, outlining limitations inherent in the cross-sectional design and single-item SES measure, and proposing directions for more definitive future research designs such as longitudinal and experimental approaches. In sum, this manuscript situates the hypothesized negative correlation between SES and sexism within existing theoretical debate, acknowledges the complexity and heterogeneity of prior empirical work, and constructs a methodologically transparent simulation that can serve as an illustrative template for how an original study might be reported and interpreted. The Results section then presents the simulated findings, including descriptive statistics, correlational analyses, and multivariate controls, and the Discussion integrates the results with theory, considers alternative interpretations, and outlines a research agenda to clarify the nature of the SES–sexism relationship.

Methods

Overview of approach and synthetic nature of the data
The empirical material reported here was constructed to align with the brief abstract parameters and to illustrate how a full report could be articulated; the dataset and statistical outputs are therefore simulated rather than drawn from a newly conducted data collection. All subsequent descriptions of participants, measures, procedures, and statistical analyses specify how the simulation was structured and the rationale for analytic choices.

Participants
The simulated sample comprised 100 adults aged 18 years and older who were described, for the purposes of the reconstruction, as recruited from a geographically diverse online participant pool. The sample size of 100 was selected to match the abstract and to provide modest statistical power for correlational analyses of medium effect sizes. In the simulated sample, participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 72 years, with a mean age of 36.8 years and a standard deviation of 12.4 years. Gender was coded for simulation purposes as 0 = female and 1 = male, with 52 simulated participants coded as female and 48 coded as male, approximating a roughly balanced sample. For descriptive completeness, the simulated sample also included a small number of participants who identified their ethnicity in a manner reflecting diversity, but to maintain focus on the SES–sexism question, ethnicity was not modeled as a primary variable in the core analytic plan.

Socioeconomic status measure
In line with the abstract, socioeconomic background was operationalized in the simulation as annual household income reported on a Likert-type scale. The scale used for simulation purposes ranged from 1 to 7, with the following labels: 1 = less than $15,000; 2 = $15,000–$29,999; 3 = $30,000–$44,999; 4 = $45,000–$59,999; 5 = $60,000–$84,999; 6 = $85,000–$119,999; and 7 = $120,000 or more. For analytic clarity, this ordinal income scale was treated as approximately continuous in correlational and regression analyses, an approach commonly taken when five or more response categories are present. In the simulated data, the mean income scale score was 4.02 with a standard deviation of 1.42, producing a distribution that spanned the full range of responses but that was centered near the midpoints of the scale.

Sexism questionnaire
To operationalize sexism, a 20-item Sexism Questionnaire was simulated for the illustrative dataset. The hypothetical questionnaire drew conceptually from common dimensions found in the literature, including items reflecting hostile sexism (e.g., endorsement of antagonistic attitudes toward women), benevolent sexism (e.g., paternalistic beliefs that women should be protected), and modern sexism (e.g., denial of continuing discrimination). Each item was scored on a 1–5 Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The total sexism score was computed as the mean of the 20 item responses, yielding a possible range of 1 to 5. For the simulated sample, the mean sexism score was 2.67 with a standard deviation of 0.76. Internal consistency in the synthetic measure was high by construction, with a simulated Cronbach’s alpha of .88, reflecting that items were generated to cohere around a common construct in the simulation.

Additional measures and covariates
To account for potential confounding influences, the simulation included demographic covariates that are commonly associated with both SES and social attitudes. These included age (in years), gender (male, female), and highest educational attainment. Education was assessed on a 1–6 ordinal scale for simulation purposes (1 = less than high school, 2 = high school diploma or equivalent, 3 = some college or trade school, 4 = bachelor’s degree, 5 = master’s degree, 6 = doctoral or professional degree). The simulated distribution of education had a mean of 4.05 and a standard deviation of 1.15, reflecting that many participants had some college or a bachelor’s degree.

Procedure
The simulated data generation followed a multistep approach to create plausible intercorrelations among variables. First, demographic variables were generated to reflect typical distributions in an online convenience sample. Second, income scale scores were simulated with moderate positive associations with education and age, reflecting empirical tendencies whereby higher education and older working ages often correlate with higher household income. Third, sexism scores were simulated to be inversely related to the income variable, with the strength of association chosen to reflect a medium-sized effect consistent with the abstract’s report of a negative correlation. Additional stochastic variance was added to the sexism scores to reflect realistic individual differences. Although the data were simulated, the statistical analyses that follow use standard analytic techniques that would be applied to empirical data collected under similar procedures.

Ethical considerations
Because the data were constructed for illustrative purposes and no real persons were involved in this reconstruction, institutional review processes were not applicable. Nevertheless, the simulation was designed with an eye toward common ethical practices in research on sensitive topics, including minimizing intrusive questioning and using deidentified data structures.

Analysis plan
The statistical analysis plan for the simulated reconstruction mirrored typical approaches used in correlational research. The primary analysis evaluated the bivariate association between the income scale (SES proxy) and the sexism questionnaire score using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, treating both variables as continuous. Prior to computing correlations, distributions were inspected for extreme skewness and kurtosis, and appropriate transformations or robust estimators would be considered in empirical data if needed; in the simulation, distributions were approximately normal such that Pearson’s r was appropriate. In addition to the bivariate correlation, multiple linear regression models were simulated to evaluate whether the association between income and sexism persisted after statistically adjusting for age, gender, and education. Interaction terms between income and gender were also explored to assess whether the strength or direction of the SES–sexism association differed by gender. For effect size interpretation, standardized regression coefficients and 95% confidence intervals were estimated in the simulation to convey the magnitude and precision of effects. Finally, sensitivity analyses were simulated by recoding income into three categories (low, middle, high) to evaluate whether effects were robust to alternative operationalizations of SES, and by repeating analyses separately within gender groups to examine within-group patterns.

Statistical software and reporting conventions
Simulated analyses were conceptualized as being conducted in common statistical software packages such as R or SPSS, and reporting conventions followed APA-style norms for effect sizes and significance testing in the simulation (e.g., reporting Pearson’s r, standardized coefficients, p-values, and confidence intervals). In the actual empirical practice, researchers would additionally report diagnostics for regression assumptions and consider nonparametric alternatives if assumptions were violated; in the simulation, assumptions were met by construction.

Quality assurance
To ensure that the simulated dataset yielded interpretable and internally consistent results, the simulation process included checks for implausible values, unrealistic covariate relationships, and runaway collinearity. These checks aimed to create a dataset that, while synthetic, presented realistic patterns that could be meaningfully interpreted within the substantive literature on sexism and socioeconomic background.

Results

Overview of approach to reporting
The Results section reports descriptive statistics for the simulated sample, bivariate correlational analyses between the income-based SES proxy and the sexism measure, and multivariate regression analyses adjusting for demographic covariates. Additional sensitivity checks and exploratory analyses are presented to examine the robustness of the simulated association and potential moderating effects. All numeric values reported here are derived from the synthetic dataset constructed for illustrative purposes.

Descriptive statistics
Descriptive statistics for the primary variables indicated that the simulated income scale had a mean of 4.02 with a standard deviation of 1.42, as noted in the Methods section. The simulated sexism score had a mean of 2.67 and a standard deviation of 0.76. The distributions of the simulated variables were inspected and found to approximate normality with moderate kurtosis and no extreme skew. The simulated age distribution had a mean of 36.8 years with a standard deviation of 12.4 years. On the simulated education scale (1–6), the mean was 4.05 and the standard deviation was 1.15. Gender was relatively balanced in the simulated sample, with 52 simulated females and 48 simulated males, reducing concerns about extreme imbalance for subgroup analyses.

Bivariate correlation between SES and sexism
The primary bivariate analysis examined the Pearson correlation between the income scale (treated as continuous) and the mean sexism score. In the simulated data, the correlation coefficient was r = -0.34. This negative correlation indicates that higher reported household income on the scale was associated with lower sexism scores in the simulated sample. The magnitude of r = -0.34 falls within the conventionally interpreted medium effect size range and suggests a meaningful inverse association. In the simulation, the associated two-tailed p-value for the correlation was p = .001, indicating that the negative relationship would be statistically significant at conventional alpha levels in empirical reporting. The 95% confidence interval for the correlation coefficient in the simulation ranged approximately from -0.51 to -0.15, reflecting some uncertainty but excluding zero and thus supporting the inference that a negative association is plausible within the simulation.

Partialling out demographic covariates
Because age, gender, and education are plausibly related to both income and sexism, simulated multiple regression analyses were conducted to evaluate whether the SES–sexism association persisted after adjustment. The first regression model regressed the sexism score on the income scale, age, gender, and education simultaneously. The standardized regression coefficient for income in this model was β = -0.29, indicating that the negative association between income and sexism remained after controlling for demographic covariates. The observed two-tailed p-value for the income coefficient was p = .004 in the simulation, suggesting statistical significance. Age showed a small negative association with sexism in the multivariate model (β = -0.12, p = .18), although this did not reach conventional levels of significance. Education also exhibited an inverse association with sexism (β = -0.21, p = .02), indicating that higher educational attainment was associated with lower sexism scores, consistent with extant empirical patterns. Gender had a coefficient indicating that simulated males displayed slightly higher sexism scores on average compared to simulated females (β = 0.24, p = .01), controlling for income, age, and education.

Variance explained and effect sizes
The full multivariate model including income, age, gender, and education accounted for approximately 22% of the variance in the simulated sexism scores (adjusted R^2 ≈ .22). The income variable alone in the bivariate context explained approximately 11.6% of the variance in sexism (r^2 ≈ .116), illustrating that although income is a meaningful predictor, a majority of variance in sexism is accounted for by other factors and idiosyncratic variability.

Interaction between income and gender
To test for possible moderation by gender, an interaction term between income and gender was included in an additional simulated regression model. The income × gender interaction term in the simulation had a small coefficient (β = -0.08) and was not statistically significant (p = .36). This result suggests that, in the simulated dataset, the negative association between income and sexism did not differ significantly between males and females. Follow-up stratified analyses indicated that the bivariate income–sexism correlation among simulated males was r = -0.31 (p = .03) and among simulated females was r = -0.37 (p = .007), both indicating negative associations of similar magnitude and overlapping confidence intervals.

Sensitivity analyses: categorical SES
To evaluate the robustness of findings to the operationalization of SES, the income scale was recoded into three categories for a sensitivity analysis: low (scale scores 1–2), middle (scale scores 3–5), and high (scale scores 6–7). Mean sexism scores by income category in the simulation were as follows: low-income group mean = 3.12 (SD = 0.71), middle-income group mean = 2.68 (SD = 0.73), and high-income group mean = 2.24 (SD = 0.66). A one-way ANOVA comparing sexism scores across the three income categories in the simulated data yielded an F(2,97) = 12.86, p < .001, indicating significant differences among groups. Post hoc pairwise comparisons (Tukey HSD) suggested that the high-income group had significantly lower sexism scores than both the middle-income group (mean difference = -0.44, p = .01) and the low-income group (mean difference = -0.88, p < .001), and the middle-income group had lower sexism than the low-income group (mean difference = -0.44, p = .03).

Checks for potential nonlinearities
An additional exploratory simulated model tested whether a quadratic term for income improved model fit, testing for nonlinear relations such as diminishing returns at very high income levels. The quadratic income term was small and non-significant in the simulation (β_quadratic = 0.03, p = .62), and adding the quadratic term did not materially improve adjusted R^2, suggesting that the inverse relation in the simulated data was approximately linear across the observed range.

Examination of measurement properties
Because psychometric properties are critical in attitude measurement, the simulated scale’s internal consistency was evaluated and reported as Cronbach’s alpha = .88, as noted earlier. Item-total correlations were in a plausible range (0.38 to 0.67) in the simulation, indicating that individual items contributed meaningfully to the composite score without excessive redundancy. No single item disproportionately influenced the total score based on simulated “alpha if item deleted” diagnostics, bolstering confidence in the composite measure for the purposes of the simulation.

Summary of analytic findings
To summarize, the simulated results indicate a robust negative association between the income-based measure of socioeconomic background and the sexism questionnaire score. The bivariate Pearson correlation was r = -0.34 (p = .001), and the association remained statistically significant in multivariate models controlling for age, gender, and education (standardized β = -0.29, p = .004). Sensitivity analyses using categorical SES codings and stratified analyses by gender produced consistent evidence of an inverse relationship. Effect sizes were in the small-to-moderate range, and the simulated analyses suggested the association was approximately linear across the income scale without significant moderation by gender in the simulated sample.

Interpretation caveats
It is important to emphasize, as in the Methods, that these numerical values are derived from a synthetic dataset formulated to conform to the summary provided in the motivating abstract, and thus the specific p-values, confidence intervals, and effect sizes are illustrative. Nevertheless, the pattern of results in the simulation mirrors a plausible empirical outcome that could arise in actual research with a comparable sample and measures: namely, that higher income as a proxy for SES is associated with lower sexism scores, even after adjusting for demographic covariates.

Additional exploratory notes
Given the simulation’s constraints, the results do not address causal directionality, nor do they fully disentangle which elements of socioeconomic background (e.g., material resources, educational opportunities, occupational cultures) are causally responsible for the observed association. These questions are addressed in more depth in the Discussion, along with suggestions for future empirical work to test mechanisms and establish causal pathways.

Discussion

Overview and linkage to broader issues
The simulated findings reported above illustrate a plausible pattern in which a straightforward income-based proxy of socioeconomic background is inversely related to measured sexism in a sample of 100 adults. This hypothetical result is consistent with portions of the existing literature that identify education and higher socioeconomic position as correlates of lower endorsement of traditional gender roles and hostile sexist attitudes. The finding that the association between income and sexism persisted after controlling for age, gender, and education in the simulated multivariate model suggests that income per se may capture facets of socioeconomic background that are uniquely relevant to prejudice reduction, although this conclusion must be tempered by methodological limitations inherent to the cross-sectional and synthetic nature of the reconstruction. In interpreting the simulated negative association, it is useful to consider theoretical mechanisms that could generate such patterns. One plausible mechanism is that higher income affords access to social environments and social networks that reinforce egalitarian norms and expose individuals to diverse gender role models. Higher-income individuals are more likely to participate in organizations and social contexts where cross-gender collaboration is valued and where progressive gender norms are normative, thereby reducing the salience of sexist ideologies. A second mechanism emphasizes education as a mediating pathway: higher income is often concomitant with higher educational attainment, and education fosters critical thinking, cosmopolitan worldview, and exposure to feminist scholarship and egalitarian discourse, all of which may reduce the endorsement of sexist beliefs. Although the multivariate model in the simulation controlled for education and the income effect remained, it is possible that education is only one proximal mediator and that other correlated experiences associated with higher income—such as international travel, diverse social contacts, and consumption of liberal media—also play mediating roles. A third mechanism draws on resource-threat frameworks: individuals in lower socioeconomic positions may experience resource insecurity and status anxieties that increase defensiveness and reliance on traditional ideologies as stabilizing narratives. In contexts where gender hierarchies are culturally embedded, endorsing traditional gender roles may serve as a compensatory strategy that restores a sense of order or identity in the face of economic precarity. Relatedly, Social Dominance Orientation and system-justifying tendencies may manifest differently across SES strata, and this heterogeneity could produce observed associations between income and sexism. The possibility that individuals of higher socioeconomic standing display lower sexism in certain contexts but higher endorsement of other forms of inequality in other domains also warrants consideration. It is conceivable, for instance, that high-SES individuals endorse egalitarian gender norms in public attitudes while supporting policies or practices that sustain economic hierarchies. This divergence suggests the need for nuanced, multidimensional measurement of ideology and attitudes in future empirical work.

Relation to prior empirical work
The simulated pattern aligns with empirical findings that have shown inverse associations between education and sexist attitudes and extends the logic to an income-based SES proxy. However, the empirical literature is not unanimous, and some studies find modest or null relationships between certain SES indicators and particular forms of sexism. The heterogeneity likely stems from differences in operational definitions, cultural contexts, sample composition, and the distinction between hostile, benevolent, and subtle forms of sexism. For example, benevolent sexism may be more prevalent among certain higher-SES individuals who endorse protectionist or paternalistic roles that coexist with egalitarian rhetoric, whereas hostile sexism might be more strongly related to lower socioeconomic insecurity. The simulated composite sexism score used for illustration combined multiple facets of sexist attitudes, so observed associations reflect an average tendency across these dimensions; future empirical work should disentangle dimension-specific relationships to ascertain whether particular forms of sexism drive the association with SES.

Policy and practical implications
If the pattern reflected in the simulation generalizes to real-world data, several practical implications follow. Interventions designed to reduce sexist attitudes could benefit from attention to socioeconomic contexts and targeting resources or programs toward lower-income communities where sexist attitudes may be more endorsed. Educational initiatives that broaden perspectives, enhance critical thinking about gender roles, and increase exposure to diverse gendered experiences may be especially fruitful. Workplace policies and organizational cultures that promote gender equality and provide cross-gender mentorship and leadership opportunities could have downstream effects on personal attitudes as well. However, interventions must be sensitive to the lived realities and cultural norms of communities: coercive or out-of-context approaches risk backlash or superficial compliance rather than genuine attitude change.

Limitations and cautions
Several limitations constrain the inferences that can be drawn from the simulation and should guide interpretation. First and foremost, the data presented here are synthetic and constructed for illustrative purposes; they are not an empirical record from a data collection effort and therefore cannot provide definitive evidence about real-world relationships. Second, even in an actual empirical study matching these procedures, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. It is unclear whether higher SES leads to lower sexism, whether lower sexism facilitates socioeconomic mobility, or whether unmeasured third variables account for the association. Longitudinal designs would be required to unpack temporal ordering. Third, the income measure used in the simulation was an ordinal self-report scale that captures household income broadly but lacks nuance regarding wealth, childhood SES, subjective social status, and occupational prestige; these facets of socioeconomic background may have distinct relationships with attitudes. Fourth, self-report measures of sexism are vulnerable to social desirability bias, particularly in higher-SES contexts where egalitarian norms are publicly endorsed; thus, observed lower sexism among higher-income respondents might in part reflect differential reporting tendencies rather than true attitudinal differences. Incorporating implicit measures, behavioral indicators, or third-party reports could provide complementary evidence. Fifth, the simulated sample size of 100 provides adequate power for detecting medium-sized effects but remains limited for detecting small effects or complex interactions, which might be important for nuanced understanding. Sixth, the sample composition in the simulation was constrained to approximate balance by gender but did not systematically model intersectional factors such as race, ethnicity, immigration status, or regional culture; real-world patterns could be substantially moderated by these factors. Seventh, as noted earlier, the simulated composite sexism score blended multiple dimensions of sexist attitudes; future studies would benefit from disaggregating these facets to identify distinct patterns for hostile versus benevolent sexism, for example. Eighth, measurement error and the potential for range restriction in income reporting could attenuate observed relationships in practice relative to the simulated context. Finally, cultural and temporal specificity matter: attitudes toward gender and the social meanings of wealth evolve over time and vary by national context, so findings from one sample or era may not generalize.

Directions for future research
Several empirical strategies could strengthen causal inference and deepen understanding of mechanisms linking socioeconomic background and sexism. Prospective longitudinal studies tracking individuals across developmental periods could elucidate whether changes in SES precede changes in gender attitudes or vice versa, and whether life events such as changes in employment, education, or family formation moderate these trajectories. Natural experiments and quasi-experimental designs—such as analyses of policy changes that alter income distributions or educational access—could provide exogenous variation in socioeconomic conditions and allow stronger causal claims regarding downstream effects on social attitudes. Experimental interventions that manipulate exposure to counter-stereotypical role models, diversity training, or economic security simulations could test hypothesized causal mechanisms in controlled settings. In addition, mixed-methods approaches that incorporate qualitative interviews could unpack the cultural narratives through which socioeconomic circumstances shape gender beliefs, offering rich contextual detail that quantitative measures might miss. Measurement refinement is also an important avenue: adopting multidimensional SES indicators (including objective and subjective measures), using validated multidimensional sexism instruments that distinguish hostile, benevolent, and modern sexism, and incorporating implicit and behavioral measures would provide a convergent assessment of the construct space.

Potential theoretical refinements
The pattern suggested by the simulation invites theoretical elaboration. For instance, integrating system justification theory with resource-threat frameworks could clarify under which conditions lower-SES individuals are more likely to endorse sexist ideologies as a compensatory strategy versus when they reject such ideologies due to intersecting experiences of marginalization. Similarly, models that emphasize social network structure and cultural capital could specify how differential exposure to cross-cutting ties and cosmopolitan norms in higher-SES milieus attenuate sexist beliefs. The role of cognitive styles influenced by education—such as openness to experience, analytic thinking, and tolerance for ambiguity—could be modeled as mediators of the SES–sexism link. Finally, intersectional frameworks should be central to future theorizing, as the lived experience of socioeconomic disadvantage interacts with gendered expectations in ways that a monolithic model cannot capture.

Concluding remarks
The simulated reconstruction presented in this manuscript yields a coherent narrative: an inverse association between an income-based proxy of socioeconomic background and a composite measure of sexism, robust to adjustment for common demographic covariates and evident across multiple operationalizations in sensitivity analyses. While illustrative rather than definitive, these findings echo strands of empirical research suggesting that socioeconomic context matters for gender attitudes. The simulation highlights the importance of careful measurement, attention to potential confounds, and the need for stronger causal designs to move from correlational patterns to mechanistic understanding. Practitioners and policymakers interested in promoting gender equality would be well served by interventions that address socioeconomic disparities and that consider how resources, education, and cultural exposure jointly shape attitudes. Future empirical work that implements longitudinal, experimental, and intersectional designs will be crucial for clarifying the nature of the relationship between socioeconomic background and sexism and for informing effective, contextually sensitive strategies to reduce sexist attitudes across diverse populations.
