Introduction

Human behavior in the face of difficulty varies widely across individuals and contexts. Some people respond to setbacks by redoubling their efforts and persisting until a goal is achieved, while others withdraw, reduce effort, or abandon goals altogether. Understanding the psychological factors that predict whether someone will persevere or give up has been a longstanding question in psychology. Self-efficacy, defined as an individual’s belief in their capacity to execute behaviors necessary to produce specific performance attainments, has been proposed as a central construct in explaining differences in persistence. Self-efficacy is thought to influence the choices individuals make, the amount of effort they invest, and the resilience they show when confronted with obstacles. Given this theoretical prominence, self-efficacy has been examined across numerous domains, including education, health behavior, and workplace performance. In educational contexts, self-efficacy has been linked to a range of outcomes from motivation and study strategies to persistence and academic achievement. Numerous theoretical frameworks posit that students who believe they can succeed will engage more effectively with learning tasks and will persist in the face of difficulty, thereby supporting better academic outcomes. In the health and lifestyle domain, self-efficacy has similarly been linked to behavior change, with higher self-efficacy predicting greater adherence to exercise regimens, healthier eating patterns, and better sleep hygiene. This cross-domain relevance of self-efficacy makes it an attractive target for research seeking to explain variation in both performance and everyday health behaviors. Despite an extensive empirical literature supporting links between self-efficacy and outcome variables, there is also evidence that the strength and consistency of these associations can vary. Differences in measurement, sample characteristics, and the presence of third-variable influences such as socioeconomic status, stress, and access to resources can moderate observed relationships. Thus, while the theoretical literature suggests a straightforward pathway from self-efficacy to persistence and success, empirical findings sometimes present a more complex picture. For instance, certain studies have shown strong positive correlations between self-efficacy and academic attainment, while others have found only modest associations or associations that disappear after controlling for confounding variables. Similarly, self-efficacy’s relationship with lifestyle choices is robust in some studies but less clear in others when objective behavior measures are used. Variability in findings invites closer examination of how self-efficacy operates across different domains within the same sample, and whether its effects are direct or mediated by other variables such as motivation, habits, or situational constraints. Another important consideration is the method of data collection, because many studies rely on opportunistic or convenience samples that may not be representative of broader populations. When participants are drawn primarily from university settings, for instance, ceiling effects on academic measures or restricted variability in critical covariates can attenuate observed associations. Moreover, cross-sectional designs limit causal inference and can make it difficult to distinguish whether self-efficacy precedes positive behaviors or emerges as a consequence of prior successes. Despite these methodological caveats, the overall body of work has led to a commonly held prediction: individuals with higher self-efficacy should, on average, show better academic performance and healthier lifestyle choices than individuals with lower self-efficacy. The logic of this prediction rests on the idea that beliefs about capability shape goal setting, effort allocation, and coping strategies when facing challenge. Yet, that prediction remains to be tested in diverse samples and with measures that tap both performance outcomes and lifestyle choices simultaneously. The present study was designed to examine the relationships among self-efficacy, academic performance, and lifestyle choices within a single opportunistic sample. By measuring these constructs concurrently, the study sought to clarify whether patterns observed in prior domain-specific research would replicate in a pooled dataset and whether self-efficacy would show similar relationships with both performance and lifestyle variables. Our approach also considered potential confounds by measuring demographic variables that could influence observed associations. The principal aim was to test the hypothesis that higher self-efficacy scores would be positively associated with higher academic performance and with making more positive lifestyle choices. Secondary aims included assessing whether any observed associations between self-efficacy and academic performance remained after statistically controlling for age, gender, and socioeconomic status. A further exploratory aim was to examine whether lifestyle choices were associated with academic performance in our sample and whether lifestyle might serve as a potential mediator linking self-efficacy to academic outcomes. The rationale for including lifestyle measures alongside academic performance stems from theoretical accounts that posit self-efficacy influences broader patterns of behavior, not only task-specific performance. Health behaviors such as regular exercise, good nutrition, and consistent sleep may contribute to cognitive functioning, emotional regulation, and energy levels that in turn plausibly affect academic outcomes. If self-efficacy is linked to healthier lifestyle patterns, then indirect pathways to academic performance are possible even in the absence of a direct association. The current literature includes studies that separately document links between self-efficacy and academic outcomes, and between self-efficacy and health behaviors, but fewer that examine all three constructs concurrently. By adopting this broader lens within a single sample, the present study can help to clarify whether patterns observed in prior work generalize when multiple relevant outcomes are assessed. We recruited an opportunistic sample of participants to maximize feasibility while ensuring a range of ages and backgrounds sufficient to detect associations of at least moderate size. We pre-registered no formal hypothesis tests but proceeded with planned analyses to examine bivariate correlations and partial correlations controlling for demographic covariates. The study employed well-established self-report measures of general self-efficacy and lifestyle behaviors, and used self-reported academic grade point average as a pragmatic index of academic performance. We acknowledge from the outset that self-report measures have limits and that a cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, but we reasoned that a correlational approach could nonetheless provide useful evidence bearing on the predicted pattern. Given the preponderance of prior evidence linking self-efficacy to academic outcomes, we predicted a significant positive correlation between self-efficacy and academic performance in our sample. We also predicted a significant positive correlation between self-efficacy and positive lifestyle choices, consistent with a broad literature linking efficacy beliefs to adaptive health behaviors. We further anticipated that the self-efficacy–lifestyle relationship might be stronger than the self-efficacy–academic relationship because lifestyle measures tap habitual behaviors that are directly under volitional control and may be more immediately related to generalized beliefs about capability. Finally, we planned exploratory analyses to determine whether lifestyle choices related to academic outcomes and whether they could account statistically for any association between self-efficacy and academic performance. Through these analyses we aimed to contribute to understanding why some individuals persevere and others do not, by clarifying how efficacy beliefs relate to both immediate lifestyle decisions and longer-term performance outcomes.

Methods

Participants and recruitment. One hundred participants were recruited using opportunistic sampling methods. Recruitment occurred via campus email lists, flyers in community centers, and brief advertisements on social media platforms targeting individuals aged 18 years and older. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years of age and fluent in English. Exclusion criteria were minimal and included inability to provide informed consent and current enrollment in an inpatient medical or psychiatric facility. The final sample comprised 100 adults with an age range from 18 to 45 years. Participants included both university students and members of the broader community to increase variability in academic performance and lifestyle factors. Demographic information was collected including age, gender, highest level of education attained by participants, and self-reported socioeconomic status measured on a 5-point Likert scale. Ethical oversight. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by an institutional ethics committee. All participants provided informed consent electronically prior to participation. Participation was anonymous and voluntary, and respondents were informed they could withdraw at any time without penalty. Measures. Self-efficacy was measured using a brief version of a widely used generalized self-efficacy scale consisting of 10 items. The items assess beliefs about one’s competence to cope with a variety of stressful situations and to exert control over challenges. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Items were summed and averaged to produce a self-efficacy score for each participant, with higher scores indicating greater perceived efficacy. Internal consistency for the scale in the present sample was examined and reported. Academic performance was assessed via self-reported grade point average (GPA) on a 4.0 scale for participants who were currently enrolled in formal educational programs. Participants not currently enrolled were asked to provide their most recent GPA or an equivalent metric of academic performance; where participants could not provide a GPA, they were asked to indicate the highest level of educational attainment and to self-rate their typical academic performance relative to peers on a 5-point scale. For analysis, self-reported GPA was used for those with numerical data and converted where necessary to a 4-point equivalent. Lifestyle choices were assessed with a composite measure designed to capture behaviors commonly associated with well-being. The lifestyle questionnaire included items assessing frequency of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (with response options from 1 = rarely to 5 = daily), dietary quality (items tapping frequency of fruit and vegetable consumption, intake of processed foods, and balanced meals, combined into a single diet quality index), sleep quality and duration (self-reported average nightly sleep and a subjective sleep quality item), and risky substance use (frequency of heavy alcohol use and current smoking status). Risky behaviors were reverse-scored so that higher composite scores reflected healthier lifestyle choices. The items were standardized and combined to form a single lifestyle composite score for each participant. Procedure. Participants completed the survey online via a secure survey platform. The survey began with informed consent and demographic items followed by the self-efficacy scale, the lifestyle questionnaire, and finally questions about academic performance. The order of the self-efficacy and lifestyle sections was randomized across participants to reduce order effects. Completion time averaged approximately 15 to 20 minutes. Data screening and handling of missing data. Data were screened for completeness and response consistency. Cases with more than 20% missing data on key variables were excluded from analysis. For cases with minimal missing data (≤ 20% on a given measure), mean imputation at the item level was used to compute scale scores. All variables were examined for normality, outliers, and linearity prior to analysis. Skewed variables were examined and, where necessary, transformed or analyzed with nonparametric methods as a robustness check. Statistical analyses. The primary planned analyses were bivariate Pearson correlations between self-efficacy, academic performance (GPA), and the lifestyle composite in the full sample. Where variables were not normally distributed, Spearman rank correlations were computed as a robustness check. Partial correlations were also computed to examine the relationship between self-efficacy and academic performance while controlling for age, gender, and socioeconomic status. Additionally, a multiple linear regression analysis was conducted predicting academic performance from self-efficacy and the lifestyle composite simultaneously, with demographic covariates added in a subsequent step. Effect sizes and confidence intervals were reported for all primary analyses. Exploratory mediation analyses were planned contingent on observed correlations consistent with an indirect pathway from self-efficacy to academic performance via lifestyle choices, using bootstrapped confidence intervals to estimate indirect effects. All statistical tests were two-tailed and an alpha level of .05 was used to determine statistical significance. Analyses were conducted using standard statistical software and scripts were archived for reproducibility. Data availability and transparency. De-identified study data and analysis scripts were stored on a secure institutional server. Aggregate descriptive statistics and primary analysis outputs are reported below. Individual-level data are available upon reasonable request subject to ethical and privacy constraints.

Results

Sample characteristics and descriptive statistics. The final sample included 100 participants. The mean age of participants was 24.6 years with a standard deviation of 5.8 years. Sixty-two percent of the sample identified as female, 36% identified as male, and 2% identified as non-binary or preferred not to say. The sample included a mix of students and non-students; 72 participants were currently enrolled in post-secondary education and reported a numerical GPA, while the remaining 28 participants reported prior academic performance or current educational attainment in categorical terms that were converted to a 4.0-equivalent for descriptive purposes. The mean self-efficacy score on the 1–5 scale was 3.78 with a standard deviation of 0.61. The self-efficacy scale showed good internal consistency in this sample with Cronbach’s alpha equal to 0.86. The mean self-reported GPA in the sample was 3.21 (SD = 0.47) on the 4.0 scale. The lifestyle composite, calculated from standardized diet, exercise, sleep, and reverse-scored substance use items, had a mean of 0 (by construction when standardized) but when converted back into the original 1–5 metric for interpretability the approximate mean was 3.37 with a standard deviation of 0.71. Preliminary checks indicated that the distributions of self-efficacy and lifestyle composite were approximately normal as assessed by skewness and kurtosis statistics within acceptable ranges, whereas the GPA distribution showed mild negative skew reflecting a moderate concentration of higher GPA values in this sample. Bivariate correlations. The primary analysis involved bivariate Pearson correlations among self-efficacy, academic performance, and the lifestyle composite. Self-efficacy and the lifestyle composite showed a moderate, positive correlation (r = .42, p < .001). This association indicates that participants reporting higher generalized self-efficacy also reported healthier lifestyle patterns, including more frequent exercise, better diet, and better sleep quality, as well as lower risky substance use. The correlation between self-efficacy and GPA was positive but small and not statistically significant (r = .12, p = .23). This result means that, in this sample, higher general self-efficacy was not significantly associated with higher academic performance as measured by self-reported GPA. The lifestyle composite and GPA were positively correlated (r = .24, p = .015), indicating that participants reporting healthier lifestyle behaviors also tended to report higher GPAs. Robustness checks using Spearman rank correlations produced similar patterns: self-efficacy and lifestyle rho = .40, p < .001; self-efficacy and GPA rho = .11, p = .26; lifestyle and GPA rho = .22, p = .03. Partial correlations controlling for demographic covariates. Given the potential influence of demographic variables, partial correlations were computed controlling for age, gender, and socioeconomic status. After controlling for these covariates, the partial correlation between self-efficacy and GPA remained small and nonsignificant (partial r = .10, p = .31). The partial correlation between self-efficacy and lifestyle remained significant and of similar magnitude (partial r = .39, p < .001). The partial correlation between lifestyle and GPA controlling for the same covariates was attenuated slightly but remained significant (partial r = .20, p = .05). Regression analyses predicting academic performance. To further evaluate the joint contributions of self-efficacy and lifestyle choices to academic performance, a hierarchical multiple regression was conducted. In the first step, demographic covariates (age, gender code, and socioeconomic status) were entered and accounted for R^2 = .04 of the variance in GPA, which was not statistically significant for the set (F(3,96) = 1.29, p = .28). In the second step, self-efficacy and the lifestyle composite were entered simultaneously. The full model accounted for R^2 = .09 of the variance in GPA (ΔR^2 = .05, F change(2,94) = 2.74, p = .069 for the change). Within the full model, the lifestyle composite was a significant predictor of GPA (standardized beta = .22, t = 2.17, p = .032), whereas self-efficacy was not a significant predictor (standardized beta = .10, t = 1.00, p = .32). These results indicate that when lifestyle behaviors are considered alongside self-efficacy and basic demographics, lifestyle behaviors uniquely account for a modest but significant portion of variance in self-reported academic performance, whereas self-efficacy does not exert a unique direct effect in this model. Exploration of potential mediation. Because self-efficacy and lifestyle were significantly correlated and lifestyle correlated with GPA, an exploratory mediation analysis was conducted to examine whether lifestyle might statistically mediate any relationship between self-efficacy and academic performance. The analysis used bootstrapped estimation with 5,000 resamples to estimate the indirect effect. The total effect of self-efficacy on GPA was small (c path = .12, p = .23). The a path linking self-efficacy to lifestyle was significant (a = .42, p < .001), and the b path linking lifestyle to GPA when self-efficacy was included in the model was also significant (b = .22, p = .032). The bootstrapped indirect effect (a*b) was .09 with a 95% confidence interval that did not include zero (.02 to .18), suggesting a small but statistically reliable indirect association between self-efficacy and GPA via lifestyle behaviors in this cross-sectional dataset. It is critical to note that this cross-sectional mediation cannot establish causality or temporal precedence, and the indirect pathway should be interpreted as a statistical association consistent with mediation hypotheses rather than as definitive evidence of causal mechanisms. Gender and subgroup analyses. Given the sample composition, exploratory analyses examined whether the primary correlations differed by gender or by student status. Correlations between self-efficacy and lifestyle were similar for females (r = .44, p < .001) and males (r = .38, p = .02). The correlation between self-efficacy and GPA was nonsignificant in both females (r = .13, p = .28) and males (r = .08, p = .63). Among participants currently enrolled in post-secondary education (n = 72), the correlation between self-efficacy and GPA was slightly larger but remained nonsignificant (r = .15, p = .20), whereas the correlation between lifestyle and GPA in this subgroup was r = .26, p = .024. These subgroup results are broadly consistent with the full-sample pattern indicating a more robust association between self-efficacy and lifestyle than between self-efficacy and GPA. Sensitivity analyses. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to check the influence of potential outliers and to verify that results were not dependent on scaling choices. Excluding cases with GPAs more than two standard deviations from the mean did not materially change the results. Similarly, employing nonparametric regression approaches and using ordinal logistic regression when treating GPA categories as outcomes yielded comparable conclusions with lifestyle showing a positive association with academic outcomes and self-efficacy not contributing uniquely beyond lifestyle. Summary of findings. In summary, the primary analyses revealed a moderate and statistically significant positive relationship between self-efficacy and healthier lifestyle choices. There was no significant direct bivariate relationship between self-efficacy and academic performance. Lifestyle choices were modestly associated with academic performance, and regression and exploratory mediation analyses suggest that lifestyle may statistically mediate a small portion of the association between self-efficacy and GPA in this sample. All reported p-values are two-tailed and the pattern of results was robust to a range of sensitivity checks.

Discussion

This study examined associations among generalized self-efficacy, self-reported academic performance, and lifestyle choices in an opportunistic sample of one hundred adults. The principal findings were twofold. First, self-efficacy was positively and moderately associated with healthier lifestyle behaviors, including greater frequency of exercise, better dietary patterns, improved sleep, and lower risky substance use. Second, contrary to some prior research and theoretical expectations, generalized self-efficacy showed only a small, nonsignificant bivariate association with self-reported academic performance in this sample. Additionally, lifestyle behaviors were modestly associated with academic performance and appeared to account for a small statistical indirect effect linking self-efficacy to GPA. These results have several implications for theory and research on perseverance, task performance, and health-related behaviors. The finding of a substantial positive association between self-efficacy and lifestyle choices aligns with theoretical accounts that posit generalized efficacy beliefs shape broader patterns of behavior beyond narrow task domains. Individuals who believe in their ability to enact change and overcome obstacles may be more likely to initiate and sustain health-promoting behaviors, to persist in complex routines, and to avoid or reduce risky habits. The moderate effect size observed here (r ≈ .42) suggests that generalized self-efficacy is meaningfully related to a cluster of lifestyle behaviors that collectively contribute to well-being. This relationship is important because lifestyle choices have downstream consequences for physical health, mental health, and functional capacity, and because they may be amenable to intervention through approaches that bolster self-efficacy. The absence of a significant correlation between generalized self-efficacy and academic performance warrants careful interpretation. Theoretical models of academic persistence often emphasize the role of task-specific self-efficacy—beliefs about one’s capability to perform particular academic tasks—rather than generalized self-efficacy. In many studies demonstrating a strong link between efficacy and academic outcomes, measures of self-efficacy are domain-specific, assessing confidence in study skills, mastery of subject matter, or capacity to perform on examinations. Generalized self-efficacy may not map closely onto specific academic competencies in the present sample, which could partially explain the nonsignificant finding. In other words, a person can hold broad confidence in their capacity to manage challenges while still lacking specific confidence in their ability to succeed in particular academic tasks, and it is the latter form of efficacy that may be more predictive of grades. Measurement differences therefore present a plausible explanation for discrepancies with prior work. Methodological factors also likely contributed to the pattern of findings. The opportunistic nature of the sample and reliance on self-reported GPA may have attenuated the observable association between generalized efficacy and academic performance. Self-reported GPA can be subject to recall biases and social desirability influences that add noise to measurement and reduce statistical power to detect associations. Further, the sample included a mix of students and non-students, and although analyses were performed within the student subgroup, the reduced sample size in subgroup analyses may have limited the ability to detect a small association. Additionally, range restriction in GPA due to the sample being drawn primarily from educationally engaged individuals could have reduced variance and thereby attenuated correlations. Another key consideration concerns the role of third variables. Socioeconomic factors, access to educational resources, and cumulative academic preparation may have larger direct influences on grades than generalized self-efficacy. Even if self-efficacy promotes persistence, the effect of persistence on grades may be constrained or amplified by structural factors such as course difficulty, instructor practices, and institutional supports. In contexts where such structural determinants are strong, the incremental influence of generalized self-efficacy on performance may be small and hard to detect with the sample sizes typical of opportunistic studies. The exploratory mediation analysis suggested that lifestyle behaviors may serve as a statistical pathway linking self-efficacy to academic outcomes. Specifically, the significant indirect effect indicates that part of the association between self-efficacy and GPA can be accounted for by healthier lifestyle choices among those with higher efficacy. This pattern is theoretically plausible: self-efficacy could promote engagement in regular exercise, better diet, and adequate sleep, which in turn support cognitive functioning, concentration, and emotional regulation that are conducive to academic success. Although this cross-sectional mediation is consistent with that model, it must be emphasized that causal directionality cannot be established from these data. Longitudinal and experimental designs are required to determine whether changes in self-efficacy lead to lifestyle improvements that subsequently enhance academic performance. The practical implications of these results are notable. If generalized self-efficacy contributes to healthier lifestyle habits, interventions aimed at increasing efficacy beliefs could yield benefits for physical and mental health. Such interventions might include mastery experiences, modeling, and verbal persuasion strategies that are central to efficacy-enhancing programs. While these programs could in principle also benefit academic outcomes, the present findings suggest that their academic impact may be more likely to occur indirectly through improvements in lifestyle rather than through a direct effect on grades. This potential mechanism emphasizes the importance of comprehensive interventions that address both psychological beliefs and behavioral routines. Several limitations of the study must be acknowledged. As already noted, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Reliance on self-report measures introduces potential biases including social desirability and inaccurate recall. The opportunistic sample and the inclusion of both students and non-students limit the generalizability of the findings to broader or more diverse populations. The use of a generalized self-efficacy measure rather than domain-specific measures of academic self-efficacy may have reduced sensitivity to detect associations with academic performance. In addition, the lifestyle composite, while comprehensive, aggregated distinct behaviors that may have different relationships with academic outcomes; for example, sleep quality may be more proximally linked to cognitive performance than dietary patterns. Future research should address these limitations in several complementary ways. Longitudinal studies tracking changes in self-efficacy, lifestyle behaviors, and academic outcomes over time would be valuable in establishing temporal precedence and testing causal models. Experimental interventions that target self-efficacy and measure subsequent changes in lifestyle and performance would provide stronger evidence for causal pathways. Incorporating objective measures of academic performance (e.g., transcript-verified grades) and objective assessments of lifestyle behaviors (e.g., activity trackers, dietary logs, actigraphy for sleep) would reduce reliance on self-report and increase measurement precision. It would also be informative to include domain-specific self-efficacy measures alongside generalized measures to disentangle the relative contributions of broad versus task-specific efficacy beliefs. Examining potential moderators, such as baseline resource availability, stress levels, and institutional supports, could clarify under what conditions self-efficacy translates into performance gains. Field experiments in educational settings where environmental constraints are manipulated or supported could test whether efficacy-enhancing interventions are more effective in resource-rich or resource-poor contexts. Theoretically, the findings invite further elaboration of how generalized beliefs interact with domain-specific skills and environmental affordances to shape performance. Models that incorporate reciprocal effects—where success experiences enhance self-efficacy and in turn promote healthier behaviors that further support performance—would be useful. Computational or formal models of these reciprocal dynamics could help to generate precise, testable predictions about trajectories of perseverance and performance under different intervention regimes. Another fruitful avenue for future work is to investigate heterogeneity in the role of self-efficacy across subgroups. For example, self-efficacy may be a stronger predictor of academic performance for students in early career stages or for those facing chaotic environments, whereas for more advantaged students the variance explained by efficacy might be smaller. More fine-grained analyses could also examine whether different components of lifestyle (e.g., sleep versus exercise) differentially mediate the self-efficacy-performance link. There are also applied implications for educational policy and practice. If lifestyle behaviors are reliably linked to academic outcomes and are influenced by self-efficacy, then programs that integrate well-being promotion with skills training and efficacy-building interventions could have synergistic benefits. Educational institutions might consider embedding well-being curricula and structured opportunities for mastery that build students’ confidence in a range of life domains. Such integrative approaches might be particularly important for students with limited external resources, for whom internal beliefs about capability can be leveraged to support healthier routines and, indirectly, better performance. In conclusion, the present study contributes to understanding why some people persevere and others do not by showing that generalized self-efficacy is closely linked to healthier lifestyle decisions, even though it did not show a direct association with academic performance in this opportunistic sample. The results support a view in which self-efficacy promotes adaptive behaviors that may, over time, support performance outcomes. However, the absence of a direct relationship between generalized efficacy and grades highlights the importance of domain specificity, measurement precision, and consideration of broader contextual factors. Future longitudinal and intervention research is needed to clarify causal pathways and to determine how best to harness efficacy beliefs in service of both health and academic goals.
