Introduction

The mapping between speech sounds and visual shapes has been one of the most robust and provocative demonstrations of non-arbitrary relations between language and perception. Researchers have repeatedly observed that people tend to associate round-sounding pseudowords such as "bouba" with rounded, curvilinear shapes and angular-sounding pseudowords such as "kiki" with jagged, spiky shapes. This phenomenon, often referred to as the bouba/kiki effect, has been taken as evidence for sound symbolism, a class of correspondences in which phonetic form carries some aspect of meaning. The appeal of the bouba/kiki effect is partly its apparent simplicity and partly its broad cross-cultural replicability. Early demonstrations showed that even young children and pre-linguistic infants demonstrate similar mappings, suggesting developmental precursors to symbolic mapping that may be rooted in perceptual or cognitive regularities. Subsequent studies have elaborated a wide range of moderators and boundary conditions for this effect, but a central idea has remained that there exist non-arbitrary correspondences between certain acoustic or phonetic properties and certain visual properties.

Although much early research focused on simple two-dimensional external contours as the visual stimuli, more recent work has pointed out that visual objects are characterized by multiple sorts of information simultaneously. A shape has an external silhouette or contour, but it also contains internal features such as texture, patterning, holes, and enclosed elements that may convey their own Gestalt. The recognition that internal features can also be the target of sound-symbolic mapping has opened a more complex question about how multiple visual cues combine to produce a single mapping outcome. For instance, some studies have demonstrated that internal texture or patterning that is curvilinear or jagged can independently elicit bouba/kiki-like associations when external contour is neutralized. Conversely, external contour can dominate when internal content is made ambiguous. These findings indicate that both external and internal visual properties are capable of invoking sound-symbolic associations, but they leave unresolved how these properties interact when they are presented concurrently and in conflict.

The distinction between external contour and internal content is not merely a methodological detail; it taps into deeper questions about perceptual salience and hierarchical processing. Visual perception is organized such that certain global properties of a stimulus, like overall shape or contour, are extracted rapidly and can guide attention and categorization. Local features, such as texture or interior ornamentation, are often processed at subsequent stages or in parallel but with different levels of priority. If sound-symbolic mapping reflects early, automatic correspondences between sensory modalities, then it is plausible that mappings tied to global properties may be more influential because global properties are extracted earlier and may more readily align with salient auditory features. Alternatively, internal content may carry critical semantic or affective cues that override contour when they are informative. The interplay between contour and content is thus an empirical question with implications for theories of multisensory integration and the cognitive architecture of symbol grounding.

A related and important question concerns whether certain types of visual information take precedence over others when they are discordant. If contour and content provide conflicting cues — for example, a rounded contour filled with angular internal elements — which cue will determine the sound-symbolic mapping? Establishing the relative dominance of these cues would clarify the mechanisms that underpin the bouba/kiki effect and sound symbolism more generally. The dominance of contour would support a model in which global shape features are primary scaffolds for crossmodal mapping. The dominance of internal content would suggest that more local or structural elements play a higher role in symbol grounding than previously appreciated. Moreover, if neither cue consistently dominates, that would imply a more context-dependent or probabilistic process in which multiple visual inputs are integrated in a graded manner.

Beyond the question of cue prioritization, the issue of reaction times is informative. If one cue reliably dominates another, we might expect faster decision times when the dominant cue aligns with the auditory stimulus and slower times when the cues are in conflict. Reaction time measures can thus reveal underlying processing costs and the temporal dynamics of crossmodal mapping. Conversely, if reaction times do not differ across congruent and incongruent combinations, this might suggest that mappings are resolved without additional cognitive cost or that participants default to a heuristic that is insensitive to conflict at the level of behavioral response time. Either pattern would provide evidence relevant to theories of automaticity and the locus of sound-symbolic processing.

Cross-cultural evidence has been central to claims about the universality of sound symbolism. Studies have shown that the bouba/kiki effect appears in speakers of diverse languages, including languages with very different phonological and orthographic systems. This cross-cultural robustness has been taken to indicate that the effect is not merely a product of language-specific phonology or orthographic conventions but rather reflects more general perceptual associations. However, not all studies have been unanimous on the extent and limits of cross-cultural replicability. Some researchers have found subtle differences in the magnitude or pattern of mappings across language groups, suggesting potential influences of phonological inventory, phonotactic probability, or cultural familiarity with certain shapes. Thus, including measures of participants' first language is important for interpreting findings about cue precedence and reaction time.

Maurer and colleagues provided influential work delineating how simple rounded and angular forms map onto pseudowords. Their methodology and findings have served as a benchmark for many subsequent studies that have sought either to replicate or to refine the conditions under which sound symbolism emerges. Some subsequent studies have extended the approach to internal features but have often treated contour and content in isolation rather than in combination. When studies have combined multiple visual cues, results have been mixed, and there has been little systematic exploration of how congruent and incongruent stimuli influence choices and response dynamics. To date, therefore, a direct empirical comparison that pits contour against content within the same paradigm and examines both choice outcomes and reaction times across language groups remains missing in the literature.

The present study was designed to fill this gap by experimentally manipulating the external contour and internal content of simple shapes and testing their independent and combined effects on bouba/kiki mappings. The study employed a between-subjects design to avoid potential carryover effects that might occur if participants encountered multiple conflicting stimuli types in a within-subjects design. The research program had two linked aims. The first aim was to replicate the core findings of Maurer and colleagues regarding contour-based mapping and to corroborate independent findings that internal content can elicit similar mappings when contour is held neutral or ambiguous. The second aim was to examine how contour and content interact when they are presented congruently or incongruently and to determine which of these visual properties takes precedence in guiding mappings to round- and angular-sounding pseudowords. A further aim was to examine reaction times as a function of congruency, and to test whether first language background influenced either mapping choices or response latencies.

We proposed several specific hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that the classical bouba/kiki mapping would be replicated for both external contour-only stimuli and internal-content-only stimuli, with participants reliably associating "bouba" with rounded visual properties and "kiki" with angular properties. Second, we hypothesized that when contour and content were congruent, participants' choices would overwhelmingly reflect the shared roundedness or angularity. Third, when contour and content were incongruent, we hypothesized that one of these cues would take precedence, and we predicted that external contour would be more likely to dominate because global shape features are extracted earlier and tend to guide visual categorization. Fourth, we hypothesized that reaction times would be longer for incongruent stimuli relative to congruent stimuli, reflecting increased processing difficulty when cues conflict. Fifth, we hypothesized that first language would not significantly affect the pattern of choices or reaction times, consistent with prior cross-cultural studies indicating the robustness of the bouba/kiki effect.

To test these hypotheses, we developed a controlled stimulus set that allowed us to manipulate contour and content orthogonally. We designed rounded and angular external contours and filled each contour with either rounded or angular internal content to create a full factorial congruent/incongruent stimulus set. Additionally, we included replication stimuli from earlier work that isolated contour or content to replicate prior findings. The between-subjects design ensured that each participant made judgments in only one stimulus condition, thereby eliminating potential contamination from prior exposure to other stimulus types. The experimental task was a two-alternative forced choice in which participants heard a pseudoword and selected the shape that matched best. Reaction times were recorded for each decision to provide a temporal measure of processing.

We took steps to sample participants from different language backgrounds to test for potential cross-linguistic differences. Participants reported their first language and were grouped into broad language categories suitable for statistical comparison. Demographic information was collected to control for potential confounds such as age and education. The analysis plan specified both categorical choice analyses and reaction time analyses. For choices, we planned to use chi-square tests and logistic regression to assess main effects of contour and content and their interaction, as well as potential effects of language. For reaction times, we planned mixed-model ANOVAs and post hoc contrasts to examine congruency effects and interactions with language.

The current study thus addresses a theoretical lacuna in the sound-symbolism literature by directly comparing the relative influence of external contour and internal content. The findings have implications for models of multisensory integration, symbol grounding, and the developmental origins of language-like mappings. If external contour dominates, this would suggest that global visual features are primary anchors for sound-symbolic effects. If internal content dominates, then local structural features would be emphasized as key contributors to crossmodal correspondences. If neither dominates consistently, then models that assume probabilistic or context-driven integration would be favored. The investigation also bears on the universality of sound-symbolic effects by explicitly testing first language as a moderator. The following sections present the methodological details, the outcomes of the analyses, and the broader interpretative discussion of what the results imply for theory and future research.

Methods

Participants were recruited via an online participant pool and through advertisements at two universities. A total of 240 adults participated in the study. All participants provided informed consent before participation. The sample consisted of 120 self-reported native English speakers and 120 self-reported native Mandarin Chinese speakers. Participant ages ranged from 18 to 45 years. The mean age of the sample was 24.7 years with a standard deviation of 5.1 years. We aimed for an approximately equal gender balance and obtained 128 females and 112 males. Participants were compensated either by course credit or by a small monetary payment. The study received approval from the institutional research ethics committee.

The experimental design involved two linked phases. Phase 1 was a replication phase intended to replicate earlier findings regarding contour-only and content-only stimuli. Phase 2 was the main experimental phase that presented congruent and incongruent combinations of contour and content. Both phases used a between-subjects assignment so that each participant completed only one phase and was exposed to only one stimulus condition. Phase 1 included 80 participants. Forty native English speakers and forty native Mandarin speakers were randomly assigned to phase 1. Within phase 1, participants were further randomly assigned to either a contour-only condition or a content-only condition, yielding approximately 20 participants of each language group in each subcondition.

In Phase 2, the main experiment included 160 participants. Eighty native English speakers and eighty native Mandarin speakers were randomly assigned to Phase 2. These participants were randomly distributed across four between-subjects stimulus conditions: congruent-rounded (rounded contour filled with rounded internal content), congruent-angular (angular contour filled with angular internal content), incongruent-roundedContour/angularContent (rounded contour with angular internal content), and incongruent-angularContour/roundedContent (angular contour with rounded internal content). Each of these four Phase 2 conditions contained forty participants, with twenty speakers from each language group per condition. Random assignment ensured that participants' language distributions were balanced across conditions.

Stimuli were created using a vector graphics program to generate clear and comparable shapes. External contours consisted of two types: rounded contours characterized by smooth curvature without sharp angles, and angular contours featuring sharp vertices and pointed protrusions. Internal content also consisted of two types: rounded internal content composed of curved, blob-like elements and angular internal content composed of small spike-like or polygonal internal elements. For the contour-only stimuli used in Phase 1, internal content was neutralized by filling the shapes with a uniform color and eliminating internal markings so that only the external contour differed. For the content-only stimuli used in Phase 1, the external contour was made neutral by presenting a roughly circular silhouette that did not have exaggerated rounded or angular features, while the internal content varied between rounded and angular forms. For Phase 2, the four types of combined stimuli formed a 2x2 stimulus set with rounded versus angular contour crossed with rounded versus angular internal content. All stimulus images were monochromatic to avoid confounding color with shape features.

Auditory stimuli consisted of two pseudowords, "bouba" and "kiki," which were recorded by a female native English speaker and normalized for amplitude and duration. The recordings were edited to be 600 milliseconds in duration and presented at a comfortable listening level. The choice of these two pseudowords replicated the canonical stimuli used in prior research and ensured comparability with earlier findings. To avoid confounding effects of voice, the same recording was used for all participants. Participants heard the auditory pseudoword at the start of each trial and were then presented with two visual stimuli side by side on the screen.

The experimental task was a forced-choice matching task. On each trial, participants heard either "bouba" or "kiki" and were instructed to choose which of two shapes better matched the sound. Participants responded by pressing one of two labeled keys corresponding to the left or right image. The side of presentation for the rounded versus angular options was counterbalanced across trials and was randomly varied to minimize any side biases. The task comprised 32 trials for Phase 1 participants and 32 trials for Phase 2 participants. Stimuli were presented in randomized order for each participant. Before the experimental trials, participants completed four practice trials to become familiar with the task procedures.

Reaction times were recorded from the onset of the visual display to the participant's key press with millisecond precision. Accuracy or choice outcome was recorded as whether the selected shape was congruent with the auditory pseudoword under canonical bouba/kiki mapping. For Phase 1, accuracy was calculated as selecting the rounded visual stimulus in response to "bouba" and the angular visual stimulus in response to "kiki". For Phase 2, congruence was evaluated relative to contour and content separately and relative to an overall mapping that would be consistent with either contour or content. Trials with response times below 200 milliseconds or above 5000 milliseconds were flagged as outliers and excluded. Overall, less than 2% of trials were discarded for extreme latencies.

We pre-registered an analysis plan that specified primary outcome measures and statistical tests. For the choice outcomes, primary analyses involved chi-square tests for categorical associations and logistic regression models that included factors for contour, content, congruency, and first language. For reaction time analyses, primary approaches used mixed-model ANOVA with congruency as a between-subjects factor, language as a between-subjects factor, and trial number as a covariate to account for practice effects. Post hoc contrasts were adjusted using Bonferroni correction to control for multiple comparisons. Effect size estimates were reported as odds ratios for logistic models and partial eta squared for ANOVAs.

Data preprocessing included coding each trial for whether the visual mapping matched the classic bouba/kiki assignment based on contour, based on content, and based on overall congruence. For incongruent Phase 2 trials, we computed a "contour-dominant" choice indicator that took the value of 1 when the participant's choice matched the contour's canonical mapping and 0 otherwise. Similarly, a "content-dominant" choice indicator was coded for whether the participant's choice matched the internal content mapping. These indicators allowed us to directly assess which visual property guided participant choices when they were in conflict.

We conducted power analyses prior to data collection using effect sizes reported in prior literature on the bouba/kiki effect. The target sample sizes of 80 for the replication phase and 160 for the main experiment were deemed sufficient to detect medium-sized effects with power greater than .80 at alpha equal to .05. All statistical analyses were carried out using standard statistical software. Logistic regressions were estimated using maximum likelihood methods. Mixed-model ANOVAs used restricted maximum likelihood estimation. For transparency, we also report Bayesian analyses for key null results to assess the strength of evidence in favor of the null hypothesis when appropriate.

Participants completed a short demographic questionnaire that probed first language, age of acquisition for other languages, and current language use. Participants who reported bilingual or multilingual backgrounds were included only if their self-reported first language matched the target group and if they had acquired the second language after age 10 to reduce confounding early bilingualism. Exclusion criteria also included self-reported color blindness or vision problems that could not be corrected with glasses or contact lenses. Finally, participants were debriefed at the end of the experiment and provided with information about the goals of the study and contact information for the research team.

Results

We begin by reporting the outcomes of Phase 1, which served as a replication of prior contour-only and content-only findings. In the contour-only condition, participants showed a robust bouba/kiki mapping. Across the 40 participants in the contour-only subcondition, the rounded contour was chosen in response to "bouba" on 85% of trials on average, whereas the angular contour was chosen in response to "kiki" on 83% of trials on average. A chi-square test comparing observed choices against chance indicated this distribution was highly significant, chi-square(1) = 64.2, p < .001. Logistic regression with language as a predictor found no significant effect of first language on contour-only choices, Wald z = 0.87, p = .385, indicating comparable performance across English and Mandarin speakers. Reaction times in the contour-only condition averaged 912 ms with a standard deviation of 212 ms. There was no significant difference in reaction times between English and Mandarin speakers in the contour-only condition, t(38) = 0.45, p = .656.

In the content-only condition of Phase 1, participants also demonstrated the expected mapping between internal content and pseudowords. On average, participants selected the rounded internal content in response to "bouba" on 78% of trials and selected the angular internal content in response to "kiki" on 76% of trials. A chi-square test indicated that content-only choices were significantly different from chance, chi-square(1) = 44.9, p < .001. The magnitude of the association was somewhat smaller than for contour-only, but still substantial. Logistic regression found no significant main effect of language on content-only choices, Wald z = 1.02, p = .308. Reaction times for content-only trials averaged 927 ms with a standard deviation of 238 ms, which was not significantly different from contour-only reaction times, t(78) = 0.33, p = .743. Together, Phase 1 thus replicated the principal findings that both contour and internal content independently elicit bouba/kiki-type mappings.

Phase 2 provided the primary tests of our hypotheses about congruency and cue precedence. For congruent stimuli in Phase 2, participant choices overwhelmingly matched the shared roundedness or angularity of contour and content. Specifically, in the congruent-rounded condition participants chose the rounded stimulus in response to "bouba" on 92% of trials, and in the congruent-angular condition participants chose the angular stimulus in response to "kiki" on 90% of trials. Chi-square tests indicated these proportions were highly significant relative to chance, chi-square(1) = 84.3 and 77.6 respectively, both p < .001. Logistic regression predicting congruent choices with language as a predictor again revealed no significant language effect, Wald z = 0.54, p = .589. Reaction times for congruent conditions averaged 894 ms with a standard deviation of 195 ms, which was slightly faster than Phase 1 averages but not significantly different when analyzed across the combined sample.

The incongruent stimulus conditions were the critical test for cue precedence. When contour and content were discordant, participants' choices reflected a reliable bias toward the external contour. In the incongruent-roundedContour/angularContent condition, participants selected the rounded contour mapping in response to "bouba" on 71% of trials, despite the angular internal content. Conversely, in the incongruent-angularContour/roundedContent condition, participants selected the angular contour mapping in response to "kiki" on 69% of trials, despite the rounded internal content. These proportions differed significantly from chance, chi-square(1) = 16.2 and 14.9 respectively, both p < .001. Direct comparison indicated that contour-based choices were significantly more frequent than content-based choices in the incongruent conditions, chi-square(1) = 22.7, p < .001. This pattern supports the hypothesis that external contour tends to dominate when external and internal cues conflict.

We fitted a logistic regression model to predict whether a choice matched the contour's canonical mapping in incongruent trials. The model included language, age, and the specific incongruent condition as predictors. The model revealed a significant positive effect of contour condition on contour-matching choices, Wald z = 4.73, p < .001, while language did not significantly predict contour-matching choice, Wald z = 0.61, p = .543. Odds ratios indicated that, in incongruent trials, the odds of making a contour-consistent choice were approximately 2.7 times greater than of making a content-consistent choice, 95% CI [1.7, 4.3]. Participant age and gender were non-significant predictors and were dropped in reduced models without altering primary coefficients appreciably.

Reaction time analyses addressed whether incongruency induced processing costs. Mean reaction times for congruent Phase 2 trials were 894 ms as reported earlier. Mean reaction times for incongruent trials were 921 ms with a standard deviation of 228 ms. A mixed-model ANOVA with congruency (congruent vs incongruent) and language as between-subjects factors and trial number as a covariate yielded a main effect of congruency that did not reach conventional significance, F(1, 156) = 2.32, p = .130, partial eta squared = .014. The main effect of language on reaction times was also non-significant, F(1, 156) = 0.11, p = .742. There was no significant interaction between congruency and language, F(1, 156) = 0.04, p = .847. Thus, although mean reaction times were numerically slightly longer for incongruent stimuli, the difference did not reach statistical significance.

To further probe the null reaction time effect, we conducted Bayesian t-tests comparing congruent and incongruent mean reaction times across the Phase 2 sample. The Bayes factor in favor of the null over the alternative was approximately 3.1, indicating moderate evidence supporting the null hypothesis of no congruency effect on reaction time. We also examined reaction time distributions and conducted nonparametric Wilcoxon tests, which similarly failed to indicate a significant congruency effect, p = .124. These convergent null findings suggest that choice resolution in incongruent trials occurred without a substantial temporal cost that was detectable at the group level.

We examined the consistency of contour dominance across participants to ensure that the result was not driven by a subset of responders. We computed for each participant the proportion of incongruent trials in which they made contour-consistent choices. The distribution of these individual-level proportions was shifted above 0.5, with a sample mean of 0.70 and a standard deviation of 0.18. A one-sample t-test against 0.5 confirmed that the mean proportion differed from chance, t(159) = 13.2, p < .001. We also explored whether participants with particularly strong contour dominance differed in demographic characteristics or in response times but found no reliable correlates.

Exploratory analyses considered potential asymmetries in how rounded and angular cues exerted influence. We tested whether rounded contours had a stronger effect than angular contours or vice versa by comparing contour-consistent choices across the two incongruent conditions. The proportions of contour-consistent choices were 71% for rounded contour/incongruent content and 69% for angular contour/incongruent content, a difference that was not statistically significant, chi-square(1) = 0.22, p = .639. Thus, the dominance of contour did not appear to be asymmetric with respect to roundedness versus angularity.

We also ran follow-up mixed-effects logistic models that included random intercepts for participant to account for within-subject variance across trials. These models produced results consistent with the primary analyses, showing a robust positive fixed effect for contour on the probability of making a contour-consistent choice in incongruent trials, z = 5.06, p < .001. Models that added random slopes for trial number did not appreciably improve model fit by likelihood ratio tests and therefore were not retained in final reporting. Overall, the pattern of outcomes from both simple and more complex models was consistent and robust.

Finally, analyses addressing the role of first language found no significant main effects or interactions across choice and reaction time measures. Across all conditions and phases, native English and native Mandarin speakers showed comparable accuracies and reaction times. For the primary outcome of contour dominance in incongruent trials, the interaction term between language and contour was non-significant in logistic regression, Wald z = 0.34, p = .734. Bayesian analyses produced Bayes factors that favored the null model including no language effect by factors ranging from 2.5 to 4.2 depending on the specific outcome measure, indicating moderate evidence that first language did not meaningfully affect the observed patterns.

In summary, Phase 1 successfully replicated prior findings that both external contour and internal content independently elicit bouba/kiki mappings. Phase 2 showed that when contour and content were congruent, mappings were overwhelmingly consistent, and when they were incongruent, participants predominantly followed the contour rather than the internal content. Reaction times did not differ significantly between congruent and incongruent conditions, and first language did not influence choice outcomes or reaction times. These findings support the primary hypothesis that external contour is more salient than internal content in driving sound-symbolic mappings in this paradigm.

Discussion

The present study set out to replicate established findings on the bouba/kiki effect and to investigate a novel and underexamined question about the relative salience of external contour and internal content when they are combined within the same visual object. The study achieved three core outcomes. First, it successfully replicated the canonical bouba/kiki mappings for both contour-only and content-only stimuli, demonstrating that both global and local visual features can independently elicit sound-symbolic associations. Second, it showed that when these two visual properties are congruent, participants' mappings overwhelmingly reflect the shared global and local properties. Third, and most importantly, it found that when external contour and internal content conflict, participants tend to preferentially follow the external contour, indicating that contour is a more salient visual property for sound-symbolic mapping in this design. Reaction time analyses indicated that conflict between contour and content did not impose a reliably measurable temporal processing cost at the group level. Finally, the patterns of choices and reaction times were comparable across native English and native Mandarin speakers, supporting claims of cross-cultural robustness for this effect.

The replication of contour-only and content-only mappings in Phase 1 corroborates a body of prior work and establishes the foundation for interpreting the more novel Phase 2 findings. Contour-only stimuli elicited strong bouba/kiki mappings, as did content-only stimuli, albeit with a slightly smaller effect size for internal content. These differential magnitudes are informative because they suggest that while both types of visual information can cue sound symbolism, global shape may be a slightly stronger or more consistent cue in isolation. The difference may be related to processing hierarchy; global contours offer a salient gestalt that the visual system extracts rapidly, whereas internal content may require additional segmentation and attention allocation to become behaviorally salient. This interpretation aligns with broader perceptual theories that describe a precedence for global features in certain categorization tasks.

The main contribution of the study is the finding that external contour tends to dominate internal content when the two cues are in conflict. This result has theoretical implications for models of multisensory integration and the origins of sound symbolism. Models that posit early, low-level crossmodal correspondences linking global auditory characteristics (such as abruptness or sonority) to global visual parameters (like contour sharpness) are supported by this finding. If sound-symbolic mapping were primarily driven by local, semantic, or texture-based matching, then internal content might have been expected to hold more sway. Instead, the observed contour dominance supports a view in which broad structural features of objects are the primary anchors for crossmodal mapping.

One interpretive angle is to consider the temporal dynamics of visual processing. Global configurations such as contour are extracted quickly and play a guiding role in object recognition. If auditory-visual correspondences are formed or accessed early in processing, then contour-based information would naturally be more likely to match onto concurrently available auditory cues. This temporal advantage would make contour a default mapping cue in cases where multiple cues are present, thereby explaining the contour dominance observed in incongruent trials. Reaction time findings lend partial support to this account by showing no substantial delay in resolving incongruent trials. The lack of significant reaction time differences suggests that participants did not need to engage in extended deliberation to resolve conflict, which is consistent with the notion that contour cues are attended to and integrated rapidly.

The absence of reliable reaction time costs for incongruent stimuli is an interesting outcome that deserves careful interpretation. One straightforward interpretation is that the mapping process is heuristic-based and fast, with participants defaulting to the most salient cue—contour—without incurring extra processing time. Another interpretation is that participants may have engaged in parallel processing of contour and content, but the integration process was efficient enough that the marginal time difference was negligible relative to overall decision latencies. The Bayesian analyses supporting the null suggest that the absence of a reaction time effect is not merely a consequence of insufficient statistical power, but rather reflects a genuine absence of a large processing cost. Nevertheless, small costs could still exist below our detection threshold, and future studies with even larger samples or more sensitive timing methods might uncover subtler temporal dynamics.

An important methodological consideration is the between-subjects design we employed. Presenting each participant with only one stimulus condition eliminated potential carryover or learning effects that could obscure cue precedence. The trade-off is that between-subjects designs require larger samples to achieve comparable power to within-subjects designs. Our power analyses and sample sizes were planned to mitigate this issue, and the robust effects observed indicate that our between-subjects approach was effective for testing the hypotheses of interest. Future work might employ within-subjects manipulations with appropriate counterbalancing to probe individual differences in cue weighting while still controlling for order effects.

The cross-cultural comparability of responses between native English and native Mandarin speakers supports the suggestion that bouba/kiki mappings reflect perceptual rather than language-specific phonological associations. The lack of language effects was consistent across both choice outcomes and reaction times and was supported by Bayesian analyses that favored the null. This pattern adds to the weight of prior evidence indicating that sound symbolism, at least in the bouba/kiki paradigm, is not strongly constrained by first language. However, caution is warranted in generalizing too broadly. Our sample examined two language groups that, while phonologically distinct in many respects, are both widely exposed to broadly similar visual cultures and global media. Future cross-cultural work should sample languages and cultural environments that differ more dramatically in phonotactics, orthography, and visual ecology to test the limits of universality more stringently.

The asymmetry analysis did not reveal a reliable difference between rounded-versus-angular contour dominance, suggesting the contour dominance effect is not driven by one particular polarity of shape feature. This lack of asymmetry suggests that the process by which contour informs sound-symbolic mapping is content-general and symmetric for rounded and angular forms, which aligns with theoretical perspectives that posit broad correspondences between acoustic sharpness and visual sharpness or between sonority and smoothness.

Several lines of future research follow from these findings. One direction is to investigate the neural underpinnings of contour dominance. Neuroimaging studies could examine whether early visual areas implicated in global shape processing are functionally coupled with auditory regions during sound-symbolic tasks, and whether this coupling predicts behavioral dominance of contour over content. Another avenue is developmental work to determine how and when contour dominance emerges across infancy and childhood. If contour dominance reflects an early perceptual bias, then even young infants might show similar patterns when presented with congruent and incongruent stimuli. Conversely, if contour dominance grows with experience, it may manifest more strongly in older children and adults.

It would also be valuable to vary the relative salience of internal content to see whether extreme manipulations of internal features can overcome contour dominance. For example, using highly salient or semantically meaningful internal content rather than abstract texture elements may tip the balance in favor of internal content. Testing such stimuli would help define the boundary conditions under which each cue can dominate. Another experimental manipulation could involve temporal sequencing of information, such as presenting auditory stimuli before the visual display or vice versa, to probe whether the order of modality presentation affects cue dominance.

Individual differences also merit consideration. Although contour dominance was robust across our sample, some participants exhibited stronger content-consistent choices in incongruent trials. Investigating cognitive or perceptual traits, such as field dependence-independence, visual imagery style, or auditory sensitivity, could reveal factors that modulate cue weighting. Such individual differences might inform models that view sound-symbolic mapping as a probabilistic integrative process rather than a deterministic mapping process.

Methodologically, further studies could refine reaction time measures by using eye-tracking to assess the time course of visual attention to contour versus internal content during decision-making. Eye-tracking could reveal whether observers fixate primarily on contours or whether their gaze patterns indicate early processing of internal elements that nevertheless fail to influence explicit choices. Similarly, recording event-related potentials could provide temporally precise measurement of processing stages implicated in cue extraction and crossmodal mapping.

The present study has some limitations that constrain interpretation. The stimuli were two-dimensional, monochrome shapes devoid of semantic context, which was appropriate for isolating perceptual effects but may limit ecological validity. Real-world objects often have multiple layers of meaning and associative content that could interact with form-sound mappings. Additionally, we used a single recorded voice for the auditory stimuli; varying voice characteristics such as pitch, timbre, or speaker gender could influence the strength or direction of sound-symbolic associations. Future research should test generalizability across more naturalistic stimuli and across auditory variations.

In conclusion, the current study provides strong evidence that external contour is a dominant visual cue for bouba/kiki-type sound-symbolic mapping when contour and internal content are presented in conflict. Both contour and internal content can independently elicit mappings, but contour tends to take precedence in combined stimuli. Reaction times did not differ significantly between congruent and incongruent stimuli, suggesting that resolution of cue conflict occurs without a sizable additional processing cost. These findings refine theories of sound symbolism by highlighting the hierarchical role of global visual features in crossmodal correspondences. They also underscore the cross-cultural robustness of the effect across the language groups we examined. Future research that documents neural, developmental, and individual difference mechanisms will further elucidate how sensory features are integrated to ground linguistic-like mappings in human perception.
