Introduction

Human perception routinely links sensory qualities across modalities, producing systematic associations between auditory and visual features that transcend arbitrary mapping. One well-known instance of such cross-modal association is the bouba/kiki phenomenon, in which observers show a strong tendency to associate certain nonsense words with rounded shapes and other words with angular, jagged shapes. The phenomenon suggests that phonetic properties of spoken sounds can carry information that listeners map onto visual shape attributes, and that these mappings are sufficiently robust to be observed with simple meaningless syllables. Early demonstrations of similar sound-shape correspondences reported consistent associations across samples and stimulus sets, implying that the phenomenon is not idiosyncratic to particular items. The bouba/kiki effect has attracted substantial interest because it bears on fundamental questions about the mapping between language and perception, the origins of sound symbolism, and the mechanisms of multisensory integration. The phenomenon therefore occupies a focal position in debates about whether aspects of linguistic form can be motivated by perceptual or articulatory properties, rather than being purely arbitrary.

Several theoretical accounts have been proposed to explain why certain phonetic features preferentially map onto particular visual forms. One account emphasizes acoustic properties of vowels and consonants and their perceptual correlates; this view holds that rounded vowels and voiced sonorants tend to produce acoustic envelopes that listeners associate with rounded visual contours, whereas high front vowels and voiceless obstruents produce sharper, more percussive acoustic signatures that align with angular contours. Another account focuses on articulatory gestures, proposing that the physical configuration of the mouth and lips during production mirrors the visual contours of shapes, and that perceivers exploit this linkage when forming cross-modal correspondences. A third account highlights learned statistical associations in the environment or language-specific distributions of sounds and shapes, suggesting that mapping arises from exposure to consistent pairings between auditory and visual experiences. These accounts are not mutually exclusive, and empirical work has sought to differentiate among them by manipulating acoustic, articulatory, and contextual parameters.

Experimental work has examined the bouba/kiki effect across modalities and populations to probe its robustness and boundary conditions. Research has demonstrated the effect in written form, where orthographic presentation of nonsense words elicits consistent matches to shapes, and in auditory form, where spoken tokens produce similar mappings. Studies comparing presentation modalities have sometimes reported modality-specific differences, but overall the effect appears to be present in both visual and auditory contexts. This cross-modal robustness suggests that the underlying mappings are not tied exclusively to either orthography or sound, but rather reflect more general correspondence tendencies. The literature has also explored developmental trajectories, with evidence that infants and young children exhibit sensitivity to sound-shape correspondences early in life, raising questions about whether such mappings are innate or rapidly learned. Cross-cultural work has sometimes reported variation in effect size and prevalence, indicating that cultural and linguistic backgrounds can modulate the phenomenon, though a core tendency often remains.

Individual differences have been investigated as potential moderators of the bouba/kiki effect. Some studies have examined whether cognitive factors such as phonological awareness, visual imagery ability, and literacy influence the strength of cross-modal mappings. Others have considered whether sensory expertise, such as musical training, might affect the degree to which listeners map auditory features to visual shapes. Musical training systematically alters auditory perception and multisensory processing in ways that could plausibly influence sound-shape correspondences. Musicians tend to demonstrate enhanced pitch discrimination, finer temporal resolution, and more robust auditory working memory. These skills might allow musicians to parse fine-grained acoustic cues in nonsense words and either strengthen or attenuate their propensity to make rounded/angular mappings depending on the nature of the cues. Moreover, musical training often involves coordinated audio-motor experiences that could heighten cross-modal integration or, conversely, foster more analytic processing that reduces reliance on broad associative mappings.

Despite these plausible links, empirical evidence for an influence of musical training on the bouba/kiki effect has been limited and mixed. Some researchers have hypothesized that trained musicians, by virtue of their enhanced auditory acuity, might produce stronger sound-shape correspondences because they can detect subtle phonetic cues that align with shape categories. Other investigators have proposed the opposite pattern, arguing that increased analytic listening might lead musicians to focus on segmental properties or lexical analogs that weaken the spontaneous association with shape. Still other accounts predict little or no effect of musical experience, on the grounds that the bouba/kiki mapping is a robust perceptual bias present across diverse populations and independent of specialized auditory skills.

Methodological factors complicate attempts to resolve these competing hypotheses. Studies vary widely in how musical training is operationalized, ranging from self-reported years of instruction to objective measures of performance or grade attainment. The threshold at which training is considered meaningful differs across studies, and many investigations use small, heterogeneous samples that limit sensitivity to detect subtle group differences. Additionally, tasks differ in the modality of presentation (visual text vs. auditory speech), stimulus construction (number and type of pseudo-words, balancing of phonetic features), and response format (forced-choice matching, rating scales), which can affect effect sizes and the detectability of moderators. Despite the variety of approaches, the central finding of non-arbitrary sound-shape correspondences is repeatedly observed, which provides a strong baseline for probing modulatory factors like musical training and presentation modality.

The present investigation was designed to address two interrelated questions about the bouba/kiki phenomenon. First, we aimed to test whether the effect would be observed robustly in a within-subjects design that contrasted visual and auditory presentation of the same nonsense words. This comparison allowed us to assess whether orthographic presentation and auditory presentation elicit equivalent mapping tendencies across the same individuals. Second, we sought to examine whether musical training, operationalized as attainment of grade 5 proficiency on a musical instrument or higher, was associated with systematic differences in mapping strength. This operational definition of training was selected as a proxy for sustained formal instruction and technical proficiency, capturing a level of practice likely to confer measurable auditory expertise.

To address these aims, we recruited a sample balanced for musical training, with half of participants reporting grade 5 or higher attainment and half reporting no formal training or training below grade 5. Each participant completed bouba/kiki matching tasks in two separate blocks corresponding to visual and auditory presentation of stimuli. Stimuli were carefully constructed to include an equal number of nonsense words with rounded phonetic profiles and with high front, sharp-sounding profiles, matched on syllabic length and other superficial characteristics. Shapes were designed to be prototypical exemplars of rounded versus jagged contours and were presented as binary choices in each trial. Trials were randomized and balanced so that each nonsense word appeared equally often with each shape across participants, and the order of modality presentation was counterbalanced across the sample.

We hypothesized that participants would, on average, choose the shape consistent with the bouba/kiki mapping at a rate significantly above chance. Regarding modality, we expected that visual and auditory presentation would both elicit the effect, but we did not have a strong directional prediction about whether one modality would produce a stronger effect than the other, given mixed evidence in the literature. For musical training, we considered competing possibilities and therefore treated the effect of musical background as an empirical question; musicians could exhibit stronger, weaker, or equivalent mapping tendencies relative to non-musicians, depending on whether enhanced auditory skills amplify, suppress, or leave unaffected the cross-modal correspondences.

Beyond these primary questions, we also planned exploratory analyses to examine response times, trial-level consistency, and potential order effects associated with modality sequencing. These additional analyses were intended to reveal whether the mechanisms underlying the mapping differ qualitatively across groups or modalities, or whether the phenomenon is mechanically similar across conditions. In particular, response time analyses can shed light on whether mappings reflect fast, automatic associations or slower, deliberative processes, as faster responses would be consistent with the former interpretation. Finally, we assessed the reliability of the matching measure and considered the implications of any ceiling or floor effects for sensitivity to group differences.

The study therefore situates itself at the intersection of multisensory perception, phonetic symbolism, and auditory expertise. By combining within-subjects modality comparisons with a between-subjects contrast of musical training, the design offers a rigorous test of whether specialized auditory experience interacts with the modality of presentation to produce differential bouba/kiki mappings. The implications of the findings extend to theories of sound symbolism, the role of sensory learning in shaping cross-modal mappings, and the generalizability of perceptual biases across individuals with different experiential backgrounds. In the next sections, we describe the methods used to operationalize these constructs, present the results of statistical analyses, and discuss the theoretical and practical implications in light of the broader literature.

Methods

Participants were 40 adults recruited from a university participant pool and the local community. Twenty participants reported having reached grade 5 or above in formal musical instrument training, and twenty participants reported either no formal musical training or attainment below grade 5. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 35 years. The mean age for the full sample was 23.8 years with a standard deviation of 4.1 years. The gender composition included 22 females and 18 males. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing, and no participant reported a history of neurological disorders that would affect auditory processing. Participants gave informed consent prior to participation and were compensated with course credit or a small monetary payment.

Musical training was assessed via a short questionnaire that asked participants to report the type of musical instruction they received, the primary instrument they studied, the highest graded level attained if any, and the total number of years of formal instruction. Participants who reported achieving grade 5 or higher on a recognized instrument certification system were classified as musically trained; those who reported no formal instruction or attainment below grade 5 were classified as untrained. The distribution of instruments among trained participants included strings, piano, woodwinds, and brass; no single instrument dominated the sample. The years of instruction among the trained group ranged from five to twelve years, with a median of seven years. The untrained group reported either no formal instruction or less than two years of sporadic lessons.

Stimuli consisted of 16 nonsense words and 16 paired shape displays. The nonsense words were constructed to instantiate two phonetic profiles hypothesized to align with rounded or jagged shapes. The set included eight "rounded-leaning" words exemplifying lower back or rounded vowels and voiced sonorant consonants; examples of rounded-leaning phonetic structure included sequences with /u/ and /o/ vowels and sonorous consonants. The set also included eight "jagged-leaning" words designed with high front vowels such as /i/ and voiceless obstruents to produce sharper phonetic profiles. All nonsense words were two-syllable strings with consonant-vowel syllables to control for length and gross prosodic contours. Orthographic renderings of the nonsense words were used for visual presentation. For auditory presentation, spoken tokens of the words were recorded by a native speaker in a neutral prosodic style at a comfortable speaking rate and intensity. Recordings were made in a quiet laboratory environment with a high-quality microphone and normalized for amplitude across stimuli.

Shape stimuli consisted of 16 pairs of two-dimensional black-and-white images displayed side-by-side on a neutral gray background. Each pair presented one rounded shape and one jagged shape that were matched for overall area and complexity. The rounded shapes featured smooth, curved contours with no sharp angles, whereas the jagged shapes contained pointed protrusions and angular edges. The shapes were designed to be perceptually salient exemplars of their respective categories and were based on commonly used stimulus sets in investigations of shape-sound correspondences. Each shape pair was presented in the same left-right spatial arrangement for all participants, but the left-right position of rounded versus jagged shapes was counterbalanced across trials and participants to prevent lateral bias.

The experimental design was within-subjects for modality and between-subjects for musical training. Each participant completed two blocks of the bouba/kiki matching task: a visual block and an auditory block. The order of blocks was counterbalanced across participants such that half completed the visual block first and half completed the auditory block first. Each block contained 16 trials, one for each nonsense word, for a total of 32 trials per participant. On each trial, participants were presented with a two-alternative forced-choice display consisting of a pair of shapes and one nonsense word. In the visual block, the nonsense word was displayed in the center of the screen in lowercase letters above the shape pair. In the auditory block, the nonsense word was presented auditorily through over-ear headphones while the shape pair appeared on the screen without orthographic text. Participants indicated which shape they felt matched the word by pressing one of two keyboard keys corresponding to the left or right shape. Responses and response times were recorded.

Trials were randomized within each block, and the order of nonsense words was randomized separately for each participant. To minimize carryover effects, a brief practice phase with four practice trials using filler words and shapes preceded the first experimental block. Participants received standardized instructions emphasizing that there were no correct answers and that they should choose the shape they thought best matched each word. They were encouraged to respond as quickly and accurately as possible. A short break was offered between blocks to reduce fatigue.

Scoring involved coding each trial as congruent or incongruent with the predicted bouba/kiki mapping. For rounded-leaning words, a response selecting the rounded shape was coded as congruent; for jagged-leaning words, a response selecting the jagged shape was coded as congruent. Congruent responses were summed across trials to produce a matching score for each participant within each modality block. Matching scores were expressed as percentages of trials consistent with the bouba/kiki mapping, yielding a score range from 0 to 100 percent for each modality. Response times were analyzed for correct and incorrect responses separately after excluding trials with response times shorter than 200 milliseconds or longer than 5000 milliseconds as outliers.

Statistical analyses were conducted using standard software. The primary inferential test was a mixed-model analysis of variance (ANOVA) with modality (visual vs auditory) as a within-subjects factor and musical training (trained vs untrained) as a between-subjects factor, with matching percentage as the dependent variable. Complementary analyses included paired-sample t-tests comparing modality means, independent-sample t-tests comparing training groups, and one-sample t-tests comparing overall matching scores to chance (50 percent). Trial-level analyses used generalized linear mixed-effects models with binomial outcomes (congruent vs incongruent) and included random intercepts for participant and item. Response time analyses used mixed-effects models on log-transformed response times. Effect sizes are reported for the principal tests, and alpha was set at .05 for inferential decisions. Where appropriate, assumptions of normality and sphericity were evaluated and addressed.

The study protocol was approved by the university institutional review board, all participants provided informed consent, and data were collected anonymously. Data quality checks included inspection for incomplete sessions, excessive non-responses, and evidence of patterned responding; no participant met exclusion criteria for data quality, and all 40 participants were retained in the final analyses. The experimental procedures were standardized across sessions, and the laboratory environment was controlled for lighting and noise to reduce extraneous variability.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary dependent measure are presented first. Across the full sample, participants selected the shape consistent with the bouba/kiki mapping on a mean of 74.5 percent of trials, with a standard deviation of 11.9 percentage points. The distribution of individual matching scores was approximately symmetrical and did not display extreme skewness. In the visual modality block, the mean congruent response rate was 75.6 percent with a standard deviation of 11.8. In the auditory modality block, the mean congruent response rate was 73.4 percent with a standard deviation of 12.7. The pattern of means suggests robust mapping in both modalities and only a modest difference between them.

A one-sample t-test confirmed that overall matching rates were significantly above chance. Comparing the overall mean of 74.5 percent to a chance level of 50 percent yielded t(39) = 11.02, p < .001, indicating a highly reliable bouba/kiki effect in the sample. The large t-value corresponds to a substantial effect size; Cohen's d for the one-sample comparison was computed as 1.74, reflecting a strong tendency to choose congruent mappings across participants. Separate one-sample t-tests for each modality also yielded significant results. For the visual block, t(39) = 10.45, p < .001, and for the auditory block, t(39) = 9.12, p < .001, confirming that both presentation modalities produced matching rates well above chance.

The primary mixed-model ANOVA examined modality (visual vs auditory) as a within-subjects factor and musical training (trained vs untrained) as a between-subjects factor, with matching percentage as the dependent variable. The ANOVA revealed a main effect of modality that was not statistically significant. The F statistic for modality was F(1,38) = 1.05, p = .312, with a partial eta-squared of .027, indicating that the modest observed difference between the visual and auditory means was compatible with sampling variability. The main effect of musical training was likewise non-significant. The F statistic for training was F(1,38) = 0.03, p = .861, with a negligible partial eta-squared of .001, indicating no evidence that musicians and non-musicians differed in overall matching rates. The interaction between modality and musical training was also non-significant, F(1,38) = 0.12, p = .731, suggesting that the small difference between modalities did not vary as a function of musical background.

Complementary paired-samples t-tests quantified the modality contrast directly. Comparing visual and auditory matching scores across participants produced t(39) = 1.02, p = .315, with a mean difference of 2.2 percentage points and a 95 percent confidence interval for the difference spanning approximately -1.9 to 6.3 percentage points. This result reinforces the ANOVA finding that modality did not exert a reliable effect on matching rates in our sample. Independent-samples t-tests comparing the two training groups similarly failed to reveal differences. Trained participants had a mean overall congruent response rate of 74.9 percent (SD = 11.9) and untrained participants had a mean of 74.1 percent (SD = 12.0). The independent-samples t-test yielded t(38) = 0.17, p = .867, indicating essentially equivalent performance across training groups.

To probe the data at a finer grain, we performed trial-level generalized linear mixed-effects modeling with a binomial outcome (congruent vs incongruent) and fixed effects for modality, training group, and their interaction, with random intercepts for participant and for item. The intercept of the model was significantly positive, indicating above-chance likelihood of congruent responses overall. The odds ratio corresponding to the intercept indicated that congruent responses were several times more likely than incongruent responses. Neither modality nor training group produced significant fixed effects in this model. The modality coefficient had an odds ratio close to 1 and a p-value well above .05, and the interaction between modality and training was likewise non-significant. The random effects showed appreciable between-subject variability but not excessive heterogeneity that would undermine aggregate inferences.

We also examined whether order of modality presentation influenced performance by including order as an additional between-subjects factor in exploratory analyses. A mixed ANOVA with modality, training, and order revealed no significant main effect of order and no significant interactions involving order. Participants who completed the visual block first did not differ from those who completed the auditory block first in their overall matching rates, and there was no evidence of practice or fatigue effects altering the modality comparison.

Response time analyses were performed on log-transformed response times for congruent and incongruent trials separately. Mean response times for congruent trials were 1.12 seconds (SD = 0.41) in the visual modality and 1.18 seconds (SD = 0.45) in the auditory modality. For incongruent trials, mean response times were slightly longer, at 1.26 seconds (SD = 0.52) visual and 1.31 seconds (SD = 0.49) auditory. A mixed-effects model on log-transformed response times with fixed effects for congruency, modality, training, and their interactions revealed a significant main effect of congruency, indicating faster responses for congruent trials than for incongruent trials. The main effect of modality on response times was non-significant, as were all interactions involving training. The congruency effect on response times suggests that congruent mappings were accessed more fluently, consistent with the view that these mappings reflect rapid perceptual associations.

To assess the internal reliability of the matching measure, we computed Cronbach's alpha across the sixteen trials within each modality block. Reliabilities were acceptable, with alpha coefficients of .78 for the visual block and .75 for the auditory block, supporting the view that the tasks measured a coherent underlying tendency. Item-level analyses revealed that most items elicited congruent responding at rates above 60 percent, though a subset of items produced lower congruency rates, indicating variation in item diagnosticity. Removing the least diagnostic items modestly increased reliability but did not change the qualitative pattern of results.

Because the literature sometimes suggests that musical training effects may be subtle, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to estimate the smallest effect size detectable with our design given the sample size of 40. For between-subjects contrasts (training group), the design had adequate power (80 percent) to detect medium-sized effects (Cohen's d around .70) but limited power to detect small effects (d around .30). For within-subjects contrasts (modality), power was adequate to detect medium effects but limited for small effects, given the observed within-subjects correlation of approximately .40. These considerations indicate that while the present study was well powered to detect medium or larger training effects, it was less sensitive to small effects that might still be theoretically meaningful.

In sum, across multiple analytical approaches, the central finding was robust: participants demonstrated the bouba/kiki effect at rates substantially above chance in both visual and auditory presentations. However, modality did not significantly alter the magnitude of the effect, and musical training, defined here by attainment of grade 5 or higher on an instrument, was not associated with differences in matching behavior. Response time data supported the interpretation that congruent mappings are processed more fluently than incongruent ones. These results provide converging evidence for a strong and modality-general bouba/kiki mapping that appears resilient to the influence of the level of formal musical training represented in the sample.

Discussion

The current study set out to examine two related questions concerning sound-shape correspondences: whether the bouba/kiki effect manifests robustly across visual and auditory presentation within the same individuals, and whether formal musical training is associated with systematic differences in the strength of these cross-modal mappings. The primary findings were clear. Firstly, participants displayed a strong and reliable bouba/kiki effect across both modalities, selecting the shape congruent with the phonetic profile of nonsense words at well above-chance rates. Secondly, contrary to some speculations about the role of specialized auditory expertise, musical training as operationalized in this study did not significantly modulate the magnitude of the bouba/kiki mapping. The absence of systematic differences between visual and auditory presentation suggests that the phenomenon is modality-general at least within the parameters of the present task, and the lack of training effects implies that attaining grade 5 or higher proficiency on a musical instrument does not robustly alter the mapping tendency.

The robustness of the bouba/kiki mapping across modalities is an important contribution to the literature. Early reports of the phenomenon demonstrated the effect in both orthographic and auditory forms, but direct within-subjects comparisons across modalities are relatively scarce. By presenting the same nonsense words as both text and spoken tokens to the same participants, this study controlled for inter-individual variability and stimulus idiosyncrasies that can obscure modality-specific effects. The observed similarity between modalities suggests that the mapping reflects an underlying correspondence that transcends the specific sensory format in which phonetic information is encountered. This finding is consistent with theoretical perspectives that posit abstract correspondences between sound features and visual forms that are instantiated across modalities rather than being tied to orthography or to acoustic signals alone.

The response time results provide further insights into the cognitive dynamics of the mapping. Congruent trials were responded to more quickly than incongruent ones, which indicates that congruent associations are accessed with greater fluency. This pattern supports the interpretation that the bouba/kiki effect is not merely a deliberative post hoc rationalization but instead taps into rapid, perhaps automatic, perceptual associations. The fluency advantage for congruent pairings aligns with accounts that posit perceptual similarity or structural resonance between auditory and visual features: when the two modalities signal similar structural properties, the perceptual system can integrate them more readily, yielding faster responses. Importantly, the congruency advantage in response times was observed in both visual and auditory modalities and did not differ across musical training groups, reinforcing the view that the mapping operates in similar fashion across individuals with differing auditory expertise.

The null finding regarding musical training warrants careful interpretation. There are at least three broad classes of explanation for why training did not differentiate performance in the current sample. First, it is possible that formal musical training, even at grade 5 or above, simply does not influence the perceptual or cognitive processes that give rise to bouba/kiki mappings. If these mappings are grounded in early-developing multisensory correspondences or in cross-modal analogies that are robust to experiential variation, then the presence or absence of musical instruction would not be expected to matter. This interpretation is consistent with findings that infants and young children exhibit sound-shape correspondences, suggesting that the mappings may be part of a more general perceptual architecture.

A second possibility is that musical training does influence auditory processing but that the influence operates in ways not captured by the present task. For example, trained musicians might be more sensitive to subtle phonetic cues or to temporal fine structure, but the particular stimuli and matching task used here may have emphasized gross phonetic features that are equally salient to trained and untrained listeners. If musical training enhances sensitivity to microprosodic or timbral cues that do not systematically align with rounded versus jagged shape categories, then training effects might not emerge in this paradigm. Moreover, musicians might apply more analytic listening strategies, focusing on segmental details rather than global phonetic character, which could offset any facilitation derived from heightened auditory discrimination.

A third class of explanations centers on methodological considerations. The operational definition of musical training used in this study—attainment of grade 5 or higher—captures a meaningful threshold of formal instruction, but it is still a coarse metric. Musical expertise is multi-dimensional, encompassing perceptual acuity, instrumental proficiency, practice intensity, ensemble experience, and more. Graded attainment may correlate with some of these aspects but not perfectly. Consequently, the trained and untrained groups in this study might not have differed sufficiently on the specific auditory or multimodal skills that would modulate bouba/kiki performance. Additionally, the sample size, while adequate to detect medium-sized effects, had limited power to detect small effects, so subtle training-related differences could have gone undetected. Future investigations could include more fine-grained measures of musical ability, such as objective assessments of pitch discrimination, rhythmic acuity, or auditory working memory, to more sensitively probe potential relationships.

The absence of a modality effect also deserves elaboration. Some prior work has reported modest differences in effect size across presentation modalities, with either auditory or visual presentation sometimes producing stronger mappings depending on stimulus design and task demands. In our study, the two modalities elicited very similar mean congruency rates, and the small difference observed was not statistically reliable. This finding suggests that when the same items and response format are used, the modality of presentation is not a dominant determinant of mapping strength. This result supports the idea that the bouba/kiki effect is instantiated at a level where phonetic form and orthographic form are both readily translated into perceptual cues that connect with visual shape representations.

The trial-level mixed-effects modeling further reinforced the conclusion that the mapping operates consistently across items and participants. The strong positive intercept in the binomial models indicated a robust tendency toward congruent responding. The absence of modality or training effects at the trial level suggests that the mapping is a generalizable property of the stimuli rather than an artifact of a subset of items or of particular listeners. Nevertheless, item-level analyses did reveal variation in diagnosticity, with some items eliciting lower rates of congruent responses. This heterogeneity points to the importance of careful stimulus construction in future research, and suggests that particular phonetic configurations are more or less strongly associated with shape categories.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the sample size, while typical for experimental studies in this area, limits sensitivity to small effects and to complex interactions. Larger samples would increase the ability to detect subtle modulatory effects of musical training or other individual differences. Second, the operationalization of musical training was coarse and relied on self-report of graded attainment. Objective measures of musical skill and more nuanced measures of musical experience would provide a clearer test of the relation between auditory expertise and cross-modal mapping. Third, the stimuli were restricted to two-syllable nonsense words and prototypical rounded and jagged shapes; different stimulus constructions, syllabic lengths, prosodic manipulations, or more graded shape continua might reveal different patterns. Fourth, the sample was drawn from a university community and adjacent areas, which may limit generalizability to other populations with different language, cultural, or auditory experience profiles.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the findings have several implications for theory. The strong modality-general bouba/kiki effect aligns with accounts that posit perceptual or structural mappings between sound and shape, potentially grounded in acoustic envelopes, articulatory gestures, or shared structural descriptors such as curvature and spectral continuity. The fact that trained musicians did not differ reliably from untrained participants indicates that whatever underlying mechanisms support the mapping, they are not easily reshaped by the level of formal musical instruction captured here. This pattern points toward a parsimonious view in which sound-shape correspondences reflect stable perceptual tendencies that are partly innate or established early in development, rather than wholly dependent on later auditory expertise.

Future research should build on these results by pursuing several avenues. Studies with larger and more diverse samples can enhance power to detect small effects and examine whether cultural or linguistic background interacts with musical training to influence mappings. Longitudinal or developmental designs could trace whether musical training, when commenced early in life, alters the trajectory of sound-symbolic tendencies. Experimental manipulations that systematically vary acoustic features, such as spectral centroid, duration, or voicing contrasts, would help to pinpoint which acoustic dimensions primarily drive the mapping and whether musicianship selectively enhances sensitivity to particular dimensions. Neuroimaging and electrophysiological studies could reveal whether neural markers of multisensory integration differ as a function of musical training during sound-shape mapping tasks. Finally, incorporating objective measures of musical ability into predictive models would allow finer-grained tests of the hypothesis that specific auditory skills, rather than broad proxies of training attainment, modulate cross-modal correspondences.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates a robust bouba/kiki effect across visual and auditory presentation modes and shows that attainment of grade 5 or higher in musical instrument training does not substantially alter this mapping. The data are consistent with a view of sound-shape correspondences as general perceptual tendencies resilient to the particular form in which phonetic information is conveyed and to the level of formal musical instruction represented here. These results refine our understanding of the boundary conditions of sound symbolism and point to promising directions for future work aimed at elucidating the mechanisms that underlie cross-modal correspondences.
