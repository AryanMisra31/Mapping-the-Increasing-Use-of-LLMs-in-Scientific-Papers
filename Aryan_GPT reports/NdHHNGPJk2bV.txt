Introduction

Human short-term memory for verbal material has been a central topic of cognitive psychology for many decades, and one of the most influential frameworks for understanding short-term verbal memory is the working memory model originally proposed by Baddeley and Hitch. The working memory model posits a phonological loop component that maintains verbal information through a combination of a temporary phonological store and an articulatory rehearsal process, and this model has generated precise and testable predictions about how verbal stimuli should be encoded, maintained, and retrieved. One important line of work deriving from this model concerns the determinants of immediate serial recall for word lists, with particular attention to whether constraints on recall arise from the number of items in the short-term store or from the temporal duration of the items. The so-called word length effect, which refers to the reliably lower recall for lists composed of polysyllabic words compared to lists of monosyllabic words, is often cited as key evidence that short-term memory capacity is determined in part by time-based constraints: longer words take more time to subvocally rehearse and therefore more of the available time-based resource is consumed. This interpretation aligns with an account in which the phonological loop has a limited temporal span and the number of items that can be maintained is a function of their articulation time rather than an absolute item count.

Despite the intuitive appeal of a time-based account, the literature on word length and the determinants of serial recall has not been uniformly consistent. Some researchers have reported data clearly supporting a time-based limitation, such as experiments in which lists composed of shorter words are recalled more accurately than lists composed of longer words even when list length is held constant. Other researchers have raised challenges to this conclusion and have emphasized item-based constraints, suggesting that recall accuracy depends primarily on the number of distinct items held in short-term memory and not necessarily their temporal duration. On this item-based view, manipulations that change phonological or lexical properties could influence recall through mechanisms other than articulation time, such as increased confusability or differential encoding.

In addition to the debate over whether short-term verbal memory is primarily time-based or item-based, another important area of research concerns the impact of concurrent auditory stimuli on verbal short-term memory. The working memory model explicitly posits that the phonological loop is susceptible to interference from irrelevant auditory input: when environmental sounds include phonological information or fluctuation that impinges on the phonological store, the fidelity of representations maintained for later recall can be degraded. This auditory-verbal interference effect has been studied in a variety of contexts, and not all results point in the same direction. Some studies have suggested that auditory background stimuli—ranging from speech to complex music—can disrupt performance on verbal short-term memory tasks, in line with the idea that competing auditory information enters or corrupts the phonological store. Other studies, however, have found negligible or even facilitatory effects of background auditory stimulation, depending on the characteristics of the stimuli (for example, simple steady-state noise versus music with salient rhythmic or melodic structure) and on participant-level factors such as musical training and attentional control.

The mixed pattern of findings on the effects of music or background sound on recall creates an important gap in our understanding of how auditory interference interacts with the phonological loop’s putative time-based constraints. If the phonological loop operates under a strict time-based capacity, then background auditory input that shares phonological or temporal characteristics with the to-be-remembered verbal material should be expected to consume temporal capacity and thereby reduce recall accuracy. Conversely, if short-term memory operates more on an item-count basis, the presence of concurrent auditory stimuli might have a more limited or qualitatively different influence on performance, perhaps only interfering when those stimuli share lexical or semantic content with the to-be-remembered items.

A particularly informative approach to the interplay between word length effects and auditory interference is to examine recall performance in a factorial design that manipulates syllabic length (monosyllabic vs. polysyllabic words) together with the presence and type of background music (for example, instrumental vs. lyrical music, or no music). A design that crosses these manipulations can reveal not only whether each factor has a main effect on recall but also whether the effect of syllabic length depends on the auditory context. If, for instance, the disadvantage for polysyllabic words is magnified when background music is present, this would be consistent with the view that music consumes temporal phonological resources; if the disadvantage is unaffected by music, this would strengthen the claim that word length effects reflect internal time-based processes of rehearsal that are largely independent of external auditory context.

A number of early experiments addressed related questions. Baddeley and colleagues reported the original demonstrations of the word length effect and argued that the effect supported a time-based capacity limit in the phonological loop. Their procedure typically contrasted lists of short and long words and found superior immediate serial recall for short words even when list length was controlled, suggesting that the articulatory duration of items limits recall. In contrast, Craik and others have reported patterns of results that challenge a purely time-based account, arguing that item-based factors such as word frequency, semantic distinctiveness, or chunking ability can play decisive roles in determining recall. These opposing lines of work have contributed to the persisting debate regarding the primary constraints on short-term verbal memory.

Research on auditory interference has likewise yielded mixed results. Some investigations have shown that white noise or steady-state irrelevant sound can actually facilitate certain types of memory tasks by masking distracting environmental sounds or by increasing arousal, whereas other investigations have observed impairments in recall following the introduction of melodic or lyrical music that shares phonological content with the memoranda. The variability of these findings raises questions about the boundary conditions under which sound functions as an interfering stimulus versus a facilitator, and it suggests that the nature of the auditory input—its regularity, complexity, presence of speech, and congruence with the target material—may critically determine its effect.

Given these unresolved issues, the present study aimed to investigate the joint effects of syllabic structure of words and background music on immediate recall, motivated by the dual goals of testing the time-based hypothesis for the phonological loop and clarifying whether music impairs or facilitates recall in the context of lists of words. We sought to replicate and extend early work by systematically manipulating word length and background music within a single repeated-measures paradigm so that each participant experienced both monosyllabic and polysyllabic lists under conditions of no music, instrumental music, and lyrical music. The repeated-measures design was selected to increase statistical power and to allow direct within-subject comparisons of the effects of word length and auditory context.

Several theoretical predictions are possible. First, if the phonological loop operates under a time-based limitation as envisioned by Baddeley and colleagues, then monosyllabic words should be recalled more accurately than polysyllabic words across auditory conditions, producing a robust main effect of syllabic length. Second, if background music competes with the phonological store and consumes temporal rehearsal resources, one would predict a detrimental effect of music on immediate recall, especially for music that contains lyrical material due to lexical competition; this pattern would manifest as a main effect of auditory condition, with poorer recall during lyrical music compared to instrumental music or silence. Third, if background music specifically compounds the difficulty of recalling longer words due to their greater articulatory demands, one would expect an interaction such that the polysyllabic disadvantage is exacerbated in the presence of music.

Despite these clear expectations, prior inconsistent findings led us to anticipate the possibility of null or nuanced effects for music, such that music may have no influence on recall in the present task even though word length effects are present. Moreover, individual difference variables like musical training and baseline working memory capacity might moderate any effects of music, as musicians often show enhanced ability to attend to task-relevant information in the presence of background music, and individuals with higher working memory capacity might be more resilient to auditory distraction.

In formulating the present experiment, we also considered methodological issues that have complicated prior work. Differences in stimulus selection, the acoustic properties of music, volume levels, presence or absence of vocals, and whether the auditory stimuli were perceived as task-relevant or irrelevant can all alter the direction and magnitude of effects. To control for these concerns, we selected validated sets of monosyllabic and polysyllabic words matched on lexical frequency and other basic linguistic properties as far as possible, used standardized recordings of instrumental and lyrical music, maintained a moderate and constant sound level across musical conditions, and verified with participants that the music was experienced as background rather than as a task cue.

The present study therefore implemented a tightly controlled within-subjects experiment to test the competing hypotheses concerning time-based versus item-based constraints on short-term memory and to assess the effects of background music on recall accuracy. All participants completed a series of immediate serial recall trials involving lists of monosyllabic and polysyllabic words presented auditorily, under silence, instrumental music, and lyrical music conditions. Performance was scored in terms of proportion of correctly recalled words in their correct serial positions, and analyses focused on the main effects of word length and auditory condition, as well as their interaction. Exploratory analyses examined serial position effects and potential moderators such as musical training.

This study contributes to theory by providing a direct empirical test of whether a robust word length effect can be observed independently of auditory background and by clarifying whether music of different types systematically influences performance in an immediate verbal recall task. The pattern of results has the potential to inform the theoretical status of the phonological loop’s time-based hypothesis and to delineate the conditions under which auditory input produces interference versus no effect. In addition, the findings can have practical implications for environments where verbal learning must occur in the presence of background music, such as classrooms and workplaces.

Given the weight of theoretical arguments and the constraints of prior work, we hypothesised that monosyllabic words would be recalled more accurately than polysyllabic words, reflecting the time-based limit of the phonological loop. Regarding music, we adopted the cautious prediction that lyrical music would be more likely to impair recall than instrumental music or silence due to phonological and lexical competition; however, we acknowledged that instrumental music might have null or even mildly facilitative effects through arousal in some participants. Finally, we predicted no strong interactions unless music competed directly with articulatory rehearsal processes, in which case the polysyllabic disadvantage should be magnified during musical conditions.

Methods

Participants

A total of 48 undergraduate volunteers participated in the experiment for course credit and a small monetary incentive. The sample consisted of 32 females and 16 males, with an age range of 18 to 26 years and a mean age of 20.1 years. All participants were native speakers of the language in which the word lists were presented and reported normal hearing and no history of neurological disorders. Participants provided informed consent before participation and were debriefed at the conclusion of the experiment. The study protocol was approved by the departmental ethics committee in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Materials

Word lists were constructed from a pool of common monosyllabic and polysyllabic words. Each monosyllabic list contained eight monosyllabic nouns chosen to be highly familiar and to have similar lexical frequency. Each polysyllabic list comprised eight multisyllabic nouns with three syllables on average, selected to match the monosyllabic set on frequency and concreteness as closely as possible. Word selection attempted to minimize semantic clustering and phonological overlap that could confound recall due to similarity-based interference.

A total of 18 lists were created for the task, six lists for each auditory condition (no music, instrumental music, lyrical music). Within each auditory condition, three lists were composed of monosyllabic words and three lists of polysyllabic words. Word order within lists was randomized for each participant to control for item-specific serial position effects across participants. All words were recorded by a native speaker with neutral prosody using a high-quality microphone and digitized at 44.1 kHz. Each word's duration was measured and monosyllabic words averaged approximately 400 milliseconds, whereas polysyllabic words averaged approximately 900 milliseconds, consistent with the intended manipulation of articulatory time.

Musical stimuli comprised commercially produced recordings selected for their instrumental quality (an instrumental piece with a steady rhythm, no vocals) and lyrical quality (a pop song with clear English lyrics). Both musical stimuli were edited to eight-minute loops for the purpose of the experiment and normalized for loudness to a level of 65 dB SPL when presented through the laboratory headphones. The instrumental and lyrical tracks were matched as closely as possible on tempo and overall spectral properties to minimize confounding acoustic differences other than the presence of lyrics.

Apparatus

Stimuli were presented in a quiet laboratory testing room using a laptop computer running experimental control software. Participants listened through calibrated circumaural headphones and responses were collected on a response sheet and later entered into the database by the experimenter. Volume levels were kept constant across participants and conditions. The testing room had low ambient noise and was free from interruptions.

Procedure

The experiment used a within-subjects design in which each participant experienced all combinations of word length (monosyllabic, polysyllabic) and auditory condition (no music, instrumental music, lyrical music). Each participant completed 18 trials in total. Trials were blocked by auditory condition to reduce task-switching costs, and the order of auditory conditions was counterbalanced across participants using a Latin square design. Within each auditory block, the three trials (two of each word length type across the experiment) were randomized and presented with a short break between trials.

Each trial began with a two-second fixation tone to orient the participant. Subsequently, words were presented auditorily in immediate succession with an interstimulus interval of 250 milliseconds. A list always comprised eight words. Following the presentation of the last word, participants were cued to recall the list aloud in the serial order that they had heard the items; their verbal responses were recorded by the experimenter and later transcribed. Participants were given up to 30 seconds to complete their recall for each list. In the no-music condition, no sound other than the recorded words was played. In the instrumental and lyrical conditions, the background music began two seconds prior to the onset of the first word and continued through the recall period to ensure that music was present both during encoding and retrieval.

Participants were given practice trials at the beginning of the experiment to familiarize themselves with the task. During practice, participants completed two practice lists, one monosyllabic and one polysyllabic, both presented in silence. The practice allowed participants to ask questions and ensured they understood the requirement to recall words in serial order.

After completion of the experimental trials, participants completed a brief questionnaire assessing their self-reported musical training, familiarity with the musical stimuli, and perceived difficulty of the task across the auditory conditions. Participants rated the music on a 7-point scale for perceived distractibility, with higher scores indicating greater distraction. These self-report measures were included for exploratory analyses of potential moderators.

Scoring and dependent measures

Primary dependent measures included overall proportion of words correctly recalled in the correct serial position for each trial, hereafter referred to as serial position-accurate recall. Secondary measures included overall proportion of words recalled irrespective of position (item-level recall) and the distribution of recall across serial positions to assess primacy and recency patterns. Responses were transcribed by the experimenter and independently checked for accuracy. In scoring, exact matches to the target words were required; phonological variants that clearly represented the same lexical item were accepted, but semantically related substitutes were not.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. The principal analysis was a two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with word length (monosyllabic vs. polysyllabic) and auditory condition (no music, instrumental, lyrical) as within-subjects factors. Assumptions of normality were assessed via inspection of residuals and skewness statistics; sphericity for the three-level auditory factor was evaluated using Mauchly’s test, and when violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. Effect sizes are reported as partial eta squared for ANOVA effects and Cohen’s d for pairwise comparisons. Where appropriate, Bonferroni-corrected pairwise post hoc comparisons were performed. Exploratory correlational analyses examined relationships between musical training, perceived distractibility ratings, and recall performance.

Power considerations

A priori power calculations conducted using standard software indicated that a repeated-measures design with 48 participants would provide adequate power (0.80) to detect medium-sized effects (partial eta squared approximately .06) for within-subjects contrasts. The chosen sample size therefore balanced practical constraints and the need for sufficient statistical sensitivity to detect the theoretically important effects of word length and auditory condition.

Results

Descriptive overview

Data from all 48 participants were included in the primary analyses. The mean proportion of words correctly recalled in the correct serial position across all trials was 0.70 with a standard deviation of 0.13. When collapsed across auditory conditions, monosyllabic lists yielded a mean serial-accurate recall proportion of 0.78 (SD = 0.12), whereas polysyllabic lists yielded a mean of 0.62 (SD = 0.14). These descriptive values suggested a substantial advantage for monosyllabic words.

When collapsed across word length, average recall proportions for the three auditory conditions were as follows: no-music mean = 0.71 (SD = 0.13), instrumental music mean = 0.69 (SD = 0.14), and lyrical music mean = 0.69 (SD = 0.15). The small differences among these means suggested little effect of auditory condition on recall at the aggregate level.

Primary ANOVA

A two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with word length (monosyllabic vs. polysyllabic) and auditory condition (no music, instrumental, lyrical) as within-subjects factors and serial position-accurate recall proportion as the dependent variable. Mauchly’s test of sphericity for the auditory condition factor was not significant (p = .21), indicating that the sphericity assumption was met for the three-level factor and that Greenhouse-Geisser correction was not required.

The ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of word length, F(1,47) = 78.32, p < .001, partial eta squared = .625. This large effect indicated that monosyllabic words were recalled more accurately in the correct serial position than polysyllabic words, supporting a robust word length effect in the current data.

The main effect of auditory condition was not significant, F(2,94) = 1.02, p = .36, partial eta squared = .021, indicating no reliable difference in serial-accurate recall across the no-music, instrumental, and lyrical conditions. Planned pairwise comparisons between no-music and instrumental conditions, no-music and lyrical conditions, and instrumental and lyrical conditions were all non-significant after Bonferroni correction (smallest p = .27).

The interaction between word length and auditory condition was not significant, F(2,94) = 0.74, p = .48, partial eta squared = .015. This lack of interaction indicated that the word length effect was consistent across the three auditory conditions and that musical background did not differentially influence recall for monosyllabic versus polysyllabic words.

Post hoc and supplementary analyses

To complement the primary ANOVA, pairwise comparisons were conducted to examine the effect of word length within each auditory condition. In the no-music condition, monosyllabic lists were recalled at a mean of 0.80 (SD = 0.11) and polysyllabic lists at 0.63 (SD = 0.14). The difference was large and significant, t(47) = 9.02, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.30. In the instrumental music condition, monosyllabic mean = 0.77 (SD = 0.12) and polysyllabic mean = 0.61 (SD = 0.13), t(47) = 8.21, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.19. In the lyrical music condition, monosyllabic mean = 0.77 (SD = 0.14) and polysyllabic mean = 0.62 (SD = 0.15), t(47) = 8.83, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 1.28. These significant within-condition differences confirm the robustness of the word length effect across auditory settings.

Item-level recall (proportion of words recalled irrespective of serial position) showed a similar pattern. Monosyllabic lists yielded higher item-level recall (M = 0.84, SD = 0.11) than polysyllabic lists (M = 0.73, SD = 0.13), t(47) = 6.42, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.93. No reliable main effect of auditory condition was observed on item-level recall, F(2,94) = 0.86, p = .43.

Assumption checks

Residuals from the ANOVA were inspected for normality and showed acceptable approximation to normality with skewness within ±1.0 for all conditions. There were no extreme outliers; the largest standardized residual did not exceed ±3.0. Given the robustness of repeated-measures ANOVA to slight departures from normality in samples of this size, the inference from the ANOVA is considered reliable.

Serial position analysis

Analyses of recall as a function of serial position revealed classic primacy and recency effects. For monosyllabic lists, the first three serial positions showed higher recall probabilities compared to middle positions, and the final two positions showed elevated recall indicative of recency. For polysyllabic lists, primacy and recency effects were present but attenuated in magnitude relative to the monosyllabic condition. A repeated-measures ANOVA with serial position (1–8) and word length as factors confirmed a significant main effect of serial position, F(7,329) = 42.55, p < .001, and a significant serial position by word length interaction, F(7,329) = 3.16, p = .003, indicating that the serial position curve differed between monosyllabic and polysyllabic items, with the monosyllabic condition showing relatively larger primacy and recency advantages.

Correlation and exploratory analyses

Exploratory correlational analyses examined whether self-reported musical training was associated with differential susceptibility to background music. Musical training was rated on a 5-point scale, and the correlation between musical training and the difference in recall accuracy between lyrical music and silence was weak and non-significant (r = .08, p = .59). Perceived distractibility ratings for the lyrical music were moderately correlated with self-reported distractibility (r = .42, p = .003), but perceived distractibility did not predict the magnitude of the word length effect (r = .11, p = .44). These analyses suggest that, in the present sample, individual differences in musical training and subjective distractibility did not substantially moderate the main outcomes.

Bayesian analysis

To further quantify evidence for the null effect of auditory condition, a Bayesian repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted. The Bayes factor comparing models with and without the auditory condition factor indicated that the data were approximately four times more likely under the model without an auditory effect than under the model including it (BF10 for model with auditory condition relative to null ≈ 0.25), providing moderate evidence in favor of no main auditory effect.

Summary of results

In summary, the experiment produced a robust and large effect of word length: monosyllabic words were recalled with substantially higher accuracy than polysyllabic words. There was no significant main effect of auditory condition and no interaction between word length and auditory condition. The pattern of serial position effects further confirmed that the advantage for monosyllabic words was manifest across serial positions, and that memory for polysyllabic words showed attenuated primacy and recency. Exploratory analyses did not identify reliable moderators among musical training or perceived distractibility. Overall, the results support a strong time-based constraint on immediate serial recall, and they show no evidence that background music, whether instrumental or lyrical, impaired or facilitated recall in this task.

Discussion

The present experiment investigated the joint effects of word syllabic length and background music on immediate verbal serial recall. The primary finding was a robust word length effect: monosyllabic words were recalled far more accurately than polysyllabic words across all auditory conditions. In contrast, background music—whether instrumental or lyrical—had no reliable main effect on recall accuracy, and it did not interact with word length. These outcomes have several implications for theories of short-term verbal memory, for the role of the phonological loop, and for the situational impact of background music on memory performance.

First, the unambiguous advantage for monosyllabic over polysyllabic words supports the time-based account of short-term memory capacity articulated by Baddeley and colleagues. The present data are consistent with the idea that the phonological loop has a limited temporal storage capacity and that longer words, which take more time to subvocally articulate, consume more of that capacity and therefore reduce the number of items that can be accurately maintained and retrieved in serial order. The analogous effect at both the serially accurate and item-level measures further suggests that the effect is not merely an artifact of positional assignment but reflects genuine differences in retention associated with articulatory duration.

Second, the lack of effect of background music in the present study is noteworthy in light of prior mixed findings. If background auditory input operates by intruding upon or degrading the phonological store, then one might expect that the presence of music, especially lyrical music with phonological content, would impair recall. Several plausible accounts can explain why such effects were not observed. One possibility is that the musical stimuli used in the present task, though audible, were not sufficiently salient or contextually relevant to enter the phonological store as competing representations. The music may have been processed largely at a perceptual level without imposing lexical or phonological representations that directly compete with the maintenance of list items. Alternatively, participants may have effectively filtered the music as irrelevant background and engaged attentional control processes that prevented the music from interfering with rehearsal; such strategies would render music less disruptive to the phonological loop.

Third, the present findings suggest that time-based constraints on short-term memory can be robust and operate independently of background auditory context under these testing conditions. The consistent word length effect across silence, instrumental music, and lyrical music indicates that articulatory duration remains a dominant determinant of immediate serial recall. This pattern argues against a strong interaction in which background music consumes articulatory or phonological capacity to a degree that differentially affects longer versus shorter words; rather, the internal rehearsal processes appear to be able to proceed in the presence of background music at least when music is present at the levels and types used here.

Fourth, the null effect of music and its lack of interaction with syllabic length raise questions about the specific characteristics of auditory stimuli that produce interference. Past studies that have observed deleterious effects of music or background speech often used stimuli with high informational content, personal relevance, or volume levels that captured attention. In contrast, the present study used a controlled loudness level and asked participants to treat music as background; participants’ post-experiment ratings indicated that most did perceive the music as extraneous rather than task-related. Therefore, the boundary between sound that is intrusive and sound that can be ignored may depend on perceptual salience and task demands as well as on the extent to which the sound contains phonological structure that maps onto the phonological store.

Fifth, the presence of classic serial position effects in both monosyllabic and polysyllabic conditions demonstrates that not only is overall recall affected by word length but the shape of the serial position curve is also subtly modulated by articulatory duration. Specifically, the larger primacy and recency advantages for monosyllabic items point to more effective allocation of encoding and rehearsal resources for shorter items, possibly because each item consumes less rehearsal time and therefore more cycles of rehearsal can be applied across the list. The attenuated primacy and recency in the polysyllabic condition reflect the limits imposed by longer articulatory duration that constrains both initial encoding depth and the ability to refresh items.

Sixth, the absence of moderation by musical training and perceived distractibility in exploratory analyses suggests that, at least in this sample, individual differences did not strongly influence susceptibility to background music. It remains possible that other individual difference measures, such as baseline working memory capacity, trait attentional control, or specific familiarity with the musical pieces, might moderate the impact of music; these factors were not extensively measured here and should be examined in future work.

Seventh, the experimental design and findings bear on the theoretical debate about time-based versus item-based accounts of short-term memory. The robust word length effect is difficult to reconcile with a purely item-based capacity limit that is agnostic about temporal duration, since item-based theories would predict that lists of the same number of items should be recalled similarly regardless of syllable count, assuming equivalently confusable items. The present data therefore provide supportive evidence for time-based constraints, though they do not preclude contributions from item-based factors under other circumstances. For instance, in tasks where word frequency, semantic clustering, or phonological similarity vary dramatically, item-based processes may exert stronger influence.

Eighth, several methodological considerations should be acknowledged. Despite careful matching of word sets on frequency and concreteness, perfect equivalence across monosyllabic and polysyllabic words is difficult to achieve; residual lexical differences could account for some portion of the observed effect, although the large effect size and alignment with articulatory duration make a purely lexical explanation unlikely. Furthermore, the sample size, while sufficient to detect medium effects, may not have been large enough to detect very small influences of music, particularly if such influences were highly variable across participants. The choice of music stimuli—one instrumental and one lyrical—provides a useful but limited sample of possible background sounds, and different genres, languages, or louder volumes may yield different outcomes.

Ninth, the implications of the findings extend beyond laboratory theory to applied settings. The absence of a general disruptive effect of moderate-level background music on short-term verbal recall suggests that, for tasks involving immediate recall of short lists, moderate instrumental or lyrical music may not impair performance for most individuals. This has potential relevance for situations in which people study or work in environments with background music; however, caution is warranted in generalizing to more complex or prolonged learning tasks, to material that is more semantically rich, or to contexts in which the music is personally salient.

Tenth, the study suggests several fruitful avenues for future research. One promising direction is systematic variation of the acoustic and informational properties of background sounds to determine the specific features that lead to interference. For example, experiments could vary the presence of speech-like phonological content independently of musicality, manipulate the complexity and predictability of rhythms, or titrate the signal-to-noise ratio of music relative to the to-be-remembered words. Another approach would be to directly manipulate articulatory suppression to test whether preventing rehearsal abolishes the word length effect under different auditory backgrounds; such a manipulation would provide stronger causal evidence regarding whether the word length effect arises from rehearsal-related temporal processes.

Further research could also examine the time course of interference by manipulating whether music is present during encoding only, during maintenance only, or during retrieval only. This would illuminate whether music primarily affects encoding, maintenance, or retrieval processes. In addition, neurophysiological measures such as EEG or fMRI could be used to investigate the neural substrates of any interference between background music and phonological rehearsal processes, potentially revealing whether music engages the same phonological or attention-related networks.

It is also important to investigate how individual differences modulate these effects more thoroughly. Larger samples that include reliable measures of working memory capacity, attentional control, auditory sensitivity, and musical expertise would enable tests of plausible moderators. For instance, highly trained musicians might show reduced vulnerability to musical interference, or individuals with higher auditory working memory capacity might be able to maintain rehearsal while concurrently processing background music.

Limitations of the current study include the use of laboratory-selected word lists that may not generalize to more naturalistic verbal materials, the relatively brief nature of the task which may not capture effects that accrue over longer learning periods, and the use of a single set of musical exemplars that cannot represent the full diversity of music encountered in everyday life. Future studies should broaden the stimulus set and assess longer-term retention as well as immediate recall.

In conclusion, the present experiment provides clear evidence that word length, operationalized as syllabic duration, exerts a strong influence on immediate serial recall, consistent with time-based constraints of the phonological loop. At the same time, moderate-level background music, whether instrumental or lyrical, did not significantly impair or facilitate recall in this paradigm, and it did not interact with word length. These results suggest that internal rehearsal processes governing the short-term retention of verbal material can be robust to certain types of background auditory stimulation and that articulatory duration remains a primary determinant of immediate serial recall. Further research that manipulates the characteristics of background sound and that explores individual differences will be valuable in refining theoretical accounts of phonological maintenance and in identifying the contexts in which auditory input does or does not disrupt verbal short-term memory.
