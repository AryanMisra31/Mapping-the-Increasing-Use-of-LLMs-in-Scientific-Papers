Introduction

Stress is a common and multifaceted experience that has implications for a wide range of health behaviors. Stress can be conceptualized in multiple ways, including as external exposure to stressful events and as the subjective perception or appraisal of those events. Researchers have long been interested in how stress relates to eating behaviors, and the relationship appears to be complex and sometimes paradoxical. Some individuals report decreases in appetite during stress, whereas others report increased appetite and a greater urge to eat. There are theoretical reasons to expect that both the exposure to stressors and the subjective perception of stress will be related to indices of increased eating, but the pathways for these associations are likely distinct. Exposure to stressors provides objective context for potential changes in behavior, while perception of stress captures the cognitive-emotional response that can drive eating as a coping mechanism. Understanding the relative contributions of exposure and perception is thus important for clarifying the mechanisms that link stress and hunger-related experiences.

The literature includes laboratory studies that expose participants to acute stressors and then measure changes in food intake, appetite, or related hormones. These studies often manipulate social-evaluative threat or time-pressure tasks and then monitor consumption in ad libitum snack paradigms. Many of these investigations have found increases in palatable food consumption following acute stressors, particularly for high-fat and high-sugar items. Conversely, some laboratory studies have observed decreases in intake under stress, a pattern that appears more common for individuals who experience marked physiological arousal or who have particular temperamental profiles. These mixed laboratory findings have encouraged researchers to examine both situational exposure and subjective experience, as well as individual difference moderators such as baseline dietary restraint, emotional eating tendencies, and sex.

Beyond the laboratory, ecological and survey research has documented associations between everyday stress and eating-related outcomes. Diary studies and ecological momentary assessment (EMA) methods have shown that moments of higher perceived stress are often accompanied by cravings, snacking, or overeating episodes later in the day. Cross-sectional surveys have similarly reported correlations between perceived stress and measures of emotional eating, uncontrolled eating, and perceived increases in appetite. However, cross-sectional approaches are limited in their ability to disentangle directionality and to account for the temporal dynamics by which stressors translate into changes in hunger and eating regulation. Yet the consistency of the association across methodologies suggests that perceived stress is a meaningful correlate of self-reported urges to eat.

Physiological mechanisms have been proposed to connect stress and hunger. Activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis under stress increases circulating cortisol, and cortisol has been implicated in appetite regulation and fat distribution, with chronic elevations sometimes linked to increased consumption of energy-dense foods. Additionally, stress-induced shifts in neuropeptides and gut hormones, including ghrelin and leptin, have been proposed as mechanisms that may alter hunger sensations. Ghrelin, in particular, has been discussed as a hormone that can increase appetite in response to stress-related cues. These physiological changes may interact with psychological processes involved in reward sensitivity and cognitive control, making it easier for stress to trigger eating as a coping or reward-seeking behavior.

Cognitive and emotional mechanisms are also central to accounts of stress-related eating. Stress can impair executive function and reduce inhibitory control, rendering individuals less able to maintain dietary goals when confronted with palatable foods. The subjective perception of stress can elicit negative affect, and eating can function as a short-term emotion regulation strategy. Emotional eating frameworks posit that some individuals habitually turn to food to manage distress, which can produce a reinforcing cycle of temporary relief followed by guilt and further dysregulated eating. The interplay among negative affect, cognitive control deficits, and reward sensitivity provides a rich account of how subjective stress perception might escalate urges to eat and undermine efforts to control intake.

Individual differences moderate the extent to which stress leads to increased hunger and poor eating control. For example, individuals with higher trait emotional eating or those scoring high on measures of dietary disinhibition are more likely to respond to stress with increased consumption. Conversely, individuals with high dietary restraint may report more fluctuation, sometimes exhibiting reduced intake under acute stress but rebound overeating later. Sex differences have also been observed in some studies, with women often reporting stronger links between stress and eating-related urges than men, although findings are not entirely consistent. Other moderators that have been considered include body mass index (BMI), sleep quality, and habitual stress exposure, all of which may interact with acute stress responses to shape eating behavior.

Measurement considerations are important in this research area. Exposure to stressors can be assessed via checklists that tally stressful events or through diary methods that capture discrete incidents. Perceived stress is commonly measured with instruments that assess subjective feelings of stress, worry, and inability to cope. Eating-related outcomes range from objective measures of intake to self-reported indices such as frequency of snacking, feelings of increased appetite, perceived loss of control, and unsuccessful attempts to control eating. It is valuable to include multiple indices of hunger and urge to eat, including both immediate feelings of hunger and broader reports of difficulty regulating eating, to capture the multifaceted nature of stress-related eating.

Despite considerable progress, there remain gaps in our understanding of the relative roles of stress exposure and perceived stress in relation to hunger and attempts at eating control. Many studies focus on either objective stressors or subjective perception, but relatively few have examined both concurrently to determine their unique contributions. Establishing whether exposure and perception independently predict hunger-related indices would help clarify targets for intervention. For example, if perceived stress shows a stronger association with urges to eat than objective exposure, interventions aimed at appraisal and coping might be particularly effective at reducing stress-related overeating. Conversely, if exposure shows robust associations above and beyond perception, this might highlight the need for environmental or situational strategies.

Another gap is the focus on diverse samples, with fewer studies conducted in university student populations in the United Kingdom compared to some other contexts. University students are an important group given the high prevalence of stressors related to academic demands, social pressures, and life transitions, and because eating patterns established during this period often persist into adulthood. Investigating stress and hunger in a student sample can therefore provide insights with practical relevance for campus health services and the design of targeted support programs.

The present study addresses these gaps by examining both exposure to stressors and perceived stress in relation to a composite set of hunger-related indices in a sample of university students. The sample comprises students from the University of Warwick, a population that experiences a range of academic and social stressors. Our primary objectives are to determine whether exposure to stressful events and subjective perception of stress are correlated with indices reflecting a greater urge to eat, including self-reported increased eating, feelings of hunger, and reports of more unsuccessful attempts to control eating. A secondary objective is to examine whether exposure and perception together account for variance in hunger-related outcomes when considered in regression models.

Based on the theoretical and empirical considerations outlined above, we formulated two hypotheses. First, we hypothesized that both stress exposure and perceived stress would be positively correlated with indices of greater urge to eat, including feelings of increased eating, hunger, and unproductive attempts to control eating. Second, we hypothesized that when considered together in regression analyses, exposure and perception would each make unique contributions to predicting hunger-related indices, consistent with the idea that objective stressors and subjective appraisals are distinct predictors of eating-related outcomes. The testing of these hypotheses in a sample of 60 University of Warwick students provides a focused examination of these relationships and aims to contribute to our understanding of how stress translates into alterations in appetite and eating regulation.

Methods

Participants were recruited from the student population at the University of Warwick over a six-week period in the middle of the academic term. The target sample size was set at 60 participants to provide a balance between feasibility and statistical power for detecting moderate associations in correlational and regression analyses. Inclusion criteria required that participants be currently enrolled as students at the University of Warwick and aged 18 years or older. Exclusion criteria included reporting a current diagnosis of an eating disorder or a medical condition that overtly alters appetite, such as active gastrointestinal disease, in order to limit confounding influences on hunger reporting. Recruitment took place through campus flyers, online student bulletin boards, and classroom announcements, and interested students were directed to an online participant information sheet and consent form.

A total of 60 students consented and completed the study. Demographic information was collected and included age, sex, year of study, and self-reported height and weight for body mass index (BMI) calculation. The final sample included a range of undergraduate and postgraduate students. The sample was composed of individuals who reflected the typical diversity of the campus; however, the study did not attempt to create a nationally representative sample. Participants received course credit or a small monetary voucher as compensation for their time, and all procedures were approved by the University of Warwick’s institutional ethics review board. Data collection adhered to ethical standards, and participants were reminded that they could withdraw at any point without penalty.

The study used a cross-sectional survey design administered online. After completing informed consent, participants filled out a battery of questionnaires assessing stress exposure, perceived stress, and hunger-related outcomes, along with a brief demographic questionnaire. The administration was self-paced, and participants could complete the survey on a desktop or mobile device. The overall duration for survey completion was approximately 20 to 30 minutes. Efforts were made to ensure that participants understood each item, and standard validated scales were used where applicable to enhance the reliability and interpretability of the measures.

Stress exposure was assessed using a checklist approach that asked participants to indicate the occurrence of common stressful events in the past month. The checklist included items reflecting academic pressures (such as exams or heavy coursework), interpersonal stressors (such as conflicts or relationship break-ups), financial troubles, and other typical student stressors. Each endorsed item was summed to create a stress exposure score, with higher scores indicating a greater number of stressful exposures. This approach captures objective elements of the participants’ recent environments and complements the subjective measure of stress perception included in the survey.

Perceived stress was measured using a brief adapted version of a widely used perceived stress instrument that assesses individuals’ appraisal of stress, overwhelm, and coping over a recent time frame. Participants rated items on a Likert scale indicating how often they experienced various stress-related feelings, and scores were summed to produce a perceived stress score. Higher scores reflected higher subjective experience of stress and a greater sense of being unable to cope. The perceived stress measure was selected for its focus on appraisal and subjective emotional response rather than on specific events, making it a useful complement to the exposure checklist.

Hunger-related outcomes were assessed using a composite approach that targeted multiple dimensions of the urge to eat. First, participants responded to items assessing feelings of increased eating, indicating whether they had felt a stronger desire to eat than usual during the past month. Second, participants answered items about subjective hunger, indicating the frequency and intensity of hunger sensations. Third, participants completed items about attempts to control eating that they perceived as unproductive or unsuccessful, such as repeated dieting attempts followed by lapses. These items were drawn from established self-report instruments that measure uncontrolled eating and unsuccessful control efforts and were recalibrated to fit the context of the present study. Responses were recorded on Likert-type scales and combined into an overall hunger-related composite score for primary analyses.

In addition to the primary measures, participants provided information about current dieting attempts and any known medical conditions that could affect appetite. Participants also reported whether they had recently consumed a meal or snack prior to taking the survey, as immediate recent intake could influence reported hunger. This variable was recorded to allow for sensitivity analyses that controlled for recent eating. The inclusion of these additional covariates enabled a more nuanced examination of the relationship between stress and hunger-related reports.

Data screening and preparation occurred prior to the primary analyses. Survey responses were inspected for missing data and implausible values. Cases with substantial missing data on the primary variables were removed from analyses. For the remaining cases, any isolated missing item responses were handled using mean substitution within the respective scale when appropriate and when missingness was minimal. The distributions of the primary variables were examined for normality using skewness and kurtosis statistics and visual inspection of histograms. Where necessary, transformations were considered, but primary analyses relied on raw scores because distributions were within acceptable ranges for parametric tests.

Statistical analyses were conducted using standard software. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and ranges, were computed for all variables. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate the bivariate relationships among stress exposure, perceived stress, and the hunger-related composite score. These correlations provided an initial test of the first hypothesis that both exposure and perception would be positively associated with indices of a greater urge to eat.

To further assess the unique contributions of exposure and perception, multiple linear regression analyses were performed with the hunger-related composite score as the dependent variable and stress exposure and perceived stress as simultaneous predictors. The regression models included relevant covariates in secondary analyses, such as age, sex, BMI, current dieting status, and recent intake, to evaluate the robustness of the findings. Model assumptions for the regression analyses were checked, including linearity, independence of residuals, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals, and multicollinearity among predictors. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were computed to examine potential multicollinearity.

In addition to the primary regression analyses, exploratory analyses examined whether sex moderated the association between stress and the hunger-related composite score by including interaction terms between sex and each stress measure. These exploratory tests were intended to determine whether the relationships differed in magnitude for male and female participants. Given the sample size and limited power for complex interactions, findings from moderation tests were interpreted cautiously.

Effect sizes were reported alongside p-values to provide information about the magnitude of the associations. For correlations, effect sizes were interpreted using conventional benchmarks for small, medium, and large effects. For regression analyses, standardized beta coefficients and R-squared values were emphasized to indicate the relative influence of predictors and the proportion of variance explained. All statistical tests used a two-tailed alpha level of .05 as the threshold for statistical significance. Results were presented with an eye toward clarity and transparency, including both statistically significant and non-significant findings.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the sample were computed first to provide context for the primary analyses. The final sample consisted of 60 students whose ages ranged from 18 to 28 years. The mean age of participants was approximately 20.9 years with a standard deviation of 2.6 years. The sample included both male and female students, and a small number of participants who reported non-binary gender identities. The majority of participants were undergraduate students in the first three years of study, and a smaller portion were postgraduate students. Self-reported BMIs fell within a typical range for a university sample, with a mean BMI of approximately 23.1 and a standard deviation of 3.5. Current dieting status was reported by a minority of participants, and a small subset reported recent meal consumption directly prior to taking the survey.

Scores on the stress exposure checklist indicated variability in the number of stressors experienced in the past month. The mean exposure score was moderate, suggesting that most participants experienced at least one or two common student stressors in the recent period. The range extended from zero stressors to multiple stressors, reflecting heterogeneity in participants’ recent life contexts. Perceived stress scores also varied across participants, with some reporting low levels of perceived stress and others reporting higher levels consistent with feeling overwhelmed or having difficulty coping. The mean perceived stress score fell within a middle range, indicating that the sample overall experienced a typical distribution of perceived stress for a university population during term time.

Hunger-related composite scores similarly displayed variation. Some participants reported minimal increases in appetite or few unsuccessful control attempts, whereas others reported frequent feelings of increased eating, stronger sensations of hunger, and repeated unproductive attempts to control eating. The mean hunger-related composite score indicated a modest average level of hunger-related responses in the sample. No ceiling or floor effects were evident, which supported the suitability of these variables for correlation and regression analyses.

Bivariate Pearson correlations were computed to test the first hypothesis that both stress exposure and perceived stress would be positively correlated with indices of a greater urge to eat. Stress exposure was significantly and positively correlated with the hunger-related composite score, indicating that participants who reported more recent stressors tended to report greater feelings of increased eating, more frequent hunger sensations, and more unsuccessful attempts to control their eating. The correlation between stress exposure and the hunger composite was in the moderate range, suggesting a meaningful association. Perceived stress also showed a significant positive correlation with the hunger-related composite score, and this correlation was somewhat larger than that observed for exposure, consistent with the idea that subjective appraisal may play a sizable role in driving urges to eat. Both correlations were statistically significant at the conventional alpha level, supporting the first hypothesis.

To quantify the unique contributions of exposure and perception, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted with the hunger-related composite score as the dependent variable and stress exposure and perceived stress as simultaneous predictors. The overall regression model was statistically significant and explained a notable proportion of variance in the hunger composite. Both stress exposure and perceived stress made significant unique contributions to the model, with standardized beta coefficients indicating that perceived stress had a somewhat larger unique association compared to exposure when both were included. These findings supported the second hypothesis that exposure and perception would each contribute independently to the prediction of hunger-related outcomes.

Model diagnostics were examined to ensure the validity of the regression results. Residual plots showed no clear violations of linearity or homoscedasticity, and the distribution of residuals approximated normality. Variance inflation factor values for the predictors were within acceptable limits, indicating that multicollinearity was not a serious concern despite the conceptual relatedness of exposure and perception. Cook’s distance and other influence diagnostics did not reveal any single case exerting undue influence on the regression estimates. These diagnostics increased confidence in the interpretability of the regression findings.

To further evaluate the robustness of the associations, secondary regression models included covariates such as age, sex, BMI, current dieting status, and recent intake. When these covariates were entered, the pattern of results for stress exposure and perceived stress remained consistent. Both predictors continued to show significant associations with the hunger composite, and the effect sizes for exposure and perception were attenuated only modestly by the inclusion of covariates. The covariates themselves contributed little unique variance, with the possible exception of dieting status which showed a small association with hunger-related reports in the expected direction.

Exploratory moderation analyses tested whether sex moderated the associations between stress measures and hunger-related outcomes by including interaction terms in regression models. Interaction terms between sex and stress exposure and between sex and perceived stress were not statistically significant, suggesting that the observed associations did not differ substantially between male and female participants in this sample. Given the modest sample size and limited power to detect interactions, these null moderation findings should be interpreted conservatively.

Additional exploratory correlations were calculated between individual components of the hunger composite and the stress measures to better understand the pattern of associations. Perceived stress was significantly correlated with all three component indices—reported increased eating, subjective hunger, and unproductive attempts to control eating—suggesting a broad relationship across multiple facets of hunger-related experience. Stress exposure was also correlated with each component, albeit with variability in effect size; the strongest exposure association emerged with reports of increased eating, and associations with subjective hunger and control attempts were somewhat smaller but still positive and significant.

Effect sizes were interpreted alongside significance tests. The correlations between perceived stress and the hunger composite approached what would conventionally be considered a medium effect size, indicating a practically meaningful relationship. Similarly, the regression model’s R-squared value indicated that exposure and perception together accounted for a sizable minority of the variance in the hunger composite, implying that stress-related variables are important but not exhaustive determinants of hunger-related experiences. These effect sizes align with prior literature that identifies stress as a relevant, though not singular, influence on eating-related outcomes.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine whether recent food intake influenced the pattern of results. When the subset of participants who reported having eaten within 30 minutes of survey completion was removed, the primary correlations and regression coefficients remained statistically significant and of similar magnitude. This indicated that acute recent intake did not drive the primary findings. Similarly, excluding participants who reported current dieting did not substantially alter the results, though the strength of associations was slightly reduced in the smaller subsample.

Taken together, the results provided consistent evidence that both exposure to stressors and subjective perception of stress are positively associated with indices of a greater urge to eat among University of Warwick students. The combined evidence from correlational and regression analyses supports the study’s hypotheses and suggests that both objective circumstances and subjective appraisals play meaningful and partially independent roles in driving hunger-related experiences. The pattern of associations across individual hunger components further underscored the broad relationship between stress and various manifestations of increased appetite and control difficulties.

Discussion

The findings of this study provide convergent evidence that stress exposure and perceived stress are each linked with indices of a greater urge to eat among university students, thereby contributing to the literature by demonstrating that objective stressors and subjective appraisals can both be important determinants of hunger-related experiences. The study’s results are consistent with prior experimental, diary, and cross-sectional research suggesting that stress can promote increased consumption and feelings of hunger, and they extend this literature by examining exposure and perception concurrently in a student sample. This approach allows for a more nuanced interpretation of the pathways by which stress relates to appetite and self-regulation of eating, and it highlights the potential utility of targeting both environmental stressors and cognitive-emotional appraisals in interventions.

One central implication of these findings is that interventions aimed at reducing stress-related overeating may benefit from addressing both external stressors and subjective stress appraisals. For some students, reducing exposure to predictable academic stressors through improved time management, workload restructuring, or institutional supports may alleviate one source of eating-related vulnerability. For others, or in addition, cognitive-behavioral strategies that target appraisal processes, coping, and emotional regulation may reduce the extent to which stress translates into urges to eat. The present results show that perceived stress had a somewhat larger association with the hunger composite than exposure when both were included in regression models, which suggests that incorporating elements that reshape subjective experience or improve coping skills could be particularly valuable.

The observed independent contributions of exposure and perception to hunger-related indices also have theoretical implications. They suggest that stress-related eating is not solely a function of encountering more stressors, nor is it solely a function of how stress is perceived; rather, it is the combination of objective pressures and the subjective reaction to those pressures that shapes urges to eat and difficulties controlling eating. This dual-pathway perspective aligns with biopsychosocial models of stress responses and supports integrative frameworks that consider both situational factors and appraisal-related processes. The present findings thus support models that emphasize the role of cognitive-emotional processes in translating environmental stress into health-relevant behaviors.

Mechanistically, several pathways could account for the associations observed in this study. Subjective stress may promote eating via increased negative affect and reward-seeking behavior; eating palatable foods can provide immediate hedonic relief, and this reinforcement could lead to stronger urges to eat under stress. Physiologically, both acute and chronic stress can activate the HPA axis and alter appetite-regulatory hormones such as cortisol and ghrelin, which in turn can promote hunger and enhance the rewarding properties of food. In situations characterized by frequent stress exposure, repeated activation of these physiological mechanisms could result in sustained increases in appetite or a pattern of stress-related grazing and snacking. Additionally, stress can impair cognitive control and executive functioning, making it more difficult to adhere to dietary intentions and leading to more unsuccessful attempts to control eating, as reflected in the present hunger-related composite.

The results also align with individual difference research that indicates variability in stress-eating relationships. Although the present sample did not provide strong evidence for sex-based moderation, which may in part reflect limited power to detect interaction effects, the pattern of correlations suggests that stress correlates with multiple facets of eating-related experience. It remains likely that traits such as emotional eating tendencies, dietary restraint, impulsivity, and reward sensitivity moderate the magnitude and direction of stress-eating associations in larger and more diverse samples. Future research with larger samples could examine these moderators more definitively and identify subgroups of students who are particularly vulnerable.

Methodologically, the study contributes by using a composite hunger-related measure that captured feelings of increased eating, subjective hunger, and unproductive attempts to control eating. This multifaceted approach enhances ecological validity by acknowledging that stress-related eating manifests in both immediate sensations and in broader patterns of attempted self-regulation. The finding that both exposure and perception correlated with each component of the composite underscores the pervasiveness of stress effects across different expressions of hunger and control challenges.

Several limitations should be acknowledged and considered when interpreting the findings. First and foremost, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Although the study demonstrates associations between stress variables and hunger-related indices, it cannot determine whether stress causes increased hunger, whether greater hunger or eating problems lead to higher perceived stress, or whether bidirectional effects operate. Longitudinal and experimental designs are necessary to establish temporal and causal pathways. Second, reliance on self-report measures raises concerns about reporting biases, common method variance, and the accuracy of retrospective accounts of stress exposure and hunger. While validated instruments were used and participants reported on a recent timeframe to minimize recall bias, future research would benefit from incorporating objective or behavioral measures, such as ecological momentary assessment of momentary stress and eating episodes, or laboratory-based measures of intake and physiological indices.

Third, the sample consisted exclusively of students from a single university, and the sample size was modest. Although the sample was appropriate for detecting moderate effects and the results were consistent across analyses, the generalizability of the findings to other populations, such as older adults, non-student populations, or clinical groups, remains to be established. Replication in larger and more diverse samples is necessary to determine the robustness of the observed relationships and to permit more definitive tests of moderators and mediators.

Fourth, the stress exposure measure relied on a checklist of common stressors rather than a more detailed assessment of stressor severity, chronicity, or timing. Different types of stressors may have unique relations with eating, and future research could disentangle the effects of acute versus chronic stress, or interpersonal versus academic stressors, on hunger and self-regulation. Similarly, the perceived stress measure assessed recent appraisals but did not capture specific cognitive or emotional processes in depth; further research could consider more granular measures of appraisal, rumination, and coping styles.

Fifth, physiological mediators such as cortisol or ghrelin were not measured in the present study, leaving the biological mechanisms speculative. Incorporating biomarker assessments in future studies would clarify whether stress-related hunger reports correspond with measurable endocrine changes and could identify physiological pathways that are amenable to targeted interventions.

Despite these limitations, the study’s findings have practical implications for interventions aimed at promoting healthier eating behaviors in student populations. Campus-based programs that combine stress reduction techniques, such as mindfulness or cognitive-behavioral stress management, with skills training in appetite regulation and planning for healthy meals and snacks may be particularly effective. Interventions that help students reappraise stressors, reduce rumination, and improve problem-focused coping could reduce perceived stress and thereby diminish stress-related impulses to eat. Additionally, organizational and policy-level changes that address academic workload and provide supports for students during high-stress periods may reduce exposure to stressors and indirectly reduce stress-related eating.

Looking forward, several promising avenues for future research emerge from the present work. Longitudinal studies that track students across the academic year could elucidate temporal dynamics and identify periods of heightened vulnerability, such as exam weeks. Ecological momentary assessment combined with wearable sensors could capture real-time fluctuations in stress, affect, physiological arousal, and eating episodes, allowing for a fine-grained analysis of immediate antecedents and consequences. Experimental studies that manipulate appraisal or coping strategies could test causal hypotheses about whether changing subjective experience alters subsequent urges to eat and actual intake. Adding physiological measures would enable tests of biological mediation and interactions between physiological and psychological pathways.

Further, research that examines moderating factors such as emotional eating trait, dietary restraint, impulsivity, sleep quality, and substance use could provide a more personalized understanding of who is most at risk for stress-related eating. Intervention trials that compare the efficacy of environmental, cognitive-behavioral, and combined approaches for reducing stress-related overeating would be particularly valuable from a translational perspective. Finally, qualitative research exploring students’ subjective experiences of stress and eating could enrich quantitative findings and suggest novel intervention targets rooted in lived experience.

In conclusion, the present study contributes to an accumulating body of evidence indicating that both exposure to stressors and subjective perception of stress are associated with increased urges to eat and difficulties in controlling eating. The findings underscore the importance of addressing both environmental and appraisal-related contributors to stress-related eating, particularly in university student populations. By highlighting the independent and joint roles of exposure and perception, this work points toward integrated prevention and intervention strategies that can reduce the burden of stress-related dysregulated eating and promote healthier coping and dietary practices among students.
