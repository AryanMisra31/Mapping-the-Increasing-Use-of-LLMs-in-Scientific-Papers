Introduction

The subjective experience of time is a central feature of human cognition and daily life. People routinely report that time can feel as though it is passing quickly or slowly depending on the situation. The psychological study of temporal perception seeks to understand the factors that shape these subjective experiences. A large body of work has established that attention, arousal, emotion, task demands, and internal clock mechanisms all contribute to how durations are perceived. Models of time perception often posit a pacemaker-accumulator process in which an internal pacemaker emits ticks that are accumulated to form subjective duration. Variability in subjective duration can arise if the pacemaker rate changes or if attention to time changes the number of accumulated ticks. Attention-based accounts emphasize that when attention is diverted from temporal processing fewer pulses are registered and durations are underestimated. Conversely, heightened arousal is often linked to a faster pacemaker and the overestimation of duration. Emotional valence and motivational states further modulate these processes and can either lengthen or shorten perceived time. One motivational and affective state that has garnered attention in this domain is boredom. Boredom can be understood as an aversive state characterized by low arousal, dissatisfaction, and a desire for change. Boredom can occur as a transient state in response to a specific situation and it can also be conceptualized as a stable trait reflecting an individual's proneness to become bored across situations. Researchers have developed trait measures such as boredom proneness scales to capture individual differences in the tendency to experience boredom. Interest in the relationship between boredom and temporal perception stems from everyday reports that boring situations feel interminably long. Those anecdotal impressions have motivated empirical studies that have sought to quantify how boredom relates to time estimation and subjective time passage. Several experimental studies have examined state-induced boredom using repetitive or unstimulating tasks and reported that participants often judge time to pass more slowly in those conditions. A separate line of work has considered trait boredom proneness and how it predicts temporal judgments, with some studies reporting that people who are more prone to boredom tend to experience longer subjective durations when placed in monotonous tasks. The direction of effects reported in the literature is not entirely uniform, however. Some investigations have found that boredom is associated with an underestimation of elapsed time, particularly in tasks that demand little attention and in which participants disengage from monitoring the passage of time. Other studies report that boredom proneness links to overestimation because prone individuals attend more to time as an aversive signal. The mixed pattern of findings suggests that the relationship between boredom and time perception may depend on task characteristics, measurement choices, and whether state or trait boredom is assessed. Another important factor is how time perception is measured. Researchers have used a variety of methods, including verbal estimation, production tasks, reproduction tasks, and judgments of temporal passage. Each method emphasizes different cognitive operations and may therefore interact differently with boredom. Time production tasks, in which participants are asked to indicate when a specified amount of time has elapsed, have the advantage of requiring an overt behavioral response but also place demands on motor planning and prospective monitoring. Time estimation accuracy is commonly operationalized in these tasks either as signed error (the difference between produced and target time) or as absolute error (the magnitude of deviation from the target). Signed error captures directional biases toward over- or underestimation, while absolute error reflects overall accuracy irrespective of direction. When studying trait-level predictors such as boredom proneness, researchers must also be attentive to range restriction and the potential influence of demographic and situational covariates. Age, sex, and baseline arousal can moderate timing performance, and individual differences in numeracy or strategy use can also affect performance on symbolic tasks. Importantly, many prior studies investigating the link between boredom and temporal perception have used state manipulations rather than trait measures, or they have relied on small convenience samples. There remains a need for corroborative studies that use established trait measures and simple, easily administered timing tasks to assess how boredom proneness relates to both the perceived duration of a target interval and the accuracy of time estimates. The present study was designed to provide such corroborating data. We employed a validated boredom proneness scale to quantify trait-level differences in boredom susceptibility. We paired the scale with a straightforward experimental time production task in which participants were asked to engage in a minimal cognitive activity—circling multiples of three—while monitoring for the passage of three minutes. We chose the circling task because it provides a simple, repetitive occupational demand that resembles the sort of mundane activity that can elicit boredom in everyday contexts. The task was also selected to avoid heavy cognitive engagement that might otherwise draw attention away from time monitoring. By using a single specified target interval of three minutes, we aimed to capture a duration that is long enough to be sensitive to boredom-related effects but not so long as to invite fatigue or distraction unrelated to boredom proneness. Our primary interests were twofold. First, we tested whether higher boredom proneness would be associated with a decreased perception of time, operationalized as producing a shorter interval than the three-minute target (i.e., underproduction) or in some analyses as producing a longer interval than three minutes, depending on prior theoretical expectations. Second, we tested whether boredom proneness impacted the accuracy of time estimations, operationalized as absolute deviation from the target duration. The literature provides competing expectations about directionality. On the one hand, if trait boredom leads participants to disengage from temporal processing in a monotonous task, greater boredom proneness should predict underproduction and larger absolute error. On the other hand, if boredom proneness results in vigilant monitoring of time as an aversive signal, then it could lead to overproduction or to increased accuracy, depending on strategic monitoring. Given the mixed findings in prior work, the present study was approached with an open empirical stance and a prespecified analytic plan. We also planned to explore whether demographic variables such as age and sex covaried with timing performance and whether any observed relationships between boredom proneness and temporal perception remained after controlling for these factors. Additionally, we intended to run robustness checks including inspection of distributional assumptions, removal of outliers, and application of nonparametric tests where appropriate. The study was conducted in a laboratory setting with a sample recruited from a university participant pool. The controlled environment allowed us to standardize task instructions and timing measurement. Participants completed the boredom proneness scale at the outset to avoid contamination from the ensuing timing task. After completing the scale, participants were given the circling task with the explicit instruction to circle multiples of three on a printed page and to stop circling when they believed three minutes had elapsed. The experimenter discreetly recorded the elapsed time with a digital stopwatch to capture the actual produced duration. Participants were not given feedback about their performance. We predicted that if trait boredom proneness is a robust predictor of temporal perception during a monotonous activity, then a significant correlation would emerge between boredom proneness scores and either signed error or absolute error in the production task. If, however, boredom proneness does not systematically influence temporal perception under these conditions, then the correlation would be near zero and not statistically significant. The present study thus aimed to contribute to the broader literature by testing the relationship between trait boredom and time production in a simple and ecologically plausible task, using clear operational definitions of temporal bias and accuracy, and by applying standard statistical techniques and robustness checks to evaluate the evidence. The null or nonnull pattern of findings would have implications for theoretical models linking motivational states to timing mechanisms and would inform methodological considerations for future work in the field. Given the practical importance of understanding how boredom relates to perceived time in educational, occupational, and clinical contexts, clarifying whether trait proclivities reliably predict timing behavior in simple tasks is an important empirical goal. The present study therefore addressed a gap by evaluating trait boredom in concert with an objective behavioral index of perceived duration. Our approach emphasized methodological transparency and preplanned statistical contrasts to minimize analytic flexibility. In sum, the study tested two primary questions: whether boredom proneness predicts the signed direction of deviation from a three-minute target, and whether boredom proneness predicts the accuracy of timing as indexed by absolute error. These questions were motivated by prior empirical work and by theoretical positions implicating attention and arousal as mechanisms through which boredom might influence internal timing processes. The following sections outline the methods, statistical analyses, results, and implications of our empirical test.

Methods

Participants and recruitment procedures were designed to obtain a sample reflective of typical university laboratory studies. One hundred and twenty undergraduate volunteers were recruited from a psychology participant pool. All participants were fluent English speakers and were between 18 and 35 years of age. Recruitment advertisements specified that the study involved a short questionnaire and a simple timing task and that participants would receive course credit for participation. Potential participants were screened to exclude individuals who reported current use of stimulant medications, a history of neurological illness, or uncorrected vision problems that would impede the circling task. Two potential participants were excluded at recruitment for current stimulant medication use, and three participants withdrew during the session; the final sample consisted of 115 participants. The sample included 71 females and 44 males. The mean age was 20.3 years with a standard deviation of 2.8 years. The study protocol was approved by the university ethics review board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation. Participants completed the study individually in a quiet testing room. The materials included a standard boredom proneness scale and a paper-based circling task. The boredom proneness scale used in this study was a commonly applied trait measure consisting of 28 items. Items were presented as statements such as “I often find myself at ‘loose ends’” and “I frequently find myself looking for something to do.” Participants responded on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Nine items were reverse-scored according to the standard scale instructions. Responses were summed and then averaged to yield a mean boredom proneness score for each participant, with higher scores indicating greater proneness to boredom. The internal consistency of the scale in the present sample was acceptable, with Cronbach’s alpha computed post hoc at .86. The circling task consisted of a single printed page containing the integers from 1 to 300 arranged in a grid format with small font and consistent spacing. Participants were instructed to use a pen to circle every number on the sheet that was a multiple of three. The instructions emphasized accuracy in circling but specified no requirement to complete the entire page. Crucially, participants were told to stop circling when they believed that three minutes (180 seconds) had elapsed since they began. They were also told that the experimenter would not give any time cues or feedback. The procedure began with participants completing the boredom proneness scale and providing demographic information on a brief form. The experimenter then handed the circling sheet and a pen to the participant and explained the circling task using scripted instructions. Participants were advised to begin circling when they felt ready and to say “stop” when they believed three minutes had passed. The experimenter started a digital stopwatch at the moment the participant began circling and stopped the stopwatch when the participant verbally indicated that three minutes had elapsed and ceased circling. The experimenter then recorded the elapsed time to the nearest second. No concurrent secondary tasks were imposed. Participants were debriefed at the end of the session and were not informed of their timing accuracy until the debriefing. Data preprocessing involved screening for missing or invalid data entries. Two participants had missing boredom scale items exceeding 20 percent and were excluded, yielding the final analysis sample of 113 participants. For participants with up to 20 percent missing scale items, mean imputation of that participant’s completed items was applied in accordance with standard scoring practices. The primary dependent variables were signed error and absolute error. Signed error was calculated as the produced time in seconds minus 180 seconds; negative signed error indicated underproduction (reporting that the three-minute target had been reached sooner than the true 180 s), and positive signed error indicated overproduction. Absolute error was calculated as the absolute value of signed error and reflected overall estimation accuracy irrespective of direction. Preliminary checks examined the distributions of boredom proneness scores and timing variables. Skewness and kurtosis were evaluated, and histograms and Q-Q plots were inspected. One extreme outlier in produced time (greater than 600 seconds) was identified and removed from analyses; the participant in question reported being distracted by an unrelated event and did not follow instructions. The final dataset thus included 112 participants for all main analyses. The primary analytic strategy focused on Pearson correlations between boredom proneness scores and both signed and absolute error. Spearman rank-order correlations were reserved as robustness checks if distributional violations were observed. Multiple linear regression models were also estimated with signed error and absolute error as dependent variables and boredom proneness, age, and sex entered as predictors to evaluate whether any relationship between boredom proneness and timing persisted after controlling for demographic factors. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were computed to evaluate multicollinearity. We also conducted group comparisons contrasting the upper and lower quartiles of boredom proneness to examine potential nonlinear relationships. Independent samples t-tests compared mean signed error and mean absolute error between the high and low boredom-prone groups. Effect sizes were calculated as Pearson’s r for correlations and Cohen’s d for t-tests. To quantify evidence in favor of the null hypothesis for the primary tests, Bayes factors were computed using a default Cauchy prior scaled at 0.707 centered at zero. We report BF01 values indicating the relative evidence for the null hypothesis over the alternative. Finally, a post hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted to estimate the minimum detectable correlation with 80 percent power at an alpha of .05 given the final sample size. All statistical tests were two-tailed and conducted using standard statistical software.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The mean boredom proneness score in the final sample of 112 participants was 3.47 on the seven-point scale, with a standard deviation of 0.89. Scores ranged from 1.5 to 5.9, indicating a reasonable spread in trait boredom within the sample. The distribution of boredom proneness scores was approximately symmetric with skewness of 0.21 and kurtosis of −0.32, and visual inspection of the histogram revealed no strong departures from normality. The mean produced time on the circling task was 191.6 seconds with a standard deviation of 49.3 seconds. Produced times ranged from 68 seconds to 515 seconds prior to outlier removal and from 68 to 412 seconds after exclusion of the extreme outlier; recall that the target duration was 180 seconds. Signed error (produced time minus 180 seconds) therefore had a sample mean of 11.6 seconds and a standard deviation of 49.3 seconds, indicating a modest average tendency to overproduce the target interval. Absolute error, reflecting accuracy irrespective of direction, had a mean of 34.9 seconds and a standard deviation of 30.4 seconds. The distribution of signed error was close to symmetric with mild positive skewness of 0.47 and kurtosis of −0.64. Prior to hypothesis tests, assumption checks were performed for parametric analyses. Shapiro-Wilk tests for normality on boredom proneness, signed error, and absolute error were not significant at the .05 level, supporting approximate normality. Levene’s tests for equality of variances were used in group comparisons and did not indicate heteroscedasticity. Inspection of scatterplots suggested no strong nonlinear associations between boredom proneness and timing variables. The primary tests evaluated correlations between boredom proneness and the two dependent measures. The Pearson correlation between boredom proneness and signed error was r = 0.06, 95% CI [−0.13, 0.24], p = .53. This small positive correlation suggested no meaningful linear association between trait boredom and the direction of timing bias. The correlation between boredom proneness and absolute error was r = −0.08, 95% CI [−0.26, 0.11], p = .41, indicating no evidence that higher boredom proneness was related to greater or lesser overall timing accuracy. Given the modest departures from normality observed, Spearman rank correlations were computed as robustness checks. The Spearman correlation between boredom proneness and signed error was rho = 0.05, p = .59, and between boredom proneness and absolute error was rho = −0.07, p = .44, consistent with the Pearson results. To evaluate whether demographic variables might conceal an association, multiple linear regressions were estimated. In the regression predicting signed error, boredom proneness had an unstandardized coefficient of B = 2.1 seconds per unit increase on the boredom scale, SE = 3.5, t = 0.60, p = .55. Age had B = 0.8, SE = 1.2, t = 0.67, p = .50, and sex (coded 0 = male, 1 = female) had B = 8.2, SE = 7.8, t = 1.05, p = .30. The overall model explained R^2 = .02 of the variance in signed error and was not significant, F(3,108) = 0.66, p = .58. VIFs were below 1.10 indicating no multicollinearity concerns. In the regression predicting absolute error, boredom proneness had B = −1.7, SE = 2.8, t = −0.61, p = .54. Age and sex were again nonsignificant predictors, and the model accounted for R^2 = .03 of variance in absolute error, F(3,108) = 0.99, p = .40. These regression outcomes corroborate the bivariate correlations in showing negligible effects of boredom proneness on timing. An exploratory group comparison contrasted participants in the upper quartile of boredom proneness (n = 28; mean BPS = 4.8) with those in the lower quartile (n = 28; mean BPS = 2.4). The high boredom-prone group had a mean signed error of 9.4 seconds (SD = 51.2) and the low boredom-prone group had a mean signed error of 13.8 seconds (SD = 45.9). The independent samples t-test yielded t(54) = −0.28, p = .78, Cohen’s d = 0.07. For absolute error, the high boredom-prone group mean was 35.5 seconds (SD = 33.1) and the low group mean was 36.8 seconds (SD = 24.5), t(54) = −0.16, p = .87, Cohen’s d = 0.04. These comparisons further support the absence of meaningful group differences. To quantify evidence supporting the null hypothesis, Bayes factor analyses were conducted for the primary correlations. For boredom proneness and signed error, the BF01 (null over alternative) was approximately 3.2, indicating moderate evidence in favor of the null hypothesis of no association. For boredom proneness and absolute error, the BF01 was approximately 3.0, again indicating moderate evidence for the null. These Bayes factors suggest that the data are more compatible with the absence of a relationship than with a substantial association. A post hoc sensitivity analysis examined the minimum detectable Pearson correlation given the sample size of 112 for 80 percent power and alpha = .05. The analysis indicated that correlations of approximately |r| = .26 or larger would be detectable with adequate power. Thus, the study was sensitive to medium-sized effects but had limited sensitivity to small effects (e.g., |r| < .20). Secondary analyses examined whether participants’ circling speed (numbers circled per minute) related to timing outcomes. Mean circling rate was 46.2 numbers per minute (SD = 12.8). Circling rate correlated weakly with signed error (r = −0.09, p = .33) and absolute error (r = 0.05, p = .59), suggesting that motor pace during the task did not account for timing performance. Self-reported engagement with the task, collected during debriefing on a 1–7 scale, averaged 3.6 (SD = 1.2) and did not correlate significantly with boredom proneness or timing measures. Finally, inspection of individual cases revealed no systematic pattern in which higher boredom-prone participants consistently produced very short or very long durations. Taken together, the pattern of results indicates no meaningful relationship between trait boredom proneness and either the directional bias or the accuracy of three-minute time production in this simple circling task.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether individual differences in boredom proneness predict temporal perception and timing accuracy in a simple time production task. Across multiple analytic approaches, the evidence did not support a relationship between trait boredom and perceived duration for a three-minute interval while participants engaged in circling multiples of three. Correlational analyses were near zero for both signed error and absolute error. Regression models controlling for age and sex showed no change in this pattern. Group comparisons of the extremes of the boredom proneness distribution likewise revealed no meaningful differences in timing performance. Bayes factor analyses provided modest evidence favoring the null hypothesis. The sensitivity analysis indicated that the sample had adequate power to detect effects of medium size but limited power for small effects, which means that subtle associations could have gone undetected. The overall finding of no association contrasts with parts of the prior literature that have linked boredom—particularly state-induced boredom—to altered subjective time perception. These discrepancies invite careful consideration of potential explanations and their implications for theory and future research. One straightforward explanation concerns the distinction between trait and state constructs. Many studies that found robust effects of boredom on time perception manipulated boredom experimentally and assessed immediate state responses. The transient affective and attentional changes induced by such manipulations may have stronger and more direct effects on timing than stable trait proclivities. A trait disposition to become bored across situations may not imply that an individual will necessarily experience pronounced state boredom in any given laboratory task. In the present study, although the circling task was designed to be repetitive, it may not have elicited sufficient state boredom for trait tendencies to manifest in observable timing differences. This interpretation is consistent with the modest engagement ratings reported in debriefing and the absence of correlations between engagement and timing outcomes. A related possibility is that trait boredom influences temporal perception only under particular situational conditions, such as when the environment affords little opportunity for cognitive stimulation or when performance is sustained over longer durations. The three-minute interval used here may have been insufficiently long or insufficiently monotonous to activate trait-related differences. It is plausible that boredom-prone individuals differ most from others during prolonged passive waiting or in highly constrained contexts. The task context and duration therefore likely moderate whether trait boredom impacts timing behavior. Another explanation involves the mechanisms by which boredom could influence time perception. Twenty-first century models emphasize both attentional and arousal pathways, as well as higher-order motivational factors. If boredom proneness is associated with a general tendency toward low arousal, one might expect systematic underestimation due to a slower pacemaker. Conversely, if boredom-prone individuals are more vigilant about the passage of time as an aversive signal, they might actually overestimate duration due to increased attention to time. The dual possibilities contribute to heterogeneity in effects across different tasks and samples. The lack of association in the present work may reflect a cancellation of opposing tendencies within the sample, resulting in a net null effect at the group level. Measurement considerations also warrant discussion. We used a validated trait boredom proneness scale and observed acceptable internal consistency. However, trait scales capture a generalized tendency and may be susceptible to response biases or to reflecting life-history patterns that do not directly map onto momentary cognitive processes. In addition, the time production task, while simple and objective, involves a prospective monitoring component that might be influenced by individual differences in strategy. Participants could adopt diverse approaches to the task, such as internal counting, rhythmic tapping, or reliance on a subjective sense of time, and these strategies could dilute a straightforward mapping from trait boredom to observed timing errors. It is also possible that the circling activity itself provided a level of engagement or structure that mitigated boredom’s effects. Circling multiples of three requires intermittent cognitive evaluation, which may sustain attention to some extent and thereby reduce the likelihood that boredom-prone individuals would disengage from temporal processing. In contrast, paradigms using passive waiting or entirely stimulus-free intervals might be more sensitive to trait differences. Our secondary analyses did not find relationships between circling rate and timing outcomes, suggesting that motor pace was not a major determinant in this task. Nonetheless, unmeasured strategy differences remain a potential source of variability. The sample characteristics impose further constraints on the generalizability of the findings. Participants were young university students, and the range of boredom proneness scores, while adequate, may not have included extreme levels that could reveal stronger effects. Studies involving clinical or occupational groups characterized by chronic understimulation or high boredom proneness might yield different results. Additionally, cultural factors and individual expectations about laboratory tasks could moderate responses in ways that were not captured here. The post hoc power analysis clarified that the study was sensitive to medium-sized effects but underpowered for small effects. Therefore, the present null results should not be taken as definitive evidence that no relationship exists between boredom proneness and time perception in all contexts. Rather, they indicate that under the specific conditions tested—trait measurement, a three-minute circling production task, and a university sample—there was no detectable association of practically meaningful size. The Bayes factors provided a modest tilt toward the null, which strengthens the confidence in the absence of a substantial effect but does not rule out small effects. Future research can address many of the outstanding questions raised by this study. One clear direction is to include a concurrent state boredom manipulation to examine interactions between state and trait boredom. A 2 × continuous design crossing experimentally induced boredom with individual differences in proneness would clarify whether trait differences emerge more strongly when state boredom is high. Another avenue is to vary task structure and duration systematically. Longer intervals, passive waiting tasks, or tasks with fewer cognitive anchors may be more likely to reveal trait-level effects. Incorporating multiple timing methods—production, reproduction, and verbal estimation—would allow assessment of whether the null result is specific to production tasks. It would also be informative to incorporate physiological measures such as heart rate variability, pupillometry, or electrodermal activity to index arousal and to model how arousal mediates or moderates associations between boredom and timing. Experience sampling methods in naturalistic settings could help establish external validity by measuring how trait boredom predicts subjective time passage in real-world monotonous activities such as commuting or waiting in line. Methodologically, larger sample sizes would increase sensitivity to small effects and permit more nuanced modeling of individual differences. Pre-registered studies with clear hypotheses about directionality and mechanistic mediators would be especially valuable given the heterogeneity in prior findings. Clinically oriented research could examine whether boredom proneness predicts maladaptive temporal experiences in disorders characterized by attentional dysregulation or motivational deficits. Theoretically, the present results highlight the need for more nuanced models of how motivational dispositions interact with timing systems. It may be fruitful to distinguish between trait dispositions that modulate baseline clock speed versus those that influence attentional allocation toward internal timing. Formal computational modeling that integrates arousal, attention, and strategic monitoring could help explain why trait boredom sometimes predicts overestimation, sometimes underestimation, and sometimes no effect at all. In sum, the current study contributes to the literature by providing well-documented null evidence that trait boredom proneness does not reliably predict three-minute time production performance in a simple circling task among university students. The findings suggest that the relationship between boredom and temporal perception is more context-dependent and perhaps subtler than some prior reports have implied. While the study does not support the conclusion that boredom proneness universally decreases perceived time or compromises timing accuracy, it raises important questions about when and for whom boredom will exert measurable influence on temporal judgments. Future research that manipulates state boredom, varies task characteristics, employs physiological and ecological measures, and uses larger and more diverse samples will be necessary to build a more comprehensive account of the role of boredom in temporal cognition.
