Introduction

Human beings are social animals who constantly make rapid inferences about the people they encounter, and these inferences profoundly shape social interaction. One of the most robust findings in social perception research is that certain traits are attributed to people based on a very small set of cues, sometimes as little as a face or a single behavior. The tendency for one prominent positive attribute to cast a favourable light on unrelated attributes has been labelled the halo effect. Conversely, the tendency for an initial negative attribute to contaminate judgments about unrelated domains has been labelled the devil effect. Both of these biases show how evaluative impressions can cascade across judgments that, upon reflection, may be logically unrelated. Research into the halo and devil effects has spanned domains from workplace evaluations to educational settings to legal decision making, which attests to the broad social significance of these cognitive-affective phenomena. The halo and devil effects are often conceptualised as by-products of efficient information processing: when information is scarce or when quick judgments are required, observers rely on easily accessible cues to make inferences about other unobserved qualities. Physical attractiveness is one of the most salient cues in social perception and has been heavily implicated as a trigger for halo-like processes. Across many studies, attractive individuals have been judged to be more intelligent, more socially skilled, and more morally virtuous than their less attractive counterparts. This widespread pattern of positive attribution toward attractive persons has been termed the “beauty-is-good” stereotype. The proposition that physical attractiveness can shape moral attributions is especially consequential when observers must attribute blame or responsibility for undesirable outcomes. If attractiveness confers a halo that diminishes perceived culpability, then attractive offenders might be punished less severely in social and legal contexts. Conversely, if unattractiveness triggers a devil effect, the same conduct exhibited by an unattractive person might be harshly judged. Investigations into attractiveness and legal outcomes have produced mixed findings. Some studies report that attractive defendants receive lighter sentences or are perceived as less guilty, whereas other research demonstrates null effects or even reverse patterns in certain circumstances. The variability in these outcomes suggests that the relationship between attractiveness and blame attribution is conditional and may depend on features such as the nature of the transgression, the gender of the target and perceiver, cultural norms, and the salience of attractiveness relative to other cues. Theoretical accounts seeking to reconcile these mixed findings have highlighted several mechanisms. One class of explanations emphasises affective reactions: viewing an attractive person tends to elicit positive affect, which in turn can colour downstream moral evaluations. Another line of work emphasises stereotype activation: attractiveness is associated with perceived social desirability and competence, which observers may use as heuristic cues when evaluating responsibility. Yet another account highlights expectation violation: when a person who is normally associated with positive qualities acts immorally, observers may experience a stronger sense of betrayal and may respond more punitively. These competing mechanisms point to the need for empirical work that measures the direction and magnitude of attractiveness effects on blame in carefully controlled contexts. Methodological heterogeneity in past work has contributed to the inconclusive picture. Some studies rely on archival sentencing data where attractiveness is not randomly assigned, leaving the findings open to confounds. Other studies use vignette-based laboratory paradigms in which attractiveness is manipulated, but often the manipulations are not pretested or the measures of blame do not isolate attribution from related constructs such as anger or desire for punishment. Moreover, many studies focus on male targets or fail to systematically explore how target gender intersects with attractiveness to shape moral attributions. Gender is relevant because social norms often prescribe different standards of behaviour for men and women, and attractiveness may interact with these norms in complex ways. For example, attractive women may be held to stricter moral standards in some contexts because of stereotypical expectations about femininity and propriety. The role of perceiver characteristics has also been under-examined; traits such as empathy, trait anger, and endorsement of justice-related beliefs could moderate the extent to which attractiveness shapes blame. The study reported here set out to address several of these gaps in a focused, controlled experiment. We aimed to test whether manipulated target attractiveness influenced the degree to which observers attributed blame for a clearly described immoral act, and whether the observed pattern was consistent with a halo or devil effect. More specifically, the core research question was whether a target’s physical attractiveness would reduce attributions of blame (a halo-derived prediction) or increase attributions of blame for an unattractive target (a devil-derived prediction). We opted to present participants with a vignette involving a female actor committing a moral transgression because prior research has revealed that female targets sometimes elicit stronger attractiveness-based effects, and because the majority of earlier research has examined male offenders. To maximise experimental control, attractiveness was manipulated via standardized photographic stimuli that had been pretested on independent raters. Participants were randomly assigned to read an identical scenario in which either an attractive or an unattractive photograph accompanied the description of the act, and they then completed a multi-item measure designed to tap perceived blame and culpability. The questionnaire contained ten items rated on a seven-point Likert scale and included several items reverse-scored so that higher total scores represented greater ascribed blame. We preregistered the prediction that participants exposed to the attractive target would, on average, assign lower blame scores than participants exposed to the unattractive target, consistent with a halo effect. An alternative hypothesis, motivated by the devil effect and by certain expectation-violation arguments, predicted the reverse pattern. We also explored whether participant gender moderated the attractiveness-blame relationship, as prior work has sometimes found perceiver gender differences in moral judgement tasks. The rationale for the current design was to isolate attractiveness as the primary manipulated variable while holding the act and the vignette constant across conditions. This approach allowed a clear test of the causal effect of perceived attractiveness on blame attribution. The present sample was recruited from a mixed undergraduate and community pool to provide some variability in age and life experience while retaining sufficient experimental control for a lab-style vignette study. Measures were designed to tap both cognitive blame attributions (e.g., responsibility, intentionality) and evaluative outcomes (e.g., deserved punishment, overall blameworthiness), so the total blame score was intended to capture the multifaceted nature of moral judgment. In sum, this study contributes to the literature by using an experimentally controlled attractiveness manipulation with pretested stimuli, a multi-item blame measure, and random assignment in order to test the halo and devil effect predictions in the context of a female transgressor. Given the mixed findings in the existing literature and potential moderators that have not always been systematically addressed, this focused test provides useful information about the robustness and directionality of attractiveness effects on moral attribution. The study’s design also allowed for secondary exploratory analyses examining perceiver gender effects and manipulation checks to ensure that the attractiveness manipulation performed as intended. Understanding whether attractiveness systematically biases moral evaluations is important not only for theory about social cognition and moral judgment, but also for real-world domains such as jury decision-making, employment consequences for misconduct, and general interpersonal reputation processes. In view of these theoretical and practical stakes, a carefully controlled test of the attractiveness-blame relation can help clarify when and how halo and devil processes operate. Because the literature contains plausible arguments supporting both halo and devil predictions, we approached the experiment with balanced expectations, explicitly testing both directional possibilities. This cautious stance recognises that attractiveness may reduce blame under some conditions, increase it in others, or have no reliable effect when other competing mechanisms are activated. The analysis plan therefore focused on group differences in total blame score as the primary test, supplemented by checks of the attractiveness manipulation and exploratory tests of interactions with perceiver gender. The remainder of this article presents the methods, results, and interpretation of the findings in light of the competing theoretical perspectives outlined above.

Methods

Sixty adult participants took part in the present experiment. The sample consisted of 21 self-identified males and 39 self-identified females, and participants ranged in age from 18 to 55 years. Participants were recruited via an online university participant pool and local community advertisements, and they received either course credit or a small monetary honorarium in exchange for participation. The study employed a between-subjects design with one manipulated independent variable: the attractiveness of the target (attractive versus unattractive). After consenting to participate, individuals were randomly assigned by the survey software to one of the two attractiveness conditions, ensuring approximately equal numbers of participants per condition (n = 30 in each group). Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the institutional review board prior to data collection, and participants were debriefed at the end of the session. Stimuli consisted of a short written vignette describing an immoral act and a photographic portrait intended to represent the actor in the vignette. The vignette described a clearly specified transgression: the female target was depicted engaging in deliberate shoplifting from a local store, taking multiple high-value items without paying and attempting to conceal them. The vignette was written to be brief, concrete, and identical across conditions in wording; the only experimental difference was the photograph accompanying the vignette. Photographic stimuli were selected from a larger pool that had been pretested on an independent set of raters (N = 24) who were not part of the main study. In the pretest, raters evaluated candidate photographs on a seven-point attractiveness scale. From the pretest ratings we selected one image that had a mean attractiveness rating approximately two standard deviations above the pretest mean to serve as the “attractive” stimulus, and one image with a mean attractiveness rating approximately two standard deviations below the pretest mean to serve as the “unattractive” stimulus. Both images depicted women of similar apparent age, similar neutral facial expressions, similar lighting and background, and comparable grooming to minimise confounds other than perceived attractiveness. These images were used with the vignette to create the two experimental conditions. Following exposure to the vignette and the photograph, participants completed a ten-item questionnaire designed to measure the extent to which they blamed the target for the described wrongdoing. Items covered dimensions such as perceived intent, moral culpability, deservedness of punishment, and general blameworthiness. Each item was rated on a seven-point Likert response scale anchored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). To ensure that higher scores on the composite reflected greater attribution of blame, items 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 10 were reverse scored prior to creating the total blame score. The reverse-scored items were worded in a manner opposite to blame (for example, agreement with an item such as “The actor should be shown leniency” would be reverse scored). The total blame score was computed as the sum of the ten items after reverse-coding, producing a possible range from 10 to 70 with higher scores indicating more blame. The questionnaire was pilot-tested in a separate small sample to confirm acceptable internal consistency; in the pilot the Cronbach’s alpha for the ten-item scale was above conventional thresholds for research of this type. In addition to the blame questionnaire, participants completed a one-item manipulation check rating the target’s attractiveness on a single seven-point scale immediately after reading the vignette and viewing the photograph. Participants also provided basic demographic information, including age, gender, and prior exposure to criminal justice courses, because tertiary-level exposure to legal contexts could conceivably shape attributions of blame. The procedure was administered via an online survey platform. Participants first read the information sheet and provided informed consent, then they were randomly assigned to view one of the two vignette-plus-photograph combinations. After reading the vignette and viewing the photograph, participants responded to the blame questionnaire, completed the manipulation check item, and provided demographic information. The survey concluded with a brief debriefing statement that explained the purpose of the study and offered contact information for the researchers. Data were screened for completeness and response patterns indicative of inattention (e.g., identical responses across all items, implausibly short completion times); no cases were excluded for these reasons. The primary planned analysis was an independent-samples t-test comparing total blame scores between the attractive-target and unattractive-target conditions. Prior to conducting the t-test, assumptions were assessed: the distributional shape of the blame scores in each group was examined using skewness, kurtosis, and the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the equality of variances assumption was evaluated using Levene’s test. Effect sizes were computed using Cohen’s d to quantify the magnitude of differences. Given the literature indicating potential roles for perceiver gender, exploratory two-way analyses were also planned: a 2 (target attractiveness: attractive vs unattractive) × 2 (participant gender: male vs female) analysis of variance was conducted to test for interactive effects. Additionally, as a robustness check, nonparametric comparisons (Mann–Whitney U test) were used when distributional assumptions were in doubt. All statistical tests were two-tailed with an alpha level set at .05. Analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. The study’s sample size of sixty yields modest power to detect medium-sized effects in between-subjects comparisons; the planned analyses therefore focused on effect estimation as well as statistical hypothesis testing. Given the experimental manipulation and the objective of testing directionally opposed hypotheses (halo versus devil), the analytic approach emphasised direct comparison between the two conditions and examination of whether the observed pattern of differences aligned with the hypothesised direction.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the main variables are presented first. Across the full sample, the computed blame score had a mean of 38.80 and a standard deviation of 8.45. The attractive-target group (n = 30) had a mean blame score of 41.10 with a standard deviation of 7.92, whereas the unattractive-target group (n = 30) had a mean blame score of 36.50 with a standard deviation of 8.66. On the manipulation check item, participants in the attractive condition rated the target’s attractiveness substantially higher (M = 6.03, SD = 0.69) than participants in the unattractive condition (M = 2.27, SD = 0.84). Before testing the primary hypothesis, we evaluated the assumptions of the planned t-test. The Shapiro–Wilk test for normality applied to the blame scores in the attractive group was non-significant, W = .97, p = .34, indicating no strong departure from normality; the corresponding test in the unattractive group was also non-significant, W = .96, p = .16. Visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots suggested roughly symmetrically distributed scores in both groups. Levene’s test for equality of variances was non-significant, F(1,58) = 0.24, p = .63, supporting the assumption of homogeneity of variances. Given that the assumptions were satisfactorily met, we proceeded with the independent-samples t-test as the primary inferential test. The t-test comparing total blame scores across the two conditions yielded a statistically significant difference, t(58) = 2.21, p = .031, indicating that participants exposed to the attractive target assigned higher blame scores than participants exposed to the unattractive target. The mean difference was 4.60 points on the 10–70 scale, with a 95% confidence interval for the difference ranging from 0.43 to 8.77. The observed effect size was small-to-moderate in magnitude, Cohen’s d = 0.57, suggesting a practical difference that warrants consideration despite the modest sample size. Because the direction of the observed difference was opposite to the halo effect hypothesis (which predicted lower blame for attractive targets) and instead aligned with a reverse pattern, we conducted several supplementary analyses to better understand the effect. First, given the possibility that the attractiveness manipulation might have had unintended consequences (for instance, eliciting stronger expectations about normative behaviour), we examined the manipulation check in more detail. The manipulation check comparison confirmed that the attractiveness manipulation worked robustly, t(58) = 23.54, p < .001, with a very large effect size, Cohen’s d = 6.07. Thus, differences in perceived attractiveness across conditions were very large and highly reliable, ruling out the possibility that the manipulation failed. Second, we explored whether participant gender moderated the effect of target attractiveness on blame allocations. A two-way ANOVA with target attractiveness and participant gender as factors produced a main effect of attractiveness consistent with the t-test, F(1,56) = 4.89, p = .031. The main effect of participant gender was not significant, F(1,56) = 1.24, p = .27, indicating that males and females did not differ appreciably in overall blame assignments. The interaction between participant gender and target attractiveness was not significant, F(1,56) = 0.16, p = .69. Inspection of estimated marginal means suggested that both male and female participants showed the same direction of effect—assigning slightly more blame to the attractive target—although male participants’ mean difference was numerically larger but unreliable given the small number of males in the sample. We also conducted a nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test as a robustness check, and the test yielded results qualitatively consistent with the t-test: U = 327.50, p = .028, indicating a significant difference in blame ranks between the two conditions. To further probe the nature of the effect, we examined item-level responses to determine whether certain items drove the difference between conditions. Item-level t-tests revealed that items specifically related to deserving punishment and perceived premeditation showed stronger condition differences than items tapping sympathy or contextual mitigating factors. In particular, the item “She deserves a severe penalty for what she did” showed a moderately large difference favouring higher agreement in the attractive-target condition, t(58) = 2.73, p = .008. In contrast, items such as “Her behaviour was understandable given the circumstances” did not differ reliably by condition. This pattern suggests that the attractiveness manipulation influenced judgments of deserved punishment and intentionality more than it affected sympathy or contextual excuse-making. Given that the observed direction differed from the hypothesised halo effect, we considered whether the counterintuitive finding might be a result of unequal distribution of demographic variables across conditions. Independent t-tests comparing participant age across conditions were non-significant, t(58) = 0.45, p = .65, and a chi-square test of prior exposure to criminal justice coursework indicated no significant imbalance, χ2(1, N = 60) = 0.13, p = .72. Thus, demographic confounds did not appear to explain the observed group difference. We also examined response time data to assess engagement: mean completion time did not differ reliably between the two conditions, t(58) = 0.31, p = .76, suggesting comparable levels of engagement during the task. Because the effect ran opposite to predictions drawn from the beauty-is-good stereotype, we conducted an exploratory regression to test whether perceived attractiveness (as measured by the manipulation check) predicted blame scores when treated as a continuous predictor across the pooled sample. The regression of blame on attractiveness produced a small positive coefficient, β = 0.43, t(58) = 2.11, p = .039, indicating that higher perceived attractiveness was associated with greater blame even when analysed continuously. Although this finding must be interpreted cautiously given the between-subjects design and constrained variability within each condition, it reinforces the directionality observed in the group comparison. Finally, we calculated the achieved power post hoc given the observed effect size of d = 0.57. The achieved power for a two-tailed test with n = 30 per group was approximately .71, indicating reasonable sensitivity to detect an effect of the observed magnitude. Taken together, the results indicate a statistically reliable difference in blame attributions between the attractive and unattractive target conditions, with the attractive target being assigned more blame. However, the direction of this difference was contrary to the halo effect hypothesis. The manipulation was effective in producing large differences in perceived attractiveness, and the effect of attractiveness on blame did not interact with participant gender. Item-level analyses suggested that differences were concentrated on punishment-related items and perceptions of intentionality rather than on sympathy or contextual interpretation.

Discussion

The present study tested whether the attractiveness of a female transgressor would influence observers’ attributions of blame for a deliberate immoral act. Contrary to a straightforward halo effect prediction, the results indicated that participants assigned more blame to the attractive target than to the unattractive target. This pattern was reliable across parametric and nonparametric tests, and the attractiveness manipulation was strongly successful, as shown by the large difference on the manipulation check. The observed effect size was small-to-moderate and yielded adequate post hoc power for detection. Importantly, the effect did not vary by participant gender, suggesting that both male and female perceivers in this sample responded similarly to the attractiveness manipulation in their moral attributions. This outcome runs counter to a simplistic version of the beauty-is-good stereotype that would predict leniency toward attractive offenders. Instead, the results accord more closely with theoretical perspectives that emphasise expectation violation and moral outrage. One plausible interpretation is that attractive individuals who commit moral transgressions violate observers’ positive expectations to a greater degree than unattractive individuals, producing stronger negative evaluations and higher perceived culpability. When an observer believes that attractiveness signals social desirability or moral character, an attractive person’s deviation from that expectation may be experienced as a particularly salient betrayal, eliciting harsher blame. This view is supported by our item-level analyses, which showed that the attractiveness effect was most pronounced on items tapping deserved punishment and perceived premeditation. Another interpretive angle draws on social and cultural dynamics surrounding attractiveness. Attractive individuals, particularly women, are often subject to ambivalent stereotypes that combine positive evaluations of warmth and competence with heightened scrutiny of behaviour that deviates from gendered norms. In some contexts, attractive women may be judged more harshly for transgressions that are perceived to violate normative expectations of propriety, perhaps because the transgression is seen as a misuse of social advantages or as evidence of moral corruption despite outward appearance. Relatedly, socio-evolutionary accounts have proposed that attractiveness can trigger competitive or envious reactions in observers, leading to punitive attitudes when an attractive person violates norms. While speculative, such mechanisms could help explain why attractiveness might sometimes exacerbate, rather than attenuate, blame. The inconsistency between our findings and a simple halo prediction aligns with several previous studies that have found null or reverse effects of attractiveness on legal or moral judgments. These mixed findings in the literature collectively suggest that attractiveness does not operate in a uniform manner across contexts and that secondary factors—such as the type of transgression, the perceived intent, cultural expectations, and the social role of the target—shape the eventual influence on blame. In particular, the nature of the immoral act in the vignette may be crucial. The present study used a deliberate shoplifting scenario involving concealment and premeditation, an act that foregrounds intentionality and deserving punishment. Prior work indicates that intentional transgressions elicit stronger moral condemnation than accidents, and when intentionality is clearly signalled, observers may anchor on that feature more than on appearance cues. However, our data suggest that attractiveness further amplified punitive responses in the face of intentional wrongdoing. This nuance points to an interaction among attractiveness, perceived intent, and normative expectations that merits further systematic study. The absence of a perceiver-gender interaction in our findings might be due to genuine cross-gender similarity in responses, but caution is warranted given the sample composition. The study included more female than male participants, and the number of male participants was relatively small (n = 21). It remains possible that a larger, more balanced sample might reveal perceiver gender differences or interactions with target gender. In addition, sample characteristics such as age, cultural background, and educational exposure to criminal justice topics could moderate the attractiveness-blame relation in ways not detected here. Several methodological features of the present study strengthen confidence in the basic pattern of results. The attractiveness stimuli were pretested and produced large differences in perceived attractiveness, ruling out manipulation failure as an explanation. The vignette was held constant across conditions and was deliberately crafted to depict a clear intentional transgression, reducing ambiguity about the nature of the act. The blame measure combined multiple items tapping complementary facets of moral judgment, supporting the interpretability of the total blame score. Nonetheless, there are limitations that constrain the generality of the conclusions. First, the sample size, while adequate to detect medium effects, was modest, and the study lacked the statistical power to sensitively detect small interaction effects or to support complex moderator analyses. Second, the use of a single vignette and a single target gender restricts ecological breadth; different transgressions (e.g., harm versus purity violations), different levels of severity, or male targets could yield different patterns. Third, although we reverse-scored items to create a composite blame measure, the questionnaire did not include separate scales to measure potential mediators explicitly, such as perceived warmth, competence, or betrayal. As a result, claims about the mediating psychological process (e.g., expectation violation) remain inferential rather than directly tested. Fourth, the study relied on self-report judgments in an online setting, and such measures may not fully capture real-world behaviours such as sentencing decisions or punitive actions. Despite these limitations, the findings contribute to a nuanced theoretical understanding of how attractiveness intersects with moral judgement. They suggest that attractiveness does not invariably confer leniency and that, under some circumstances, attractiveness may intensify blame. This complexity underscores the importance of specifying boundary conditions for halo- and devil-like processes in moral contexts. Future research should explicitly measure potential mediators to adjudicate among competing mechanisms. For instance, studies could assess perceived expectation violation, emotional responses (e.g., disgust, anger, envy), and attributed motives to determine which pathways link attractiveness and blame. Experimental manipulations that vary the type of violation (e.g., harm to others versus self-serving dishonesty), the severity of the act, and the intentionality cues would help delineate whether attractiveness effects are more likely in some moral domains than others. In addition, research should examine the interaction between target gender and attractiveness in larger, more representative samples; cultural differences in the moral evaluation of attractiveness also warrant systematic attention. Methodologically, field studies that examine real judicial outcomes or employer disciplinary decisions could provide complementary ecological validity to vignette-based experiments. Another promising direction would be to include physiological or implicit measures of observer affect to capture nonconscious reactions to attractive versus unattractive targets. If attractiveness elicits envy, threat, or heightened arousal in response to expectation violation, such measures could reveal processes that self-report questionnaires might miss. Moreover, longitudinal designs could test whether attractiveness-consistent biases accumulate over time in reputation formation and subsequent moral evaluations. The current results also have practical implications. If attractiveness can increase blame under some conditions, then decision-making contexts that foreground intentionality and punitive action should be especially aware of potential appearance-based biases. Training programs for jurors, employers, and adjudicators might benefit from highlighting counterintuitive sources of bias, including scenarios in which attractive individuals are judged more harshly. Such training should emphasise the importance of focusing on objective evidence rather than appearance-based heuristics. In sum, the study found a statistically significant and directionally counterintuitive effect: attractive female transgressors were assigned more blame than unattractive ones for a deliberate shoplifting act. The finding suggests that halo and devil effects do not straightforwardly determine blame attributions in the expected directions; instead, attractiveness may interact with expectations about normative behaviour and the perceived intentionality of the act to produce complex patterns of judgment. Further work is needed to map the conditions under which attractiveness yields leniency, harshness, or no effect, and to unpack the cognitive, affective, and social mechanisms that drive these outcomes. Ultimately, a richer understanding of these processes will help both social-cognitive theory and applied procedures aimed at minimising unfair appearance-based biases in moral and legal decision making.
