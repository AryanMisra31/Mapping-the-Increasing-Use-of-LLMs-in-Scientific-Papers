Introduction

Short-term memory is a fundamental aspect of human cognition that supports many everyday tasks. It is often described as the capacity to hold a small amount of information in an active, readily available state for a brief period. The study of short-term memory has historically focused on how information is encoded, maintained, and retrieved over short intervals. Immediate serial recall tasks, in which participants must recall a sequence of items in the order presented, have been a central paradigm for probing these processes. In particular, the immediate serial recall of words provides a tractable means to investigate the representational and processing constraints of short-term memory. One well-established phenomenon in this area is the word length effect, in which lists composed of shorter words are typically recalled more accurately than lists composed of longer words. The word length effect has been interpreted in various theoretical frameworks as arising from articulatory rehearsal constraints, temporal capacity limits, or differences in the time required to refresh phonological representations. Another important empirical regularity is the concreteness effect, wherein concrete words that evoke sensory or perceptual representations are generally better remembered than abstract words. Concreteness is closely related to imageability and the extent to which words have readily accessible referents in the world, and it is thought to enhance recall through stronger semantic associations and richer encoding. Despite robust findings for both word length and concreteness effects, the relationship between these two variables in immediate serial recall is not fully resolved. Some theoretical accounts predict interactions between word length and concreteness, while others predict largely additive influences. For example, models that emphasize phonological loop capacity suggest that word length affects recall via articulatory duration independently of semantic richness. On the other hand, dual-coding or semantic-enhancement perspectives emphasize that concreteness may recruit nonphonological representational resources, potentially mitigating the impact of phonological length. Empirical work has produced mixed evidence regarding the joint effects of word length and concreteness. Some studies have reported that concreteness reduces the magnitude of the word length effect by providing compensatory semantic-based support for longer items. Other studies have found that word length and concreteness independently influence recall with little or no interaction, suggesting separate mechanisms. Methodological differences across studies, including variations in list length, presentation rate, scoring criteria, and stimulus control for lexical properties, contribute to this inconsistency. In addition, the role of serial position in moderating these effects is under-investigated, as word length and concreteness may differentially influence primacy and recency portions of the serial position curve. The serial position function is informative because it reflects the interplay of encoding, rehearsal, and retrieval processes across list positions. Resolution of how word length and concreteness jointly affect immediate serial recall is important because it bears on the architecture of short-term memory and on the extent to which phonological and semantic representations interact during maintenance. If effects are additive, this supports modular or separable contributions of phonological duration and semantic richness. If effects interact, this supports more integrated models in which semantic representations can buffer phonological limitations or where phonological constraints affect semantic access. The current study was designed to test the independent and interactive effects of word length and concreteness on immediate serial recall performance in a tightly controlled experimental setting. We manipulated word length in terms of articulatory duration approximated by syllable count and phonological length while controlling for word frequency and phonological similarity. We manipulated concreteness by selecting words with clear concrete referents and words judged to be abstract with low imageability. The experimental design was a 2 x 2 factorial crossing of word length (short vs long) and concreteness (concrete vs abstract). We presented sequences of words for immediate serial recall under conditions that are standard in the literature: paced presentation, fixed list length, and strict serial scoring. Based on prior findings, we predicted main effects of word length and concreteness such that shorter words and more concrete words would be recalled with higher accuracy overall. We also considered two possible outcomes for the interaction: an absence of interaction, which would indicate additive effects and support separable phonological and semantic contributions; or a significant interaction in which concreteness attenuates the negative effect of word length on recall, indicating that semantic richness can compensate for phonological demands. In addition to the primary analyses of overall recall accuracy, we planned secondary analyses of serial position curves, error types, and item-level predictors to provide a fuller account of how these variables operate across the temporal structure of recall. We also planned to conduct supplementary analyses controlling for secondary lexical variables such as subjective familiarity to ensure that observed effects are not confounded by uncontrolled properties. The rationale for this approach is that a well-controlled factorial experiment with sufficient power to detect medium-sized interactions will help clarify the relationship between phonological and semantic influences in immediate serial recall. Clarifying this relationship has implications for theoretical models of short-term memory and for applied contexts where word complexity and concreteness influence learning and retention. If concreteness exerts a robust advantage independent of phonological length, it suggests that educational and mnemonic strategies can capitalize on semantic elaboration to improve short-term retention. Conversely, if word length remains the dominant predictor regardless of concreteness, this underscores the centrality of temporal capacity and articulatory rehearsal in short-term memory. Beyond theoretical implications, the present study’s findings can help refine stimulus design in future experimental studies of verbal short-term memory by highlighting which properties must be controlled to isolate target effects. The current experiment thus aims to contribute empirical clarity and theoretical leverage to a longstanding question about how different dimensions of word representation jointly contribute to immediate serial recall. In what follows, we report a preregistered study in which we implemented stringent stimulus controls and sufficiently powered statistical tests to evaluate main effects and interactions. We then interpret the pattern of results in light of competing theories and propose mechanistic accounts and future tests that can further adjudicate between them.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from the local community. Seventy-two adults completed the study and provided usable data. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 35 years and reported English as their first language. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no reported history of neurological disorders. Participants gave informed consent prior to beginning the experiment and were reimbursed with course credit or a modest payment. The experimental design was a within-subjects 2 x 2 factorial crossing word length (short, long) and concreteness (concrete, abstract). The experiment used a stimulus set of 120 monosyllabic and multisyllabic words that were selected to populate four cells of 30 items each: short-concrete, short-abstract, long-concrete, and long-abstract. Short words were operationalized as words with one to two syllables and short articulatory duration, while long words had three or more syllables and appreciably longer articulatory duration. Concrete words were those that receive high ratings on established concreteness or imageability scales, and abstract words were those with low imageability and without a readily accessible physical referent. To minimize confounds, word frequency was matched across cells using a standardized frequency index, and subjective familiarity ratings were monitored to avoid large discrepancies between cells. Phonological similarity was minimized within lists by excluding items with high onset or rhyme overlap, and items with unusual orthographic patterns were avoided. Stimuli were presented visually in the center of a computer monitor in a clear sans-serif font. Each trial consisted of a list of five words presented sequentially at a paced rate of one word per 1000 ms, followed by a 1000 ms retention interval and then a recall prompt. The list length of five items was chosen because it is short enough to yield relatively high recall rates and long enough to reveal serial position effects. Participants were instructed to read each word silently as it appeared and to attempt to remember the exact order of the items. No articulatory suppression was used, because the study aimed to examine spontaneous maintenance under typical verbal conditions. After the last retention interval, a prompt appeared instructing participants to type the words in the order they remembered them using the keyboard. Typed responses were entered on a single line separated by spaces. Participants completed 48 experimental trials organized into 12 blocks of four trials, with each block containing one list from each of the four experimental cells presented in random order. Items were sampled without replacement within a block to avoid immediate repetition and were randomized across trials and participants so that each participant saw a balanced distribution of items across conditions. Prior to experimental trials participants completed six practice trials that mimicked the task structure and rate. The entire experiment took approximately 40 to 50 minutes per participant to complete. Scoring of recall used strict serial scoring: an item was scored as correct only if it appeared in the correct serial position on the response line. For each trial, the number of correctly recalled items in correct serial positions was tallied, yielding a trial score from 0 to 5. Primary dependent variables were mean proportion correct per condition and serial position-specific accuracies. Error types were coded into omissions, intrusions (items not present in the list), and transpositions (items present but in wrong positions) for exploratory analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using standard statistical software. The primary analysis was a repeated-measures analysis of variance with two within-subjects factors: word length and concreteness. Assumptions of sphericity were trivially satisfied because each factor had only two levels, and normality of residuals was assessed through inspection of Q-Q plots. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared for ANOVA results. Complementary Bayesian repeated measures ANOVA was conducted to quantify evidence for or against the interaction term. Planned follow-up analyses included pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction and investigation of serial position curves via a 5 (serial position) x 2 x 2 mixed analysis. Item-level linear mixed-effects regression models were also specified to predict item recall probability from length, concreteness, and controlled lexical covariates, with random intercepts for participants and items. Error analyses used chi-square tests and logistic mixed models where appropriate to compare distributional differences across conditions. A target sample size of 72 was determined a priori to provide adequate power (≈0.80) to detect medium-sized main effects and interactions given within-subjects design efficiencies. Data exclusion criteria were minimal; participants who failed to complete the task or whose overall accuracy was at chance (operationalized as mean proportion correct < .10) would be excluded, but no participant met exclusionary criteria in the collected sample. All materials and analysis scripts were archived for transparency.

Results

All 72 participants completed the task and were included in the analyses. Mean overall recall accuracy across all conditions was 0.62 (SD = 0.14) in terms of proportion of items recalled in the correct serial position. Descriptive statistics broken down by condition revealed that short-concrete items had the highest mean accuracy, followed by short-abstract, long-concrete, and long-abstract. Specifically, mean proportion correct was 0.72 (SD = 0.13) for short-concrete items, 0.66 (SD = 0.14) for short-abstract items, 0.61 (SD = 0.15) for long-concrete items, and 0.49 (SD = 0.16) for long-abstract items. These descriptive trends suggested both main effects and an apparent additive pattern. The primary repeated-measures ANOVA testing the effects of word length and concreteness on recall showed a significant main effect of word length, F(1,71) = 55.24, p < .001, partial η² = .44, indicating better recall for shorter words. The main effect of concreteness was also significant, F(1,71) = 38.91, p < .001, partial η² = .35, indicating better recall for concrete words. The interaction between word length and concreteness was not significant, F(1,71) = 1.67, p = .20, partial η² = .02, providing no evidence of a multiplicative effect between length and concreteness in this sample. Complementary Bayesian repeated measures ANOVA yielded a Bayes factor in favor of the model with main effects over the model with interaction BF10_main = 1.8 x 10^4, whereas the inclusion of the interaction term decreased model evidence, yielding BF10_interaction = 0.28 relative to the main-effects model, indicating substantial evidence against the interaction. Pairwise comparisons showed that short-concrete items were recalled significantly better than long-concrete items, t(71) = 5.84, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.69. Short-abstract items were recalled significantly better than long-abstract items, t(71) = 6.02, p < .001, d = 0.71. Concrete-short versus abstract-short also differed significantly, t(71) = 3.64, p < .001, d = 0.43, and concrete-long versus abstract-long differed significantly, t(71) = 2.97, p = .004, d = 0.35. Serial position analyses revealed a typical bowed serial position curve with enhanced recall for early and late positions. A 5 (serial position) x 2 (length) x 2 (concreteness) repeated measures ANOVA confirmed a strong main effect of serial position, F(4,284) = 169.63, p < .001, partial η² = .71. The length by serial position interaction was significant, F(4,284) = 6.19, p < .001, partial η² = .08, indicating that word length had a larger effect in the middle positions than at terminal positions. The concreteness by serial position interaction was also significant, F(4,284) = 4.02, p = .004, partial η² = .05, suggesting that concreteness conferred advantages across positions but with slightly larger effects in mid-list positions. The three-way interaction was not significant, F(4,284) = 0.53, p = .71. Error-type analyses showed that omissions were the most common error overall, and intrusions were relatively rare. Long-abstract lists elicited more omissions than short-concrete lists, χ²(1) = 18.22, p < .001. Transposition errors, or items recalled but in incorrect positions, were more frequent for long words than for short words, χ²(1) = 9.45, p = .002, indicating that phonological length may lead to positional instability. Item-level logistic mixed-effects regression predicting the probability that an item was recalled in the correct serial position included fixed effects for syllable count (treated continuously), concreteness rating, word frequency, and subjective familiarity, and random intercepts for participant and item. Syllable count negatively predicted recall probability (β = -0.18, SE = 0.03, z = -6.00, p < .001). Concreteness rating positively predicted recall probability (β = 0.25, SE = 0.04, z = 6.25, p < .001). Word frequency and familiarity were not significant predictors in the presence of length and concreteness (ps > .10), indicating that the effects were not driven by these lexical covariates. A model that included an interaction term between syllable count and concreteness did not show a significant interaction (β = -0.02, SE = 0.01, z = -1.50, p = .13), consistent with the ANOVA. A post-hoc power analysis based on observed effect sizes confirmed that the study had >.80 power to detect the observed main effects and was underpowered to detect very small interactions (observed partial η² for interaction = .02). Mean response times for typed recall did not differ systematically across conditions, suggesting that speed-accuracy trade-offs were unlikely to account for accuracy differences. Reaction times for entry of correct items averaged 2.2 s per item (SD = 0.6) and were comparable across the four cells. Exploratory analyses comparing male and female participants did not reveal reliable sex differences in the pattern of effects. Finally, inspection of individual differences showed that participants with higher overall working memory spans (assessed via a brief operation span administered post hoc) tended to show higher recall overall but the pattern of length and concreteness effects remained consistent across span levels, as indicated by non-significant interaction terms with span in separate regression models.

Discussion

The present study investigated the effects of word length and concreteness on immediate serial recall using a tightly controlled 2 x 2 within-subjects design. The primary outcomes were clear: both word length and concreteness exerted robust and independent influences on the probability that an item would be recalled in its correct serial position. Specifically, shorter words were recalled better than longer words, and concrete words were recalled better than abstract words. There was no reliable interaction between word length and concreteness, a pattern that was consistent across ANOVA, Bayesian model comparison, and item-level mixed-effects regression analyses. These findings have several important implications for theories of short-term verbal memory. First, the replicated word length effect supports accounts that emphasize temporal or articulatory constraints on phonological maintenance. Shorter words likely require less time to articulate or rehearse, allowing more items to be refreshed within the limited temporal window available to the maintenance system. This temporal limitation appears to operate independently of semantic richness because the word length effect persisted across both concrete and abstract items. Second, the concreteness advantage indicates that semantic and perceptual representations contribute meaningfully to immediate serial recall. The concrete words’ superior recall likely reflects richer encoding and additional retrieval cues, such as imagery or sensorimotor associations, that augment phonological trace maintenance. The concreteness effect was not eliminated by phonological brevity or length, indicating that semantic enhancement provides an additional source of mnemonic support rather than a replacement for phonological rehearsal. Third, the absence of a significant interaction suggests additive rather than multiplicative influences of phonological duration and semantic richness. An additive pattern is most compatible with models in which phonological rehearsal and semantic representational support operate as partially independent contributors to item stability in short-term memory. In such architectures, limitations in articulatory rehearsal reduce phonological fidelity regardless of semantic richness, while semantic richness supplements recall via separate pathways that do not directly alter articulatory timing constraints. The serial position analyses provided further nuance by revealing that word length effects were more pronounced in mid-list positions whereas concreteness advantages were distributed across positions but with modestly larger effects in the middle positions as well. This suggests that both phonological constraints and semantic support are particularly consequential when neither strong primacy encoding nor recency-based retention mechanisms dominate performance. The mid-list vulnerability is consistent with estimates of limited rehearsal opportunities: items in the middle of a list are less likely to receive repeated rehearsal and thus more susceptible to phonological fading, whereas concreteness can partially bolster these items by providing stronger semantic traces. Error analyses offered convergent evidence for the locus of these effects. Long words produced more transposition errors, suggesting that phonological length contributes to positional uncertainty, perhaps by increasing the cognitive load associated with sequential encoding. Concrete words produced fewer omissions, indicating that semantic richness reduces outright failures to recall. The mixed-effects regression further confirmed that syllable count and concreteness independently predicted recall probability even when controlling for word frequency and familiarity, mitigating concerns that uncontrolled lexical factors drove the observed effects. Taken together, these pattern of results align with a hybrid view of verbal short-term memory in which multiple representational systems contribute to performance. Phonological processes tied to articulatory rehearsal and temporal capacity impose constraints that give rise to the word length effect. Concurrently, semantic and imagery-based encoding mechanisms improve retention and retrieval and give rise to the concreteness effect. The independence of these effects in our data argues against a strong compensatory account in which semantic richness fully offsets phonological demands, at least within the parameter space tested here. The findings also have practical implications for domains where short-term retention is critical. For example, educational materials that leverage concrete, imageable terms may enhance immediate retention even when phonological complexity is high. Similarly, user-interface design and verbal instructions can be optimized by favoring shorter words or adding concrete referents to reduce immediate recall errors. Several limitations of the present study temper these conclusions and point to promising directions for future research. First, although we controlled for frequency and familiarity as best as possible, complete elimination of all lexical confounds is difficult. Future studies could further refine matching or use larger stimulus sets combined with item-response modeling to more stringently control lexical covariates. Second, the task employed visual presentation and silent rehearsal without articulatory suppression, so conclusions about purely phonological maintenance versus articulatory rehearsal processes are inferential. Direct manipulation of rehearsal, for example through articulatory suppression, would help determine whether the word length effect observed here is primarily tied to articulatory rehearsal duration or to broader phonological trace decay. Third, the list length was fixed at five items to balance sensitivity and feasibility, but different list lengths might yield different interaction patterns. For instance, at longer list lengths where phonological capacity is more heavily taxed, semantic compensation might become more apparent and could produce an interaction that was not detectable here. Fourth, the participant sample consisted primarily of young adult native speakers, which limits generalizability to other populations. Examining developmental trajectories or clinical groups with known short-term memory deficits could reveal differential reliance on phonological versus semantic resources. Fifth, the lack of neurophysiological or neuroimaging measures precludes direct inferences about the neural substrates supporting the observed effects. Future research that combines behavioral manipulations with EEG or fMRI could identify whether separate neural networks underlie phonological and semantic contributions and whether their engagement varies by position in the list. Sixth, although our Bayes factor analyses provided evidence against the interaction within the sampled parameter space, very small interactions could remain undetected and might be theoretically meaningful under some models; larger samples or meta-analytic aggregation across studies would help adjudicate such subtle effects. Seventh, the present study used visual presentation, which interacts with modality-specific rehearsal processes; auditory presentation and presentation modality manipulations could test the robustness of the observed effects across input channels. Several concrete experimental extensions could build on the present findings. A study that manipulates both articulatory suppression and concreteness would reveal whether semantic benefits persist when overt rehearsal is disrupted. Another useful manipulation would be to vary presentation rate to alter the window for subvocal rehearsal and measure whether the length effect scales accordingly while concreteness remains stable. A developmental study could test whether children and older adults show similar additivity, or whether they rely more heavily on one representation system over the other. Computational modeling work that implements separable phonological and semantic modules could also be used to fit the present data and provide mechanistic accounts of how these influences accumulate over serial positions. Additionally, cross-linguistic work examining languages with different phonological and morphological complexity could test the generality of the word length effect and its interaction with semantic concreteness. In conclusion, the present experiment provides clear and robust evidence that word length and semantic concreteness independently influence immediate serial recall. The findings support theoretical perspectives that posit separable phonological and semantic contributions to verbal short-term memory and argue against strong compensatory interactions under the tested conditions. These results advance our understanding of the multiple representational systems that support short-term retention and identify fertile ground for future work aimed at delineating the mechanisms by which phonological and semantic information interact in the service of memory.
