Introduction

Siblings are a pervasive and enduring feature of many childhoods, and they play multiple roles in socialization, identity formation, and the development of interpersonal styles. Across developmental contexts, interactions with siblings provide practice in negotiation, conflict resolution, and the management of limited family resources, and they therefore have the potential to shape enduring tendencies such as competitiveness. The experience of competing with brothers and sisters for parental attention, material resources, and personal recognition can be a formative training ground for the development of competitive orientations that extend beyond the household. At the same time, siblings share many environmental and familial characteristics, and similarity in upbringing may either attenuate or amplify competitive dynamics depending on how resources and affection are distributed. These basic everyday realities make the sibling relationship a logical focus for understanding the origins of individual differences in competitive behavior.

Competitiveness is not a unitary phenomenon; rather, it tends to manifest differently across domains and social settings. Some individuals display heightened competitiveness in athletic or physical domains, others show it in academic or achievement settings, and still others may be more competitively oriented in social status contexts. The developmental roots of these domain-specific competitive tendencies likely involve a mix of structural family features, such as the number of siblings and birth order, and relational features, such as the quality of sibling interactions and the presence of overt rivalry. Structural aspects like sibling count have been discussed in the literature as shaping the distribution of parental attention and resources, leading to theories such as resource dilution which suggest that each additional sibling reduces per-child access to parental time and materials. A complementary perspective highlights that larger sibling networks may create more frequent opportunities for direct competition, which could socialize children into more competitive orientations as they vie for a limited set of parental reinforcers.

Relational aspects of the sibling bond, however, may be especially consequential. The degree to which siblings engage in rivalry, emulate each other, or support each other can vary substantially even among children from the same family. Some sibling relationships are characterized by cooperative learning and mutual support, while others are marked by frequent conflict and explicit comparison. It follows that the intensity and nature of sibling competition as experienced by an individual may better predict their later competitive behavior in other settings than simple counts of how many siblings they have. In other words, sibling rivalry as a psychological construct captures the lived experience of being in competition within the family, which is conceptually closer to a stance of competitiveness in external social contexts.

Empirical work has shown that sibling interactions can influence traits like sociability, aggression, and prosocial behavior, and there are theoretical reasons to expect an effect on competitiveness as well. For example, social learning processes operating in sibling dyads can teach strategies for winning, bargaining, and outperforming others; repeated practice of such behaviors might make a child more likely to behave competitively in peer contexts, including the classroom. Conversely, a child who experiences secure and cooperative sibling relations might be less inclined to approach same-age peers with an overtly competitive stance. These possible pathways suggest that sibling relational quality could be an important predictor of domain-specific competitiveness, including academic competitiveness with classmates.

Academic competitiveness is a particularly salient area to investigate because schooling places children in repeated, measurable competitive contexts such as grades, class rankings, and teacher attention. Academic competitiveness with classmates reflects the tendency to strive to outperform peers in school-related tasks, to be motivated by relative academic standing, and to persist in efforts to gain recognition for scholastic achievement. If sibling rivalry socializes children toward a zero-sum perspective on obtaining scarce parental or adult attention, this orientation may smoothly transfer to classroom contexts in which status and rewards are similarly distributed and visible. Alternatively, if sibling rivalry mainly reflects short-term positional struggles specific to family dynamics, it may not generalize to academic settings.

A different prediction can be derived from structural accounts. If the number of siblings increases everyday competition for parental resources, then greater sibling counts might lead to more generalized competitiveness, including academic competitiveness with classmates. Under such an account, each additional sibling would incrementally raise the likelihood that children need to fight for attention and develop a competitive strategy, producing a positive association between number of siblings and later competitiveness outside the family. Structural accounts also sometimes invoke mechanisms linked to socioeconomic strain: families with many children might induce children to fend for themselves more, potentially developing competitive skills. Yet such structural accounts assume homogeneity of sibling relationships; they do not directly account for the possibility that a single highly competitive sibling dyad in a small family could have more influence than multiple siblings in a harmonious household.

The distinction between structural and relational sibling factors matters for both theory and intervention. If sibling count is the primary driver of external competitiveness, then demographic trends and family planning patterns could be seen as shaping population-level changes in competitiveness among children. If sibling relational quality is more important, then interventions to reduce harmful sibling rivalry might be able to moderate competitive dispositions that carry over into school. An empirical test that simultaneously examines number of siblings and the degree of expressed sibling competitiveness therefore promises to adjudicate between these complementary perspectives.

Sport participation is another variable that could plausibly affect competitive orientations and the relationship between sibling influences and academic competitiveness. Participation in sports often provides explicit training in competition, rules of engagement, and the cultivation of winning strategies, and athletic involvement can socialize children to value outperforming others in performance contexts. Thus, children who participate in sports may display higher levels of general competitiveness. Sport participation could therefore confound or mediate any observed relationship between sibling variables and academic competitiveness if children from larger families or those with higher sibling rivalry are also more likely to play sports. Alternatively, sport participation might interact with sibling dynamics, such that the combination of competitive sibling relationships plus athletic socialization amplifies competitiveness in academic settings.

The literature on sibling effects and competitiveness contains mixed evidence regarding the relative importance of structure versus relationship. Some studies report modest associations between larger sibling numbers and competitive traits, whereas others find that the emotional tone of sibling interactions predicts later social outcomes more strongly than does simple sibling count. Few studies, however, have directly tested the hypothesis that sibling rivalry predicts domain-specific competitiveness outside the family while controlling for sibling count and sport participation. Moreover, much of the extant research has been conducted with younger children or has relied on parent reports rather than self-reports of competitive orientation in adolescence and early adulthood. There remains a need for studies that measure both structural and relational sibling variables, assess domain-specific competitiveness such as academic competitiveness with classmates, and consider other activities like sports that could influence competitiveness.

The present study sought to address these gaps by examining two primary hypotheses in a single correlational design. First, we tested whether having more siblings is associated with greater academic competitiveness with classmates, and whether that relationship persists after controlling for sport participation. This hypothesis operationalizes the structural account of sibling influence on competitiveness. Second, we tested whether greater sibling competitiveness—capturing the subjective degree of rivalry and competition experienced within the family—is associated with higher academic competitiveness with classmates. This hypothesis operationalizes the relational account. The sample consisted of 100 participants who completed a 19-item questionnaire that assessed number of siblings, sibling competitiveness, academic competitiveness with classmates, and sport participation. Psychometric properties such as internal consistency and face validity were evaluated for the multi-item scales.

A clear pattern favoring the relational over the structural explanation would be indicated by a significant positive association between sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness coupled with a null association between number of siblings and academic competitiveness. A different pattern would emerge if number of siblings also shows a similar association, suggesting that both structural and relational sibling features are important. If sport participation accounts for variance in academic competitiveness or moderates the sibling-competitiveness link, that would highlight the importance of considering extracurricular socialization in models of competitive development. By measuring these variables together, the study aims to provide an integrated look at how family composition and sibling relational processes relate to competitive orientations in an achievement domain.

Methods

Participants were 100 young adults recruited from a university research participant pool and from community advertisements in a mid-sized city. The sample size of 100 was chosen to provide adequate statistical power to detect moderate correlations and to allow for exploratory analyses such as multiple regression and simple moderation tests. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 25 years, with a mean age of 20.3 years and a standard deviation of 2.1 years. The sample included both males and females; 56 participants identified as female and 44 identified as male. Participants represented diverse family backgrounds in terms of parental education and socioeconomic descriptors, although the sample was drawn from a predominantly urban and suburban catchment area and therefore may not capture all population subgroups.

Recruitment materials invited individuals who had at least one sibling or who had grown up in a household with siblings, although participants without siblings were not excluded if they were willing to answer questions about family experiences relevant to the measures. In practice, 92 percent of the sample reported having at least one sibling at the time of the measure or in early childhood; the remaining 8 percent reported being an only child but were included for completeness of the sibling count variable. Participants received course credit or a small monetary compensation for participation, and they provided informed consent in accordance with institutional review procedures.

Measures consisted of a 19-item questionnaire specifically developed for the study to assess four core constructs: number of siblings, sibling competitiveness, academic competitiveness with classmates, and sport participation. The number of siblings was a single numeric item asking participants to report how many biological or legally recognized siblings they had (full, half, or step-siblings were included in the count if the participant lived with or regularly interacted with them during childhood). For analytic purposes, the number of siblings variable was treated as a continuous count variable and was examined also in descriptive terms (mean, range, distribution shape).

Sibling competitiveness was assessed with a seven-item scale designed to capture the participant’s subjective perception of rivalry, competition for parental attention, comparison, and conflict with siblings during upbringing. Items included statements such as "I often felt I had to compete with my siblings for my parents’ attention," and "My interactions with my siblings frequently involved trying to outdo one another." Participants rated each item on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). After data collection, item analyses were conducted to estimate internal consistency reliability, and a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was computed.

Academic competitiveness with classmates was measured with a seven-item scale aimed at tapping the participant’s tendency to strive to outperform peers in educational settings, to be motivated by relative academic standing, and to engage in academic behaviors motivated by competition. Example items included "I care about doing better than my classmates in school," and "I feel motivated to study harder when I know a classmate is getting higher grades." These items used the same five-point Likert response format as the sibling competitiveness items. Internal consistency reliability was estimated using Cronbach’s alpha, and scale scores were computed by averaging item responses for each participant.

Sport participation was assessed with a single item that asked participants to report whether they had participated in organized sports during childhood or adolescence (yes/no) and a follow-up frequency item that asked how many seasons per year on average they participated in organized sports (scored 0 to 4+). For the primary analyses, sport participation was coded dichotomously (participated in organized sports at least once during childhood/adolescence = 1, did not participate = 0) to provide a conservative test of its potential confounding influence. Supplementary analyses used the frequency score as a continuous measure.

Survey administration was conducted in group sessions in a supervised laboratory setting and through an online secure survey portal for those who could not attend in person. Both modalities presented the 19-item questionnaire in the same order and with identical instructions. Participants were instructed to respond based on their recollections of their childhood and adolescence up to the age of 18, a convention intended to anchor responses to the developmental period relevant for sibling socialization and organized sport experiences. To minimize demand characteristics, the cover sheet described the study broadly as investigating "family and school experiences" and did not explicitly emphasize competitiveness.

Data screening procedures included checks for missing data, response sets, and outliers. Missing item responses were rare (less than 1 percent of item responses across the dataset) and were handled by mean substitution at the scale level for participants missing no more than one item on a given multi-item scale. Participants who failed attention checks or who provided identical responses across all items were examined for potential exclusion; no participant met the exclusion criteria. Scale distributions were inspected for skewness and kurtosis; when necessary, transformations were considered, but none were required given acceptable distributional properties.

Statistical analyses were specified in advance and were designed to answer the two main hypotheses. First, Pearson product-moment correlations between number of siblings and academic competitiveness were computed to test the claim that having more siblings is associated with higher academic competitiveness. A partial correlation controlling for sport participation was also computed to determine whether any observed association held when accounting for athletic socialization. Second, Pearson correlations between sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness were computed to test the relational hypothesis. A partial correlation controlling for sport participation was likewise computed. To provide a multivariate perspective, hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted in which number of siblings and sport participation were entered in the first step, and sibling competitiveness was entered in the second step to examine incremental predictive value and to quantify effect sizes.

Additional exploratory analyses included testing for moderation by sport participation and gender. Interaction terms were created for sport participation by sibling competitiveness and were entered into regression models to test whether the association between sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness differed by sport involvement. Gender differences in the main variables were examined using t-tests. Assumptions for correlational and regression analyses, including linearity, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity, were examined using scatterplots, residual plots, and variance inflation factors.

All statistical tests used two-tailed significance levels with an alpha of .05. Effect sizes were reported as Pearson r for bivariate associations and as standardized beta coefficients for regression analyses. Confidence intervals around key correlation coefficients and regression coefficients were computed to provide precision estimates. Given the sample size of 100, the study had adequate power (> .80) to detect correlations of approximately r = .28 and larger at the conventional alpha level.

Ethical procedures included informed consent, anonymization of survey responses, and secure data storage. The local institutional review board reviewed the procedures and granted approval prior to data collection. Participants were debriefed following completion of the survey and provided with contact information for the research team in case they had questions about the study.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the main variables are presented first. The number of siblings reported by participants ranged from 0 to 5, with a mean of 1.72 and a standard deviation of 1.05. The distribution of sibling counts was slightly positively skewed but not excessively so; the modal value was 2 siblings. The sibling competitiveness scale had a mean of 3.45 on the 1-to-5 response scale with a standard deviation of 0.78, indicating that, on average, participants reported moderate levels of perceived sibling rivalry. The academic competitiveness with classmates scale had a mean of 3.30 with a standard deviation of 0.84, likewise indicating moderate tendencies toward academic competitiveness. Sixty-two participants (62 percent) reported participation in organized sports during childhood or adolescence, whereas 38 participants (38 percent) reported no organized sport participation.

Internal consistency estimates supported the reliability of the multi-item scales. The sibling competitiveness scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of .82, and the academic competitiveness scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of .86. Item-total correlations were generally strong and positive for both scales, suggesting coherent unidimensional constructs that were appropriate for averaging into scale scores. The sport participation dichotomy showed expected associations with the frequency follow-up, with those coded as participating reporting a median frequency of 2 seasons per year.

Turning to the primary hypothesis tests, the correlation between number of siblings and academic competitiveness was small and not statistically significant. The Pearson correlation coefficient for number of siblings with academic competitiveness was r = .07, with a two-tailed p-value of .48. The 95 percent confidence interval for this correlation included zero (approximately −.12 to .24), indicating no reliable linear association in this sample. Visual inspection of a scatterplot did not reveal a nonlinear relationship or clear pattern of association that might have been obscured by using Pearson correlation; points were broadly dispersed across the number of siblings range without a consistent slope.

A partial correlation controlling for sport participation was computed to check whether sport involvement masked any association between sibling count and academic competitiveness. The partial correlation controlling for sport participation was rpartial = .05 with a two-tailed p-value of .63, which again indicated no significant relationship. Inclusion of sport participation as a covariate did not meaningfully change the association or the effect size estimate.

In contrast, the relational hypothesis received support. The correlation between sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness with classmates was positive and statistically significant. The Pearson correlation coefficient was r = .31, with a two-tailed p-value of .002, indicating a medium-sized effect. The 95 percent confidence interval for this correlation ranged approximately from .13 to .47, providing evidence that the association was unlikely to be due to sampling variability alone. Visual inspection of a scatterplot of sibling competitiveness against academic competitiveness showed a positive monotonic pattern, with higher reported sibling rivalry tending to co-occur with higher reported academic competitiveness.

When sport participation was controlled for in a partial correlation, the association between sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness remained reliable. The partial correlation controlling for sport participation was rpartial = .29 with a two-tailed p-value of .004. This indicates that the observed association was not an artifact of participation in organized sports; sibling rivalry accounted for unique variance in academic competitiveness beyond any effect of sport involvement.

To provide a more complete multivariate picture, a hierarchical regression analysis was conducted with academic competitiveness as the dependent variable. In the first step, number of siblings and sport participation were entered. This initial model accounted for a small proportion of variance in academic competitiveness (R^2 = .02), and the overall model was not statistically significant (F(2,97) = 1.08, p = .34). Neither number of siblings (standardized beta = .07, p = .48) nor sport participation (standardized beta = .11, p = .27) was a significant predictor in step one.

In the second step, sibling competitiveness was added to the model. The addition of sibling competitiveness produced a significant increment in explained variance (ΔR^2 = .08, p = .003), bringing the total R^2 to .10 for the full model. In the final model, sibling competitiveness emerged as a significant predictor of academic competitiveness (standardized beta = .29, p = .003), while number of siblings remained non-significant (standardized beta = .04, p = .66) and sport participation remained non-significant (standardized beta = .09, p = .33). These results indicate that sibling competitiveness uniquely accounted for variance in academic competitiveness beyond structural sibling count and sport involvement.

Given the theoretical possibility that sport participation might moderate the relationship between sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness, an interaction term was tested. The interaction between sport participation (dichotomous) and sibling competitiveness was entered in a third regression step, but the interaction term did not yield a significant effect (standardized beta for interaction = .08, p = .28), and the ΔR^2 attributable to the interaction was negligible (< .01). This suggests that the positive association between sibling rivalry and academic competitiveness did not differ meaningfully between participants who had and had not participated in organized sports.

Gender differences in the main variables were also assessed. Independent-samples t-tests revealed no significant differences between males and females on sibling competitiveness (t(98) = 0.84, p = .40) or on academic competitiveness (t(98) = 1.02, p = .31). There was likewise no meaningful gender difference in the number of siblings reported (t(98) = 0.31, p = .76) or in the proportion reporting sport participation (chi-square = 0.95, p = .33). These analyses suggest that gender did not confound the primary associations of interest in this sample.

Assumptions for the correlational and regression analyses were examined and found to be adequately met. Residual plots from the final regression model displayed no obvious patterns indicative of heteroscedasticity, and variance inflation factors for predictor variables were all below 1.5, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. Cook’s distance values for individual cases were all below the commonly used threshold of .5, suggesting that no single case exerts undue influence on the results.

A post hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted to estimate the detectable effect size with the given sample size. With N = 100 and alpha = .05 (two-tailed), power analyses indicate that the study had greater than .80 power to detect correlations of approximately r = .28 or larger, which aligns with the observed significant correlation of r = .31 for sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness. The null relation between sibling count and academic competitiveness therefore likely reflects a true small effect rather than a large but undetected effect.

To explore potential nonlinearities further, number of siblings was categorized into three groups (0–1, 2, 3+) and compared on academic competitiveness using one-way ANOVA. The ANOVA showed no significant group differences (F(2,97) = 0.52, p = .60), and post-hoc contrasts revealed no systematic pattern. This categorical analysis corroborated the correlational findings that sibling count was not related to academic competitiveness in the sample.

Finally, supplementary correlational analyses showed that number of siblings was only weakly correlated with sibling competitiveness (r = .12, p = .22). This indicates that structural family features such as how many siblings one has were not strongly related to the subjective experience of rivalry in this sample. Together, the findings demonstrate a consistent pattern: the qualitative experience of sibling competitiveness predicts academic competitiveness with classmates, whereas the mere count of siblings does not.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether structural aspects of family composition (number of siblings) and relational features of sibling dynamics (self-reported sibling competitiveness) predicted academic competitiveness with classmates, and whether sport participation accounted for or moderated these associations. Two primary findings emerged. First, number of siblings was not associated with academic competitiveness in this sample; neither a bivariate correlation nor adjusted analyses controlling for sport participation yielded evidence that more siblings predicted greater academic competitiveness. Second, sibling competitiveness—the subjective perception of rivalry and competition within the family—was positively and reliably associated with academic competitiveness with classmates, and this association persisted when controlling for sport participation. Sport participation itself did not explain the sibling-competitiveness relationship nor did it moderate the effect.

These results carry theoretical implications for understanding how family experiences translate into competitive orientations in achievement settings. The null finding for sibling count suggests that structural family features alone are insufficient to account for individual differences in competition outside the home. This result casts doubt on simplistic versions of structural or resource-dilution arguments which posit a monotonic relationship between more siblings and greater competitiveness. Structural perspectives may overestimate the homogeneity of sibling influences; families with the same number of children can vary dramatically in parenting style, sibling relational quality, and resource availability, factors that may all influence competitiveness more directly.

In contrast, the robust association between perceived sibling competitiveness and academic competitiveness supports a relational mechanism in which the lived experience of rivalry socializes children to adopt competitive stances in other contexts. Social learning and repeated practice of competition within the family likely produce transferable strategies, motivations, and expectancies that make an individual more inclined to treat school settings as domains for social comparison and striving to outperform peers. Sibling rivalry entails not only the experience of competition but also the emotional meanings attached to wins and losses, the modeling of aggressive or strategic approaches to outperforming others, and the reinforcement contingencies that make competitive behavior rewarding. These relational processes are plausibly more proximal to domain-specific competitive conduct than numerical counts of siblings.

The finding that sport participation did not explain the relationship between sibling rivalry and academic competitiveness suggests that the sibling-based socialization of competitiveness operates independently of athletic socialization, at least in this sample. While organized sports provide a structured context in which children learn to compete, the fact that sibling competitiveness predicted academic competitiveness beyond sport involvement indicates that family dynamics contribute uniquely to academic competitive orientations. Moreover, the lack of moderation by sport participation implies that sports do not exacerbate or attenuate the sibling-to-academic competitiveness linkage in a straightforward way; rather, sibling rivalry may be a consistent predictor across different extracurricular backgrounds.

These findings invite further theoretical refinement. A plausible account is that competitive orientations are multi-determined, with both family relational experiences and extrafamilial socialization contributing in domain-specific ways. Sibling rivalry may be particularly potent for shaping competitiveness in domains where interpersonal comparison is salient and the rewards are social or symbolic—such as classroom performance. Sports may cultivate physical or procedural competition skills that do not necessarily transfer to academic competition unless reinforced by family or school contexts that link athletic success to scholastic status. Understanding the boundaries of such transfer requires more fine-grained measurement of the content of competitiveness learned in different contexts.

The weak association observed between number of siblings and reported sibling competitiveness in this study also illuminates why sibling count may not predict external competitiveness: having more siblings does not automatically generate greater rivalry. Some large families prioritize cooperative values, and parents may intentionally structure sibling interactions to minimize competition. Conversely, small families can contain intense rivalry due to differential treatment, close age spacing, or other dynamics. Thus, relational factors mediate or moderate the translation of structural features into behavioral tendencies. The results underscore the importance of assessing both structural and relational dimensions in any account of family influences on personality and social behavior.

Several methodological considerations shape the interpretation of the findings. The cross-sectional and correlational design precludes strong causal inferences. Although the pattern of associations is consistent with the hypothesis that sibling rivalry socializes children to be more competitive academically, it is also possible that inherently competitive children elicit more rivalry from siblings, or that a third variable (such as parental emphasis on achievement) fosters both sibling rivalry and academic competitiveness. Longitudinal designs that track sibling dynamics and competitive orientations over time would be better suited to disentangle directionality.

Another limitation concerns measurement. The study relied on retrospective self-reports of sibling rivalry and sport participation, and such reports may be affected by memory biases, current mood states, or self-presentational concerns. Moreover, the questionnaire was developed for the study and, while the multi-item scales showed acceptable internal consistency, their construct validity would be strengthened by convergent evidence from observational data, parent reports, or objective measures of competitive behavior. Future research could incorporate multimethod assessments to triangulate the construct of sibling competition and to observe whether self-reported tendencies predict real-world competitive actions.

Sample characteristics also constrain generalizability. Participants were young adults, largely drawn from a university and local community, and the developmental timing of measurement may capture a particular snapshot in the life course. Competitiveness may manifest differently in adolescence versus early adulthood, and cultural, socioeconomic, and regional differences could moderate the generalizability of the findings. For example, in cultures that emphasize collective values or de-prioritize individual achievement, the transfer of sibling rivalry to academic competitiveness might be attenuated. Thus, replication across diverse samples and age groups is warranted.

The measures of sport participation used here were relatively coarse, and deeper differentiation between types of sport (individual vs. team), levels of competitiveness within sport, and the social climate of athletic contexts would provide a richer test of potential moderating effects. It remains possible that participation in elite-level competitive sports or in team contexts that emphasize inter-team rivalry could interact differently with sibling dynamics to shape academic competitiveness.

Future research directions include longitudinal studies that measure sibling relational processes prospectively and track academic competitiveness and achievement outcomes over time. Such designs could clarify the temporal precedence and potential reciprocal relations between sibling rivalry and competitiveness in school. Experimental or intervention studies that reduce harmful sibling rivalry through parent training or family therapy could also test whether modifying sibling interactions leads to changes in children’s competitive orientations in peer contexts. Observational studies of sibling interaction patterns, coded for competitive behaviors, parental responses, and conflict resolution strategies, would also enrich understanding of the micro-processes through which familial competition is transmitted.

Additional important extensions involve examining moderators such as birth order, age spacing between siblings, gender constellation (same-sex versus mixed-sex siblings), and parental differential treatment. Birth order, for example, has been theorized to relate to competitiveness and achievement orientation, but its effects likely interact with the intensity of sibling rivalry and family context. Age spacing could influence the degree of direct competition experienced; closely spaced siblings may be more likely to directly compare and compete, whereas widely spaced siblings may have more differentiated roles. Clarifying how these factors combine would refine both theory and practical recommendations.

The relation between sibling rivalry and academic competitiveness also raises questions about outcome valence. Competitiveness can be motivationally adaptive, fostering persistence, improved study habits, and higher performance; alternatively, it can be maladaptive if it undermines cooperation, increases stress, or prompts unethical tactics. Future research should therefore examine whether sibling-driven academic competitiveness predicts objective academic outcomes, well-being, peer relationships, and ethical behavior. Understanding when competitiveness is functional versus harmful is crucial for guiding parents and educators in shaping sibling and school environments.

Practical implications follow from the study’s pattern of results. If sibling relational dynamics influence academic competitiveness, parents and practitioners could focus on fostering healthy sibling interactions that emphasize mastery, cooperation, and mutual support rather than zero-sum contest. Schools could be mindful that some students might approach classroom activities with a rivalry-laden orientation rooted in family dynamics and could provide opportunities for cooperative learning and de-emphasize hyper-competitive ranking where appropriate. Coaches and sport instructors might also be mindful that athletic competitiveness does not operate in isolation from family influences and should balance competitive skill-building with development of prosocial values.

In sum, the study contributes to the literature by demonstrating that subjective experiences of sibling rivalry predict academic competitiveness with classmates, whereas simple counts of siblings do not. These findings highlight the primacy of relational family processes over structural sibling features in shaping domain-specific competitive orientations. The results encourage a shift in focus from mere demographic descriptors toward the qualitative nature of sibling interactions when trying to understand the developmental origins of competitiveness. Future longitudinal, multimethod, and culturally diverse research will be needed to further unpack causal mechanisms, moderators, and the implications of sibling-driven competitive orientations for academic and social outcomes.
