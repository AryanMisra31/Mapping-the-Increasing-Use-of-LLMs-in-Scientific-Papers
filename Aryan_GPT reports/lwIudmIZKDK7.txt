Introduction

Self-efficacy is a central construct in understanding how people translate intentions into actions. Bandura originally articulated self-efficacy as people’s judgments of their capabilities to organize and execute courses of action required to attain designated types of performance. From this perspective, self-efficacy is both motivational and instrumental because it shapes the choices people make, the effort they expend, and their persistence in the face of difficulty. Over the decades, research has linked self-efficacy to a wide array of outcomes across domains such as education, health, work, and social functioning. In the educational domain, self-efficacy has been proposed as a key predictor of academic motivation, engagement, and ultimately academic performance. Students with higher academic self-efficacy are theorized to select more challenging tasks, invest more effort, and persist longer on difficult problems, all of which should translate into better grades and learning outcomes. In the health domain, self-efficacy has been invoked as an important determinant of health behaviors. People who believe they can enact healthy behaviors are more likely to adopt and maintain those behaviors, from exercise routines to dietary choices and adherence to medical regimens.

Empirical work has provided substantial, but not unequivocal, support for these theoretical claims. Across many studies, higher self-efficacy has been associated with higher academic achievement and with healthier lifestyle choices. However, the magnitude of these associations varies by context, the measures employed, and the populations sampled. Some studies report strong, significant associations between self-efficacy and academic grades or standardized test performance. Other studies report more modest or even nonsignificant associations, particularly when self-efficacy measures are domain-general rather than task-specific. Similarly, research on health behaviors shows that self-efficacy is often an important predictor of behaviors such as exercise adherence and smoking cessation, but the strength of prediction depends on whether self-efficacy is measured in relation to the specific behavior and whether objective or self-reported behaviors are used as outcomes.

A number of mechanisms have been proposed to explain why self-efficacy should relate to both academic performance and healthy lifestyle choices. First, self-efficacy influences the selection of goals and activities. People high in self-efficacy may set higher performance standards and choose study strategies and health behaviors that align with long-term goals. Second, self-efficacy shapes effort and persistence. When difficulties arise, those with higher self-efficacy are more likely to sustain effort and apply problem-focused coping strategies that facilitate success. Third, self-efficacy affects cognitive processes such as attention, planning, and the perceived utility of tasks, which can improve learning and the adoption of health-supportive routines. Fourth, emotional reactions to setbacks are moderated by self-efficacy; those with higher self-efficacy tend to experience less debilitating anxiety and depressive symptoms when facing obstacles, which preserves functioning in academic and health domains.

Despite these well-developed theoretical links, some factors complicate the relationship between self-efficacy and outcomes. Measurement issues are prominent among these complications. Studies that use unvalidated or domain-general measures of self-efficacy may underestimate the true association with specific outcomes. Similarly, reliance on self-reported outcome measures introduces shared-method variance that can inflate associations, whereas objective measures can provide a sterner test of hypothesized relations. Sample characteristics and size also matter. Correlations that are robust in large, representative samples can appear weak or nonsignificant in smaller, convenience samples. Cultural factors may moderate associations as well, because the meaning and behavioral manifestations of self-efficacy can differ across cultural contexts. For example, the pathways linking self-efficacy to performance may be altered by norms about effort, modesty, or interdependence. Gender can also play a moderating role, as gendered socialization may lead to differences in domain-specific self-efficacy beliefs and in the behavioral expression of those beliefs.

Given this background, it is important to consider prior work that has examined links between self-efficacy, academic achievement, and health behaviors in more detail. Studies focusing on adolescents and young adults often show positive associations between academic self-efficacy and academic performance, with effect sizes that vary but are frequently in the small-to-moderate range. Research that examines health-related self-efficacy commonly finds that greater self-efficacy predicts a higher likelihood of engaging in exercise and healthier eating behaviors. Interventions that explicitly target self-efficacy through mastery experiences, social modeling, and verbal persuasion often produce improvements in the targeted behaviors, lending support to a causal interpretation of the association. Nevertheless, replication attempts sometimes yield weaker effects, particularly when measures are not tailored or when researchers use cross-sectional designs that limit causal inference.

The literature also contains findings related to moderating roles of gender and culture. Some studies report that female students may report different levels of academic self-efficacy than male students in particular domains, such as mathematics or science. Other work indicates that cultural groups differ in their mean levels of self-efficacy or in how self-efficacy relates to outcomes, with interdependent cultures sometimes showing weaker direct effects of self-efficacy on individual performance outcomes, possibly because collective factors and social expectations play a larger role. Comparisons between ethnic groups within the same educational systems have sometimes revealed small but meaningful differences in self-efficacy and related behaviors. However, these patterns are not universal and often depend on socio-economic context, stereotype threat, and the specificity of the self-efficacy measure.

Another important consideration is the distinction between general self-efficacy and domain-specific self-efficacy. General self-efficacy scales aim to capture a broad sense of competence across a variety of situations. Domain-specific scales, in contrast, assess beliefs about one’s abilities within a particular area, such as academic tasks or health behaviors. Research suggests that domain-specific measures are typically better predictors of behavior in their respective domains than are general measures. For instance, academic self-efficacy tends to predict academic performance better than general self-efficacy measures. Likewise, exercise self-efficacy is a stronger predictor of physical activity than general self-efficacy. The implication is that researchers and practitioners need to align their measures to the behavioral domain under study in order to obtain accurate assessments of the predictive role of self-efficacy.

Methodological weaknesses in existing research also merit attention. A substantial proportion of studies rely on convenience samples, cross-sectional designs, and self-report instruments that have not been validated for the specific population under study. These limitations constrain our confidence in the generalizability and interpretation of findings. Cross-sectional studies can document associations but cannot establish temporal order or causality. Self-report measures are susceptible to social desirability and recall biases, which may be particularly salient when assessing health behaviors or academic achievement in contexts where participants may feel evaluated.

Given these theoretical considerations and empirical patterns, the present study sought to examine the relationships among self-efficacy, academic performance, and lifestyle choices in a convenience sample of young adults. Our primary aim was to test whether self-efficacy would be positively associated with both academic performance and healthy lifestyle choices, consistent with the bulk of prior research. We also aimed to explore whether gender and cultural background—operationalized here as self-identified White and Asian cultural groups—moderated these associations. Finally, we sought to examine the psychometric properties of the measures used in this study and to reflect on methodological lessons for future work.

We recognized that several constraints would shape our investigation. Our study used a cross-sectional correlational design and a self-report questionnaire to collect data. The instrument included a self-efficacy scale that we developed for this project, as well as brief measures of academic performance and health-related lifestyle choices. Because the self-efficacy scale was self-designed, we anticipated that its psychometric properties might be imperfect. We nonetheless proceeded with the study because exploratory evidence can be valuable in identifying robust patterns that deserve follow-up in more rigorous designs.

We formulated three explicit hypotheses to guide the analysis. First, we hypothesized that self-efficacy would show a positive association with academic performance, although we acknowledged that the association might be modest given the use of a general self-efficacy measure. Second, we hypothesized that self-efficacy would be positively and significantly associated with healthy lifestyle choices, given the consistent findings in the health behavior literature. Third, we hypothesized that gender and cultural group might account for additional variability in the outcomes and might moderate the strength of the associations between self-efficacy and the two outcome domains. We anticipated that any moderating effects would be exploratory given sample size and measurement constraints.

The present study therefore contributes to the broader literature by providing an exploratory test of these hypotheses in a sample that allowed for comparisons across gender and two cultural groups. The study also provides an opportunity to reflect on measurement practices and to draw lessons about the importance of validated instruments and design choices in self-efficacy research. In light of these goals, we collected data from 100 participants and conducted correlational and group comparison analyses to test the hypotheses described above. The next sections describe the methods used, the results obtained, and a detailed discussion of implications, limitations, and directions for future research.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university and community sample. The total sample consisted of 100 participants. Participants ranged in age from late adolescence to middle adulthood, but the majority were emerging adults between 18 and 25 years old. Gender composition was approximately balanced but slightly skewed; the sample included both females and males and a small number of participants who identified as nonbinary. For analytic purposes we focused on the two predominant gender categories to preserve statistical power. Participants self-identified their cultural background. The most common self-identified cultural groups were White and Asian, and these two groups formed the basis for cultural comparisons in our analyses. Individuals from other cultural groups were present in the dataset but in numbers too small to support meaningful group comparisons, and so they were not included in the cultural moderation analyses.

Participants were recruited via flyers, classroom announcements, and online postings. The recruitment material invited volunteers to complete an online questionnaire about study habits and lifestyle behaviors. Participation was voluntary and participants provided informed consent prior to beginning the questionnaire. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by an institutional review process consistent with standard ethical practices for minimal-risk research. Participants were free to skip any question and could withdraw at any time without penalty. No identifying personal data were collected, and responses were stored on a secure, password-protected server.

The questionnaire consisted of demographic items, a self-efficacy scale developed for this project, a brief academic performance measure, and a lifestyle choices inventory. The self-efficacy scale contained 12 items designed to assess participants’ beliefs in their general ability to organize and execute behaviors leading to desired outcomes in academic and daily-life contexts. Items were phrased to capture mastery expectations and confidence about overcoming obstacles. Example items included statements such as "I can usually figure out how to study effectively when material is difficult" and "When obstacles arise, I can find ways to continue working toward my goals." Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Items were summed to yield a total self-efficacy score, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy.

The academic performance measure was a brief self-report indicator of current academic standing. Participants reported their cumulative grade point average (GPA) when applicable, or they provided a categorical rating of their academic performance on a scale from 1 (below average) to 5 (excellent) if they were not in a formal graded program. For the purposes of correlation and regression analyses, we converted categorical ratings to a continuous scale and standardized the values to enable comparison across participants. We acknowledged that self-reported GPA is less reliable than official records but included it because it was a pragmatic way to obtain academic information from both students and non-students.

The lifestyle choices inventory assessed behaviors relevant to healthy living. This inventory consisted of 10 items that queried frequency of engagement in health-promoting activities over the past month. Items included regular physical activity, consumption of fruits and vegetables, limited intake of processed foods, regular sleep schedule, and avoidance of excessive alcohol consumption. Responses were made on a 5-point frequency scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Items were combined to form a composite lifestyle index, with higher scores indicating healthier lifestyle choices. The inventory was designed to capture a broad set of behaviors relevant to well-being rather than to provide a clinically validated health behavior assessment.

Before fielding the main study, the questionnaire was piloted with a small convenience sample to ensure clarity of instructions and items. The pilot feedback led to minor wording changes but did not include formal validation or factor analysis. We recognized at the outset that the self-efficacy scale and lifestyle inventory were self-designed instruments and therefore lacked the extensive psychometric validation typical of standardized measures. Despite this limitation, we proceeded because the goal was exploratory: to assess whether expected patterns would emerge in the data.

Data collection occurred over a period of several weeks. Participants completed the questionnaire in an online format that required approximately 15 to 20 minutes. Response data were inspected for completeness and quality. Cases with extensive missing data (more than 20% of items unanswered) were removed from analyses. A small number of participants had minor missing item responses, which were mean-imputed within scale to preserve sample size. After data cleaning, the final analytic sample remained at 100 participants because all recruited participants provided sufficiently complete data.

Statistical analyses were planned to examine descriptive statistics, internal consistency of scales, bivariate correlations, and group comparisons. We calculated means, standard deviations, and scale reliabilities (Cronbach’s alpha) for the self-efficacy and lifestyle indices. Bivariate correlations were computed using Pearson’s r to test associations among self-efficacy, academic performance, and lifestyle choices. We examined the statistical significance of these correlations using two-tailed tests. To explore the unique contribution of self-efficacy controlling for demographic factors, we conducted multiple regression analyses with academic performance and lifestyle index as dependent variables in separate models. Predictors included self-efficacy, gender, and cultural group, and interaction terms were included to probe potential moderating effects of gender and culture on the self-efficacy-outcome relations.

Group comparisons were conducted using independent samples t-tests. We compared mean self-efficacy, academic performance, and lifestyle index scores across gender groups and across the White and Asian cultural groups. Where appropriate, effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d to provide a standardized metric of group differences. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Statistical significance was evaluated at the conventional alpha level of .05. We also interpreted effect sizes to assess practical significance given the exploratory nature of the study and the limited sample size.

In addition to inferential tests, we conducted diagnostic checks for normality and distributional assumptions. Scale distributions were inspected for skewness and kurtosis, and variables showing substantial deviation from normality were transformed or analyzed with nonparametric methods as a robustness check. We also considered the possibility of multicollinearity in regression models by inspecting variance inflation factors for predictor variables. Data management and analyses were documented in a reproducible script to ensure transparency of analytic decisions.

Finally, we pre-specified sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of measurement choices. Because the academic performance measure combined GPA and categorical ratings, we ran parallel analyses restricted to participants who reported GPA to confirm that the overall pattern of findings was consistent. We also ran models excluding participants who identified as nonbinary due to small numbers to confirm that the gender-related findings were not driven by a very small subgroup. These planned sensitivity checks provided confidence that the reported patterns were not artifacts of particular coding decisions.

Results

Descriptive statistics are presented first to provide an overview of the data. The self-efficacy scale had a mean score indicating moderate to high levels of perceived capability in the sample. The lifestyle index likewise showed a mid-range average, with variability indicating that some participants reported very health-promoting choices while others reported fewer such practices. The academic performance measure exhibited a positive skew among participants who reported GPA, and the combined academic index showed reasonably normal distribution after standardization. Cronbach’s alpha for the self-efficacy scale was calculated to assess internal consistency. The self-efficacy scale returned an alpha coefficient indicative of acceptable internal consistency for exploratory research, but the value suggested room for improvement in future iterations of the measure. The lifestyle inventory produced a Cronbach’s alpha that reflected acceptable internal reliability, although the alpha was not so high as to suggest redundancy among items.

Bivariate correlations were computed to test the primary study hypotheses. Self-efficacy showed a positive correlation with academic performance, but this relation did not reach conventional levels of statistical significance. The magnitude of the association was modest, suggesting a trend in the expected direction but insufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis in this sample. In contrast, self-efficacy showed a positive and statistically significant correlation with the lifestyle index. This latter association was moderate in size and consistent with prior literature indicating that higher self-efficacy is associated with healthier lifestyle choices.

Because the self-efficacy-academic correlation was non-significant, we examined possible reasons for the weaker-than-expected result. First, we checked descriptive properties and found that academic performance scores among participants were somewhat compressed at the high end, suggesting a ceiling effect for some respondents. This restriction of range could attenuate the observed correlation between self-efficacy and academic performance. Second, we noted that the self-efficacy measure was general rather than strictly academic, which could reduce its predictive power for academic outcomes. Third, the modest sample size limited statistical power to detect small-to-moderate associations.

Multiple regression analyses were conducted to examine the unique contribution of self-efficacy to academic performance and lifestyle choices while adjusting for demographic variables. In the model predicting academic performance, self-efficacy did not emerge as a significant predictor when gender and cultural group were included as covariates. The regression coefficients suggested a positive effect of self-efficacy on academic performance, but the standard error estimates were large relative to the coefficient, producing non-significant t-values. Gender and cultural group did not account for a large proportion of variance in academic performance, although cultural group differences showed a small effect in the direction of slightly higher academic index scores among participants who identified as Asian compared to those who identified as White.

In the regression model predicting the lifestyle index, self-efficacy was a significant positive predictor even after adjusting for gender and cultural group. The standardized coefficient indicated that self-efficacy accounted for a meaningful proportion of variance in healthy lifestyle choices. Gender did not significantly predict lifestyle index scores in this model, but cultural group differences emerged as a small-to-moderate effect, with the White group reporting slightly higher lifestyle index scores on average than the Asian group. The interaction terms testing moderation by gender and culture on the self-efficacy-lifestyle relation were not statistically significant, suggesting that the association between self-efficacy and healthy lifestyle was broadly similar across these demographic subgroups.

Independent samples t-tests were conducted to compare mean scores on the main variables across gender groups. Females reported slightly higher self-efficacy scores on average than males, but the difference was not statistically significant. Females and males did not differ substantially in lifestyle index scores in t-tests, although descriptive trends suggested marginal differences that did not withstand correction for multiple comparisons. For academic performance, gender differences were also non-significant in t-tests. These t-test findings were broadly consistent with regression models that failed to identify robust gender effects in the current sample.

Group comparisons between participants who identified as White and those who identified as Asian revealed a few statistically detectable differences. The Asian group reported marginally higher academic performance on average compared to the White group, and this difference reached statistical significance in the t-test. Conversely, the White group reported slightly higher lifestyle index scores than the Asian group, and this difference was statistically significant in the t-test. Self-efficacy means did not differ significantly between these cultural groups. These group differences were of small-to-moderate effect size, and they persisted in regression models controlling for gender and self-efficacy.

To probe whether the self-efficacy-academic null finding could be due to measurement issues, we conducted sensitivity analyses restricted to participants who reported GPA values. In this subsample, the correlation between self-efficacy and GPA was slightly larger than in the full sample but remained non-significant. This pattern suggested that the use of combined categorical and continuous academic measures was not the primary cause of the weak association. We also ran robustness checks using nonparametric Spearman correlations, which produced a similar pattern of results: a non-significant association between self-efficacy and academic performance and a significant positive association between self-efficacy and lifestyle choices.

We examined potential multicollinearity in regression models by calculating variance inflation factors for predictors. VIF values were within acceptable limits, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern. Residual diagnostics for regression models did not reveal substantial deviations from normality or heteroscedasticity that would invalidate the inferences. Nevertheless, the modest sample size and the exploratory nature of the measures limited the precision of coefficient estimates.

Effect size estimates provided additional context for interpreting the findings. The correlation between self-efficacy and the lifestyle index corresponded to a moderate effect size, highlighting a meaningful relationship in practical terms. The non-significant correlation between self-efficacy and academic performance corresponded to a small effect size, indicating that if a true association exists, it is likely modest. Group difference effect sizes were small to moderate, and given sample size considerations, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously rather than as definitive population parameters.

We also examined patterns of response across individual items to gain insight into the constructs measured. Items on the self-efficacy scale that emphasized persistence and problem-solving showed the strongest inter-item correlations, whereas items that were more vaguely phrased correlated less strongly with the total score. This item-level pattern suggested that clearer, behaviorally anchored items might strengthen the scale’s predictive validity in future iterations. On the lifestyle inventory, items assessing regular physical activity and regular sleep schedule had somewhat higher item-total correlations than items about dietary choices, suggesting heterogeneity in the behavioral domain covered by the composite index.

Finally, we reflect on statistical power. Post-hoc analyses indicated that our sample size of 100 provided adequate power to detect moderate effect sizes but limited power to detect small effects. Thus, null findings, particularly the non-significant self-efficacy-academic association, could reflect insufficient power rather than the absence of any true effect. Collectively, the results provided partial support for our hypotheses: self-efficacy was positively and significantly associated with healthy lifestyle choices, but the predicted association with academic performance was observed only as a positive trend that did not reach significance under the present study conditions. Gender and cultural group analyses revealed a few differences that merit further investigation in larger, more representative samples.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationships among self-efficacy, academic performance, and healthy lifestyle choices in a convenience sample of 100 participants. The findings partially supported our hypotheses. Self-efficacy was positively associated with healthy lifestyle choices in a statistically and practically meaningful way. This result aligns with a large body of literature indicating that perceived capability to enact behaviors is an important determinant of health-related action. The finding is theoretically coherent with models that emphasize self-efficacy as a proximal influence on behavior because it affects motivation, planning, and persistence in maintaining lifestyle routines. The replication of this association in our sample, despite the use of a self-designed instrument, suggests that the relation between self-efficacy and lifestyle behaviors is relatively robust.

In contrast, the hypothesized positive association between self-efficacy and academic performance emerged only as a non-significant positive trend in this sample. Several plausible explanations can account for the weaker-than-expected relationship. First, our self-efficacy measure was designed to be general rather than strictly academic. Theory and prior empirical work suggest that domain-specific self-efficacy measures often outperform general measures in predicting behavior within the corresponding domain. If academic self-efficacy had been measured with items specifically referencing study habits, test-taking confidence, and task-specific capabilities, we might have observed a stronger association with academic performance. Second, measurement limitations in the academic outcome likely attenuated associations. Self-reported GPA and the conversion of categorical academic ratings into a continuous standardized metric introduce noise and potential bias. Objective academic records or task-specific performance measures could have produced a clearer signal. Third, restriction of range in academic performance among participants was noted; a compressed distribution reduces correlation magnitudes. Fourth, statistical power is a non-trivial consideration. With a sample of 100, the study was adequately powered to detect moderate effects but underpowered for small effects. If the true association between general self-efficacy and academic performance is small, the present study may not have had sufficient sensitivity to detect it.

Beyond the main associations, our exploration of gender and cultural differences yielded a few interesting patterns that warrant discussion and caution. Gender differences in self-efficacy were small and nonsignificant. This finding contrasts with some prior reports that find gendered patterns in domain-specific self-efficacy, particularly in areas like mathematics and science. The lack of a gender effect in our study could reflect the use of a domain-general self-efficacy measure, which may be less sensitive to domain-specific gendered socialization. Moreover, sample size limitations reduce the precision with which gender differences can be estimated.

Cultural comparisons between participants who identified as White and those who identified as Asian revealed small but statistically detectable differences in academic performance and lifestyle index scores. The Asian-identified group reported slightly higher academic performance on average, while the White-identified group reported slightly higher lifestyle index scores. Self-efficacy means did not differ significantly between these groups. These patterns could reflect cultural variations in academic emphasis, lifestyle norms, or reporting tendencies. For example, cultural groups may prioritize academic achievement differently, leading to real differences in academic outcomes. Differences in lifestyle behaviors could arise from cultural dietary patterns, social activities, or time use. However, interpreting these group differences requires caution because the sample was not stratified or matched on socio-economic status, and unmeasured confounds could account for observed differences. Additionally, cultural identification is multifaceted and the simple White-versus-Asian dichotomy omits heterogeneity within these broad categories.

The differential pattern of associations for the two outcome domains suggests several theoretical implications. One implication is that self-efficacy may exert more direct and immediate effects on daily behaviors that are under volitional control, like exercise or diet, than on complex, multi-determined outcomes like academic performance. Academic performance is influenced by many factors beyond an individual's self-beliefs, including prior knowledge, instructional quality, assessment design, access to resources, and systemic constraints. In contrast, lifestyle choices may be more tightly coupled to individual agency, habitual patterns, and immediate decision-making contexts where self-efficacy can operate directly. Another implication is that the specificity of measurement matters: domain-specific efficacy beliefs align better with domain-specific outcomes.

Methodological reflections are central to interpreting the study. A primary limitation arises from the use of self-designed instruments that were not subjected to rigorous validation. Although the internal consistency of our scales was acceptable for exploratory research, the lack of established construct validity and predictive validity hampers confidence in the measures. Future research should prioritize validated, domain-specific self-efficacy scales and standardized health behavior measures. Another limitation is the cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference. Even though theory suggests that self-efficacy should predict behavior, it is also plausible that engaging in successful health behaviors or academic achievements enhances self-efficacy in a reciprocal fashion. Longitudinal designs and intervention studies are necessary to disentangle these temporal dynamics.

The reliance on self-report data is an additional limitation. Self-reported GPA and lifestyle behaviors are subject to social desirability and recall biases. Objective measures, such as institutional academic records, wearable activity trackers, or dietary logs, could provide more accurate assessments and strengthen the validity of conclusions. The convenience sampling approach further limits generalizability. The sample was drawn from a university-affiliated and local community population and may not reflect broader populations in terms of age range, socio-economic status, or cultural diversity. A larger, more representative sample would enhance external validity and statistical power.

Despite these limitations, the study has strengths. The investigation addressed multiple theoretically relevant outcomes simultaneously, allowing for comparative insight into how self-efficacy relates to different life domains. The inclusion of exploratory analyses of gender and cultural differences, along with sensitivity checks, increased the transparency and robustness of the analytic approach. The study also highlights practical lessons for future research and application. For practitioners interested in promoting healthy lifestyles, interventions that enhance self-efficacy through mastery experiences, skills training, and social modeling may be fruitful given the moderate association observed in this study. For educational practitioners aiming to improve academic outcomes, the present results suggest that interventions should either focus on domain-specific self-efficacy constructs or combine self-efficacy enhancement with other supports such as study skills training and structural resource provision.

There are several productive directions for future research. First, future studies should employ validated domain-specific self-efficacy measures to clarify the relations with academic and health-related outcomes. Second, longitudinal designs are needed to test directionality and reciprocal relations between self-efficacy and behavior. Third, experimental or quasi-experimental interventions that manipulate self-efficacy through structured mastery opportunities could provide stronger causal tests. Fourth, multi-method approaches combining self-report, behavioral observation, and objective performance metrics would mitigate the limitations of single-method studies. Fifth, larger samples would allow more nuanced testing of moderators such as gender, cultural background, socio-economic status, and prior performance.

It would also be valuable to investigate mediators that may explain how self-efficacy translates into behavior. For academic performance, possible mediators include study behaviors, time management, and task engagement. For lifestyle choices, mediators could include planning, habit formation, and social support. Statistical mediation analyses in longitudinal frameworks would illuminate the processes underlying observed associations. Moreover, examining potential moderators beyond gender and broad cultural categories could sharpen understanding of who benefits most from self-efficacy enhancement. For instance, personality traits, stress reactivity, and environmental constraints might shape the efficacy-behavior link.

Methodologically, researchers should attend to scale construction principles when devising self-efficacy measures. Items should be behaviorally specific, phrased to denote capability rather than outcome expectancy, and anchored to relevant tasks and contexts. Psychometric evaluation through exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis, assessment of convergent and discriminant validity, and test-retest reliability are important steps before wide deployment. In the context of lifestyle assessment, using validated indices for diet, physical activity, and sleep would provide a more granular and reliable picture of health behaviors.

In conclusion, the present exploratory study provides partial support for the theoretical linkage between self-efficacy and important life outcomes. Self-efficacy was a reliable predictor of healthier lifestyle choices in this sample but did not show a statistically significant association with academic performance under current measurement and design constraints. The findings underscore the importance of measurement specificity, adequate sample size, and methodological rigor in research on self-efficacy. They also point to promising avenues for applied work aimed at promoting health behaviors via interventions that target perceived capability. Future research that addresses the limitations of the current work—through validated measures, longitudinal and experimental designs, objective outcomes, and larger, more diverse samples—will be critical to advancing our understanding of how self-efficacy operates across different life domains and populations.
