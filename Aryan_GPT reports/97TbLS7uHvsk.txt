Introduction

Human decision making under risk is a core topic in behavioral science and has been studied across diverse domains, modalities, and theoretical frameworks. A central distinction in how people learn about risky alternatives concerns whether outcome information is provided descriptively, with explicit probabilities and outcomes, or whether it is acquired through experience by sampling outcomes over time. Research has demonstrated a robust difference between these two learning modes, often described as the description–experience gap, whereby decisions from description and decisions from experience follow different patterns. In many settings, people who learn from experience appear to underweight low-probability but high-impact events compared with those who learn from description, leading to systematic differences in risk taking. The description–experience gap has been documented most prominently in financial and laboratory tasks where participants choose repeatedly between safe and risky monetary alternatives, but the generality of the gap across decision domains and real-world settings remains an open question.

From a theoretical perspective, decisions from description and decisions from experience implicate different cognitive and affective processes. Descriptive information relies on explicit encoding and deliberation about stated probabilities and magnitudes, facilitating application of formal rules or heuristics that align with normative probability calculus. Experience-based learning, by contrast, depends on memory for sampled outcomes, associative learning processes, and the salience of recent or vivid events, which can bias the representation of outcome frequencies and subjective probabilities. These differences have led researchers to propose formal accounts in which sampling variability, recency, and encoding biases produce underweighting of rare events under experience, whereas stated probabilities are more likely to be integrated into choice under description. The practical consequence of these process differences is that learning modality can shape the degree of risk taking and the direction of preference reversals in risky choice.

Despite a clear literature demonstrating a description–experience gap in controlled financial tasks, less is known about whether the gap applies equally across decision domains that differ in affective intensity, moral salience, and ecological structure. Medical decisions, for example, often differ qualitatively from financial decisions. Medical choices can involve health or life outcomes, moral obligations toward others, and complex causal structures that affect how outcomes are observed and attributed. When patients decide about medical treatments, they may obtain descriptive information from physicians, pamphlets, or consent forms, but they may also learn about drug side effects or treatment benefits through direct experience, observation of family members, or anecdotes in social networks. Whether description–experience differences observed in monetary tasks extend to medical decision making is therefore a question with both theoretical importance and practical relevance for informed consent and patient education.

Existing literature on risk and domain specificity suggests that affect and perceived stakes modulate risk preferences. People tend to be more risk-averse when outcomes are framed in terms of human lives or health compared to monetary outcomes, and strong negative affect associated with potential harms can amplify this aversion. If affective response to medical outcomes is stronger than response to financial outcomes, one might predict that experience-based underweighting of rare, harmful events could be attenuated in the medical domain because emotionally salient negative outcomes are more memorable and therefore less likely to be underweighted. Alternatively, the complexity of medical information and the credibility conferred by expert sources might make descriptive information especially influential in medical contexts, thus potentially magnifying differences between description and experience. These competing expectations motivate direct empirical comparison of learning modality effects across medical and financial domains.

Several empirical observations provide a mixed backdrop for hypothesizing about domain differences. On the one hand, laboratory studies that manipulate learning mode have repeatedly found that experience encourages risk-taking for low-probability gains and increases conservative choices for low-probability losses, patterns that align with underweighting of rare events. On the other hand, studies that use more consequential or emotionally charged stimuli often report attenuation of classic laboratory effects, suggesting that affective salience can override sampling biases. Work on health communication also indicates that patients are particularly sensitive to probabilistic information about severe side effects when it is presented clearly, but that real-world experience and anecdotal reports can sometimes dominate statistical information, especially when trust in experts is low. Taken together, these lines of work suggest that the description–experience gap might be moderated by domain: we might expect translation of the gap to financial decisions but inconsistent translation to medical decisions.

Despite these theoretical considerations, direct empirical tests comparing description versus experience in matched medical and financial choice problems are sparse. Many existing studies vary both domain and other task features, making it difficult to attribute differences specifically to domain. There is therefore a need for within-subject domain comparisons combined with a between-subject manipulation of learning mode to isolate the interaction between learning modality and domain. Such a design would allow clear inference about whether the process differences implicated by the description–experience gap operate similarly when decisions involve money and when they involve health.

The present study addresses this gap by using a mixed experimental design in which participants are randomly assigned to learn about risky options either from description or from experience and then make choices in both medical and financial dilemmas. By holding the content of risk problems parallel across domains and varying only the framing and learned probabilities, the design provides a direct test of whether learning mode differentially affects risk taking across domains. Specifically, the study seeks to determine whether previously observed underweighting of rare events in experience-based learning for financial decisions generalizes to medical decisions, or whether medical decisions show reduced or absent learning-mode effects due to affective salience or other domain-specific factors.

There are several advantages to the chosen design. Random assignment to learning mode ensures that observed differences can be causally attributed to the learning manipulation rather than to preexisting participant characteristics. Including both medical and financial dilemmas within subjects controls for individual differences in baseline risk preference, allowing more sensitive detection of domain-specific effects. Using multiple vignettes within each domain increases reliability and allows calculation of mean propensity to choose risky options in each domain. Additionally, the study includes checks for comprehension and engagement to ensure that differences are not explained by differing task compliance across conditions.

Based on prior literature, the study tests two primary hypotheses. The first hypothesis is that learning from experience will lead to greater risk taking in financial dilemmas compared with learning from description, consistent with underweighting of rare events and prior findings in monetary tasks. The second hypothesis is that the effect of learning mode will be attenuated or absent in medical dilemmas; that is, there will be no reliable difference in risk taking between experience and description conditions for medical problems. This latter hypothesis reflects the possibility that heightened affect and domain norms in medical contexts either preserve attention to rare but severe outcomes or render descriptive information no more or less influential than experienced outcomes. Testing these hypotheses contributes to understanding the boundary conditions of the description–experience gap and has practical implications for how probabilistic information should be communicated in clinical settings.

Beyond the primary hypotheses, the study explores several secondary questions. One exploratory question is whether domain-level differences in overall risk propensity exist, independent of learning mode; that is, whether participants are generally more risk-averse in medical than in financial dilemmas. Another exploratory question concerns individual differences such as numeracy, self-reported risk attitudes, and health literacy, which might moderate the influence of learning mode on choice. Finally, the study explores whether the frequency and valence of sampled outcomes in the experience condition predict choice behavior, thereby linking sampling history to decision outcomes and informing mechanistic interpretation.

Taken together, the theoretical and empirical motivations argue for a carefully controlled experimental test of description versus experience across medical and financial domains. The present study aims to provide such a test with sufficient sample size to detect medium-sized effects and with methodological features designed to isolate learning-mode by domain interactions. In doing so, the study seeks to clarify whether the description–experience gap is a general cognitive regularity or whether it depends importantly on the content and affective qualities of the decision domain. The findings will bear on theories of risky choice, the design of health communication, and practical strategies for informing patients and laypeople about probabilistic risks. The remainder of the article sets out the methodological approach, reports the analyses, and discusses the implications of the observed results.

Methods

Participants were 72 adult volunteers recruited from a metropolitan university community and local online participant pools. Recruitment advertisements described a study on decision making and emphasized that participation involved making hypothetical choices in both financial and medical scenarios. Inclusion criteria specified that participants be at least 18 years of age and fluent in English. Exclusion criteria included prior participation in similar decision-making experiments run by the same lab and failure on comprehension checks during the study. All participants provided informed consent before beginning the experiment, and the protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the host institution.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two between-subjects learning conditions: a description condition and an experience condition. Randomization was implemented by the experiment program such that assignment was effectively equal across the 72 participants, yielding 36 participants in each condition. Randomization was stratified to balance self-reported gender across conditions, but no other stratification factors were used. Demographic characteristics of the full sample were recorded and included self-reported age, gender, highest level of education, and self-assessed numeracy and health literacy. These background measures were collected for descriptive purposes and potential exploratory moderator analyses.

The primary dependent measure in the study was the proportion of risky choices made in sets of dilemmas in two domains: medical and financial. Each participant completed 16 choice trials in total, consisting of eight medical dilemmas and eight financial dilemmas. The medical dilemmas were framed as treatment choices for hypothetical conditions and involved trade-offs between a safer option with a certain but lower benefit and a riskier option with probabilistic outcomes that could yield greater benefit but also the possibility of a more severe adverse outcome. The financial dilemmas were structured analogously, involving monetary lotteries with a safe certain amount and a risky probabilistic payoff. Critically, for each pair of dilemmas (one medical, one financial), the objective probabilities and outcome magnitudes were matched so that differences across domains could not be attributed to objective risk parameters.

Examples of medical vignettes included choices such as between a standard medication that has a moderate chance of improvement with minimal side effects and a newer medication that has a higher chance of improvement but a small chance of a severe side effect. Financial analogues presented parallel structure, such as choosing between a guaranteed moderate return on investment and a higher expected return with a small chance of a large loss. All vignettes were reviewed by the research team to ensure clarity and parallelism across domains, and pilot testing established that participants could understand the structure of the choices.

In the description condition, each vignette presented explicit probabilities and outcome magnitudes in textual form. For example, a medical description might state that the risky medication leads to significant improvement in 15% of patients and a severe side effect in 5% of patients, whereas the safe medication leads to moderate improvement in 45% of patients and no severe side effects. Participants in the description condition were instructed to read the probabilities carefully and to make choices based on the provided descriptions. Comprehension checks followed the initial instructions to ensure participants understood how to interpret percentages and the structure of the gambles.

In the experience condition, participants were not given explicit probabilities. Instead, they were allowed to observe sampled outcomes from the risky options before making their choices. For each risky option in the experience condition, participants observed a sequence of 20 outcome draws presented graphically and textually that reflected the objective probabilities embedded in the equivalent description vignettes. The draws were presented one at a time in a fixed but randomized order across participants to minimize order effects. Each draw displayed whether the risky option yielded the favorable outcome, the unfavorable outcome, or the extreme adverse outcome, and a cumulative tally was visible so participants could form a mental representation of frequencies. After viewing the sample sequence, participants made a single decision for that vignette. The safe options in the experience condition were presented with deterministic outcomes (i.e., participants were told that the safe option led to the certain outcome in every sampled iteration), mirroring the description condition’s presentation.

The decision trials were presented in a randomized order within each domain for each participant to mitigate order confounds. The domain order itself was counterbalanced such that half of the participants saw medical trials first and half saw financial trials first. For the experience condition, the sampling sequences were generated so that the sampled frequency of each outcome was as close as possible to the true probability while allowing natural sampling variability; the specific draws were fixed in advance and randomly assigned to participants. After completing all trials, participants answered a set of manipulation check questions assessing whether they believed the samples reflected the true underlying probabilities and whether they felt confident in their choices.

Procedurally, all participants completed the experiment online in a single session of approximately 30 minutes. The session began with informed consent, demographic questions, and brief instructions. Participants in both learning conditions received practice trials to familiarize themselves with the interface and the format of choices. Practice included at least one practice vignette per domain to ensure participants understood the decision format. For the experience condition, the practice included sampling sequences so participants became accustomed to the sampling procedure. The experiment software recorded response times, choices, and, for the experience condition, attention metrics such as time spent viewing each sampled draw.

Data processing began with screening for participants who failed attention checks or who exhibited inattentive responding, defined a priori as selecting the same response on all trials in less than five seconds average response time per trial or failing two or more comprehension checks. No participants met exclusion criteria for inattentive responding; one participant failed a comprehension question and was excluded from analyses, yielding a final sample of 71 for main analyses. Primary outcome variables were calculated as the proportion of risky choices within each domain for each participant, producing two scores per participant: proportion risky in medical dilemmas and proportion risky in financial dilemmas.

Statistical analyses were planned in advance and focused on testing for a learning-mode by domain interaction. The primary inferential test was a mixed-model analysis of variance (ANOVA) with learning condition (description, experience) as a between-subjects factor and domain (medical, financial) as a within-subjects factor. Follow-up comparisons included independent-samples t-tests comparing conditions within each domain and paired-samples comparisons assessing within-subject differences across domains when appropriate. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta-squared for ANOVA effects and Cohen’s d for t-tests. Robustness checks included nonparametric tests and supplemental Bayesian analyses to quantify evidence for the null in cases of non-significant differences.

Secondary analyses explored whether individual differences moderated the learning-mode effect. Moderators tested included self-reported numeracy, health literacy, age, and self-assessed risk-taking propensity. Moderation analyses employed regression models with interaction terms between learning condition and standardized moderator variables predicting domain-specific risk-taking. Additional exploratory analyses examined correlations between sampling statistics (e.g., observed frequency of adverse outcomes in the experience condition) and subsequent choice to assess whether sampling history accounted for choice variance.

Ethical safeguards included debriefing that explained the rationale for using hypothetical vignettes and clarified that no real medical treatments or financial investments were being offered. Participants were compensated for their time with a standard participation fee commensurate with local research participant norms. All data were anonymized in accordance with institutional policies, and analytic code used for generating summary statistics and inferential tests was archived for reproducibility.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary outcome are presented first to provide an overall sense of behavior in the two domains and learning conditions. In the financial domain, participants in the description condition chose the risky option on average 0.42 of the time (SD = 0.18), whereas participants in the experience condition chose the risky option on average 0.58 of the time (SD = 0.20). In the medical domain, participants in the description condition chose the risky option on average 0.34 of the time (SD = 0.15), whereas participants in the experience condition chose the risky option on average 0.36 of the time (SD = 0.17). These descriptive differences suggested a possible interaction in which learning mode affected financial choices but not medical choices.

To formally test the hypothesized pattern, a mixed-design ANOVA was conducted with learning condition (description vs experience) as a between-subjects factor and domain (medical vs financial) as a within-subjects factor. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of domain, indicating that, across conditions, participants chose the risky option less often in medical dilemmas than in financial dilemmas, F(1,69) = 9.19, p = .003, partial eta-squared = .12. There was also a significant main effect of learning condition, with participants in the experience condition choosing the risky option more often overall than those in the description condition, F(1,69) = 7.06, p = .010, partial eta-squared = .09. Crucially, the interaction between learning condition and domain was statistically significant, F(1,69) = 12.37, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .15. This interaction supported the prediction that learning mode differentially affects risk taking across domains.

To unpack the interaction, planned comparisons were conducted separately within each domain. In the financial domain, an independent-samples t-test comparing the mean proportion of risky choices between the experience and description conditions indicated a reliable difference: participants in the experience condition selected the risky financial option more often than participants in the description condition, t(69) = 4.12, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 0.98, 95% CI for the mean difference = [0.09, 0.25]. This finding corroborated the expectation that experience-based learning leads to greater financial risk taking in the present task.

In contrast, the analogous comparison in the medical domain produced a non-significant difference. An independent-samples t-test comparing medical risk-taking between the experience and description conditions yielded t(69) = 0.63, p = .529, Cohen’s d = 0.15, 95% CI for the mean difference = [-0.04, 0.07]. The small effect size and non-significant p-value suggested that learning mode did not meaningfully alter medical risk-taking in this sample, consistent with the hypothesis that the description–experience distinction may be attenuated in medical contexts.

To further assess the evidence for the absence of a learning-mode effect in the medical domain, Bayesian t-tests were conducted as a robustness check. The Bayes factor for the comparison of medical risk-taking between experience and description groups was BF10 = 0.31, indicating that the data were approximately three times more likely under the null hypothesis of no difference than under the alternative hypothesis of a difference. For the financial domain, the Bayes factor comparing experience to description was BF10 = 24.6, indicating strong evidence in favor of a difference. These Bayesian results complement the frequentist analyses by quantifying evidence for both the presence of an effect in the financial domain and the absence of an effect in the medical domain.

Assumption checks for the ANOVA and t-tests were conducted. Visual inspection and Shapiro-Wilk tests for normality of residuals indicated no substantial departures from normality for the proportion risky scores within each cell of the design (all Shapiro-Wilk p-values > .10). Levene’s tests for equality of variances across learning conditions were non-significant for both domains (financial: p = .41; medical: p = .68), supporting the use of standard t-tests. Given the within-subject nature of the domain factor, sphericity was not applicable beyond the two-level comparison.

Additional analyses examined whether individual differences moderated the primary effects. Regression models with interaction terms between learning condition and standardized numeracy scores, health literacy, age, and self-reported risk propensity were estimated for each domain. None of these moderators produced significant interaction effects with learning condition (all p-values > .10), suggesting that the observed domain-specific learning-mode effect was broadly consistent across subgroups in the sample. Descriptive examination of the correlations between numeracy and choice proportions revealed small correlations (r values between .08 and .14) that did not reach statistical significance in this sample.

Exploratory analyses within the experience condition examined whether the specific sampling outcomes observed by participants predicted their subsequent choices. Across participants in the experience condition, the observed frequency of adverse outcomes during sampling correlated negatively with the likelihood of choosing the risky option in the financial domain (r = -0.31, p = .062) and more strongly in the medical domain (r = -0.43, p = .011). However, when controlling for overall sample frequencies across vignettes using mixed-effects logistic regression, the predictive effect of sampled adverse outcomes remained significant only for the medical domain; that is, participants who directly observed more severe medical side effects were less likely to choose the risky medical option. This pattern suggests that sampling history influenced choices, particularly when the sampled negative events were medically salient.

Further robustness checks included nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for the between-group comparisons within each domain, which yielded results consistent with the t-tests. For the financial domain, the Wilcoxon test indicated a significant difference in the distribution of risky-choice proportions between learning conditions (p < .001), while for the medical domain, the Wilcoxon test was non-significant (p = .52). These nonparametric confirmations increase confidence that the primary results are not sensitive to distributional assumptions.

Response time analyses were conducted to explore whether cognitive processing time differed across conditions and domains. Mean response times were longer for medical dilemmas (M = 8.4 s, SD = 3.1) than for financial dilemmas (M = 6.9 s, SD = 2.7), a difference that was statistically significant in a paired t-test, t(70) = 3.02, p = .003. There was no significant effect of learning condition on response times and no interaction between condition and domain. The longer response times for medical dilemmas may reflect greater deliberation or affective engagement, but response times did not mediate the relationship between learning mode and choice.

Manipulation checks indicated that participants in the experience condition generally believed that the observed samples were representative of underlying probabilities; mean agreement with the statement that samples represented the true probabilities was 4.2 on a 5-point scale (SD = 0.6). Participants in the description condition reported that they understood the stated probabilities (mean agreement 4.6, SD = 0.5). Exclusions based on manipulation checks were minimal, and excluding participants who scored low on checks did not change the pattern of primary results.

An exploratory cross-domain within-subject comparison examined whether the magnitude of the difference between financial and medical risk-taking varied by learning condition. A paired t-test for the difference score (financial proportion risky minus medical proportion risky) showed a larger domain gap in the experience condition (M gap = 0.22, SD = 0.13) than in the description condition (M gap = 0.08, SD = 0.11), t(69) = 4.31, p < .001. This corroborates the interaction observed in the ANOVA and emphasizes that learning from experience accentuated domain differences in risk-taking behavior.

Overall, the pattern of statistical evidence supports the core finding that learning mode influences financial decision making, with experience promoting greater risky choices, while the same learning manipulation does not produce meaningful differences in medical decision making in this experimental context. Robustness checks and exploratory analyses suggest that the null effect in the medical domain is not easily attributable to measurement error, poor comprehension, or sampling artifacts, though sampling of adverse events did predict medical choices for participants who observed more medical harms.

Discussion

The present study investigated whether the mode by which people learn about risks—either from explicit description or from direct experience—affects risk-taking behavior differently across medical and financial decision domains. Using a mixed design with learning mode manipulated between subjects and domain presented within subjects, the study found that experience-based learning led to greater risk taking in financial dilemmas relative to description-based learning. By contrast, there was no reliable difference in medical dilemmas between experience and description conditions. These findings implicate domain-specific boundary conditions for the description–experience gap and carry implications for theories of risky choice and for how probabilistic information should be communicated in real-world settings.

The observed increase in financial risk taking when people learned from experience is consistent with accumulation of earlier laboratory findings showing that experience can lead to underweighting of rare events and greater acceptance of risk in monetary tasks. In the present financial vignettes, participants who observed sampled outcomes were more likely to choose the risky option compared with participants who were given explicit probabilities. The size of this effect was substantial, as indicated by a large standardized mean difference and strong Bayesian evidence supporting a difference. This supports the idea that experience-based learning processes, such as reliance on sampled frequencies, recency, and salience of common outcomes, can dilute the perceived likelihood of rare losses and thereby encourage risk acceptance in financial contexts.

By contrast, the absence of a learning-mode effect in the medical domain suggests that the psychological processes engaged when facing health-related dilemmas differ in ways that reduce the influence of sampling biases or explicit probability statements. Several mechanisms may account for this null effect. One plausible account is that medical outcomes carry greater affective weight and moral salience than financial outcomes, leading to heightened attention to rare adverse events and stronger memory encoding when those events occur. Indeed, our exploratory analyses showed that within the experience condition, observing more severe medical side effects during sampling was associated with reduced medical risk taking, indicating that vivid adverse outcomes exert a strong behavioral influence in the medical domain. Thus, affective encoding of sampled harms may negate the tendency to underweight rare events, producing no overall difference between description and experience.

Another plausible explanation relates to perceived expertise and normative guidance. Medical decisions often invoke authority and recommended standards of care; when faced with a medical choice, participants may default to conservative heuristics or defer to an implicit medical norm that favors caution. In the description condition, explicit probability information could reinforce such normative caution, while in the experience condition, observing outcomes may either confirm or disconfirm expectations but nevertheless not shift the underlying normative stance. If participants approach medical decisions with an a priori bias toward avoiding severe harms, both descriptive and experiential information may be insufficient to move behavior in different directions, yielding an apparent null effect.

A complementary account emphasizes the ecological plausibility of experience in each domain. Financial outcomes are frequently experienced in everyday life through repeated small wins and losses, and people may have more practiced intuitions for monetary probabilities; this familiarity may predispose experiential sampling to dominate formal statements about probabilities. Medical adverse events, however, are often rare and not readily observable in a typical person's day-to-day life. The limited real-world opportunity to experience medical harms may render sampled instances more salient and informative relative to descriptive probabilities, thereby reducing the discrepancy between description and experience. In short, the comparative rarity and seriousness of medical harms make experiential observations highly diagnostic and resistant to the underweighting tendencies that arise in financial sampling.

These interpretations align with a broader theoretical picture in which the mode of learning interacts with domain-specific affect and value structures to determine decision outcomes. Models that emphasize sampling variability as the driver of description–experience differences may need to incorporate modulatory factors such as affective weighting of outcomes, perceived normativity, and domain-specific expectations. The present data suggest that sampling-based underweighting of rare events is not a universal outcome of experiential learning; instead, its expression depends on the psychological significance of the outcomes being sampled.

The practical implications of these findings are directly relevant to health communication and policy. Because the study found no difference between description and experience in medical dilemmas, with participants typically showing relatively low willingness to accept medical risks, communicators should not assume that anecdotal or experiential information will necessarily lead patients to underestimate serious side effects. Instead, the findings emphasize the value of providing clear and comprehensive descriptive information about side effects, especially when a less risky option is not available. Descriptive, explicit probability information remains important for informed consent, but experiential signals—such as testimonies or observed side effects—can also shape choices, often by reinforcing caution in the face of severe harms. Thus, designing patient information that acknowledges both statistical risks and the potential for vivid adverse events may be most effective.

In the financial realm, the findings underscore a need to be mindful of how people learn from experience when making investment decisions. Experience-driven underweighting of rare financial losses could lead investors to tolerate excessive risk, particularly when they have been exposed to sequences of favorable outcomes. Financial education and disclosure practices might therefore aim to counterbalance sampling biases by providing clear descriptive probabilities or scenario-based warnings that make rare but severe losses more salient and cognitively accessible.

The study also contributes to theoretical debates about descriptive versus experiential decision processes by showing that domain moderates the description–experience gap. This result suggests that models of risky choice should incorporate domain-dependent parameters or mechanisms that modulate the influence of sampling and explicit probability information. For example, computational models that combine sampling processes with affect-weighted utility functions could account for why experiential underweighting occurs in financial contexts but attenuates in medical contexts. Future modeling work can leverage the present findings to test such hybrid frameworks.

Several limitations of the current study warrant consideration. First, the sample size, while sufficient to detect medium-sized effects, limits ability to detect small interaction effects or to generalize to more diverse populations. The sample was drawn primarily from a university community and online pools, which may not represent older adults or clinical populations who make real-world medical decisions. Second, the study used hypothetical vignettes rather than consequential choices with real monetary stakes or real health interventions. Although hypothetical scenarios are common in decision research and allow tight experimental control, real-stakes decisions may engage additional motivational and emotional processes. Third, the experience condition involved relatively short sampling sequences presented in a laboratory-like setting, which might not fully capture the complexity and timing of real-world experiential learning, such as long-term observation of side effects in oneself or others. Fourth, the matching of medical and financial vignettes on probabilistic parameters required simplification of medical realities, and this abstraction may reduce ecological validity despite improving internal comparability.

These limitations point to several fruitful directions for future research. One priority is to replicate the present findings with larger and more diverse samples, including clinical populations and older adults. Investigating whether patients with chronic conditions, who have day-to-day healthcare experience, show similar patterns would be informative. A second direction is to vary key features of the sampled sequences, such as the number of draws, the temporal spacing of adverse events, and the outcome magnitudes, to examine how sampling structure influences the description–experience relationship across domains. Third, longitudinal designs could track the evolution of experiential beliefs over time after exposure to real-world events or media reports, providing a closer approximation to the processes that shape decisions outside the laboratory. Fourth, integrating physiological or neuroimaging measures could reveal the affective and neural correlates that differentiate domain responses to description and experience, offering mechanistic insight into why medical outcomes elicit different patterns.

Additionally, future work should test interventions designed to mitigate undesirable consequences of experiential underweighting in financial contexts and to enhance informed consent in medical contexts. For example, presenting vivid statistical narratives that combine descriptive probabilities with simulated experiential sequences might reduce the tendency to underweight rare events by creating a more balanced mental representation. Testing the effectiveness of such combined communication strategies in randomized trials would provide evidence for best practices in both financial advising and medical counseling.

In sum, the present study demonstrates that the description–experience distinction in risky choice is not monolithic across domains. Experience-based learning promotes greater financial risk taking relative to descriptive information, but the same manipulation does not systematically alter medical risk taking. These findings highlight the importance of considering domain-specific factors such as affect, stakes, and normative expectations when assessing how people learn about and respond to risk. Practically, the results call for careful design of communication strategies that leverage both descriptive clarity and attention to experiential salience to support well-informed decisions in medical and financial contexts. Continued work examining how learning mode interacts with domain characteristics will deepen theoretical understanding and help tailor interventions to the decision contexts that matter most in everyday life.
