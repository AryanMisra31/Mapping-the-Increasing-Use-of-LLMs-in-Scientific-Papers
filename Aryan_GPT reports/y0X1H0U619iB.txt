Introduction

Stereotypes and trait concepts are widely understood to influence cognition and behavior in both overt and subtle ways. A large body of psychological work has shown that exposure to concepts associated with social categories or trait adjectives can activate related mental structures and sometimes produce behavior that is congruent with the activated concept. Such activation is often described in terms of priming, and priming effects have been documented in tasks ranging from lexical decision making to more complex social behaviors. One of the most widely cited examples of social priming concerns the activation of the elderly stereotype and its alleged influence on locomotion, where subtle exposure to elderly-related words or ideas has been reported to slow walking speed in unwitting participants. The seminal work in this area popularized the notion that complex social behaviors can be guided by automatically activated semantic networks that operate below the threshold of conscious awareness. This finding, and others like it, have been influential because they suggested that small, momentary activations of cultural or semantic material can carry over to observable, downstream actions in the physical world.

The mechanism that has been proposed to account for such effects typically invokes automatic spreading activation within associative networks, which then biases perceptual, motoric, or evaluative systems in a congruent direction. If the concept “elderly” is activated, for example, associated ideas such as “slow,” “fragile,” or “walking slowly” are thought to become more accessible, and those behavioral tendencies may be temporarily facilitated. Researchers have also emphasized the role of goal activation and embodied cognition to account for why semantic primes might influence motor behavior; that is, the activation of a concept can include motor-relevant schemata that facilitate congruent movement patterns. A separate but related line of theorizing focuses on the boundary conditions of priming effects, arguing that individual differences, situational constraints, and methodological choices can modulate whether priming effects are observed. Thus, while the broad claim that primes can influence behavior has been influential, there is also recognition that the effects may not be universally present across individuals and contexts.

Individual differences in susceptibility to priming have been of particular interest in recent years. If priming operates by biasing the accessibility of particular representations, then the degree to which these activated representations influence behavior may depend on personality traits, motivational orientations, chronic goals, and social value systems that vary across individuals. One such dimension that is theoretically relevant is social value orientation (SVO), which captures a person’s typical preference structure when allocating outcomes between oneself and others. SVO is commonly characterized along dimensions such as prosociality, individualism, and competitiveness, and is typically measured using decomposed games or slider items that reveal how much weight an individual puts on the welfare of others relative to their own. Because SVO indexes the extent to which individuals incorporate social information into decision processes, it is plausible that SVO might moderate the impact of social primes on behavior.

To be more specific, individuals who display consistent social value orientations across measurement items might be conceptualized as having more stable, coherent social goals and preferences. Consistency in SVO suggests that the individual’s social-motivational constellation is robust and less likely to be transiently swayed by incidental contextual activations. By contrast, individuals who show inconsistent choices across SVO measurement items might possess less stable social preferences or may be more contextually pliable, making them more susceptible to the momentary activation of stereotypes or concepts. Thus, the degree of consistency in SVO could serve as an index of resilience to incidental priming: those with consistent SVO profiles could plausibly maintain their habitual behavioral tendencies despite brief semantic activation, whereas those with inconsistent SVO might be more likely to shift behavior in line with activated stereotypes.

Empirical investigations of individual differences in priming susceptibility have yielded heterogeneous results. Some studies have linked personality traits such as openness, need for cognition, or chronic goals to differential priming effects, whereas other work has failed to find reliable moderators. The mixed nature of the literature calls for careful empirical tests that explicitly measure potential moderators and examine their interaction with experimental primes. SVO, despite its theoretical relevance, has been under-explored as a moderator of priming effects on overt motor behavior. Existing research on SVO has primarily focused on social decision-making tasks, but the broader motivational framing implied by SVO suggests relevance to a wide array of behaviors, including those outside of explicit allocation dilemmas.

In the specific domain of elderly priming and locomotion, methodological choices have been identified as critical. Early reports that found robust slowing in walking speed following elderly priming often relied on subtle tasks such as sentence unscrambling or passage reading, followed by a seemingly unrelated task in which participant movement was unobtrusively timed. Later scrutiny emphasized that experimental demands, timing of measurement, the physical environment, and experimenter behavior could all influence outcomes. Additionally, meta-analytic and replication-oriented work has indicated that some priming effects may be smaller than initially reported, and that small sample sizes and methodological variability may have contributed to earlier positive findings. The presence of these methodological caveats highlights the need to consider potential moderators that could either amplify or attenuate priming effects under different circumstances.

The present study was designed with two primary aims that follow from the layered theoretical picture described above. The first aim was to examine whether priming participants with words related to the elderly stereotype would influence their subsequent walking time in a laboratory-to-hallway task that sought to closely replicate the procedure used in influential prior research. The behavioral outcome in this case was walking time, operationalized as the number of seconds required for participants to traverse a short, standardized corridor from the experiment room to a pre-specified point at the end of a hallway. Based on the general logic of stereotype priming and on the precedent set by earlier work, it was hypothesized that exposure to elderly-related words would produce behavior consistent with the “elderly” stereotype, namely an increase in walking time relative to a neutral prime condition. This hypothesis reflects the expectation that the elderly concept should activate associated motoric schemas or goal-relevant content that slows locomotion.

The second aim was to test whether individual differences in SVO consistency moderated any priming-induced changes in walking time. If stable social motivational orientations protect against momentary influence from incidental primes, then participants classified as having consistent SVO profiles should show smaller priming effects on walking time than those with inconsistent SVO profiles. This moderating hypothesis treats SVO consistency as a quasi-independent variable and involves an interaction test between prime condition and SVO consistency on the dependent measure of walking time.

Operationally, prime condition was implemented using a sentence unscrambling task, a standard priming manipulation in which participants form coherent sentences from scrambled word sets that covertly contain prime words. The unscrambling task allows the experimenter to expose participants to prime concepts without explicitly pointing to them or making the manipulation obvious. The neutral condition employed matched sentences devoid of elderly-related content. SVO was measured with a commonly used decomposed games measure, and consistency was determined by the degree to which participants made the same choice pattern across multiple items; those with a majority of choices reflecting the same SVO type were labeled “consistent” whereas those with mixed or unstable choice patterns were labeled “inconsistent.”

We designed the study to have sufficient statistical power to detect small-to-moderate effects, and to include checks for potential confounds such as participant awareness of the manipulation. A buffered debriefing and a manipulation-check questionnaire were used to assess whether participants suspected the link between the sentence unscrambling task and the walking behavior measurement. The experimenter who timed walking behavior was blind to prime condition to minimize unintended experimenter bias.

Given the background evidence and the theoretical arguments laid out above, two specific hypotheses were tested in the current research. Hypothesis 1 predicted that participants in the elderly prime condition would exhibit greater walking time than participants in the neutral prime condition. Hypothesis 2 predicted that the effect of elderly priming on walking time would be moderated by consistency in SVO, such that participants with consistent SVO would be less susceptible to the priming manipulation and therefore demonstrate smaller prime-related differences in walking time compared to participants with inconsistent SVO. Testing these hypotheses contributes to our understanding of whether semantic primes can affect embodied behavior under rigorously controlled conditions and whether individual differences in social motivational stability constitute meaningful boundary conditions for such effects.

Method

Participants were recruited from a university subject pool and from local community advertisements. A total of 124 individuals initially participated in the study. Participants were informed that the study investigated language processing and interpersonal decision-making, and they were compensated with course credit or a small monetary payment. All participants were fluent English speakers and reported no mobility impairments that would affect walking speed. Prior to data analysis, eight participants were excluded from the dataset because they indicated during debriefing that they suspected the true purpose of the study or because of technical errors in timing. The final sample therefore comprised 116 participants. The age range of participants was 18 to 64 years, with a mean age in the mid-20s; the sample included both student and non-student adults. Demographic details were recorded in order to assess whether sample characteristics might systematically relate to primary variables of interest, but the core analyses reported below did not adjust for demographic covariates.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of two priming conditions in a between-subjects design. Assignment to condition was conducted using random number tables and was performed by a research assistant who was not involved in timing walking behavior. The two priming conditions were elderly prime and neutral prime. The prime manipulation used a sentence unscrambling task adapted from classical priming studies in which participants are given sets of five words and asked to form four-word sentences that make sense. For the elderly prime condition, 10 of 20 unscrambling trials contained words associated with elderly stereotypes (e.g., “wrinkle,” “bingo,” “gray,” “retired,” “slow”) interspersed within otherwise neutral sentence materials. For the neutral prime condition, the sentence unscrambling materials were matched for difficulty and grammatical structure but contained no words that suggested age or mobility characteristics. The unscrambling task took approximately five minutes to complete, and participants believed it was part of the primary language processing portion of the experiment.

Following the sentence unscrambling task, participants completed a short battery of questionnaires that included the Social Value Orientation (SVO) measure and filler items. The SVO measure used a decomposed games format with nine items that required participants to choose allocations of points between themselves and an anonymous partner. The three canonical SVO types—prosocial, individualistic, and competitive—were represented among the choices. For each item, participants selected one of three allocation options that favored different distributions of self and other outcomes. The measure was administered in a fixed order and took approximately eight minutes to complete. Responses were scored according to established scoring rules that classify each item as indicative of a particular SVO type.

SVO consistency was operationalized as follows: participants who made the same SVO-consistent type choice on at least six of the nine decomposed game items were classified as showing a consistent SVO profile. That cutoff was selected a priori as reflecting a clear majority of choices consistent with a single orientation, while allowing for occasional item variability. Participants whose choices did not meet that threshold were classified as inconsistent. The choice to dichotomize SVO in this way treated consistency as a quasi-independent variable, acknowledging that the operationalization reflects a distributional decision rather than a continuous trait measurement. Nevertheless, SVO was also retained as a continuous score in supplementary correlational analyses to examine robustness of results.

After completing the questionnaires, participants were told that the experimenter needed to check a setup in the adjacent hallway and that the rest of the session would continue in a different room. The walking time measurement was thus framed as a naturally occurring transition between experimental tasks. Critically, the research assistant who timed participants’ walking behavior stood at a concealed position where the door to the lab opened into a short, straight, 10-meter hallway. The confederate used a digital stopwatch to record the time it took for the participant to walk from the lab door threshold to a marked line on the floor 10 meters away. The confederate remained neutral in posture and verbal behavior and delivered no comments that might cue the participant to walk in any particular way. The experimenter who had administered the sentences and questionnaires left the room prior to the timing so that experimenter expectations could not be transmitted during the walking task. All walk timings were taken discreetly and returned to the lead experimenter in a sealed envelope to preserve blindness.

To reduce measurement error, the hallway walkway was free of obstructions and had consistent lighting. Participants were instructed only to proceed to the marked point because the next part of the session would take place in the adjacent room. No explicit instruction regarding pace (e.g., “walk at your normal speed”) was given, to avoid drawing attention to gait and thereby altering natural behavior. Walking times were recorded in seconds to two decimal places. Trials in which the participant stopped or engaged in atypical behavior (e.g., turning to talk to the confederate, stopping to pick up an object) were flagged and, if necessary, excluded from the analysis; this procedure affected two cases that were removed prior to the final exclusion count noted earlier.

Following the walking measurement, participants were brought to the next room for a short filler task and then underwent a standard debriefing that included a funneling procedure to assess awareness of the prime. The funneling debrief included open-ended questions about whether participants suspected any connection between the language task and their walking, and whether they noticed particular words in the sentence unscrambling task. Participants who directly reported awareness that the sentence unscrambling task had anything to do with their walking or who reported deliberately trying to walk faster or slower because of the sentences were excluded as noted above. The majority of participants (over 90%) reported no suspicion and did not report noticing that a subset of inscribed words had an age theme. The debriefing also concluded with a general explanation of the actual aims of the study, an opportunity for participants to ask questions, and the option to withdraw their data if they were uncomfortable.

Statistical analyses were pre-specified in an analysis plan that included both the principal ANOVA and supporting analyses. The primary analysis was a two-way between-subjects analysis of variance (ANOVA) with priming condition (elderly, neutral) and SVO consistency (consistent, inconsistent) as independent variables predicting walking time in seconds. The ANOVA approach was selected because it directly tests the hypothesized interaction between prime condition and SVO consistency. Prior to conducting the ANOVA, the walking time distribution was inspected for skewness and outliers. A small number of extreme values were trimmed if they exceeded three standard deviations from the mean in sensitivity analyses, but all primary analyses reported below were conducted on the full sample of 116 participants. In addition to the ANOVA, supplementary analyses included independent t-tests comparing the two priming conditions collapsed across SVO and correlational analyses between continuous SVO scores and walking time. A Bayesian ANOVA was also conducted to evaluate the relative evidence for the null versus alternative models.

Effect sizes for ANOVA effects are reported as partial eta-squared values, and for t-tests as Cohen’s d. Confidence intervals for mean differences and for effect sizes were computed using standard procedures. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with an alpha criterion set at .05. Analyses were performed using standard statistical software.

Results

Preliminary data inspection showed that walking times were approximately normally distributed with a small rightward skew, but no substantial skewness that warranted a nonparametric approach. The mean walking time across the full sample of 116 participants was 7.45 seconds with a standard deviation of 1.36 seconds. The elderly prime condition included 58 participants and the neutral prime condition included 58 participants. The number of participants classified as having consistent SVO was 62, while 54 participants were classified as inconsistent. The distribution of SVO consistency did not differ significantly across prime condition, as confirmed by a chi-square test of independence, indicating that random assignment to prime condition was successful with respect to SVO consistency.

Descriptive statistics for the four experimental cells are shown here in narrative form. Participants in the elderly prime/consistent SVO cell (n = 31) had a mean walking time of 7.62 seconds with a standard deviation of 1.21 seconds. Participants in the neutral prime/consistent SVO cell (n = 31) had a mean walking time of 7.48 seconds with a standard deviation of 1.10 seconds. Participants in the elderly prime/inconsistent SVO cell (n = 27) had a mean walking time of 7.39 seconds with a standard deviation of 1.40 seconds. Participants in the neutral prime/inconsistent SVO cell (n = 27) had a mean walking time of 7.21 seconds with a standard deviation of 1.56 seconds. These cell means suggested no large or systematic differences attributable to prime condition and only modest variation across the SVO consistency grouping.

The primary two-way ANOVA testing the effects of prime condition and SVO consistency on walking time yielded no significant main effect of priming. The effect of priming condition (elderly vs. neutral) on walking time was not statistically significant, F(1, 112) = 0.12, p = .73, partial eta-squared = .001. The observed mean difference between the elderly prime and neutral prime conditions was small (mean difference = 0.13 seconds) and the 95% confidence interval for this difference included zero, indicating a lack of reliable effect in the direction predicted by Hypothesis 1. The main effect of SVO consistency on walking time was also not statistically significant, F(1, 112) = 2.01, p = .16, partial eta-squared = .018. Although the consistent SVO group had a slightly higher mean walking time than the inconsistent group in the sample, the difference did not reach conventional statistical thresholds.

Critically, the interaction between priming condition and SVO consistency was non-significant, F(1, 112) = 0.03, p = .86, partial eta-squared < .001. The absence of a significant interaction indicates that the effect of elderly priming on walking time did not differ between participants with consistent versus inconsistent SVO profiles, contrary to Hypothesis 2. Inspection of simple effects within each SVO consistency group showed no reliable priming effect in either group when analyzed separately. For consistent SVO participants, the elderly prime versus neutral prime mean difference was 0.14 seconds, t(60) = 0.66, p = .51, Cohen’s d = 0.08. For inconsistent SVO participants, the elderly prime versus neutral prime mean difference was 0.18 seconds, t(52) = 0.74, p = .46, Cohen’s d = 0.10.

Given the non-significant omnibus tests, we conducted supplementary analyses to further probe the data and assess the robustness of the null effects. A Bayesian ANOVA comparing models that included the main effects and interaction to a null model favored the null model; the Bayes factor in favor of the null (BF01) was approximately 4.2, which can be interpreted as moderate evidence supporting the absence of priming and interaction effects in these data under the priors used. Sensitivity analyses that excluded the two walking trials that had been flagged for atypical behavior yielded the same pattern of results, with no main effect of priming and no interaction.

We also examined whether individual differences in the continuous SVO score related to walking time using Pearson correlations. The correlation between continuous SVO index and walking time was small and non-significant, r = .08, p = .38, indicating that greater prosociality or other continuous variations in SVO were not systematically related to walking speed in this study. To guard against potential non-linearity, we examined scatterplots of SVO score versus walking time and found no discernible curvilinear pattern.

Manipulation checks indicated that explicit awareness of the prime was uncommon in our sample. In the funneling debrief, five participants (4.3% of the final sample) reported noticing words related to age themes in the sentence unscrambling task, but none of these participants reported believing that those words could affect their walking. Excluding those five participants did not change the ANOVA results. A comparison of walking times for these five participants versus the remainder of the sample showed no consistent pattern suggesting that awareness accounted for the overall null effect.

Given that prior literature has sometimes found small effects, we also computed achieved power based on the observed variance and the observed small effect sizes. The current study had high power to detect medium-sized effects (greater than 0.80 power to detect an effect corresponding to Cohen’s d of approximately 0.50) but was underpowered to detect very small effects (e.g., d = 0.10). Effect size estimates from the present data indicate that any priming effect on walking time, if present in the population, is likely to be smaller than Cohen’s convention for a small effect size.

Finally, exploratory analyses examined whether participant age or self-reported familiarity with elderly stereotypes correlated with walking time or interacted with priming condition. Age was unrelated to walking time in this sample, and self-reported familiarity with elderly stereotypes did not moderate priming effects. These supplementary findings provide little support for the notion that sample heterogeneity along these dimensions masked a true effect.

Discussion

The present study tested whether exposure to elderly-related words in a sentence unscrambling task would influence subsequent walking time and whether consistency in social value orientation (SVO) would moderate any such effect. Our primary hypotheses were not supported. There was no main effect of elderly priming on walking time, and SVO consistency did not serve as a meaningful moderator of any priming effect. Supplementary analyses, including Bayesian model comparisons and sensitivity tests excluding potentially influential cases, converged on the conclusion that the data provided no evidence for the predicted priming effects under the present experimental conditions.

These findings add to a growing body of literature grappling with the robustness and boundary conditions of social priming effects, particularly those that appear to carry over from semantic activation to overt motoric behavior. While early influential studies reported relatively large effects of elderly priming on walking speed, subsequent replications and meta-analytic work have painted a more complex picture. Our results are consistent with more conservative interpretations that suggest priming effects may be smaller, less reliable, and more context-dependent than early accounts implied. The absence of an observable effect in the present sample suggests that, at least under the laboratory procedures we employed, elderly-related semantic activation did not translate into measurable differences in locomotion.

Several methodological and theoretical explanations may account for the null findings in this study. First, the strength of the priming manipulation may have been insufficient. Although the sentence unscrambling task is a standard priming method, the particular words chosen and their embedding within neutral sentence frames may not have produced strong or sufficiently salient activation of elderly-related concepts for participants in our sample. We deliberately sought to use subtle primes to maintain ecological validity and to parallel earlier work, but the degree of activation necessary to affect motoric behavior may be larger than what subtle linguistic manipulations achieve in many contexts.

Second, the timing and measurement of the walking behavior may have influenced the outcome. Priming effects are often temporally sensitive, and the time interval between the prime and the behavioral measurement can determine whether an effect emerges. In this experiment, there was a short but non-zero interval between completing the sentence unscrambling and the walking task due to questionnaire completion and the transition period. It is possible that any activated elderly-related representations decayed prior to the walking measurement, thereby attenuating the behavioral effect. Future studies might manipulate the delay systematically to map the temporal dynamics of any potential priming effect.

Third, our measurement of walking time may have lacked the sensitivity to detect very subtle shifts in gait. We used a single, short corridor and measured the time to traverse it; more fine-grained measures of gait, such as stride length, variability, or full-body motion capture, might detect micro-differences that simple timing cannot. If priming effects on locomotion operate through subtle changes in posture or cadence rather than overall pace over a short distance, such changes might not translate into statistically detectable differences in seconds over a 10-meter walk.

Fourth, and relevant to the second hypothesis, our operationalization of SVO consistency as a dichotomous variable may have obscured nuanced moderating relationships. The choice to categorize participants based on a majority threshold across nine decomposed game items provided a clear split for analysis, but it also simplified what is likely a more continuous psychological construct. Although we supplemented the categorical analysis with correlational tests using continuous SVO scores, it is possible that more refined models of social motivation, including measures of situationally activated goals versus stable preferences, might better capture individual differences relevant to priming susceptibility.

Fifth, there is the possibility that cultural and sample characteristics play a role. The influence of elderly-related stereotypes may vary across cultures and cohorts, depending on the salience of age-related beliefs and the personal relevance of participating in a university setting. Our sample composed largely of younger adults may have different baseline associations with elderly concepts than samples used in earlier studies, and these differences could attenuate priming effects if the stereotype network is organized differently.

Sixth, the role of experimenter behavior and context remains an important consideration. We took measures to blind the person timing walking and to minimize experimenter effects, but subtle experimenter cues or the laboratory environment itself may still interact with priming in complex ways. The presence or absence of explicit cues, the perceived expectations about speed of walking in a given facility, or the participants’ own precautions (e.g., being careful not to run or to avoid looking hurried) could moderate expression of any primed tendency.

Beyond methodological considerations, the theoretical implications of our null findings warrant discussion. If priming effects on overt motor behavior are not robust and require specific conjunctions of prime strength, timing, and individual susceptibility, then theoretical accounts that invoke automatic spreading activation as a ubiquitous pathway to behavior should be tempered by the recognition of boundary conditions. The absence of an SVO moderation effect in our data also suggests that the relationship between stable social preferences and momentary susceptibility to semantic activation may be more complex than a simple resilience model posits. It could be that the kind of goals indexed by SVO—preferences about resource allocation—operate in a different motivational domain than those that govern locomotion, and thus do not generalize as moderators for motoric priming.

The role of individual awareness is also relevant. Studies that have found robust effects often emphasize participants’ lack of awareness of primes. In our study, only a small minority noticed the age-themed words, and awareness did not account for the lack of effect. This suggests that explicit awareness alone is not enough to produce behavioral change, but it also means that unconscious activation alone, at least as induced by our materials, was insufficient.

It is likewise useful to consider statistical and power-related explanations. Our sample size was adequate to detect medium-sized effects, and Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence in favor of the null over models including priming. Nevertheless, we cannot rule out the presence of very small priming effects that our study was not powered to detect. The practical significance of such very small effects, even if real, would be limited unless such small shifts aggregate over important ecological timescales.

In terms of future research directions, several concrete steps could help clarify the phenomenon. First, preregistered multi-lab replications that systematically vary prime strength, prime modality (e.g., visual, auditory, embodied tasks), and timing would provide stronger evidence regarding the generalizability of priming effects on locomotion. Second, employing more sensitive gait measurement techniques, such as motion capture or wearable accelerometers, could reveal subtle motoric changes that simple timing cannot detect. Third, refining the measurement of individual differences relevant to priming—perhaps focusing on constructs more directly tied to motoric expression or contextual pliability than on SVO—may yield stronger moderator effects. Fourth, manipulating incentives or situational goals explicitly could test whether priming effects emerge only when participants’ goals are orthogonal to experimental demands; for example, if participants are motivated to appear unsullied by stereotypes, priming effects may be suppressed.

Finally, theoretical development is needed to better articulate when and how semantic activations should be expected to translate into observable action. Models that integrate associative spreading activation with motivational and embodiment frameworks could generate more precise predictions about the necessary conditions for priming-to-action effects. Such theoretical refinement should also incorporate the role of individual differences, measurement sensitivity, and temporal dynamics.

In conclusion, the present study found no evidence that elderly-related semantic priming influenced walking time, nor that consistency in social value orientation moderated any such effect. While these null findings do not conclusively disprove the possibility of priming effects in other contexts or under different conditions, they underscore the importance of examining boundary conditions and of employing robust, well-powered designs in investigations of social priming. The pattern of results reported here highlights the need for careful replication and methodological refinement in order to better understand the true scope and limits of semantic priming on embodied behavior.
