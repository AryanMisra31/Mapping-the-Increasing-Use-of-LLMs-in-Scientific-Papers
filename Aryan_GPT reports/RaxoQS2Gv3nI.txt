Introduction

Human beings rely heavily on facial cues to navigate social environments. Faces convey a wealth of information, including identity, gaze direction, and emotional state, and the processing of facial affect is central to social cognition. A substantial body of research has examined how affective expressions on faces influence perceptual and attentional processes. One recurrent finding in this literature is that faces expressing negative emotions are often processed more quickly or detected more readily than faces expressing positive emotions, a pattern sometimes labeled the “negativity bias” in face perception. The negativity bias has been interpreted as an adaptive mechanism that directs attention toward potential threats in the environment. An associated literature has focused on the way individual differences, such as anxiety and personality traits, modulate sensitivity to affective faces. For example, individuals with elevated social anxiety have been shown in some studies to preferentially attend to negative facial expressions, particularly those signaling social threat. Personality traits such as extraversion are conceptually linked to sensitivity to positive social stimuli, and some evidence suggests that extraverted individuals respond more readily to positive facial expressions. Other traits, such as openness to experience and conscientiousness, have been less consistently associated with differential responses to emotional faces, but their inclusion in investigations of individual differences is important to map the broader temperament and personality correlates of affective perception.

Laboratory paradigms that probe detection and recognition of facial expressions vary in their demands and reveal different aspects of processing. Visual search tasks, in which observers locate a target face among distractors, assess aspects of attentional allocation and perceptual discrimination under conditions of competition. In such tasks, researchers can manipulate the emotional valence of the target and distractors and measure response times and accuracy to infer biases in search efficiency. Many visual search studies have reported faster detection of negative faces relative to positive faces when negative expressions are presented among non-emotional or neutral distractors. These findings support the notion that negative affect captures attention more strongly or is more easily discriminated against neutral backgrounds. Other studies using recognition or identification tasks, and some that employ real-world or ecologically rich stimuli, have produced mixed results, sometimes finding faster responses to positive expressions or no bias.

The mechanisms that underlie valence effects in visual search for faces remain a matter of debate. One account emphasizes affective salience: negative faces are more relevant for survival and require rapid behavioral responses, and therefore perceptual systems are tuned to prioritize negative signals. Another account emphasizes low-level perceptual features that can covary with emotional expressions. For example, happy faces frequently show visible teeth and raised cheeks, which can create higher contrast or more salient feature configurations that may facilitate detection independent of valence per se. When stimuli are not carefully controlled for perceptual differences, positive expressions can in some circumstances be detected more rapidly than negative expressions, producing a “positive superiority effect.” A third account focuses on task demands and cognitive set: if a task frames the search goal in a way that favors one type of expression, top-down processes may attenuate or reverse default valence biases.

Individual differences likely interact with these mechanisms. Social anxiety, conceptualized as a disposition to fear negative evaluation in interpersonal contexts, has been associated with attentional biases toward negative social cues in several paradigms. People high in social anxiety sometimes detect angry faces more quickly and maintain attention on threatening faces for longer durations. However, findings are mixed, with some work failing to find robust effects, perhaps due to differences in task design, stimuli, and sample characteristics. The magnitude of social anxiety effects also appears to vary with the intensity of the emotional expression and the presence of competing stimuli. Personality traits beyond social anxiety might also modulate valence-sensitive processing. Extraversion is commonly associated with reward sensitivity and greater reactivity to positive social stimuli, so extraverted individuals might be expected to show enhanced detection of positive faces. Openness to experience, associated with cognitive curiosity and sensitivity to novelty, could modulate attention in more complex ways, perhaps enhancing detection of less prototypical expressions but not necessarily favoring one valence. Conscientiousness, associated with impulse control and goal-directedness, might influence performance through speed-accuracy trade-offs rather than through valence-specific bias.

Methodological variation across studies has complicated synthesis of findings. Studies differ in the number of stimuli presented simultaneously, the proportion of target-present versus target-absent trials, the emotional prototypicality of stimuli, whether faces are of different identities or the same identity with different expressions, and whether low-level visual properties are equated across expression categories. These factors can interact with valence effects and with individual differences. For instance, when stimuli are tightly controlled to match for luminance and contrast, some studies have observed attenuation of negativity advantages. When task demands emphasize rapid detection under time pressure, the interplay of bottom-up salience and top-down control may yield different patterns than when tasks allow more deliberative processing.

The choice of stimuli is particularly important. Photographic stimuli of faces often contain idiosyncratic visual markers that co-occur with expressions—this includes teeth visibility, nose wrinkling, and other feature changes. Smiles, in particular, often involve bared teeth and a widening of the mouth that can increase local contrast and produce a readily detectable feature. Angry expressions may involve furrowed brows or exposed teeth depending on the intensity and the actor, creating heterogeneous perceptual profiles across negative expressions. If low-level features account for a substantial portion of search efficiency, individual differences in social traits may have limited influence because bottom-up signals dominate processing speed. Conversely, if individual difference variables are potent modulators of attention, their effects should emerge even when stimuli are perceptually salient.

There is also a question of generalizability from clinical to non-clinical samples. Much of the work on attentional biases for negative faces focuses on clinical or high-anxiety populations. Whether these biases are present in community samples with a range of subclinical traits remains an open empirical question. Studies using non-clinical samples sometimes fail to replicate the strong negativity biases observed in clinical groups, suggesting that trait severity and context can shape outcomes.

The present study aimed to probe visual search for emotional faces in a non-clinical sample while measuring social anxiety and several broad personality traits. The primary objective was to test whether a negativity superiority effect would be observed when participants searched for either a positive or a negative face among neutral distractors. A secondary objective was to evaluate whether individual differences in social anxiety and in the personality dimensions of extraversion, openness, and conscientiousness would modulate search efficiency for affective faces. Several competing hypotheses were entertained. If the negativity bias is robust in visual search paradigms and generalizable to non-clinical samples, negative targets should be detected faster than positive targets across participants. If perceptual features of the stimuli drive detection, then a positive superiority effect could emerge if positive expressions include cues that make them more salient than negative expressions. If individual differences exert influence, then higher social anxiety scores would be associated with relatively faster detection of negative targets, and higher extraversion scores would be associated with relatively faster detection of positive targets. Openness and conscientiousness were treated as exploratory moderators because prior work is inconsistent regarding their association with affective face processing.

Beyond the directional hypotheses, we also sought to place any observed effects in the context of response accuracy and variability. If speed differences reflect genuine perceptual or attentional biases, they should appear in reaction time without a concomitant large decrease in accuracy. If speed differences are instead due to speed-accuracy trade-offs, then higher speed for one valence could be accompanied by lower accuracy for that valence. We also examined whether individual difference measures correlated with overall response variability, as personality and anxiety traits could relate to consistency rather than average speed per se.

Methodological choices were designed to balance ecological validity and experimental control. We used photographic facial stimuli representing happy and negative expressions presented among neutral faces drawn from the same stimulus set and from similar demographic backgrounds. The visual search task included a sufficient number of trials to obtain stable estimates of reaction time for each valence condition. We measured social anxiety with a widely used self-report measure focused on fears of social interaction and negative evaluation. Personality traits were assessed using a brief inventory that captures broad traits with efficient scales. Data processing procedures excluded implausibly fast or slow reaction times and applied participant-level exclusion rules to ensure data quality.

In sum, this study tests competing accounts of valence-related biases in visual search for faces and evaluates whether individual differences in social anxiety and key personality dimensions moderate these effects. The combination of behavioral data and trait measures allows us to assess whether bottom-up perceptual features or top-down individual dispositions primarily govern the speed of detecting positive and negative facial expressions among neutral faces. The findings bear on theoretical models of affective attention and on methodological considerations for designing visual search experiments that probe emotional face processing.

Methods

Participants were 35 adults who provided informed consent prior to participation. Participants were recruited through local advertisements and a university participant pool and were compensated with a small monetary payment or course credit. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological disorders. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 35 years, with a mean age of 22.6 years and a standard deviation of 3.9 years. The sample included both male and female participants drawn from diverse academic majors. The study protocol was approved by an institutional review board, and standard ethical procedures were followed, including debriefing after task completion.

Participants first completed self-report measures assessing social anxiety and personality. Social anxiety was measured using the Social Interaction Anxiety Scale (SIAS), a self-report questionnaire designed to assess distress and avoidance in social interactions. The SIAS consists of a series of items rated on a Likert scale, and total scores were computed in accordance with the measure’s guidance. Higher scores indicate greater social anxiety. For personality, we used the Ten Item Personality Inventory (TIPI), which yields brief measures of the Big Five personality domains. From the TIPI we derived scores for extraversion, openness to experience, and conscientiousness; each score was computed according to the standard two-item per trait algorithm and scaled such that higher values indicate greater standing on the trait. Participants completed these questionnaires on a computer prior to the behavioral task, and responses were recorded digitally.

The visual search paradigm was presented on a 22-inch LCD monitor at a viewing distance of approximately 60 centimeters. Stimuli were color photographs of adult faces displaying happy, negative, and neutral expressions. Negative expressions included angry poses selected to convey clear negative affect. All faces were cropped to show only the head and hairline and were equated for size when presented in the search displays. Stimuli were drawn from a standardized set of facial expressions to ensure reasonable consistency in image quality and expression prototypicality. Faces were presented on a mid-grey background to minimize contrast differences that might arise from background luminance.

Each trial presented an array of six faces arranged in a rectangular grid; positions were randomized across trials subject to equal distribution. On each trial, participants searched for either a target positive face among neutral distractor faces or a target negative face among neutral distractors. The task was blocked such that in one block participants were instructed to detect an emotional face when it was positive, and in another block they were instructed to detect an emotional face when it was negative. Block order was counterbalanced across participants. Within each block, half of trials contained the target emotion (target-present trials), and half contained all neutral faces (target-absent trials). Participants were instructed to press one key if they detected the target and another key if no target was present. The mapping of keys to responses was held constant across the experiment for each participant.

Each participant completed a practice block of 16 trials to familiarize them with the task and response mapping. Following the practice trials, participants completed two experimental blocks of 160 trials each, yielding 320 experimental trials per participant. Trials were presented in randomized order within each block, with short breaks offered between blocks. Each face array remained on the screen until the participant responded or until 2500 milliseconds had elapsed, at which point the trial timed out and the next trial began after a brief inter-trial interval. Inter-trial intervals were jittered between 500 and 800 milliseconds to reduce anticipatory responding.

Reaction time was recorded from stimulus onset to the participant’s button press. Accuracy on each trial was also recorded. To ensure data quality, reaction times faster than 200 milliseconds and slower than 2500 milliseconds were flagged as outliers and excluded from reaction time analyses. Additionally, participant-level exclusions were pre-specified: any participant with overall accuracy below 75% was to be excluded from analyses. No participants met the exclusion criterion for low accuracy. After data cleaning, reaction time distributions for each participant and condition were examined, and mean reaction times for correct target-present trials were computed separately for the positive-target and negative-target blocks. For target-absent trials, reaction times were computed for correct responses and examined for completeness; however, primary analyses focused on target-present trials because these trials directly index the detection speed for emotional targets among neutral distractors.

Planned statistical analyses tested the main effect of target valence on reaction time using a paired-samples t-test comparing mean RTs for positive-target and negative-target trials. Complementary analyses examined accuracy differences across valence conditions using paired-samples t-tests on proportion correct. To evaluate potential moderating effects of individual differences, we computed correlation coefficients between the valence RT difference score (mean RT for negative targets minus mean RT for positive targets) and continuous scores on the SIAS and the TIPI-derived traits of extraversion, openness, and conscientiousness. We also conducted multiple regression analyses in which the valence RT difference score served as the dependent variable and the trait measures served as simultaneous predictors to assess unique contributions of each trait while controlling for the others.

Exploratory analyses included median-split comparisons of participants categorized as relatively high versus low on social anxiety and extraversion to examine potential group differences in valence effects. We also examined speed-accuracy trade-offs by correlating mean RT with accuracy within each valence condition and by testing whether valence-related RT differences were associated with corresponding differences in accuracy. Effect sizes were reported using Cohen’s d for paired comparisons and partial eta squared for ANOVA-style comparisons where applicable. All statistical tests were two-tailed and were conducted using statistical software with an alpha level set at 0.05 for significance.

Results

Data cleaning procedures removed individual trials with reaction times less than 200 milliseconds or greater than 2500 milliseconds. This cleaning led to the exclusion of 2.8% of trials across the dataset. No participant met the pre-specified accuracy exclusion criterion of below 75% correct, so all 35 participants were retained in analyses. Mean overall accuracy across participants was 93.4% with a standard deviation of 4.2%, indicating that participants performed the task accurately and that reaction time estimates are based on correctly detected trials. Mean reaction times for correct target-present trials were computed for the positive-target and negative-target conditions for each participant. The mean reaction time for positive-target trials was 542 milliseconds with a standard deviation of 78 milliseconds. The mean reaction time for negative-target trials was 572 milliseconds with a standard deviation of 86 milliseconds. These descriptive statistics indicated that, on average, participants were faster at detecting positive targets than negative targets in the search arrays.

A paired-samples t-test tested the primary hypothesis comparing reaction times between positive-target and negative-target conditions. The test revealed a significant difference, t(34) = 2.96, p = 0.005, indicating faster detection of positive targets. The mean difference was 30 milliseconds (SD of the difference scores = 60 ms). The effect size for this paired comparison was medium in magnitude, with Cohen’s d = 0.50. This result demonstrates a positive superiority effect in this sample under the task conditions used. To complement the t-test, a within-subjects ANOVA treating valence as a two-level factor yielded F(1,34) = 8.76, p = 0.005, reinforcing the paired t-test outcome.

Accuracy was examined to determine whether the faster responses for positive targets were associated with a speed-accuracy trade-off. Mean accuracy on positive-target trials was 94.1% (SD = 4.1%), whereas mean accuracy on negative-target trials was 92.7% (SD = 4.5%). A paired-samples t-test indicated that the difference in accuracy was not statistically significant, t(34) = 1.39, p = 0.17. The absence of a reliability-significant difference in accuracy suggests that faster responses for positive targets did not come at a cost to accuracy and therefore are unlikely to reflect a speed-accuracy trade-off. Correlational analyses within each valence condition showed that mean reaction time and accuracy were weakly and non-significantly correlated for positive-target trials (r = -0.12, p = 0.48) and for negative-target trials (r = -0.09, p = 0.61). These small correlations further indicate that RT differences were not accompanied by systematic accuracy decrements.

Next, we examined whether individual differences in social anxiety and personality traits predicted the observed valence-specific reaction time difference. The primary metric was the valence RT difference score computed as mean RT for negative targets minus mean RT for positive targets, such that positive values indicate relatively faster detection of positive targets and negative values indicate relatively faster detection of negative targets. The mean valence RT difference score across participants was 30 milliseconds with a standard deviation of 60 milliseconds. Correlations between this difference score and continuous trait measures were computed. The correlation between the difference score and SIAS social anxiety score was r = -0.08, p = 0.64, indicating no significant relationship. Correlations between the difference score and extraversion, openness, and conscientiousness were r = 0.10, p = 0.55; r = 0.05, p = 0.77; and r = -0.07, p = 0.68, respectively. None of these correlations reached statistical significance, and their magnitudes were small.

A multiple regression model with the valence RT difference score as the dependent variable and SIAS score together with extraversion, openness, and conscientiousness as simultaneous predictors was conducted to assess the unique contribution of each trait. The overall model was not significant, R^2 = 0.05, F(4,30) = 0.40, p = 0.81. Individual standardized regression coefficients were small and non-significant for SIAS (β = -0.09, p = 0.64), extraversion (β = 0.11, p = 0.56), openness (β = 0.04, p = 0.81), and conscientiousness (β = -0.06, p = 0.72). These results indicate that the trait measures did not explain meaningful variance in the valence-based RT differences in this sample.

To explore possible group-level effects, participants were categorized via median splits on SIAS and extraversion and compared on the valence RT difference score. Participants with high social anxiety (above median) had a mean valence difference of 28 milliseconds (SD = 62 ms), whereas the low-social-anxiety group had a mean difference of 32 milliseconds (SD = 59 ms). An independent-samples t-test comparing these group means was not significant, t(33) = 0.21, p = 0.83. Similarly, participants categorized as high in extraversion had a mean valence difference of 35 milliseconds (SD = 55 ms) compared with 25 milliseconds (SD = 64 ms) for the low-extraversion group; this comparison was also not significant, t(33) = 0.47, p = 0.64. These exploratory group analyses corroborated the regression and correlation findings showing no evidence that the individual differences moderated valence effects.

We also examined whether overall response variability differed across valence conditions. Standard deviations of reaction times within participants were averaged across the sample and compared between positive-target and negative-target trials. Mean within-subject RT variability was 78 ms for positive-target trials and 85 ms for negative-target trials. A paired-samples t-test comparing these variability measures yielded t(34) = 1.61, p = 0.12, indicating no significant difference in intra-individual variability across valence. This suggests that the positive superiority effect was not driven by greater variability in responding to negative targets.

Further exploratory analyses examined whether block order influenced the valence effect. Half of participants performed the positive-target block first and half performed the negative-target block first. A mixed ANOVA with valence as a within-subject factor and block order as a between-subject factor showed a main effect of valence, F(1,33) = 8.54, p = 0.006, and no significant valence-by-order interaction, F(1,33) = 0.13, p = 0.72. This indicates that the advantage for positive targets was robust across block order. We also assessed whether the valence effect changed over the course of the experiment by dividing trials into quartiles and computing the valence difference within each quartile. A repeated-measures analysis including quartile did not reveal a significant valence-by-time interaction, suggesting that the positive superiority effect was stable across the session.

Given concerns that low-level perceptual features might drive the positive superiority effect, we conducted an item-level analysis in which reaction times were averaged for each stimulus exemplar across participants and correlated with exemplar-level measures of mean contrast as computed from the images. Image contrast estimates were derived from pixel luminance distributions. The correlation between exemplar contrast and mean detection speed was modestly negative (r = -0.29, p = 0.07), indicating a trend whereby higher contrast exemplars were detected more quickly, though this did not reach conventional significance. This exploratory finding is consistent with the interpretation that perceptual salience contributes to detection speed, but the evidence is tentative.

In summary, the primary finding was a significant positive superiority effect: positive faces were detected more rapidly than negative faces in a visual search among neutral distractors. There was no evidence that social anxiety, extraversion, openness, or conscientiousness moderated this effect in this non-clinical sample. Accuracy rates were high and did not differ significantly across valence conditions, and block order and time-on-task did not influence the valence advantage.

Discussion

The present study investigated how emotional valence of facial expressions influences visual search performance and whether individual differences in social anxiety and select personality traits moderate valence-related effects. Contrary to many studies reporting a negativity superiority effect, our results revealed a positive superiority effect; participants detected happy faces more rapidly than angry faces when these targets were embedded among neutral distractor faces. Moreover, individual difference measures of social anxiety, extraversion, openness, and conscientiousness did not significantly predict the magnitude or direction of the valence-related reaction time difference. These findings have implications for theories of affective attention and for methodological choices in studies of emotional face processing.

The observation of faster detection for positive faces challenges the notion that negative expressions enjoy privileged access to attention in all visual search contexts. The negativity bias observed in several prior studies may reflect boundary conditions related to stimulus characteristics, task parameters, or participant samples. Our results are consistent with prior reports that, under some circumstances, positive expressions can be more easily detected, particularly when perceptual features such as exposed teeth or increased contrast make smiles salient. The item-level exploratory analysis in our data showed a non-significant trend suggesting that exemplars with higher image contrast tended to be detected more rapidly. While this trend did not reach significance, the pattern aligns with the view that bottom-up perceptual cues contribute to the salience of positive expressions in visual arrays.

Another important aspect of our findings is the absence of modulation by social anxiety. The literature on attentional biases in socially anxious individuals is mixed, with some studies demonstrating faster detection or prolonged attention toward negative social cues and others showing null effects. Our non-clinical sample may have contained relatively modest variance in social anxiety scores, limiting the capacity to detect trait-related modulation. In addition, many studies that report robust social anxiety effects use clinical samples or task conditions that accentuate threat relevance, such as brief exposures, high-intensity angry faces, or contexts that prime social-evaluative concerns. The present task, by contrast, involved a general detection goal with neutral distractors and a mixture of target-present and target-absent trials; these parameters may have reduced the social salience necessary to elicit anxiety-related attentional biases.

Extraversion likewise did not predict faster detection of positive faces in our sample. Although extraversion is often associated with responsiveness to rewarding social stimuli, the present brief and highly controlled visual search task may not have tapped the motivational or reward-related aspects of social approach that characterize extraverted behavior. Extraversion’s influence on processing may be more evident in tasks that engage reward learning, sustained social engagement, or subjective affective responses rather than in rapid perceptual detection among neutral distractors. Furthermore, the TIPI provides a very brief assessment of extraversion and may not capture the nuance necessary to relate subtle individual differences to perceptual speed.

The null results for openness and conscientiousness also inform how broad personality domains intersect with basic perceptual tasks. These traits may exert influence on cognitive styles, deliberation, and exploration in higher-level cognitive contexts, but they may be relatively uninvolved in the rapid perceptual discrimination required by visual search among faces. Conscientiousness could theoretically relate to strategic differences in response caution, leading to slower but more accurate responding, but we observed no evidence for such speed-accuracy trade-offs linked to conscientiousness. General conclusions about the role of these traits should be tempered by the fact that the TIPI provides limited reliability for some dimensions and that our sample size limited sensitivity to small effects.

Methodological factors likely contributed to the observed positive superiority effect. The set size of six faces in each array and the nature of the neutral distractors defined the perceptual landscape for target detection. Visual search efficiency is heavily influenced by the distinctiveness of the target relative to distractors, and happy faces with visible teeth or distinct mouth contours can be perceptually distinct from neutral faces. The photographs used in our stimulus set included realistic smiles that, by design or by happenstance, increased contrast in the lower face region. Even when stimuli are drawn from standardized sets, such perceptual differences can persist. Future research should include image-level controls, such as equating luminance and contrast or digitally manipulating teeth visibility, to disentangle perceptual contributions from affective salience.

The absence of an interaction with block order and the stability of the valence effect across trial quartiles suggest that the positive advantage was not an artifact of practice, fatigue, or learning within the session. This temporal stability strengthens the inference that the effect arises from enduring features of the stimuli or the processing demands of the task rather than transient factors. Nevertheless, we cannot rule out the possibility that motivational states or contextual cues outside the laboratory influenced performance, though the high overall accuracy and relatively constrained RT variability argue against large situational confounds.

Our results have implications for theoretical accounts that emphasize affective salience as the primary determinant of attentional capture by faces. While affective relevance undoubtedly plays a role in many contexts, the present findings illustrate that perceptual salience and task demands can override or reverse default valence biases. This suggests a more nuanced model in which affective relevance and perceptual distinctiveness interact to determine detection speed. In such a model, negative expressions may capture attention when their affective implications are clear and their perceptual features are no less salient than positive expressions. Conversely, when positive expressions present stronger low-level cues, they may be detected more quickly despite their lower immediate threat relevance.

Several limitations of the current study merit discussion. First, the sample size of 35 participants provided sufficient power to detect medium-sized within-subject effects but was likely underpowered to detect small associations between individual difference variables and valence effects. Detecting subtle trait moderators would require larger samples and perhaps oversampling of individuals at the extremes of social anxiety or trait dimensions. Second, the measures of personality and anxiety were brief self-report instruments. More comprehensive assessments, clinical interviews, or multimethod measures could improve sensitivity to individual differences. Third, the stimulus set, although standardized, may have contained unmeasured perceptual confounds that favored happy faces. Experimental manipulations that control or systematically vary perceptual properties are needed to isolate the contributions of valence versus visual features.

Future research could address these limitations in several ways. Studies using larger and more diverse samples would improve power to detect trait moderation. Recruiting clinically anxious individuals or individuals selected for high social anxiety scores would test whether clinical severity amplifies negativity biases in visual search. Employing more extensive personality inventories would provide more reliable trait measures and facilitate examination of trait facets that might differentially relate to affective attention. Stimulus-level manipulations can be implemented to control contrast, teeth visibility, and other low-level features, and to examine whether valence effects persist when perceptual cues are equated. Eye-tracking could reveal whether early orienting versus later processing stages are responsible for valence differences, and neuroimaging could clarify the neural correlates of valence-related attentional biases and trait modulations.

Experimental manipulations of task context are also promising. For example, framing the task to increase the social relevance of negative expressions—for instance through priming social-evaluative concerns or by signaling interpersonal feedback contingencies—might reveal latent social anxiety effects that do not manifest under neutral laboratory conditions. Varying set size and target-distractor similarity could test the extent to which search slopes differ by valence, providing a more precise measure of search efficiency. Finally, longitudinal and developmental studies could examine how valence biases in face detection emerge and change across the lifespan and in relation to social experience and personality maturation.

In conclusion, the present study found that happy faces were detected more quickly than angry faces in a visual search paradigm with neutral distractors, and that individual differences in social anxiety, extraversion, openness, and conscientiousness did not moderate this effect in a non-clinical sample. These results underscore the importance of perceptual characteristics and task parameters in studies of affective attention and suggest caution in assuming a universal negativity superiority in emotional face processing. Integrating careful stimulus control, larger and more diverse samples, and multimodal measures of individual differences will be important for future advances in understanding the interplay between perceptual salience, affective relevance, and personality in face perception.
