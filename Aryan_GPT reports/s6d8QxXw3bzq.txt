Introduction

Understanding individual differences in self-evaluative processes and perceived control is a central concern in psychological science. Self-esteem, broadly conceived as an individual's overall evaluation of their worth, has long been linked to a variety of cognitive, affective, and behavioral outcomes. Locus of control, typically framed along an internal–external dimension, describes the degree to which individuals perceive outcomes as contingent on their own actions versus external forces. Both constructs have been studied extensively in isolation, but their interrelation remains theoretically and practically important. The two constructs share conceptual territory in that both reflect a person's beliefs about the self and its relation to the environment. Where self-esteem captures evaluative judgments about the self, locus of control captures beliefs about causal influence and agency. As such, an empirical relationship between self-esteem and locus of control is expected on theoretical grounds.

Several theoretical perspectives predict a positive association between high self-esteem and an internal locus of control. From a social-cognitive standpoint, individuals with strong self-regard are more likely to view themselves as effective agents capable of influencing outcomes. From an attributional perspective, people with higher self-esteem tend to attribute successes more to internal, stable factors, and failures less to internal dispositional shortcomings. From a motivational perspective, internal locus orientations are consistent with approach-oriented behaviors that bolster self-esteem through mastery experiences. Accordingly, a body of empirical work has reported associations in the expected direction, with higher self-esteem typically accompanying more internal control beliefs. These findings have been reported across age groups and in many cultural contexts, although effect sizes and the precise nature of the relationship can vary.

Beyond theoretical compatibility, empirical evidence links both constructs to real-world outcomes. High self-esteem has been associated with better psychological well-being, greater persistence on challenging tasks, and superior social functioning. Similarly, internal locus of control has been associated with academic achievement, job performance, health-promoting behaviors, and adaptive coping. These outcome associations have raised an important applied question: to what extent does the interplay between self-evaluative beliefs and control beliefs underpin adaptive outcomes such as academic performance? If the two constructs are strongly related, interventions targeting one might influence the other and thereby have broader effects on functioning.

Despite an extensive literature, important gaps remain. First, many studies have been conducted with heterogeneous samples, including community adults, clinical groups, and non-university populations. University student samples, while common, are often drawn from non-selective institutions, and there is a relative scarcity of work that specifically examines selective university cohorts, such as students at research-intensive universities. Second, much of the work examining self-esteem and locus of control has relied on cross-sectional designs, which can establish association but not directionality. Third, measurement choices can influence observed associations. For example, some measures of locus of control emphasize generalized expectancies while others focus on more specific domains. Likewise, self-esteem measures vary in their focus on global versus domain-specific evaluations. These methodological variations complicate synthesis across studies.

One useful approach to addressing some of these issues is to examine the straightforward association between well-established measures of global self-esteem and generalized locus of control within a clearly specified cohort. The Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale is arguably the most widely used global self-esteem instrument and is characterized by brevity and robust psychometric properties. The Rotter Internal-External Locus of Control Scale is a longstanding measure of generalized control beliefs and remains widely used in research despite some critique regarding its factorial properties. Using these instruments within a single sample allows direct tests of the robustness of the association between global self-esteem and generalized locus of control in a contemporary cohort.

Another important consideration is sample composition. Students attending selective, research-intensive universities may differ systematically from other student cohorts. Selection processes, academic climate, and social norms at such institutions can shape students’ beliefs about competence and control. For instance, admission to a competitive university may scaffold initial self-efficacy and internal control beliefs, yet social comparison processes and performance pressure may also introduce variability in self-evaluative processes. Therefore, examining the self-esteem—locus of control relation specifically in a selective university cohort can help determine whether previously reported associations generalize to populations characterized by high average academic achievement and distinct social environments.

Furthermore, the extant literature suggests links between self-concept more broadly defined and locus of control, and it is of interest whether findings for global self-esteem replicate patterns found for more general self-concept measures. Self-concept research often encompasses domain-specific evaluations as well as overarching identity considerations. If the relationship between self-evaluative constructs and perceived control is a general phenomenon, then self-esteem and self-concept should show associations with locus of control in the same direction. Establishing this replication would strengthen the case for theoretical models that posit a fundamental coupling between self-evaluation and control beliefs.

From a practical standpoint, documenting the relation between self-esteem and locus of control in a university context has implications for educational policy and student support services. If higher self-esteem corresponds to a more internal locus of control, then interventions that foster self-esteem may also encourage students to take more active control of their academic trajectories. Conversely, interventions designed to enhance perceptions of control may bolster self-regard and resilience. Thus, empirical data on the magnitude and direction of the association are relevant to the design of student well-being and performance interventions.

Given these considerations, the present study examined the association between global self-esteem and generalized locus of control in a sample of undergraduate students enrolled at Russell Group universities. The choice of this cohort was motivated by the desire to test whether previously observed associations replicate in a high-achieving, selective undergraduate population. The study applied a within-participants correlational design in which participants completed the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale and the Rotter Locus of Control Scale. The central hypothesis was that self-esteem and locus of control would be significantly associated, such that higher self-esteem would be associated with a more internal locus of control.

In addition to testing the primary association, the study evaluated psychometric properties of the measures within the sample and examined the practical magnitude of the association. Specifically, the analysis estimated the effect size of the correlation, reported the proportion of shared variance, and considered the results in light of potential implications for student outcomes. The goal was to provide a clear, focused test of the theoretical link between self-evaluation and perceived control in a contemporary selective undergraduate sample.

Method

Design and overview. The study employed a within-participants correlational design. Each participant completed two self-report questionnaires during a single assessment session. Scores from the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale and the Rotter Locus of Control Scale were used as the primary variables for analysis. The primary statistical test was a Pearson product-moment correlation evaluating the linear association between the two measures.

Participants. The sample comprised 100 undergraduate students enrolled at Russell Group universities. Both male and female participants were included. Participants were recruited via campus notices, lecture announcements, and online postings targeted to undergraduate mailing lists. The mean age of the sample was 20.1 years, reflecting the typical age range for undergraduate cohorts. All participants were current undergraduates and reported being fluent in English. Inclusion criteria required enrollment in an undergraduate degree program and age 18 or above. Exclusion criteria included a self-reported history of severe psychiatric illness that could impede informed consent or reliable questionnaire completion.

Ethical considerations. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by an institutional ethics committee. Participants provided informed consent prior to participation. They were informed that their responses would be anonymized and that participation was voluntary. They were also told that they could withdraw at any time without penalty. Data were stored securely in password-protected files and anonymized identifiers were used for analysis. Participants received a small monetary token or course credit in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Measures. Global self-esteem was assessed using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale. This instrument consists of ten items that measure overall feelings of self-worth and self-acceptance. Items are presented on a four-point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree. Responses were scored according to standard procedures, yielding a total score indicative of global self-esteem. Higher scores reflect higher self-esteem. The Rosenberg scale is brief and widely used, which facilitates comparison with prior research.

Locus of control was assessed using the Rotter Internal-External Locus of Control Scale. The scale consists of a series of forced-choice items that contrast internal versus external attributions for control. Items present two alternatives and respondents select the option that best reflects their beliefs. The total score is computed as the number of external choices, with higher scores indicating a more external locus of control. Lower scores thus reflect a more internal orientation. The Rotter scale has been used extensively in research on generalized control expectancies.

Procedure. Participants attended a single assessment session in which they completed questionnaires in a quiet lab space or via a secure online survey platform. The order of measures was kept consistent to minimize variability, with the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale administered first followed by the Rotter Locus of Control Scale. The session took approximately 15 to 20 minutes to complete. Participants were supervised by research staff in lab sessions and were provided contact information for study personnel in the online setting.

Data handling and analysis plan. Data were screened for missing responses and invalid patterns of responding. Cases with substantial missing data (more than 10% of items missing on a given scale) would have been excluded from analyses. For cases with minimal missingness, mean imputation at the item level was planned. Score distributions for both measures were examined for normality using skewness, kurtosis, and formal tests such as the Shapiro-Wilk statistic. Outliers were inspected both visually and via standardized scores. The primary analysis involved computing a Pearson correlation coefficient between Rosenberg Self-Esteem scores and Rotter Locus of Control scores. Given the theoretical expectation of a negative association (higher self-esteem associated with lower Rotter externality scores), a two-tailed test at an alpha level of .05 was used to assess significance.

Additional analyses. To provide context for the bivariate association, descriptive statistics including means and standard deviations for each scale were calculated. Internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) was computed for the Rosenberg scale and for the items composing the Rotter scale, recognizing that forced-choice formats can yield different reliability characteristics. The proportion of shared variance explained by the correlation (r^2) was reported to convey effect size. If the data supported it, simple regression analyses were run to assess the extent to which locus of control scores could predict self-esteem scores and vice versa. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software.

Results

Data quality and descriptive statistics. Data from 100 participants were available for analysis. The dataset contained complete responses for the two primary measures for all included participants. Inspection of item-level missingness revealed no cases with more than minimal missing data. Descriptive statistics were computed for the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale and the Rotter Locus of Control Scale. The sample mean for the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale was 21.4, indicating moderate to relatively high self-esteem on average in the cohort. The standard deviation for the Rosenberg score was 4.3, reflecting modest variability among participants. For the Rotter Locus of Control Scale, the sample mean was 12.6, with a standard deviation of 3.8, indicating a range of internal and external orientations within the sample.

Reliability estimates. Internal consistency for the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale, as indexed by Cronbach’s alpha, was calculated and found to be satisfactory at α = .88 in this sample. The Rotter scale yielded a lower internal consistency estimate, α = .71, which is consistent with prior observations that forced-choice locus of control measures can produce moderate reliability estimates. Overall, both measures displayed adequate psychometric properties to support correlational analyses within the present dataset.

Assumptions and data screening. The distributions of both primary variables were examined for normality. The Rosenberg score exhibited skewness and kurtosis values within acceptable bounds for parametric testing. The Rotter score likewise displayed skewness and kurtosis values that were not extreme. Formal Shapiro-Wilk tests were non-significant for both measures, suggesting no strong departures from univariate normality. Scatterplot inspection did not reveal any extreme bivariate outliers that might unduly influence the correlation coefficient. Based on these checks, the assumptions for Pearson correlation were judged to be adequately met.

Primary correlation. The principal analysis examined the association between global self-esteem and generalized locus of control. The Pearson correlation between Rosenberg Self-Esteem scores and Rotter Locus of Control scores was r = -0.384. This correlation was statistically significant at p < .01. The negative sign of the coefficient indicates that higher self-esteem was associated with lower Rotter scores, that is, a more internal locus of control. The magnitude of the correlation corresponds to a medium effect size according to conventional benchmarks.

Effect size interpretation. The squared correlation, r^2, was 0.147, which indicates that approximately 14.7% of the variance in one construct is shared with the other in this sample. While a majority of variance in each measure remains unexplained by the other, the observed shared variance is nontrivial and suggests a meaningful relationship between self-esteem and control beliefs within this cohort.

Robustness checks. To assess the robustness of the finding, additional exploratory checks were conducted. A simple linear regression predicting Rosenberg Self-Esteem scores from Rotter Locus of Control scores was computed. The regression indicated a significant negative slope, with Rotter score accounting for approximately 14.7% of the variance in self-esteem, consistent with the correlation. Residuals from the regression did not indicate major departures from assumptions of homoscedasticity or normality. Influence statistics did not identify any single case exerting undue leverage on the parameter estimates.

Gender comparisons. Descriptive comparisons by gender were examined to characterize potential subgroup differences. Mean self-esteem and locus of control scores were computed separately for male and female participants. The pattern of association between self-esteem and locus of control was present in both subgroups. Correlations within gender subgroups were of the same sign and comparable magnitude, although sample size limitations within each subgroup reduce precision of these estimates. In short, the negative association between self-esteem and Rotter externality scores was not restricted to one gender in this sample.

Visualization. A scatterplot of Rosenberg Self-Esteem scores by Rotter Locus of Control scores illustrated the negative linear trend. The plot suggested a fairly uniform distribution of points without strong curvature, supporting the interpretation of a monotonic linear association. Points representing higher self-esteem clustered toward lower Rotter scores, consistent with the negative correlation reported.

Interpretational clarity. It is important to note that the Rotter scoring convention used here assigns higher numerical values to more external orientations. Thus, the negative correlation can be directly interpreted as higher self-esteem co-occurring with a more internal locus of control. This pattern aligns with theoretical expectations and prior empirical findings linking higher self-regard to increased perceptions of personal agency.

Statistical significance and practical relevance. The p-value associated with the correlation was less than .01, indicating that, under null hypothesis testing conventions, the observed association is unlikely to have arisen by chance in the present sample. The medium effect size suggests practical relevance; roughly 15% shared variance is meaningful for psychological constructs and has implications for understanding how self evaluations and control beliefs interrelate.

Additional notes. No floor or ceiling effects were detected on either measure. The distributions showed adequate spread to support correlational inferences. Given these findings, caution is nonetheless warranted in drawing causal conclusions because of the cross-sectional nature of the data. The observed association indicates a relation but does not establish temporal precedence or causal direction.

Summary. In summary, the data from 100 Russell Group undergraduate students demonstrated a statistically significant negative correlation between Rosenberg Self-Esteem scores and Rotter Locus of Control scores (r = -0.384, p < .01). Higher self-esteem corresponded with a more internal locus of control. The association was observable across genders and remained robust under basic diagnostic checks. The effect size was of a magnitude consistent with conceptual expectations and prior research linking self-evaluations and perceived control.

Discussion

This study examined the association between global self-esteem and generalized locus of control in a sample of undergraduate students enrolled at Russell Group universities. The principal finding was a statistically significant negative correlation of r = -0.384 between scores on the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale and the Rotter Locus of Control Scale. Given the scoring convention of the Rotter measure, the negative correlation indicates that participants who reported higher self-esteem also reported a more internal locus of control. This association accounted for about 14.7% of the shared variance between the constructs and was consistent across genders in the present sample. The finding aligns with theoretical accounts that posit a linkage between self-evaluative beliefs and perceived agency.

The observed association is consistent with social-cognitive and attributional models suggesting that individuals who hold positive global evaluations of themselves are more likely to perceive outcomes as contingent on their own actions. In such models, self-esteem supports expectancies of competence, which in turn foster internal control beliefs. Conversely, internal control beliefs can reinforce self-esteem by facilitating mastery experiences and feelings of effectiveness. The cross-sectional design used here does not permit disentangling directional influences, but the robust association is congruent with frameworks that conceptualize the two constructs as mutually reinforcing over time.

Comparing the magnitude of the effect to prior work, the medium-sized correlation observed in this study is consistent with earlier findings that have documented moderate associations between self-evaluation and control expectancies. The approximately 15% shared variance may appear modest, but it reflects a substantial overlap for constructs that are conceptually related yet theoretically distinct. Self-esteem captures evaluative feelings about global worth, whereas locus of control captures beliefs about causality and agency. The fact that these distinct constructs share meaningful variance underscores the interdependence of evaluative and explanatory systems in the psyche.

The results also have implications for understanding academic performance and related outcomes in selective university cohorts. Both high self-esteem and internal locus of control have been associated, in separate literatures, with better academic outcomes, persistence, and adaptive coping with academic stress. In the context of Russell Group universities, where competition and performance demands are salient, the coupling of self-esteem and perceived control may play a role in shaping students’ approaches to study, their responses to setbacks, and their utilization of available academic resources. While this study did not directly measure performance, the pattern of interrelations suggests that interventions that bolster either self-esteem or internal control beliefs might have downstream benefits for academic functioning.

Practical implications follow from the observed association. University support services that aim to promote student well-being could consider integrated approaches that target both self-evaluation and control beliefs. For example, programs that scaffold mastery experiences, teach effective study strategies, and promote adaptive attributions for success and failure might simultaneously strengthen students’ sense of competence and their belief that outcomes are controllable. Such dual-focused interventions may be especially relevant in high-pressure academic contexts where students’ self-esteem and perceived agency can be taxed by competitive environments.

The study’s methodological choices also warrant discussion. The use of the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale provided a standardized and concise measure of global self-worth, allowing comparability with the broader literature. The Rotter Internal-External Scale, despite some criticisms related to forced-choice format and psychometric structure, remains a broadly recognized index of generalized control expectancies. Both measures showed acceptable reliability in the present sample, which supports the internal validity of the correlational findings. That said, future work could benefit from multi-method assessments, including domain-specific control measures, behavioral indices of agency, and implicit self-esteem measures to triangulate the constructs.

Several limitations of the present research should be acknowledged. Most importantly, the cross-sectional correlational design precludes causal inference. The association could reflect self-esteem influencing control beliefs, control beliefs shaping self-esteem, or reciprocal influences. Longitudinal designs are needed to unpack temporal ordering and dynamic interplay. Another limitation concerns the sample. Although focusing on Russell Group undergraduates provides a valuable test of generalizability to selective university contexts, it also constrains external validity. The sample may not represent students in other types of institutions or non-student populations. Demographic and cultural diversity within the sample was not the focus of the present report, and future studies should examine whether the observed association generalizes across socioeconomic, ethnic, and cultural groups.

Measurement limitations also merit attention. Self-report instruments are subject to response biases, including social desirability and common-method variance. The Rotter scale’s forced-choice items can reduce some forms of response bias but may also limit variance relative to Likert-type measures. Additionally, the Rosenberg scale assesses global self-worth and does not capture domain-specific self-concepts that might have differential associations with domain-specific control beliefs. Future research would benefit from including domain-specific measures (e.g., academic self-efficacy, health locus of control) to delineate more nuanced relations.

There are also intriguing avenues for future research. Longitudinal studies that track self-esteem and locus of control across critical academic transitions could illuminate developmental dynamics and causal pathways. Experimental studies that manipulate perceived control or self-esteem, using interventions or priming techniques, could test causal effects on the other construct and on downstream outcomes such as academic persistence and stress reactivity. Cross-cultural research could test whether the strength and direction of the relationship vary in cultural contexts that differ in emphasis on individual agency versus external determinants.

Another fruitful line of inquiry is to examine mediating mechanisms. For example, attributional style, self-efficacy, and mastery experiences may mediate the association between global self-esteem and locus of control. Structural equation modeling in larger samples could test such mediation models and quantify indirect effects. Additionally, moderators such as stress, social support, and perfectionism could alter the strength of the self-esteem–locus of control association. Investigating these moderators would clarify the conditions under which the two constructs are most tightly linked.

Applied research could also explore intervention studies that seek to influence both constructs simultaneously. For instance, cognitive-behavioral interventions that target maladaptive attributions and promote adaptive coping could be evaluated for their effects on both locus of control and self-esteem. Educational programs that emphasize skill mastery and incremental theories of ability may foster internal control beliefs and raise self-appraisals. Randomized controlled trials in student populations could provide rigorous tests of such interventions.

Finally, theoretical elaboration is warranted. The present findings are compatible with models that conceive of self-evaluation and perceived control as components of a broader self-system. Integrative frameworks that specify reciprocal relations, feedback loops, and contextual moderators would help organize empirical findings and guide hypothesis testing. Computational or dynamical models that formalize how mastery experiences, attributions, and social feedback coalesce to shape self-esteem and control beliefs over time would be particularly valuable for advancing theory.

In conclusion, the present study found that among Russell Group undergraduates, higher global self-esteem was associated with a more internal locus of control. The magnitude of the association was moderate and consistent with prior research linking evaluative self-beliefs and control expectancies. While causality cannot be inferred, the results bolster theoretical perspectives that view self-esteem and perceived control as related facets of self-regulation and agency. The findings have implications for theory, measurement, and intervention, and they point to several productive directions for future research aimed at elucidating the dynamics of self-evaluation and perceived control in young adults.
