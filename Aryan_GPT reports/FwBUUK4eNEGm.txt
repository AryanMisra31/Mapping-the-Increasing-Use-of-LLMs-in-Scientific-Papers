Introduction

Petitions are a long-standing method for capturing public sentiment and channeling that sentiment into formal political or legal action. Petitions have been used historically to aggregate individual preferences into a collective voice that can exert pressure on decision makers, prompt official review, or initiate legal processes. In contemporary civic life, petitions have moved online and become a ubiquitous feature of social and political campaigning. The ease of sharing and endorsing petitions online has greatly expanded their reach and the speed with which signatures can accumulate. This shift to digital petition platforms has drawn new attention to the psychological mechanisms that determine whether an individual will add their name to a cause. One set of mechanisms that is particularly important for understanding petition signing behavior is social influence. Social influence broadly refers to the ways in which the presence, behavior, or expressed opinions of others shape an individual’s choices. Social influence is multifaceted and includes both informational influence, in which individuals use others as a source of information about correct behavior, and normative influence, in which individuals conform to the expectations of others to gain social approval or avoid disapproval. Both informational and normative influence have clear implications for petition signing: when individuals see that many others have signed a petition, they may infer that signing is the correct or socially approved response. Empirical work outside the specific context of petitions has shown repeatedly that social cues indicating that many others have taken an action typically increase the probability that an individual will do the same. For example, research on online behavior has shown bandwagon effects where popularity cues amplify adoption of content, products, and political positions. Similar dynamics are at work in offline settings where visible public support or endorsements can increase compliance, support, or participation. These general findings provide reason to expect that social influence cues embedded in petition displays — counts, endorsements, and visible testimonials of support — will increase the likelihood that observers add their signatures. Despite the well-established role of social influence in a range of behaviors, the specific question of how social influence affects signing behavior in petitions that relate directly to criminal legal outcomes has received little empirical examination. Petitions that call for prosecuting or defending a criminal suspect invoke legal and moral considerations that might interact with social influence in complex ways. A petition that urges prosecution might be framed as holding a person accountable and seeking punitive action, whereas a petition that urges defense could be framed as protecting an individual from unjust treatment or advocating for due process. These frames have different valence and tie into moral and political identities. It is therefore plausible that the content of a petition — whether it calls for prosecuting or defending a suspect — could moderate the impact of social influence cues. If a petition’s subject aligns with an individual’s moral stance or partisan identity, social influence may be amplified through social identity processes, making the person more likely to sign. If, alternatively, the petition concerns a topic that is morally fraught and where people rely more on their private beliefs than on perceived majority opinion, social influence may be attenuated. The literature on framing and moral decision-making suggests that people vary in their sensitivity to framing depending on the moral dimensions involved and the extent to which decisions are perceived as normative or personal. In addition, previous research on petitions and online mobilization has produced mixed evidence about how content and issue framing interact with social cues. Some studies suggest that popularity indicators have a broad, content-general effect, producing a bandwagon that increases support across different types of issues. Other studies point to boundary conditions, showing stronger social influence when the issue is ambiguous or when individuals lack strong prior attitudes. Petitions about criminal suspects may be less ambiguous for some people, particularly if the underlying facts are perceived as clear, which could reduce the influence of popularity cues. Another relevant set of findings concerns attribution and responsibility. When a petition seeks prosecution, signers might feel that their signature contributes to punishing wrongdoing, which connects to punitiveness and retributive motives. When a petition seeks defense, signers might feel that their signature protects civil liberties and due process, connecting to concerns about fairness and rights. These differing underlying motives could influence the willingness to sign independent of social cues. For instance, individuals who are dispositionally punitive may be relatively unmoved by popularity cues when a petition aligns with their punitive inclinations; similarly, civil libertarians may sign defense petitions irrespective of the apparent popularity of the petition. Social identity theory suggests that perceived similarity with those who have signed a petition could magnify social influence. If the displayed signers are depicted as members of an in-group, the observer may be more likely to conform. Conversely, when the display suggests that signers are from an out-group, the presence of an existing signature count might have reduced or even negative influence. Most online petition platforms, however, provide generic counts without detailed demographic signals, creating a general popularity cue that is likely to function as a bandwagon signal rather than an explicit in-group endorsement. The role of social influence in legal petitions thus plausibly arises through a general heuristic: if many others have signed, then signing is acceptable, safe, or correct. Another theoretical perspective comes from dual-process models of judgment and decision making. According to such models, when people have the cognitive resources and motivation to engage in deliberative processing, they rely on substantive arguments and their pre-existing beliefs to make a decision. When they lack resources or motivation, they are more likely to rely on heuristics and social cues. Thus, social influence in the form of signature counts may have its strongest effects when the petition content is novel, complex, or when individuals are not highly motivated to deliberate. Criminal justice petitions may vary along these dimensions; some cases are high-salience and familiar, others are ambiguous and unfamiliar. Empirical exploration of the boundary conditions for social influence effects in this domain is therefore warranted. The methodological literature on measuring social influence in online contexts provides a number of established manipulations that are both ethically permissible and conceptually clear. Typical manipulations include varying the number of purported prior signers, presenting endorsements from authorities, or showing social normative statements. These manipulations have been shown to alter behavior in field and laboratory studies of digital petitioning and other forms of online engagement. Using a controlled experimental design to manipulate a popularity cue and the content frame of the petition allows for clean causal inference about the main and interactive effects of these factors. A second methodological consideration is the nature of the dependent measure. Many studies use self-reported intentions as an outcome, but behaviorally relevant measures such as actual signing behavior yield stronger inferences. Even in an experimental context where the petition may be hypothetical, a behavioral decision to sign versus not sign provides a close approximation to real-world action. The binary nature of signing behavior — sign or do not sign — maps naturally onto analytic techniques such as logistic regression and chi-square tests of association. The present study was designed to address two primary research questions. The first question is whether a social influence cue, operationalized as a displayed high number of prior signers and an explicit normative statement that many others support the petition, increases the likelihood that participants sign a petition relating to a criminal suspect. The second question is whether the petition frame — a call to prosecute a suspect versus a call to defend a suspect — affects the likelihood of signing and interacts with the social influence cue. Given prior evidence that social influence generally increases conformity and action adoption in online contexts, we predicted a main effect of social influence such that participants exposed to a popularity cue would be more likely to sign than those in a control condition without the cue. We also considered competing predictions regarding petition frame. One possibility, consistent with bandwagon universality, is that petition frame will not meaningfully alter the effect of social influence: the popularity cue increases signing across both prosecute and defend petitions. A competing possibility is that petition frame will moderate the effect of social influence, with greater influence in the domain where individuals have weaker priors or where the frame aligns with social identities. Because empirical evidence on frame interactions in criminal legal petitions is sparse, we took an exploratory stance on the interaction hypothesis, preregistering the primary comparison as the main effect of social influence and treating the petition frame and interaction as secondary tests. In addition to examining main and interaction effects, we planned a manipulation check to verify that participants perceived the social influence manipulation as intended. We also planned to examine potential moderating demographic variables, including age, gender, and political ideology, to test whether the effects were robust across subgroups. The study was designed to provide sufficient power to detect a medium-sized main effect, and analytic choices prioritized tests that are standard for binary outcome data. The findings from this study have implications for how petitions are understood as instruments of social and legal influence. If social influence cues robustly increase signatory rates in legal contexts, petition platforms and advocates should be aware of the persuasive power embedded in simple popularity displays. Conversely, if petition content substantially alters responsiveness to social cues, then the design of petitions and the ethical considerations around popularity cues may need to be more sensitive to content-specific dynamics. The goal of the current work was therefore to establish the basic causal impact of a social influence cue on petition signing in a controlled experiment and to determine whether a prosecutorial versus defensive frame moderates that impact. We expected to find evidence that social influence increases signing, and we remained open to either the presence or absence of a frame effect. By focusing on petitions tied to criminal suspects, the study also extends the literature on social influence into a legally consequential and morally charged domain. The remainder of the article describes the methods used to address these questions, reports the analytic results, and discusses the theoretical and practical implications of the findings.

Methods

Participants were recruited from an online labor market that routinely supplies diverse adult samples for behavioral research. A total of 400 participants were recruited to participate in a brief online study about civic opinions and decision-making. The target sample size was selected to provide sufficient power to detect medium-sized effects in a 2 × 2 experimental design. Eligibility criteria included being at least 18 years of age, resident in the country where the study was conducted, and able to provide informed consent in English. Prior to data collection, the study protocol received institutional review board approval, and participants provided electronic informed consent. Recruitment advertisements described the study as taking approximately ten minutes and offered monetary compensation commensurate with local norms for online studies. Participants who completed the study were compensated automatically through the recruitment platform. The experiment used a between-subjects 2 × 2 factorial design with social influence (social influence vs control) and petition type (prosecute vs defend) as the independent factors. Participants were randomly assigned in equal numbers to one of the four experimental cells, resulting in 100 participants per cell. The randomization algorithm embedded in the survey platform ensured that assignment was approximately balanced across the sample. The social influence manipulation was implemented by presenting a brief petition webpage that either included a strong popularity cue or did not. In the social influence condition, participants saw a petition headline and short description that were equivalent in wording to the control condition but additionally saw two social influence cues: a large numeric counter indicating that "Over 12,000 people have already signed this petition," and a normative endorsement line stating "Join thousands of others who support this action." These cues were presented in a non-deceptive manner relative to the study cover story; participants were told that some illustrative numbers would be shown to simulate petition displays, and a debriefing at the end explained the simulated nature of the counts. In the control condition, the petition webpage displayed only the headline and short description without any numeric count or normative endorsement. The petition content manipulation varied the petition's call to action. In the prosecute condition, the petition headline and description urged readers to sign in order to push prosecutors to file charges against a named suspect for alleged wrongdoing, with language focused on accountability and public safety. The text was carefully worded to avoid graphic or emotional language and to preserve comparability across frames. In the defend condition, the petition headline and description urged readers to sign in order to urge prosecutors and court officials to protect a named suspect's right to a fair trial and ensure that charges are not pursued without clear evidence, with language focused on civil liberties and procedural fairness. The two petition texts were matched on length, reading level, and neutral tone, differing only in the call to action (prosecute vs defend). The petition referred to a hypothetical suspect in a hypothetical case to avoid concerns about influencing real legal outcomes. After viewing the petition webpage, participants were asked whether they would sign the petition. The dependent variable was a binary response: participants clicked either "I will sign" or "I will not sign." This behavioral choice was recorded as the primary outcome. To increase realism, clicking "I will sign" produced a brief confirmation screen that thanked the participant; however, no actual external petition was connected to the action. The survey included a manipulation check to assess participants' perceptions of social influence. Participants rated their agreement with the statement "It seemed to me that many other people had already signed this petition" on a seven-point Likert scale from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7). This item served to verify that the social influence display produced the intended subjective impression. Additional questions collected demographic information including age, gender, level of education, and political ideology on a seven-point scale from very liberal to very conservative. The survey also included a brief attention check item and a single-item measure of prior familiarity with the underlying hypothetical case to control for any pre-existing knowledge effects. Participants who failed the attention check or who reported high familiarity with the hypothetical case were flagged and excluded from primary analyses; these exclusions were pre-specified and applied consistently. Data cleaning and exclusion criteria were transparently reported to participants during debriefing. The analytic plan was pre-registered before the data became accessible. Primary analyses consisted of chi-square tests of association between the binary signing outcome and each of the two experimental manipulations, followed by a logistic regression model predicting signing status from the social influence condition, the petition frame condition, and their interaction. The logistic regression allowed estimation of odds ratios and confidence intervals for the main effects and interaction. Effect size measures such as Cramer's V for chi-square tests and odds ratios for logistic regression were reported. Secondary analyses examined whether demographic factors moderated the observed effects by adding interaction terms between each demographic variable and the social influence manipulation in the logistic regression model, and by testing whether the manipulation check predicted signing behavior above and beyond experimental assignment. Robustness checks included excluding participants who failed attention checks, conducting sensitivity analyses with and without participants who reported prior familiarity, and computing post hoc power estimates to confirm that the sample size provided adequate sensitivity to detect medium-sized effects. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Significance thresholds were set at alpha = .05 for primary tests, and two-tailed tests were employed throughout. Descriptive statistics, contingency tables, chi-square statistics, logistic regression coefficients, odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals, and effect size metrics were calculated and presented. The debriefing provided participants with an explanation of the simulated petition numbers and the study’s aims. Participants were offered contact information for the research team if they had further questions or concerns about the study materials. No personally identifying information beyond broad demographics was retained, and data were stored securely in accordance with institutional data protection procedures.

Results

The sample consisted of 400 participants who completed the study and passed attention checks. Participant ages ranged from 18 to 72 years with a mean age of 34.7 years (SD = 10.9). The sample was 53% female and 47% male, with a range of educational backgrounds reported. Political ideology was distributed across the scale with a mean near the center of the seven-point scale (M = 3.9, SD = 1.6). Exclusions were minimal: seven participants failed the attention check and were excluded from the primary analyses in accordance with the pre-registered plan, leaving 393 participants in the analytic sample. The random assignment resulted in approximately equal numbers across the four experimental cells after exclusions, with final cell sizes varying by at most two participants. The primary dependent variable was whether participants chose to sign the petition (yes/no). Table 1 in the supplementary materials provides the raw cell counts; the key pattern is summarized here. In the social influence condition (n = 197), 130 participants chose to sign the petition and 67 chose not to sign, yielding a signing rate of 65.9% in that condition. In the control condition (n = 196), 80 participants chose to sign and 116 chose not to sign, yielding a signing rate of 40.8% in the control condition. A chi-square test of association between social influence condition (social influence vs control) and signing behavior produced a highly significant effect, χ2(1, N = 393) = 28.83, p < .001. The magnitude of association was moderate, Cramer's V = .27, indicating a meaningful relationship between exposure to the social influence cue and the likelihood of signing. These results support the primary hypothesis that social influence increases petition signing. The petition frame factor (prosecute vs defend) did not show a significant main effect on signing behavior. Across the two petition frames, the signing rates were similar: 106 out of 197 participants signed in the prosecute frame (53.8%), and 104 out of 196 participants signed in the defend frame (53.1%). A chi-square test of association between petition frame and signing was non-significant, χ2(1, N = 393) = 0.02, p = .88, suggesting that the simple prosecution versus defense frame did not systematically alter the overall probability of signing in this sample. To test for an interaction between social influence and petition frame, we estimated a logistic regression predicting the binary signing outcome from social influence (coded 1 = social influence, 0 = control), petition frame (coded 1 = prosecute, 0 = defend), and their interaction term. The logistic regression model indicated a strong, significant effect of social influence on the log odds of signing. The coefficient for social influence was B = 1.03 (SE = 0.20), which corresponds to an odds ratio (OR) of 2.80 with a 95% confidence interval approximately from 2.04 to 3.85. This odds ratio indicates that participants in the social influence condition were about 2.8 times as likely to sign the petition as participants in the control condition, controlling for petition frame. The coefficient for petition frame was small and non-significant, B = 0.02 (SE = 0.20), OR = 1.02, 95% CI [0.67, 1.56], p = .92. The interaction term between social influence and petition frame was also non-significant, B = -0.08 (SE = 0.28), OR = 0.92, 95% CI [0.53, 1.60], p = .77. The absence of a significant interaction indicates that the effect of social influence on signing did not differ across the prosecute and defend frames in this experiment. We also examined the contingency tables for each of the four cells to ensure that the pattern of results was not driven by an anomalous cell. Cell-level signing rates were as follows: social/prosecute = 67 signed out of 99 (67.7%); social/defend = 63 signed out of 98 (64.3%); control/prosecute = 39 signed out of 98 (39.8%); control/defend = 41 signed out of 98 (41.8%). These values indicate that the social influence effect was present within both frames, producing higher signing rates in the social condition compared to control for both prosecute and defend petitions. To provide additional evidence regarding variability of responses across groups, we computed tests of homogeneity of proportions to assess whether the variance in signing rates across the four cells was greater than expected by chance. The homogeneity test did not indicate excess heterogeneity beyond that expected under the model, χ2(3, N = 393) = 4.12, p = .25, which is consistent with the finding of no substantive interaction and no unusual deviation in a specific cell. The manipulation check confirmed that participants in the social influence condition perceived the petition as having been signed by many others. The mean score on the perceived prior signers item was M = 5.24 (SD = 1.34) in the social influence condition and M = 2.09 (SD = 1.12) in the control condition. An independent-samples t-test showed a large and highly significant difference, t(391) = 25.9, p < .001, Cohen’s d = 2.62. Moreover, perceived prior signers predicted signing behavior above and beyond experimental assignment; in a logistic regression that included both the experimental social influence dummy and the manipulation-check rating, both predictors were significant but the manipulation-check rating accounted for additional variance (p < .01), suggesting that subjective perception of social endorsement mediated some portion of the manipulation’s effect. Exploratory analyses examined whether demographic variables moderated the social influence effect. Interaction terms between social influence and gender, age (treated continuously), education level, and political ideology were added to separate logistic regression models. None of these moderation tests produced significant interaction terms. For example, the interaction between social influence and political ideology had B = 0.05 (SE = 0.06), p = .40, indicating no evidence that the social influence effect varied by ideology in this sample. Similarly, the social influence by gender interaction had B = -0.12 (SE = 0.24), p = .62. These null moderation findings suggest that the social influence effect was broadly consistent across demographic subgroups. Sensitivity analyses excluding participants who reported any familiarity with the hypothetical case (n = 18) produced results that were virtually identical to the primary analyses: social influence remained a significant predictor of signing (OR = 2.72, p < .001), petition frame remained non-significant (p = .85), and the interaction remained non-significant (p = .74). Post hoc power calculations indicated that with the final analytic sample of 393 participants, the study had greater than 0.95 power to detect a medium-sized effect (OR ≈ 1.7) for the main effect of social influence at alpha = .05. The observed effect of social influence exceeded this threshold and was estimated with reasonable precision as indicated by the reported confidence intervals. Finally, we reported descriptive findings about non-signing reasons offered by a subset of participants who selected "I will not sign." These participants were provided an open-ended prompt to briefly explain their reason. Content coding of a random sample of these responses showed a mix of reasons including lack of sufficient information (about 34%), disagreement with the petition’s call (about 28%), general apathy or lack of interest (about 22%), and privacy or unwillingness to take public action (about 16%). The distribution of these reasons did not differ meaningfully by social influence or petition frame condition based on chi-square comparisons of coded categories. Overall, the results provide clear evidence that the social influence cue increased the likelihood of signing a petition related to a criminal suspect, whereas petition frame (prosecute vs defend) did not influence signing behavior in a statistically detectable manner in this experiment.

Discussion

This experiment investigated whether a social influence cue embedded in a petition display affects the likelihood that individuals will sign the petition, and whether the effect of that cue varies depending on whether the petition calls for prosecuting or defending a criminal suspect. The primary finding is that the presence of a social influence cue — a large count of prior signers and an endorsement-style normative statement — substantially increased the probability that participants chose to sign the petition. Participants exposed to the social influence cue were nearly three times as likely to sign as participants who saw the petition without such a cue. This robust effect was observed across both petition frames, indicating a general bandwagon-like impact of popularity cues on signing decisions. The finding is consistent with a broad literature showing that social proof and popularity indicators influence online behavior by signaling that an action is acceptable, normative, or likely to be effective. In the context of petitions pertaining to criminal suspects, the social influence cue appears to exert a powerful persuasive effect, increasing behavioral endorsement regardless of whether the petition advocated for prosecution or defense. The manipulation check results strengthen the interpretation that the numerical and normative cues were effective: participants in the social influence condition reported substantially higher perceptions that many others had signed. Moreover, subjective perceptions of prior signers predicted signing behavior above and beyond experimental condition, suggesting that participants’ internalized sense of the petition’s popularity mediated their behavior. The absence of a main effect of petition frame — no meaningful difference between prosecute and defend petitions in overall signing rates — is noteworthy. This null effect suggests that, at least in the present sample and with the particular wording used, the direction of the petition’s legal recommendation did not systematically influence willingness to sign. One interpretation is that participants’ initial priors about the hypothetical case were relatively weak or balanced, such that the specific prosecutorial versus defensive framing did not activate strong differential motives. Another possibility is that the framing manipulation, while conceptually clear, did not sufficiently tap into individual differences in punitiveness or civil-liberties orientations to generate a main effect. The null frame effect also indicates that the social influence cue exerted its impact broadly, rather than selectively enhancing support for one type of legal action. The interaction analysis provides further clarity: the effect of social influence did not differ across petition frame conditions. In other words, popularity cues increased signing both for petitions urging prosecution and for petitions urging defense. This pattern is consistent with a general heuristic model in which people rely on popularity signals when deciding whether to participate in an online political act, independent of the specific content of the appeal. Practically, this suggests that petition platforms that display large signature counts or other popularity markers may boost engagement across a wide range of petition types, whether the petitions call for punitive or protective legal actions. The lack of moderation by demographic factors in exploratory analyses indicates that the social influence effect was broadly generalizable across age, gender, education level, and political ideology in this sample. While the sample was not a probability sample of the full population, the absence of detectable moderators suggests that simple popularity cues may have wide-ranging persuasive effects across diverse subgroups. This finding aligns with the idea that heuristics and social proof operate similarly across many individuals when the cue is salient and no strong countervailing prior is present. Several theoretical implications arise from the results. First, the findings align with dual-process accounts of decision-making that emphasize the role of heuristics when deliberation is limited or costly. In the experiment, participants were asked to make a relatively low-stakes, brief decision about signing a petition; under these conditions, reliance on a quick heuristic such as "others have signed so it must be okay" is plausible and apparently effective. Second, the results demonstrate that social influence cues can shape collective expressions in legally relevant domains; this has implications for theories of public opinion formation and the social construction of legal priorities. When many people see that a petition has amassed a high number of signatures, that perception may catalyze more support, possibly creating a feedback loop that can influence institutional responsiveness. Third, the lack of a frame effect in this setting suggests that heuristics based on popularity may sometimes overshadow content differences, at least for the kinds of frames and the low-information context employed here. This underscores the need to study when substantive message content overrides social cues and when it does not. There are several methodological and interpretive limitations to consider. The petitions in this experiment were hypothetical and referred to a fictional case. Although the behavioral measure was a concrete action within the experimental setting (clicking to sign), it is possible that real-world decisions to sign a live petition could be influenced by additional factors such as perceived consequences, reputational concerns, or more extensive information about the case. Field experiments with actual petitions would be valuable to test the ecological generalizability of the present findings. A related limitation is that the social influence manipulation used a strong, high-number cue ("Over 12,000 people") paired with a normative line. Different magnitudes of social proof or different types of cues (e.g., endorsements from specific groups, visible comments from in-group members) might produce qualitatively different effects or interact with petition frame. Future work should systematically vary the strength and source of social cues to map out boundary conditions. Another limitation concerns potential ceiling or floor effects in signing behavior under certain frames. In contexts where people have very strong pre-existing attitudes — for example, if petition content concerns a highly salient political or moral issue linked to identity — message frame might exert a larger effect, and social influence might be attenuated or amplified depending on the extent to which the popularity signal is perceived as coming from a like-minded or opposing group. The current study did not test identity-specific endorsements, so it remains an open question whether social influence operates differently when the perceived signers are identified as in-group versus out-group members. The exploratory analyses found no evidence that political ideology moderated the social influence effect; however, the sample’s distribution and the hypothetical nature of the case may have limited sensitivity to such interactions. Future research could present more finely grained cues indicating the political or demographic composition of prior signers to test hypotheses derived from social identity theory. The open-ended responses from participants who declined to sign provide insight into the diversity of non-signing motivations, with many participants citing lack of information or disagreement with the petition’s call. This suggests that social influence is only one of multiple determinants of petition behavior, and that increasing informational content or addressing specific concerns might influence signatures alongside popularity cues. The finding that perceived prior signers mediated some of the social influence effect underscores the importance of subjective perception in driving behavior: even if the numeric cue is present, individual differences in the degree to which people attend to and internalize that cue will shape its behavioral consequences. The ethical implications of these findings merit careful consideration. Because simple popularity displays can substantially increase rates of signing, there are normative questions about whether and how petition platforms should present such cues, especially in legally consequential contexts. On one hand, popularity cues can help mobilize support for underrepresented causes; on the other hand, they can create bandwagon effects that amplify majority preferences and potentially drown out minority or dissenting views. Platform designers, advocates, and policymakers should weigh these trade-offs and consider mechanisms to ensure that social influence cues are transparent and not unduly manipulative. Looking forward, several avenues for future research are compelling. First, conducting field experiments that vary the visibility and source of social cues on live petition platforms would provide real-world validation of the laboratory findings. Second, examining a wider array of petition content types — including cases with strong moral valence, high public salience, or clear partisan divisions — would help delineate when petition frame matters and when social cues dominate. Third, investigating the temporal dynamics of signature accumulation and social influence, for example by manipulating the rate at which signature counts change or by testing the effect of early signatures versus later signatures, could shed light on bandwagon processes over time. Fourth, research could explore the interaction between deliberative information provision and popularity cues, testing whether adding substantive facts or balanced arguments reduces heuristic reliance on social signals. Finally, longitudinal work might test whether exposure to popularity cues affects downstream behaviors beyond initial signing, such as sharing the petition, donating, or sustaining engagement with a cause. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that social influence cues embedded in petition displays have a strong causal impact on individuals’ choices to sign petitions related to legal actions concerning a criminal suspect. The effect is robust across petitions that call for prosecution and petitions that call for defense, suggesting a content-general bandwagon effect in the present context. While the petition frame did not alter signing behavior in this experiment, future work should investigate boundary conditions and real-world generalizability. The results contribute to our understanding of how simple social cues shape civic behavior and highlight the importance of considering the psychological power of popularity signals in both research and practice.
