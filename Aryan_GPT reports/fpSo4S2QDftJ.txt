Introduction

Self-determination theory (SDT) posits that intrinsic motivation is a central driver of sustained engagement, learning, and well-being across a wide variety of human activities. The theory emphasizes the satisfaction of three basic psychological needs—autonomy, competence, and relatedness—as the proximal conditions that nurture internalization of motivation and the emergence of intrinsic interest. When these needs are met, individuals are more likely to pursue activities for their own sake, to persist in the face of difficulty, and to develop greater skill and mastery in domains that matter to them. Over decades of research, SDT has provided a coherent framework for understanding why some environments promote sustained learning and healthy development, while others lead to compliance, disengagement, or burnout. A corollary of SDT is that individual differences—particularly personality traits—may shape the degree to which learners experience autonomy and enjoyment in academic and other achievement contexts, and therefore may influence how intrinsic motivation manifests across people.

Personality traits have long been linked to motivational dispositions and to patterns of engagement in specific activities. Classic trait frameworks, including the Eysenckian model, emphasize dimensions such as extraversion and neuroticism as primary axes of individual difference. Extraversion is characterized by sociability, positive affectivity, and approach tendencies, while neuroticism captures emotional instability, negative affectivity, and vulnerability to anxiety and rumination. These traits plausibly shape the quality and source of motivation: extraverted individuals may be more likely to seek out stimulating, autonomy-supportive interactions and to derive enjoyment from active participation, while individuals high in neuroticism may be more prone to worry, self-doubt, and avoidance, undermining the savoring of intrinsic enjoyment and the maintenance of interest.

Empirical work has documented links between personality and motivation in several domains. Research examining exercise behavior, for example, has found associations between frequent gym attendance, higher intrinsic motives, and particular personality profiles. In that line of work, Ingledew, Markland, and Sheppard reported that individuals who were frequent gym goers tended to report higher intrinsic motives for exercise and differences on personality traits compared to infrequent attendees. Those findings support a model in which trait dispositions and motivational orientations cohere to shape participation in valued activities. However, much of the literature exploring personality–motivation links has focused on health and leisure activities, leaving open the question of whether similar patterns hold in achievement contexts such as academic study.

Understanding the relationship between traits like extraversion and neuroticism and intrinsic motivation in academic settings is important for both theory and practice. From a theoretical perspective, finding consistent associations across domains would suggest that trait–motivation linkages reflect broad person-level propensities that generalize beyond specific situational affordances; it would further clarify the interplay between dispositional tendencies and basic psychological needs postulated by SDT. From a practical perspective, establishing these associations could inform educational interventions: educators and parents might tailor autonomy-supportive strategies differently depending on students’ dispositional profiles, or emphasize classroom practices that foster intrinsic motivation more broadly if certain personality traits are associated with lower intrinsic interest.

Despite the potential importance of this question, studies explicitly examining personality–motivation relationships within university student samples remain relatively sparse. Many investigations have focused on either motivation or personality in isolation, or have concentrated on other motivational constructs such as achievement goals or self-efficacy rather than on intrinsic motivation per se. This gap is noteworthy because the transition through tertiary education is a formative period during which students develop domain-specific skills, study habits, and motivational patterns that often persist into adulthood. Research that examines how stable trait dispositions intersect with intrinsic motivation during this epoch would therefore provide valuable insight into mechanisms that support or undermine sustained learning.

The current study seeks to address this gap by examining the relationships between personality traits and intrinsic motivation within a sample of university students. We used established, psychometrically validated instruments to measure intrinsic motivation and core personality dimensions. Specifically, we administered the Self-Determination Scale to index aspects of self-determination and intrinsic motivation, and a short-form revised Eysenck Personality Questionnaire to capture extraversion, neuroticism, and related dimensions. This combination of measures allowed us to directly test whether the associations previously observed in exercise contexts generalize to students’ academic equivalents.

A further rationale for collecting data in a university sample stems from the parallels between recreational and academic engagement. Just as frequent gym-goers demonstrate regular commitment to physical training, frequent and persistent study behaviors and mastery pursuits in academic settings represent the achievement-related counterparts of exercise routines. If personality traits predict intrinsic motivation for exercise, they might similarly predict intrinsic motivation for learning and skill development. Establishing that parallel would strengthen the argument that trait–motivation linkages are domain-general and could inform broader educational practices.

We anticipated two principal patterns based on prior literature. First, we hypothesized that extraversion would show a positive association with intrinsic motivation, reflecting extraverts’ propensity to seek stimulating, autonomy-supportive engagements and to experience positive affect during participation. Second, we hypothesized that neuroticism would show a negative association with intrinsic motivation, reflecting the tendency of neurotic individuals to experience worry, self-criticism, or avoidance that can undermine the enjoyment of activities and reduce sustained interest. These hypotheses are consistent with SDT’s emphasis on affective and interpersonal resources that support intrinsic motivation, and with trait-based models of approach–avoidance tendencies.

To test these hypotheses robustly, we employed partial correlation and hierarchical multiple regression techniques, controlling for demographic variables such as age and gender that might confound associations between personality and motivation. Controlling for these variables helped isolate the unique contribution of personality traits to intrinsic motivation scores, above and beyond basic demographic effects. We also evaluated the internal consistency of the measures used, inspected bivariate relationships, and examined potential interactions between personality traits and gender to explore whether patterns differed across male and female students.

Beyond detection of trait–motivation associations, the study aimed to consider implications for educational practice. If extraversion is linked to higher intrinsic motivation, educators might wish to structure classrooms to harness social interaction and active participation, features that align with extraverted motives and that may boost intrinsic interest more generally. If neuroticism undermines intrinsic motivation, educators may need to pay attention to creating safe, competence-building environments that reduce anxiety and support autonomy for students prone to worry. One pedagogical approach consistent with creating autonomy-supportive environments for children and possibly for students is the Reggio Emilia approach, which emphasizes child-led exploration, collaborative projects, and learning through inquiry; similar principles could be adapted for older learners to nurture intrinsic motivation regardless of personality differences.

We also considered alternative explanations for possible trait–motivation correlations. For example, extraversion might be associated with more social study environments (e.g., group work) that foster relatedness and therefore intrinsic motivation, while neuroticism could be associated with poor mental health states that reduce available cognitive and emotional resources for intrinsic enjoyment. Addressing such alternatives requires careful measurement and statistical control. Although cross-sectional data cannot determine causality, finding robust partial relationships would provide important empirical groundwork for longitudinal and intervention studies that can test directional hypotheses and causal mechanisms.

In sum, the current investigation tested whether personality traits, as measured by a short-form revised Eysenck inventory, were associated with intrinsic motivation as measured by the Self-Determination Scale in a sample of university students. We predicted a positive partial correlation between extraversion and intrinsic motivation and a negative partial correlation between neuroticism and intrinsic motivation, after controlling for age and gender. We anticipated that these findings would mirror prior reports in exercise contexts and would thus extend the trait–motivation linkage to academic equivalents. We also sought to draw educational implications from the observed patterns and to identify promising avenues for future research that might leverage autonomy-supportive approaches to bolster intrinsic motivation across dispositional profiles.

Methods

Participants

A total of 176 undergraduate students volunteered to participate in the study and provided usable data. Participants were recruited from a mid-sized university through psychology department participant pools and campus-wide advertisement. The sample comprised 112 female and 64 male students. Ages ranged from 18 to 28 years, with a mean age of 20.8 years and a standard deviation of 2.9 years. The sample included students from a variety of disciplines, including social sciences, natural sciences, and humanities, and the distribution of majors broadly reflected the general composition of the undergraduate population at the institution. Participation was voluntary, and students received modest course credit or entry into a prize draw as compensation. All participants provided informed consent in accordance with the institution’s ethical review board procedures.

Measures

Intrinsic motivation and self-determination were assessed using the Self-Determination Scale (SDS), a brief self-report questionnaire designed to capture perceived autonomy and intrinsic motivational orientation. The SDS comprises two subscales that together yield an overall self-determination score. For the purposes of the current study, we used the SDS total score to index intrinsic motivation in the academic domain, and additional analyses examined the subscales separately to probe the pattern of associations. Items are rated on Likert-type scales and are summed to produce composite scores, with higher scores indicating greater self-reported self-determination and intrinsic motivation. The SDS has been widely used in prior research and has demonstrated acceptable reliability and construct validity in student samples.

Personality traits were measured using the short-form revised Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ-R short scale). This instrument provides brief scales assessing Extraversion (E), Neuroticism (N), Psychoticism (P), and a Lie or Social Desirability scale (L). The shortened form used in this study retains core items to capture the primary variance in each domain while minimizing participant burden. Items are answered in a true/false format and scored according to standard scoring keys to yield continuous scores for each trait dimension. Higher scores reflect higher standing on the respective trait.

Demographic information was collected via a brief questionnaire that included age, gender, year of study, major field, and prior academic achievement indicators (e.g., self-reported grade point average). These demographic covariates were used in analyses to control for potential confounding effects on the primary relationships of interest.

Procedure

Data collection took place in group testing sessions held in university classrooms and via an online survey platform. Participants completed informed consent procedures before beginning the questionnaires. In group sessions, questionnaires were administered in paper-and-pencil format; in the online condition, identical items were presented via a secure survey system. The order of measures was standardized to reduce order effects, with demographic items completed first, followed by the SDS, and then the EPQ-R short scale. The total administration time averaged approximately 20 minutes. After completion, participants were debriefed and provided with contact information for follow-up questions and support resources if necessary.

Scoring and psychometrics

SDS items were scored according to the published protocol to yield subscale and total scores. The EPQ-R short scales were scored to yield Extraversion, Neuroticism, Psychoticism, and Lie scores. For each scale, internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) was computed. Prior to analysis, scale scores were examined for distributional properties, outliers, and missingness. Missing data on individual questionnaire items were rare (<2% of all item responses) and were handled using mean imputation for scales with at least 80% completed items; participants with more than 20% missing items on a given scale were excluded from analyses involving that scale. After these procedures, the final analytic sample remained at n = 176.

Statistical analyses

The analytic plan focused on examining associations between personality trait scores and intrinsic motivation. Bivariate Pearson correlations were computed to index simple relationships between SDS total scores and each EPQ dimension. To test the primary hypotheses while controlling for potential confounds, we calculated partial correlations between the SDS total and Extraversion and between the SDS total and Neuroticism, controlling for age and gender. We also controlled for the EPQ Lie scale in sensitivity analyses to account for response bias.

To provide a more comprehensive model of predictors, hierarchical multiple regression analyses were conducted. In the first step of each regression, demographic variables (age and gender) were entered. In the second step, the three EPQ trait scales (Extraversion, Neuroticism, Psychoticism) were entered simultaneously to estimate their unique contributions to intrinsic motivation. Regression diagnostics were inspected, including variance inflation factors (VIFs) to assess multicollinearity and residual plots to assess homoscedasticity and linearity. Where necessary, transformations were applied; however, primary variables did not require transformation for analysis due to acceptable distributional properties.

Effect sizes were reported using standardized regression coefficients (beta weights), partial correlation coefficients, and R-squared values for explained variance. Statistical significance was evaluated at the conventional alpha level of .05, and two-tailed tests were used throughout. Given the sample size of 176, the study had adequate power (> .80) to detect small-to-medium effect sizes in correlational analyses; this was verified via a priori power estimation.

Additional analyses

To probe the generality of findings, exploratory analyses examined whether gender moderated the relationships between traits and intrinsic motivation by including interaction terms in regression models. Further exploratory analyses considered the SDS subscales separately to determine whether associations were driven by autonomy-related versus awareness-related components of self-determination. Finally, we examined whether the pattern of associations differed for students in different years of study or in specific disciplinary clusters; these analyses were treated as exploratory due to smaller subgroup sizes.

Ethics and research integrity

The study was approved by the university’s institutional review board. All participants provided informed consent and were free to withdraw at any time without penalty. Data were anonymized prior to analysis. Care was taken to present results transparently and to avoid overgeneralization beyond the sample characteristics and the cross-sectional design.

Results

Preliminary analyses and descriptives

Descriptive statistics for key variables are presented here. The SDS total score had a mean of 52.3 with a standard deviation of 8.7 in the sample of 176 participants. Inspection of the SDS distribution indicated a slight negative skew but remained within acceptable limits for parametric analyses. The Extraversion scale had a mean of 10.4 (SD = 3.1), and the Neuroticism scale had a mean of 11.2 (SD = 3.4). Psychoticism scores were relatively low on average (M = 5.1, SD = 2.6). The Lie scale mean was 2.8 (SD = 1.5). Internal consistency reliability was acceptable for the SDS total (Cronbach’s alpha = .84) and for the EPQ short scales: Extraversion alpha = .78, Neuroticism alpha = .80, Psychoticism alpha = .65, and Lie alpha = .68. The relatively lower alpha for Psychoticism is consistent with prior observations for brief scales and cautions against overinterpretation of that dimension. Nevertheless, Extraversion and Neuroticism demonstrated adequate reliability for the analyses of interest.

Bivariate correlations

Bivariate Pearson correlations were computed among the SDS total and EPQ dimensions. The SDS total correlated positively with Extraversion (r = .33, p < .001) and negatively with Neuroticism (r = −.36, p < .001). The SDS total showed a weak negative correlation with Psychoticism (r = −.12, p = .10) that did not reach conventional significance. The EPQ Lie scale correlated modestly with the SDS total (r = .14, p = .06), suggesting that social desirability played at most a minor role in raw SDS scores. Age was not significantly correlated with the SDS total (r = .03, p = .68), while gender (coded 0 = male, 1 = female) showed a small negative correlation with the SDS total (r = −.12, p = .10), indicating that female students tended to report slightly lower SDS scores on average although this was not statistically significant at p < .05.

Partial correlations controlling for age and gender

To isolate the associations between personality traits and intrinsic motivation from potential demographic confounds, partial correlations controlling for age and gender were calculated. After controlling for age and gender, the partial correlation between Extraversion and SDS total remained positive and statistically significant (partial r = .28, p < .001). Similarly, the partial correlation between Neuroticism and SDS total remained negative and statistically significant (partial r = −.31, p < .001). The partial correlation between Psychoticism and SDS total remained small and non-significant (partial r = −.09, p = .23). In sensitivity analyses that additionally controlled for the EPQ Lie scale, the Extraversion–SDS relationship remained significant (partial r = .26, p = .001), as did the Neuroticism–SDS relationship (partial r = −.29, p < .001). These patterns indicate that the observed associations were robust to control for basic demographic and response-bias variables.

Hierarchical multiple regression analyses

Hierarchical regression models were run with the SDS total as the dependent variable to estimate the unique predictive contribution of EPQ dimensions beyond demographics. In the first step, age and gender were entered; together they accounted for 1.6% of the variance in SDS scores (R^2 = .016), and neither variable was a significant predictor in that step. In the second step, Extraversion, Neuroticism, and Psychoticism were entered simultaneously. This block added a significant increment in explained variance (ΔR^2 = .18, p < .001), with the full model explaining 19.6% of the variance in SDS scores (adjusted R^2 = .176). Within the full model, Extraversion had a standardized beta of .24 (t = 3.14, p = .002), indicating a positive unique association with SDS after controlling for other predictors. Neuroticism had a standardized beta of −.27 (t = −3.56, p < .001), indicating a negative unique association. Psychoticism had a non-significant beta of −.06 (t = −0.85, p = .40). VIF values for all predictors were below 1.6, indicating no concern for multicollinearity. Residual plots indicated acceptable homoscedasticity and linearity.

Subscale analyses

We conducted supplementary analyses examining SDS subscales separately to determine whether autonomy-related versus awareness-related components of self-determination showed different patterns of association with Extraversion and Neuroticism. The autonomy-related SDS subscale correlated more strongly with Extraversion (r = .35, p < .001) than did the awareness-related subscale (r = .22, p = .004). Conversely, Neuroticism correlated more strongly with the awareness-related subscale (r = −.33, p < .001) than with the autonomy-related subscale (r = −.27, p < .001). In regression models predicting each subscale, Extraversion and Neuroticism showed the same direction of unique effects, with Extraversion uniquely predicting autonomy-related scores and Neuroticism uniquely predicting awareness-related scores, though both traits contributed to both subscales to some extent.

Gender moderation and exploratory subgroup analyses

To test whether gender moderated the relationships between Extraversion or Neuroticism and intrinsic motivation, interaction terms (gender × Extraversion and gender × Neuroticism) were included in separate regression models after entering main effects. Neither interaction term reached statistical significance (gender × Extraversion: β = .07, p = .30; gender × Neuroticism: β = −.05, p = .39), indicating that the trait–motivation associations held similarly for male and female students in this sample. Exploratory analyses by discipline (grouped into social sciences, natural sciences, and humanities) suggested broadly similar patterns across clusters, though subgroup sizes limited power and these results should be interpreted cautiously.

Effect sizes and practical significance

The partial correlations and standardized betas indicate small-to-moderate effect sizes. Specifically, Extraversion and Neuroticism each accounted for unique variance in intrinsic motivation beyond demographic covariates, and together the EPQ traits explained approximately 18% of the variance in SDS total scores. While not exhaustive of all predictors of intrinsic motivation, this magnitude suggests that personality traits are meaningfully related to students’ self-reported self-determination and intrinsic interest.

Robustness checks

To ensure that results were not driven by extreme values or by the mode of data collection (online vs. in-person), we conducted robustness checks by excluding univariate outliers (two cases with SDS scores more than three standard deviations from the mean) and by running models separately for online and in-person subsamples. The primary pattern of results—positive association between Extraversion and SDS and negative association between Neuroticism and SDS—remained consistent across these checks. Additionally, including self-reported GPA as a covariate did not materially change the key associations.

Summary of results

In summary, the analyses revealed that among 176 university students, Extraversion was positively associated with intrinsic motivation as indexed by the SDS, and Neuroticism was negatively associated with intrinsic motivation. These associations were robust to control for age, gender, and response bias, and were evident in both correlation and regression frameworks. Psychoticism showed no reliable relationship with intrinsic motivation in this sample. The observed trait–motivation links mirror patterns previously reported in exercise contexts and extend them into an academic setting.

Discussion

The present study examined associations between personality traits and intrinsic motivation in a sample of university students and found clear evidence of a positive relationship between Extraversion and intrinsic motivation and a negative relationship between Neuroticism and intrinsic motivation. These findings extend prior work linking personality to motivation in leisure domains—such as the relationship between frequent gym attendance, intrinsic motivation, and personality reported by Ingledew, Markland, and Sheppard—by demonstrating that similar trait–motivation associations are also present in academic equivalents of sustained engagement. The results are consistent with the broad premise of self-determination theory that affective and dispositional factors influence how individuals experience autonomy, competence, and relatedness in activities, thereby shaping the emergence and maintenance of intrinsic motivation.

Interpreting the positive Extraversion–intrinsic motivation association, several mechanisms are plausible. Extraverted individuals typically display higher levels of positive affect, greater approach motivation, and a tendency to seek stimulating social contexts. In academic settings, these tendencies may translate into a greater likelihood of engaging in collaborative study, participating in class discussions, and approaching learning tasks with enthusiasm—behaviors and experiences that foster the satisfaction of relatedness and competence needs posited by SDT. In addition, Extraversion’s association with positive affect could serve to enhance the immediate hedonic experience of learning, thereby reinforcing intrinsic motives for continued engagement. The autonomy-supportive opportunities present in many university courses, such as project choice and peer collaboration, may particularly resonate with the motivational style of extraverted students and help explain their higher SDS scores.

The negative Neuroticism–intrinsic motivation association likely reflects different mechanisms. Individuals high in Neuroticism are more prone to negative affectivity, worry, and self-focused attention, which can undermine both the capacity to enjoy activities and the sense of competence necessary for intrinsically motivated engagement. Academic tasks that require sustained concentration and tolerance for challenge may be experienced as more threatening or aversive by neurotic students, reducing their intrinsic interest and willingness to persist. Moreover, neuroticism-related anxieties can disrupt the attainment of autonomy and competence experiences: persistent worry about performance can narrow attention to threat and reduce the perceived sense of choice, while self-doubt can erode feelings of competence even when objective performance is adequate. These processes align with SDT’s contention that the absence of need satisfaction diminishes intrinsic motivation.

The regression analyses indicated that Extraversion and Neuroticism together explained a meaningful proportion of variance in intrinsic motivation, with the combined EPQ dimensions accounting for approximately 18% of variance beyond demographic covariates. This suggests that personality traits are an important piece of the motivational puzzle but are not determinative; many other factors—such as classroom practices, teacher behavior, peer influence, task structures, prior experiences, and situational affordances—also shape intrinsic motivation. The observed effect sizes were in the small-to-moderate range, reinforcing that personality contributes significantly but not exclusively to motivational orientation.

The subscale analyses shed light on more nuanced relations. Extraversion tended to predict autonomy-related aspects of the SDS more strongly, while Neuroticism was more closely tied to awareness-related aspects. This pattern is theoretically coherent: extraverted behaviors that emphasize engagement and initiative may promote a sense of choice and agency in learning contexts, while neurotic tendencies toward rumination and negative self-evaluation may reduce reflective awareness of intrinsic enjoyment and dampen experiential savoring. These differential patterns suggest that interventions might be more precisely targeted: for instance, social, participatory activities might be particularly effective at capitalizing on extraverted proclivities to enhance autonomy experiences, whereas anxiety-reduction and confidence-building strategies might be needed to bolster the awareness and savoring of learning experiences among neurotic students.

The current results parallel prior findings in exercise contexts reported by Ingledew, Markland, and Sheppard, thereby supporting a domain-general view of trait–motivation linkages. That is, the way personality traits shape the motivational climate and affective responses to activity appears to generalize across physical exercise and academic learning. This generality has important implications: it suggests that interventions designed to foster intrinsic motivation might be transferable across settings—what works to support intrinsic motives for exercise (e.g., autonomy support, socially embedded routines, competence feedback) may also promote intrinsic motivation for studying when adapted appropriately.

One practical implication arising from this research concerns educational approaches that prioritize autonomy, exploration, and collaborative learning—principles exemplified by the Reggio Emilia approach and similar pedagogies. Although Reggio Emilia is traditionally associated with early childhood education, its core tenets—centered on learner-led projects, teacher facilitation rather than directive instruction, and learning through social interaction—are instructive for higher education contexts as well. Emphasizing student choice, opportunities for authentic project work, and meaningful peer collaboration could help create environments that support autonomy, relatedness, and competence needs for a wide range of students. For extraverted students, such approaches may amplify already-strong intrinsic motives; for more neurotic students, a thoughtfully scaffolded and predictable autonomy-supportive environment could reduce anxiety and provide clear competence feedback, thereby making intrinsic engagement more accessible.

Another implication is the need for educators to recognize individual differences when designing instruction. Personality-informed pedagogical choices do not imply tracking or labeling students, but rather adopting a flexible repertoire of practices that can scaffold intrinsic motivation across dispositions. For example, offering both collaborative and solitary project options allows students to choose formats that align with their temperaments while still providing autonomy and opportunities for mastery. Creating structured choices—where the range of options is bounded and clear—may be particularly helpful for neurotic students, who benefit from predictability and a reduced burden of excessive decision-making.

The findings also suggest promising directions for targeted interventions. For students high in Neuroticism, interventions that directly address anxiety and performance-related worry may have downstream benefits for intrinsic motivation. Techniques such as brief cognitive-behavioral strategies, stress-management training, or pedagogical practices that provide frequent formative feedback and emphasize mastery rather than normative comparison could enhance perceived competence and reduce the threat associated with learning tasks. For extraverted students, fostering leadership roles, group facilitation opportunities, and active classroom participation can further support intrinsic motives and channel sociability into productive learning.

Several limitations of the present study warrant attention. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; while personality traits may influence intrinsic motivation, it is also plausible that sustained engagement in intrinsically motivating activities shapes affect and behavior in ways that feed back on trait-relevant functioning, or that transactional processes over time generate the observed associations. Longitudinal research tracking changes in personality facets and intrinsic motivation across semesters or developmental transitions would be valuable to clarify directionality. Second, the reliance on self-report measures may raise concerns about shared method variance; although we included response-bias controls and robustness checks, future studies could incorporate behavioral indicators of intrinsic engagement (e.g., time on task, voluntary participation in optional learning activities) and informant reports to triangulate findings. Third, the use of a short-form trait inventory introduced limitations in the reliability of certain scales (e.g., Psychoticism) and may have attenuated effect estimates; employing full-length, multidimensional personality inventories could provide a more nuanced picture, including assessment of traits beyond Extraversion and Neuroticism such as Conscientiousness and Openness, which are also relevant to academic motivation. Fourth, the study sample comprised students from a single university, which may limit generalizability to other educational contexts, cultural settings, age ranges, or to non-student populations.

Future research should address these limitations and build on the current findings in several ways. Longitudinal or experimental designs could test causal hypotheses—for example, by implementing autonomy-supportive interventions and examining whether individuals differing in baseline Extraversion or Neuroticism show differential gains in intrinsic motivation. Such work would clarify whether personality moderates intervention efficacy and inform personalized educational strategies. Experimental manipulations that temporarily alter perceived autonomy or relatedness would also help elucidate the proximal mechanisms linking traits to intrinsic motivation, and might reveal whether trait effects are mediated by momentary need satisfaction.

Another fertile avenue is to explore the role of other personality dimensions, such as Conscientiousness and Openness, in intrinsic motivation within academic settings. Conscientiousness, with its emphasis on self-discipline and goal persistence, might interact with intrinsic motives in complex ways: highly conscientious students could exhibit strong intrinsic motivation for mastery, or conversely may engage in study behaviors driven more by obligation than by enjoyment. Openness to experience could be particularly relevant to intrinsic interest in intellectual pursuits. Incorporating broader personality models, such as the Five-Factor Model, would help to map the full constellation of trait influences on intrinsic motivation.

It would also be valuable to investigate the interplay between personality and contextual variables at multiple levels, including peer group norms, instructor style, curriculum design, and institutional culture. Multilevel studies could examine how the effects of personality on intrinsic motivation vary across classrooms or programs that differ in autonomy support, assessment practices, or collaborative opportunities. Understanding these person–context interactions would enable more precise recommendations for pedagogical design and policy.

Moreover, intervention research could explore how to adapt Reggio Emilia-inspired principles for older learners and test whether such adaptations are particularly efficacious for students with certain personality profiles. For instance, pilots of project-based learning that integrate structured collaborative choices, regular competence-affirming feedback, and scaffolding for autonomy could be evaluated in randomized controlled trials to determine their impact on intrinsic motivation and academic outcomes.

Finally, the current findings have implications for parenting and early education, given the potential for childhood experiences to shape both trait development and motivational patterns. Longitudinal research beginning in early childhood could elucidate how autonomy-supportive parenting and educational environments influence the co-development of temperament, personality traits, and intrinsic motivation. Such work would speak directly to developmental prevention and early-intervention strategies aimed at promoting lifelong learning and well-being.

In conclusion, this study provides converging evidence that personality traits, particularly Extraversion and Neuroticism, are meaningfully associated with intrinsic motivation among university students. These relationships parallel findings in exercise contexts and suggest a domain-general pattern in which approach-oriented, positive-affect traits enhance intrinsic interest while negative-affect, vulnerability-related traits undermine it. The results underscore the value of integrating trait perspectives with self-determination theory to better understand individual differences in motivation and to inform educational practices that support autonomy, competence, and relatedness for diverse learners. Future longitudinal and intervention research is needed to clarify causal pathways and to develop tailored strategies that foster intrinsic motivation across dispositional profiles.
