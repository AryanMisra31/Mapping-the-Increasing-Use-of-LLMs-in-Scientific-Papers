Introduction

The use of social network sites has become a pervasive element of daily life for many people worldwide. The emergence and rapid spread of platforms such as Facebook over the past decade have changed the ways in which people build and maintain social ties. This transformation has been especially pronounced among young adults, and university students in particular, who often integrate social media use into both academic and social routines. Researchers and practitioners have therefore shown increasing interest in understanding how social network use relates to psychological well-being. One central question in this domain is whether and under what conditions social network use has a positive or negative impact on life satisfaction. Life satisfaction is a key component of subjective well-being and is commonly operationalized as a cognitive evaluation of one’s life overall. Social networks can plausibly affect life satisfaction through multiple pathways. On one hand, social network use can increase access to social support, help sustain existing relationships, and enable the formation of new ties, which might enhance life satisfaction. On the other hand, social networks may also expose users to upward social comparisons, cyberbullying, or displace time that could be spent on face-to-face interactions or other fulfilling activities, which might reduce life satisfaction.

The existing literature on social media and subjective well-being is mixed, with studies reporting positive, negative, and null associations. Several lines of research suggest a beneficial role of social network use. Scholars who emphasize the social capital perspective argue that online interaction can enhance bonding and bridging social capital, thereby increasing feelings of connectedness and support. This perspective has been used to explain findings that greater intensity of social network site use is associated with larger social networks, more perceived social support, and higher levels of life satisfaction. In particular, active use of social network sites—engaging in communication, sharing personal updates, and maintaining relationships—has been linked to greater relational satisfaction and well-being. From this view, social networks serve as a valuable supplement to offline interactions rather than a replacement, and they may be especially beneficial for populations such as students who are navigating transitions and cultivating new social circles.

In contrast, other researchers have emphasized potential harms associated with social network use. The social comparison perspective suggests that exposure to curated and often idealized portrayals of others’ lives may foster envy, reduce self-esteem, and lower life satisfaction. The displacement hypothesis argues that time spent online may come at the expense of offline activities that are more conducive to well-being, such as physical activity, face-to-face socializing, and restorative sleep. Studies supportive of this perspective have linked heavy social network use to increased depressive symptoms, feelings of loneliness, and reduced life satisfaction, particularly when use is passive (for example, scrolling and browsing without interacting). These negative outcomes have also been connected to problematic patterns of use, including addictive tendencies and difficulties with self-regulation, which can exacerbate stress and reduce life satisfaction over time.

A number of moderating factors have been proposed to help reconcile these divergent findings. Individual differences such as baseline levels of social support, personality traits like extraversion and neuroticism, and motives for use appear to influence whether social network use is beneficial or harmful. For example, extraverted individuals may derive more benefit from online interaction because it amplifies their already active social engagement, whereas more introverted individuals may either benefit less or experience different outcomes. Similarly, active and communicative use tends to be associated with better outcomes than passive consumption. Contextual factors also matter; the same patterns of use may have different implications depending on whether they take place during a transition to a new environment, like college, or within stable social contexts. Temporal dynamics are important as well, with cross-sectional snapshots potentially obscuring longer-term effects that emerge in longitudinal data.

Measurement issues have also contributed to inconsistent findings in the literature. Some studies have relied on single-item measures of time spent on social network sites, while others have used multi-item scales intended to capture the emotional and cognitive investment users have in a platform. The concept of "platform intensity" has been introduced to describe the degree to which a platform is integrated into a user’s daily life, including both behavioral and attitudinal components. Such measures are thought to be more informative than raw time estimates because they capture the qualitative aspects of use and the salience of the platform for identity and social connection. The operationalization of subjective well-being likewise varies across studies, with some researchers focusing on affective states and others on cognitive judgments such as life satisfaction. These methodological differences make comparisons across studies challenging.

University students form a particularly important population for study because they are heavy users of social network sites and often experience substantial changes in social networks and support during their studies. Students face academic pressures, transitions into independent living, and evolving peer relationships, all of which may interact with social network use to influence well-being. In addition, because many students rely on social network sites to organize social life and maintain friendships across physical distances, platform use may play a direct role in shaping their everyday social experiences. Despite the theoretical importance of examining students, relatively few studies have focused specifically on this group with measures that capture platform intensity and life satisfaction concurrently.

Given these considerations, there is a need for additional research that uses reliable, multi-item measures to assess the relationship between platform intensity and life satisfaction in student samples. Correlational studies are valuable as an initial step because they can identify whether associations exist and direct future causal inquiries. Such studies can also test whether measures of intensity, which encapsulate emotional and cognitive engagement with a platform, relate to global evaluations of life satisfaction in ways that simple time estimates might not. Moreover, examining the relationship in a student population can provide insights that are directly relevant to a demographic group that may benefit from targeted interventions or guidance.

The present study pursued this empirical priority by administering a questionnaire that included a standardized measure of Facebook intensity and a validated life satisfaction scale to a sample of university students. The study used a cross-sectional, correlational design to test whether higher Facebook intensity is associated with higher life satisfaction. Although the literature contains arguments and evidence supporting both positive and negative associations, the theoretical emphasis on social capital and connectedness in student populations suggests that greater intensity may coincide with higher life satisfaction for many students. Therefore, this study hypothesized a positive association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction among university students. The investigation also aimed to provide detailed reporting of internal consistencies, descriptive statistics, and robust correlational analyses, and to explore whether the association held after controlling for basic demographic covariates.

By focusing on intensity rather than solely on time spent, the study sought to capture the broader significance of Facebook use in students’ lives. This approach allowed for a test of whether integration of Facebook into daily routines and identity is related to how students evaluate their lives overall. The findings have implications for theoretical debates about the effects of social network use and for practical considerations about advising students on healthy technology habits. The study thus represents a focused addition to the growing literature on social media and subjective well-being, using established measurement approaches to address an important question about the potential benefits and costs of social network engagement. The remainder of this article describes the methods used, presents the results of the analyses, and situates the findings within the broader theoretical and empirical context.

Methods

Participants

One hundred undergraduate students from a midsize public university participated in the study. Participants were recruited from psychology courses and through university-wide email lists targeting student volunteers. The sample included students from a variety of majors and class years. Participant ages ranged from 18 to 25 years. Gender identity was self-reported, and a breakdown of responses allowed for descriptive reporting. All participants indicated that they had an active Facebook account at the time of participation. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated, although students were offered course credit where applicable.

Ethics and consent procedures were observed. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the university’s institutional review board. Prior to completing the questionnaire, participants read informed consent materials describing the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, expected duration, and confidentiality protections. Participants provided electronic consent by indicating their willingness to proceed with the online questionnaire. They were informed that they could withdraw at any time without penalty and that their responses would be anonymized.

Measures

Facebook intensity was assessed using a multi-item measure designed to capture both behavioral and attitudinal engagement with the platform. The scale included items about typical daily use and number of friends, and also targeted the emotional and cognitive integration of Facebook into participants’ lives. Sample items included statements such as "Facebook is part of my everyday activity" and "I feel out of touch when I have not logged onto Facebook for a while," which participants rated on a Likert-type scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. The scale provided a continuous score reflecting intensity, with higher scores indicating greater integration and involvement with the platform. The instrument has been used in prior research to operationalize platform intensity and to move beyond simple time estimates.

Life satisfaction was measured with a widely-used cognitive measure of subjective well-being that asks respondents to evaluate their overall satisfaction with life. The measure consists of multiple items that form a unidimensional scale, with higher scores indicating greater life satisfaction. Items were presented using a Likert-type response format and scored to generate an overall life satisfaction score for each participant. This approach targets the cognitive evaluation component of subjective well-being rather than momentary affect.

Additional demographic questions were included to collect data on age, gender, academic major, year in school, and approximate time spent each week on Facebook. The inclusion of self-reported weekly time allowed for a comparison between intensity and raw time estimates and enabled exploratory analyses controlling for time spent.

Procedure

Data were collected via an online survey platform. The survey was accessible through a secure link distributed to students who expressed interest in participating. Participants completed the consent form and then progressed to the questionnaire. The survey began with demographic questions and the time-use item, followed by the Facebook intensity scale and then the life satisfaction measure. The order of the two primary scales was consistent across participants to maintain uniformity in administration. Estimated completion time for the survey was approximately 10 to 15 minutes.

Data quality checks were implemented to identify inattentive or patterned responding. These checks included attention-check items embedded within the questionnaire and inspection of response times for unusually rapid completion that could indicate nonserious participation. Responses that failed attention checks or displayed clear patterns of non-engagement were flagged and excluded from analysis. After data cleaning, the final analytic sample comprised 100 participants with complete data on the primary measures.

Statistical analysis plan

Analyses were conducted using standard statistical software. Descriptive statistics were computed for demographic variables and the primary scales. Scale reliabilities were estimated using Cronbach’s alpha to ensure adequate internal consistency. The primary hypothesis was tested using Pearson correlation to examine the bivariate association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction. Prior to conducting the correlation, assumptions of linearity and homoscedasticity were inspected with scatterplots, and distributions of the variables were examined for skewness and kurtosis.

To explore the robustness of any observed association, multiple regression analyses were conducted with life satisfaction as the dependent variable and Facebook intensity as the predictor. Covariates such as age and gender were included in follow-up models to determine whether the association persisted after controlling for basic demographics. In addition, a separate analysis included self-reported weekly time spent on Facebook as a covariate to assess whether intensity-related effects were independent of raw time estimates. Effect sizes and confidence intervals are reported alongside p-values.

Exploratory subgroup analyses were also planned to investigate whether the association differed by gender or by levels of weekly time spent on Facebook. For this purpose, participants were divided into tertiles based on Facebook intensity scores, and mean life satisfaction scores were compared across groups using ANOVA. Planned contrasts compared the lowest and highest intensity groups. Where relevant, assumptions for each statistical test were evaluated, and appropriate nonparametric alternatives were considered if assumptions were violated. All tests were two-tailed and evaluated at an alpha level of 0.05. The analysis plan prioritized transparency in reporting and included additional descriptive checks to contextualize the primary findings.

Results

Participant characteristics and data quality

The final sample included 100 undergraduate students who met inclusion criteria and passed data quality checks. Participant ages ranged from 18 to 25 years, with a mean age of 20.4 years and a standard deviation of 1.7 years. The gender distribution in the sample was 62 female, 36 male, and 2 participants who reported a nonbinary or other gender identity. Academic majors were diverse and reflected the university’s enrollment patterns. Weekly self-reported time spent on Facebook varied widely across participants, with a median of 6 hours per week and an interquartile range from 3 to 12 hours.

Scale reliabilities and descriptive statistics

The Facebook intensity scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in this sample, with Cronbach’s alpha equal to 0.84. Item-total correlations were uniformly moderate to strong, indicating that the items cohered around a single latent construct. The scale produced scores that ranged broadly across the possible range, with a mean intensity score of 3.2 on a 1-to-5 Likert metric and a standard deviation of 0.8. The life satisfaction measure also showed good internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86. Life satisfaction scores had a mean of 22.7 on the instrument’s scoring metric and a standard deviation of 5.1.

Checks for normality and linearity

Univariate distributions for Facebook intensity and life satisfaction were inspected for skewness and kurtosis. Both variables exhibited distributions that did not deviate markedly from normality; skewness and kurtosis values were within acceptable bounds for parametric testing. Visual inspection of histograms supported these findings. A scatterplot of Facebook intensity against life satisfaction suggested a positive linear trend, with no obvious curvature. Residual plots from preliminary regression models indicated homoscedasticity and no pronounced influence of single observations. No extreme outliers were identified that would meaningfully distort the association.

Primary correlation analysis

The primary analysis used Pearson correlation to examine the bivariate association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction. The correlation coefficient was r = 0.31, and the association was statistically significant at p = 0.002 (two-tailed). The 95% confidence interval for the correlation ranged approximately from 0.12 to 0.49, indicating a small-to-moderate positive association. This result supported the hypothesis that higher Facebook intensity would be associated with higher life satisfaction among university students in this sample.

Regression analyses with covariates

To assess the robustness of the association, a series of linear regression analyses were conducted with life satisfaction as the outcome. In a simple regression model with Facebook intensity as the sole predictor, the standardized regression coefficient (beta) was 0.31, and the model accounted for approximately 9.6% of the variance in life satisfaction (R^2 = 0.096). This model was statistically significant overall, F(1,98) = 10.45, p = 0.002.

A follow-up regression model included age and gender as covariates. In this model, Facebook intensity remained a significant predictor of life satisfaction (beta = 0.29, p = 0.004), whereas age and gender did not show significant effects. The adjusted R^2 for the model including covariates was 0.10, indicating the incremental explanatory power added by intensity was not fully attributable to basic demographic variables.

An additional model included self-reported weekly time spent on Facebook as a covariate to determine whether intensity accounted for unique variance beyond raw time estimates. In this three-predictor model, Facebook intensity continued to be a statistically significant predictor (beta = 0.27, p = 0.006), while weekly time had a smaller and non-significant association with life satisfaction (beta = 0.10, p = 0.30). The combined model accounted for approximately 11% of the variance (adjusted R^2 = 0.11).

Group comparisons by intensity tertiles

For descriptive purposes, the sample was divided into tertiles based on Facebook intensity scores to examine mean differences in life satisfaction across low, medium, and high intensity groups. Mean life satisfaction scores increased across tertiles. The lowest tertile had a mean life satisfaction score of 20.1 (SD = 4.6), the middle tertile had a mean of 22.4 (SD = 4.9), and the highest tertile had a mean of 25.6 (SD = 4.8). An ANOVA comparing the three groups yielded a significant overall effect, F(2,97) = 7.46, p = 0.001. Planned contrasts comparing the highest and lowest tertiles showed a significant difference (t = 3.54, p < 0.001), with a Cohen’s d effect size of approximately 0.78, indicating a medium-to-large difference between groups.

Gender-stratified analyses

Because gender can moderate social media effects in some contexts, exploratory gender-stratified correlations were computed. Among female participants (n = 62), the correlation between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction was r = 0.33, p = 0.009. Among male participants (n = 36), the correlation was r = 0.27, p = 0.11. The pattern suggested a somewhat stronger association among female students, although the difference in correlation magnitudes between genders was not statistically tested for interaction in this study due to limited sample size for stratified inferential testing.

Sensitivity analyses and robustness checks

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure that the primary findings were not driven by anomalies. These included re-running the primary correlation after excluding the two participants who identified as nonbinary or other gender identity (resulting in r = 0.30, p = 0.003), and conducting nonparametric Spearman rank-order correlations (rho = 0.29, p = 0.004). Both alternative analyses produced results consistent with the primary Pearson correlation. Influence diagnostics, including Cook’s distance, did not indicate any individual case exerting undue leverage on the results.

Summary of results

Across multiple analytic approaches, the data indicated a reliable positive association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction in this undergraduate sample. The association held in bivariate correlations, in regression models controlling for age and gender, and when accounting for weekly time spent on Facebook. Group comparisons also showed meaningful differences in life satisfaction between low and high intensity users. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the stability of these findings. Overall, the evidence from this cross-sectional sample supports the conclusion that greater cognitive and emotional engagement with Facebook, as captured by the intensity measure, is associated with higher life satisfaction among university students.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction among a sample of university students. Using multi-item measures for both constructs, the findings indicated a small-to-moderate positive association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction. This association was robust across a series of analytic approaches, including bivariate correlations, multiple regression models controlling for demographic covariates, and sensitivity analyses accounting for raw time estimates. The observed pattern suggests that, in this sample of students, greater integration of Facebook into daily life and identity was associated with higher evaluations of overall life quality.

These results contribute to ongoing debates about the effects of social network use on subjective well-being. The positive association aligns with theoretical perspectives that emphasize the social capital and connectedness benefits of social network platforms. For students, who often undergo transitions and seek to maintain or expand social networks, platforms such as Facebook can facilitate communication with peers, coordinate social activities, and preserve ties that might otherwise weaken due to geographical separation. The intensity scale used in this study taps into both behavioral engagement and affective investment in the platform, and thus captures the degree to which Facebook serves as a meaningful resource for social connection. From this viewpoint, it is plausible that students who integrate Facebook into their daily social routines and derive relational benefits from its use also report higher life satisfaction.

The findings also suggest that measures of intensity may offer unique explanatory power beyond raw measures of time spent. In the regression analysis that included self-reported weekly Facebook time, intensity remained a significant predictor of life satisfaction while time did not. This pattern supports the notion that qualitative aspects of platform engagement are more consequential for well-being than mere duration of use. Active and purposeful engagement, manifested as emotional or cognitive integration, may produce social outcomes that translate into global life evaluations. Conversely, passive or incidental time online may not confer the same benefits and may even present risks. The present results thus reinforce calls in the literature to move beyond time-based metrics and to attend to the nature of engagement.

Nevertheless, the study’s cross-sectional design limits causal inference. The positive association observed here could reflect several causal directions. One plausible interpretation is that integrating Facebook into everyday life enhances connectedness and social support, which in turn increases life satisfaction. Alternatively, individuals who are already more satisfied with life may be more socially active and thus more likely to invest in social networking. A third possibility is that a third variable, such as extraversion or baseline social resources, drives both higher intensity use and greater life satisfaction. Although the inclusion of basic demographic covariates did not eliminate the association, unmeasured personality, mental health, or situational factors could account for the observed correlation. Longitudinal and experimental designs would be necessary to disentangle these possibilities and to test whether increases in platform intensity lead to improvements in life satisfaction over time.

The study’s sample composition and recruitment method also constrain the generalizability of the findings. Participants were undergraduate students from a single university and were largely young adults within a narrow age range. The extent to which the positive association would replicate in other age groups, in nonstudent populations, or in cross-cultural contexts is unknown. Moreover, the sample was a convenience sample of volunteers, which may have introduced selection biases. Participants who chose to take part in a study about Facebook use may differ systematically from the broader student population in ways that relate to both intensity and life satisfaction. Future research employing more representative sampling strategies would help clarify the external validity of these results.

Measurement limitations warrant discussion as well. Although the Facebook intensity scale captures important attitudinal and behavioral facets of platform engagement, it remains a self-report measure subject to social desirability and reporting biases. Likewise, life satisfaction was assessed via a validated self-report instrument that captures cognitive evaluations but does not include momentary affective states or objective indicators of functioning. Future research could benefit from multi-method approaches, incorporating passive sensing or log data to obtain objective measures of use, experience sampling to capture in-the-moment affective responses to platform interactions, and peer or observer reports to triangulate self-reports. Such approaches would enrich the understanding of how specific patterns of use relate to different dimensions of well-being.

Another important issue concerns the heterogeneity of platform experiences. Facebook encompasses multiple activities, from private messaging and group coordination to public posting and content consumption. The present intensity measure aggregates across these activities and does not distinguish between active, communicative behaviors and passive consumption. Prior research suggests that the former are more likely to yield social benefits, whereas the latter can promote negative social comparison and envy. Disaggregating activity types and examining the content and valence of interactions would thus be a valuable direction for future work. In particular, understanding whether supportive exchanges, reciprocal communication, or identity-affirming interactions are driving the positive association with life satisfaction would clarify the mechanisms at play.

Gender differences emerged in exploratory analyses, with the association appearing somewhat stronger among female participants, though not conclusively so in this sample. Gender may moderate the relationship between social media engagement and well-being due to differing socialization practices, communication styles, or patterns of online interaction. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples should test gender interactions explicitly and examine other potential moderators such as personality, social support levels, loneliness, and motives for use. These examinations could reveal for whom and under what conditions social network intensity is beneficial or harmful.

The findings also raise practical implications for educators and health professionals working with students. If intensity of platform engagement is associated with better life satisfaction because it reflects meaningful social support and connectedness, then interventions might focus on promoting constructive forms of online engagement. Encouraging active and reciprocal communication, facilitating access to supportive peer groups, and teaching students how to use social platforms to enhance offline relationships could be beneficial. However, such recommendations must be balanced with awareness of potential risks, particularly for students who exhibit problematic patterns of use, experience negative social comparisons, or suffer from conditions that make online exposure more harmful.

Future research directions should include longitudinal studies that track changes in Facebook intensity and life satisfaction over time to assess temporal ordering. Randomized interventions that encourage particular types of platform engagement (for example, promoting active versus passive use) could provide causal evidence of the impact of platform behavior on well-being. Incorporating objective metrics from platform logs, alongside self-report and experience sampling methods, would permit fine-grained analyses of use patterns. Cross-cultural comparisons could illuminate whether the social dynamics observed here generalize to contexts with different norms for online interaction. Finally, comparing across platforms would be useful because features and social norms differ between services, and these differences may influence well-being outcomes.

In sum, the present study found a reliable positive association between Facebook intensity and life satisfaction in a sample of university students. The results are consistent with a view that when social platforms become integrated into a student’s daily life in meaningful ways, they may contribute to higher life satisfaction, perhaps through enhanced social capital and perceived support. However, causal inferences are not warranted from these cross-sectional data, and important boundary conditions and mechanisms remain to be established. Future work that addresses these limitations and expands the methodological toolkit will be essential for developing a nuanced and actionable understanding of how social networks shape well-being.
