Introduction

Memory for language has long been a central topic in cognitive psychology. Human readers and listeners extract and retain information from verbal input in order to support later comprehension, inference, and action. The nature of the representations that support memory for language has been debated for decades. One influential strand of work emphasizes that memory for language is organized at the level of meaning rather than exact surface form. According to this view, representations preserve propositional content or gist over detailed syntactic or lexical form. An alternative view holds that surface form can be retained under many conditions, particularly when the form is salient or attention is directed to it. Empirical studies have tested these competing perspectives by examining recognition memory for sentences and passages, comparing memory for exact wording versus meaning. Early experimental work showed that participants are often poor at recognizing verbatim wording after a delay while they retain the underlying meaning. This pattern suggests that the memory system compresses or abstracts information away from surface details in favor of a propositional representation that captures content. At the same time, other work has demonstrated that under certain conditions participants can retain surface details, such as word order or specific lexical choices, especially when those details are distinctive or emphasized. Thus, the existing literature paints a picture in which both surface and gist information can be present, but the relative availability of each may vary with task, time, attention, and other factors.

One classic experimental approach assesses recognition memory by manipulating wording in a later probe and asking participants whether a probe sentence was exactly the same as an earlier sentence. Studies using this approach typically manipulate different types of change between the original and the probe, such as semantic changes that alter meaning, syntactic changes that preserve meaning but alter structure, and lexical or surface changes that alter some words without changing propositional content. These manipulations allow investigators to test which level of representation supports accurate recognition. Across many such studies, semantic changes are often easiest to detect, consistent with the idea that memory preserves meaning. In contrast, surface changes are frequently missed because the preserved gist is consistent with both original and altered surface forms. The sensitivity to syntactic changes has been more variable across studies, reflecting the complexity of how structural information is encoded and retained.

Another line of research has connected individual differences in working memory capacity to language processing and memory. Working memory has been implicated in parsing complex sentences, maintaining referents during discourse, and supporting inference making. Individual differences in working memory capacity have been shown to predict comprehension under demanding conditions and to relate to the ability to resist interference from irrelevant information. Because working memory supports the temporary storage and manipulation of information, it is plausible that working memory capacity would influence how much surface detail is retained from a passage and how well participants can detect changes in subsequent probes. For instance, individuals with higher working memory capacity might better maintain verbatim or surface details during initial encoding, or they might more effectively rehearse or rehearse content in a form that preserves surface features. Conversely, it is equally plausible that working memory capacity is more relevant for online comprehension demands while long-term recognition memory for meaning versus form is less dependent on individual differences in working memory.

The relationships among recognition memory for language, levels of representation, and working memory capacity are therefore of central theoretical interest. A seminal study investigated recognition memory for language using probes that either matched the original wording or differed in specified ways. The original experiment reported a pattern in which recognition was dominated by propositional content: participants frequently failed to recognize exact wording but reliably detected changes that altered meaning. Memory for surface form was relatively poor, particularly over longer delays, whereas memory for gist or propositional content was robust. This pattern has been widely cited as evidence that surface representations decay faster than meaning-based representations in memory for language.

Despite the impact of that seminal work, subsequent studies have produced a mixture of replications and qualified extensions. Some replications strongly supported the dominance of propositional memory, while other studies found conditions under which surface detail was available. One source of variation across studies may be participant-level differences such as working memory capacity. Another source of variation may be the particular materials and instructions used: tasks that emphasize exact wording during encoding or that use distinctive surface features may produce better surface memory. Delay intervals, interference tasks between study and test, and the relative salience or plausibility of changed probes also influence outcomes.

The present project was motivated by the desire to replicate the classical pattern of recognition memory effects for language while explicitly testing whether working memory capacity accounts for individual differences in sensitivity to different change types. The study was designed to closely follow the original paradigm that assessed recognition memory for passages followed by probe sentences that either were unchanged or contained specific types of alterations. At the same time, the present study added a standardized measure of working memory capacity as a covariate to examine whether capacity predicts detection and identification of change types. By bringing together a careful replication and an individual-differences measure, the study aimed to clarify whether working memory contributes to retention of surface versus propositional information.

The central questions addressed were threefold. First, would the basic pattern reported in the seminal work be replicated, namely robust memory for meaning and relatively weak memory for surface form? Second, would change detection (i.e., recognizing that a change has occurred) be generally easier than identification of the specific type of change (i.e., correctly classifying the change as semantic, syntactic, surface, or none)? Third, would individual differences in working memory capacity, as measured by a digit span task, predict either detection accuracy or identification accuracy across change types and positions?

We expected to replicate the original finding that semantic changes are most readily detected because they alter propositional content. We also expected that participants would be more accurate at judging whether a change had occurred than at identifying the specific type of change, since detection could rely on a global sense of mismatch whereas identification requires more detailed discrimination. Regarding working memory, we hypothesized that higher working memory capacity might be associated with better performance on tasks that demand retention of surface form or precise detail, because maintaining surface information requires attentive maintenance and perhaps rehearsal. Therefore, a modest positive correlation between working memory and performance on surface-change trials was anticipated. However, given the strength of propositional memory effects and prior mixed findings, we also acknowledged the possibility that digit span would not significantly predict recognition performance, particularly if the recognition task primarily tapped long-term memory processes that are less dependent on working memory.

In addition to the primary hypotheses, we considered the role of the position of the changed sentence within the passage. Memory is often recency-weighted such that items occurring later in a passage are better remembered. Therefore, we expected improved detection and identification for changes occurring in later positions compared to early positions. We also considered interactions: working memory might interact with position, such that capacity differences are most evident for earlier positions where maintenance across intervening material is required. Finally, we anticipated overall high accuracy on no-change trials because correctly rejecting identical probes is usually straightforward when participants recall propositional content.

To address these questions we ran seventy-four participants through a recognition memory task modeled on the classical paradigm. Participants read a set of passages, each followed by a probe sentence that either matched the original sentence or was altered in a specific way. The manipulations of change type and position were fully crossed across items to allow for generalization. All participants also completed a digit span task to provide an estimate of working memory capacity. The dependent measures were binary detection accuracy (did the participant correctly state whether a change had occurred) and categorical identification accuracy (did the participant correctly identify which type of change occurred).

The present study makes two important contributions. First, it provides a direct replication of a key empirical phenomenon in memory for language, thereby testing the robustness of the original effects in a new sample. Second, by including a working memory covariate, the study evaluates a theoretically important source of individual differences that could explain variability in recognition performance and account for conditions under which surface form is preserved. The combination of replication and individual differences assessment strengthens conclusions about the generality of the original findings and about mechanisms underlying memory for language.

Given the theoretical stakes and the mixed prior literature, the replication with an added working memory measure offers a clear test of competing possibilities. Successful replication with no effect of working memory would underscore the primacy of propositional memory representations and suggest that the processes that support recognition memory for meaning are relatively invariant across individuals. Conversely, a reliable effect of working memory would point to an important role for capacity in preserving surface detail and in discriminating changes. Either outcome would refine our understanding of the relationship between immediate memory capacity and longer-term language memory. The remainder of this article reports the methods used, the statistical analyses performed, the results observed, and a discussion that situates the findings within the broader literature and outlines directions for future research.

Methods

Participants were recruited from an undergraduate subject pool at a mid-sized university. In total, seventy-four participants completed the experiment. Participants were native speakers of English and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision. They received course credit for participation. The age range of participants was 18 to 24 years, with a mean age of 19.7 years. Participation was voluntary and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the experimental session. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the university.

Materials consisted of twelve short passages adapted from classical recognition-memory paradigms. Each passage contained three critical sentences positioned early, middle, and late in the passage. The critical sentences were constructed so that they could be manipulated to produce one of four change types or remain unchanged. The change types were defined as follows: semantic changes altered the propositional content of a sentence such that the meaning was different from the original; syntactic changes altered the structure or word order of a sentence while preserving propositional content; lexical/surface changes replaced a non-key word with a different word or synonym in a way that preserved meaning; and no-change trials presented the original sentence without alteration. Care was taken to ensure that semantic changes were plausible but clearly meaning-altering and that syntactic and lexical changes maintained grammaticality and plausibility. For each passage, four probe versions were prepared corresponding to the three change types and the no-change condition. Items were balanced so that each change type occurred equally often in each of the three positions across the stimulus set.

The design crossed change type (semantic, syntactic, lexical, no-change) with position (early, middle, late). Each participant saw twelve trials in total, one from each passage, such that they received a single probe per passage. The assignment of change type and position to passages was counterbalanced across participants using a Latin square design so that each participant saw each change type and each position an equal number of times across the session. This counterbalancing ensured that item-level effects were distributed across conditions.

The working memory measure was a standardized digit span forward task administered after the recognition memory trials. Digit span forward was chosen as a simple and reliable index of short-term memory capacity and to provide comparability with previous studies that used digit measures. Participants heard a sequence of digits presented aurally and repeated the sequence aloud in the same order. The length of digit sequences increased gradually, and span was scored as the total number of correctly recalled sequences at each length, with a maximum possible score of nine trials per length. The digit span score was computed as the maximum sequence length correctly recalled on at least two trials, producing a single-span measure for each participant. For supplementary analyses, we also computed a continuous total-correct score across trials to assess finer-grained variability in capacity.

The procedure began with instructions indicating that participants would read several short passages and then judge whether a subsequent sentence matched the passage or had been changed. Participants were told to read carefully because their memory for the passages would be tested. Passages were presented on a computer screen one sentence at a time, with participants advancing at their own pace by pressing the space bar. The presentation format allowed participants to spend more time on sentences they found difficult. After each passage, a probe sentence was displayed and participants were required to make two judgments in sequence. First, they made a binary change-detection judgment by pressing one of two keys to indicate whether the probe sentence was the same as a sentence in the passage or whether it was different. Second, they made a change-identification judgment by selecting which type of change best described the probe from a list of options presented on the screen: semantic, syntactic, lexical/surface, or no-change. The order of these judgments was kept constant to mirror procedures in prior work: detection first, and identification second. Participants were encouraged to make accurate judgments and there was no speed pressure. Short breaks were offered after every four trials to reduce fatigue.

Responses were collected and coded automatically by the experimental software. Detection accuracy was scored as correct if the participant correctly judged whether the probe had been changed relative to the passage. Identification accuracy was scored as correct if the participant correctly selected the change type that matched the true relationship between the probe and the original sentence. For no-change trials, the correct identification response was "no-change." Trials in which participants failed to make a response within sixty seconds were rare and were excluded from analyses; such exclusions amounted to less than 1% of trials.

After completing the recognition trials and the digit span task, participants filled out a short demographic questionnaire. The session lasted approximately 35 minutes per participant. Data were anonymized and stored securely.

Analyses were conducted primarily using mixed-effects logistic regression for binary outcomes and generalized linear models for categorical identification outcomes. Detection accuracy was modeled as a binary dependent variable with fixed effects of change type (dummy coded with semantic as reference), position (centered), digit span (standardized), and the two-way interactions of digit span with change type and position. Random intercepts for participants and items (passages) were included to account for repeated measures. Identification accuracy was similarly modeled, treating correct identification as a binary variable for each trial. In addition to the mixed-effects models, descriptive analyses computed mean accuracy rates and standard errors for each condition. Exploratory analyses examined correlations between digit span scores and condition-specific accuracy rates, and tested for potential ceiling or floor effects that might obscure capacity correlations.

Model selection proceeded from maximal random effects justified by the design, progressively simplifying random slopes when convergence issues arose. Fixed effects were evaluated using likelihood-ratio tests and Wald z-tests, and effect sizes were reported as odds ratios for logistic models. For ANOVA-style summaries, we also ran repeated-measures analyses with participant-level means to provide F-statistics for main effects and interactions. All reported p-values are two-tailed and alpha was set at .05. Supplemental Bayesian analyses were conducted for the primary hypotheses concerning digit span but are reported only where relevant to interpreting null effects.

Prior to hypothesis testing, data screening involved checking for outliers and ensuring adequate variability in digit span scores. The distribution of digit span scores approached normality with no extreme outliers. Detection and identification accuracy distributions were examined for extreme skewness. No-change detection showed elevated accuracy as expected. Where necessary to meet model assumptions, we trimmed extreme trials or used robust estimation techniques. All analyses were conducted in a standard statistical computing environment using widely available mixed-model packages.

Results

Descriptive statistics for detection and identification accuracy are presented first. Across all trials, mean detection accuracy was 0.78 (SD = 0.12) when averaged over participants. Mean identification accuracy was lower, at 0.59 (SD = 0.15) across conditions. For no-change trials, mean detection accuracy was substantially higher than for changed trials, with a mean of 0.92 (SD = 0.08). Semantic change trials had higher detection rates (mean = 0.85, SD = 0.10) than syntactic change trials (mean = 0.71, SD = 0.13) and lexical/surface change trials (mean = 0.66, SD = 0.14). Identification accuracy followed a similar pattern but with smaller absolute values: no-change identification was high (mean = 0.95, SD = 0.05), semantic identification was moderate (mean = 0.61, SD = 0.17), syntactic identification was lower (mean = 0.48, SD = 0.16), and lexical identification was lowest (mean = 0.42, SD = 0.18).

A mixed-effects logistic regression model predicting detection accuracy included fixed effects for change type (semantic as reference), position (early, middle, late entered as categorical indicators), digit span (standardized), and participant and item random intercepts. The model showed a significant main effect of change type. Semantic trials had higher odds of correct detection than lexical trials (odds ratio = 2.1, z = 3.34, p < .001) and higher odds than syntactic trials (odds ratio = 1.8, z = 2.98, p = .003). No-change trials had the highest odds of correct detection relative to semantic trials (odds ratio = 1.7, z = 2.81, p = .005). The main effect of position was also significant. Late-position trials were more likely to be detected correctly than early-position trials (odds ratio = 1.6, z = 2.45, p = .014). Middle-position trials were intermediate and differed marginally from early-position trials (odds ratio = 1.2, z = 1.40, p = .16). There was no reliable interaction between change type and position in the detection model; likelihood-ratio tests comparing models with and without interaction terms did not show improved fit (chi-square = 2.1, df = 6, p = .90).

Critically, digit span did not predict detection accuracy. The coefficient for standardized digit span in the detection model was small and non-significant (odds ratio = 1.03, z = 0.42, p = .67). Interactions between digit span and change type were likewise non-significant (all z-values < 1.0, all p-values > .30). An exploratory analysis examining digit span correlations with detection accuracy within each change type yielded small and non-significant correlations: semantic r = .09 (p = .45), syntactic r = .07 (p = .56), lexical r = .03 (p = .78), and no-change r = .05 (p = .66). These results indicate no evidence that digit span predicted detection performance either overall or within particular change types.

The identification accuracy model produced a similar pattern with respect to change type and position but again showed no effect of digit span. In a mixed-effects logistic regression predicting correct identification, change type was a strong predictor. Semantic trials were more likely to yield correct identification than lexical trials (odds ratio = 2.6, z = 4.02, p < .001) and than syntactic trials (odds ratio = 1.9, z = 3.08, p = .002). No-change identification was near ceiling and thus had a very high odds ratio relative to semantic trials (odds ratio = 3.5, z = 5.21, p < .001). Position had a modest effect on identification accuracy with late-position trials having higher odds of correct identification than early-position trials (odds ratio = 1.4, z = 2.00, p = .046). Again, the digit span term did not reach significance in the identification model (odds ratio = 1.01, z = 0.17, p = .86). No interactions of digit span with change type or position were significant.

To further characterize the difference between detection and identification, a paired-sample analysis at the participant level compared detection accuracy and identification accuracy across trials. A repeated-measures ANOVA with within-subject factor of judgment type (detection vs identification) confirmed a main effect: participants were significantly more accurate at detecting that a change had occurred than they were at identifying the type of change (F(1,73) = 182.4, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .71). This large effect was consistent across change types. Follow-up analyses indicated the detection-identification gap was largest for lexical/surface change trials and smallest for no-change trials. For lexical trials, average detection-identification difference was 0.24 (SD = 0.10), while for semantic trials the difference was 0.24 (SD = 0.12) as well, but the absolute identification performance differed across types.

Given the null findings for digit span, we conducted a set of sensitivity analyses to ensure that the lack of effect was not due to restricted range in the span measure or ceiling/floor effects in the dependent variables. The digit span scores ranged from 4 to 9 with a mean of 6.2 and a standard deviation of 1.1, providing adequate variability. Excluding the no-change trials to reduce ceiling effects did not reveal a hidden association: when analyses were restricted to changed trials only, digit span remained non-significant for both detection (odds ratio = 1.05, z = 0.52, p = .60) and identification accuracy (odds ratio = 1.02, z = 0.28, p = .78). Bayesian analyses produced Bayes factors in favor of the null over the alternative for the digit span effect on detection (BF01 = 4.5) and identification (BF01 = 3.9), suggesting the data provided moderate evidence against a substantial digit span effect.

Item-level analyses examining whether certain passages or item characteristics influenced the presence of digit span effects were also performed. Mixed models that included item-level length, word frequency of manipulated words, and plausibility ratings as covariates did not change the conclusions: digit span remained non-significant, and the main effects of change type and position persisted. In addition, we computed within-subject reliability for detection and identification measures across the twelve trials. Cronbach’s alpha for detection was .74 and for identification was .71, indicating acceptable internal consistency and supporting the reliability of the measures for correlational analyses.

Reaction time data were examined as a secondary dependent variable to explore whether digit span might influence processing speed rather than accuracy. Mean reaction times for detection judgments did not correlate reliably with digit span (r = .05, p = .63). Likewise, reaction times for identification judgments were unrelated to digit span (r = .08, p = .49). There was no indication that higher-capacity participants traded speed for accuracy or vice versa.

Given the consistency of null digit span effects, we checked demographic moderators. Neither age nor gender interacted with digit span to predict detection or identification. The small age range limited the power to detect age-related moderation, but gender-balanced analyses showed no indications of differential effects. Finally, exploratory analyses considered whether the order of trials influenced performance; there were small practice effects across the session such that detection accuracy increased marginally from early to late trials (slope = 0.02 per quartile, p = .08), but these trends did not interact with digit span.

In summary, the results replicated the original pattern observed in prior work: participants were better at detecting changes that altered meaning than at detecting surface or syntactic changes, and they were more accurate at detecting whether a change had occurred than at identifying the specific change type. Position within the passage influenced performance with later sentences yielding better recognition. However, contrary to the hypothesis that working memory capacity would predict performance, the digit span measure showed no reliable relationship with either detection or identification accuracy.

Discussion

The present study replicated and extended a classical line of research on recognition memory for language. Using a procedure modeled on earlier paradigms, the study found a clear pattern in which participants were generally sensitive to semantic changes, less sensitive to syntactic and lexical/surface changes, and particularly accurate at correctly identifying when no change had occurred. These effects were robust across items and participants and were moderated modestly by the position of the critical sentence in the passage. Importantly, the inclusion of a digit span measure as an index of working memory capacity did not account for variability in detection or identification performance. These findings both corroborate and refine prior conclusions about the nature of memory for language.

The replication of classic findings is a key contribution of the study. The superiority of semantic-change detection relative to surface-change detection aligns with the view that memory for language prioritizes propositional content or gist. When participants read a sentence embedded in a passage, they appear to encode and retain the underlying meaning in a way that allows them to distinguish probes that alter that meaning. This pattern was evident in both the binary detection judgments and the categorical identification judgments. The relatively poor performance on lexical/surface change trials supports the notion that surface details are more vulnerable to decay or are less likely to be encoded into durable memory representations when not explicitly required by the task. Syntactic changes, which preserve propositional content, produced intermediate performance and varied in detectability across items.

The finding that detection accuracy exceeded identification accuracy across conditions is theoretically meaningful. Detecting that a change has occurred can be accomplished by a global mismatch signal between the stored representation and the probe. This signal does not require fine-grained discrimination of what aspect of the probe is discrepant. In contrast, identifying the specific type of change requires a more analytic comparison and access to information about the level of representation affected. The large detection-identification gap suggests that recognition memory tasks can permit successful global mismatch detection even when the stored representation lacks the granularity needed for precise classification. This asymmetry echoes other work showing that gist-based memory can support categorical judgments while failing to support verbatim recall or discrimination.

The position effect observed in the data, with later sentences being better detected and identified, is consistent with recency advantages commonly reported in memory studies. Items presented later in a passage are less likely to be subject to interference from intervening material and may be more accessible due to their relative temporal proximity to the test probe. The graded effect observed—late > middle > early—supports a gradient of accessibility rather than an all-or-none recency phenomenon. That position exerted a modest influence on both detection and identification suggests that temporal factors interact with representational form; perhaps surface details degrade over time while propositional content remains accessible, producing relatively stable semantic detection across positions but more pronounced position effects for surface information.

The null result for digit span in predicting detection and identification merits careful consideration. One interpretation is that the cognitive processes tapped by the recognition task are primarily long-term memory processes that are not strongly constrained by short-term storage capacity as measured by digit span. If encoding into a durable, gist-like representation occurs rapidly and automatically during comprehension, then individual differences in digit span might not play a substantial role in determining recognition performance. This interpretation aligns with theories positing that comprehension involves rapid construction of propositional representations that are consolidated into a long-term memory trace independent of working memory capacity.

Another interpretation is that digit span, as employed in this study, is an imperfect proxy for the aspects of working memory most relevant to language encoding. Digit span forward primarily measures short-term storage rather than complex working memory operations such as simultaneous storage and processing. Many studies linking working memory to language comprehension have used complex span tasks that require participants to alternate processing and storage operations. These complex span measures often correlate more strongly with language-related tasks than does simple span. Thus, the absence of an effect for digit span does not rule out the possibility that other components of working memory, particularly those involved in controlled attention or executive processes, could influence surface-detail retention.

Methodological factors could also explain the lack of digit span effects. The digit span distribution in the sample, although adequate, may have been insufficiently broad to reveal correlations with recognition performance. University undergraduate samples often show restricted variability in cognitive measures, which can attenuate observed relationships. Additionally, the recognition task featured relatively few trials per condition (effectively four trials per change type across positions for each participant), which limits the reliability of individual difference estimates and reduces power to detect correlations. Although the internal consistency of the measures was acceptable, more trials would increase reliability and potentially reveal smaller effects.

Ceiling and floor effects are another consideration. No-change trials were near ceiling for both detection and identification, and lexical change identification showed relatively low accuracy. Ceiling effects can attenuate correlations by reducing variance, whereas floor effects similarly compress variability at the bottom of a scale. In our sensitivity analyses excluding no-change trials, digit span remained non-significant, but the reduced number of trials in those restricted analyses further limited power. Future work employing larger item sets and more balanced difficulty could provide a more sensitive test of working memory effects.

The present null result for digit span also contributes to theoretical refinement. If short-term storage does not predict recognition memory for language, then models of language memory should emphasize the role of encoding processes that transform input into a representational format that is relatively stable across individuals. Alternatively, it could be that working memory influences initial comprehension under high-demand conditions (e.g., syntactically complex or semantically ambiguous sentences), but not the later recognition judgment when comprehension was relatively straightforward. The passages used here were designed to be naturalistic and not overtly taxing; thus, working memory constraints may not have been activated to a degree that would produce measurable individual differences.

The present findings also have implications for the debate about surface versus gist memory. The robustness of semantic detection and the poor retention of surface details support models in which meaning-based representations are the primary currency of memory for language. This outcome is consistent with pragmatic considerations: remembering meaning is typically more useful for guiding behavior and communication than remembering exact wording. However, the fact that surface information was sometimes detected and occasionally identified suggests that encoding of verbatim features is not impossible; rather, it is less likely under typical reading conditions. Conditions that promote attention to surface form—such as instructions emphasizing exact wording, repetition, or use of distinctive word choices—may enhance surface memory and alter the balance between form and meaning.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. As noted, the sample consisted of university students and thus may not generalize to other populations with different ranges of working memory capacity or language experience. The working memory measure was limited to digit span forward; inclusion of complex span and other executive control measures would provide a more comprehensive assessment. The number of trials per condition was modest, which constrained the reliability of individual difference estimates. The stimuli, while controlled, were relatively brief passages and may not capture longer discourse processes that engage working memory more fully. Finally, the experimental procedure used an immediate recognition probe for each passage; longer retention intervals and interpolated tasks could differentially reveal the role of working memory in maintenance and consolidation processes.

Future research can address these limitations by incorporating several design improvements. First, employing diverse working memory assessments—including complex span tasks, operation span, and tasks tapping controlled attention—would allow finer-grained tests of which components of working memory, if any, influence recognition memory for language. Second, increasing the number of items per condition and diversifying stimuli to include more complex and longer discourse would improve reliability and ecological validity. Third, manipulating instructions to emphasize either exact wording or general meaning at encoding could determine whether deliberate strategy use modulates the balance of surface and gist memory and whether working memory capacity interacts with such strategies. Fourth, introducing delays of varying lengths and intervening tasks could test the temporal dynamics of surface and gist retention and whether working memory supports maintenance across distraction.

From a theoretical standpoint, integrating approaches from memory research and language processing will be fruitful. Neurocognitive methods such as EEG or fMRI could reveal whether neural signatures of encoding or retrieval differ as a function of change type and relate to individual differences in working memory. Developmental and clinical populations may also shed light on how working memory deficits or maturational differences affect language memory. For example, testing children or older adults could illuminate whether the propensity to retain gist over form changes across the lifespan and whether working memory modulates these developmental trajectories.

In conclusion, the present study replicated key findings that recognition memory for language favors propositional content over surface detail and that participants are generally better at detecting that a change has occurred than at identifying its precise nature. The position of the critical sentence within a passage influenced performance modestly, with later items better remembered. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, digit span forward did not predict detection or identification accuracy, suggesting that simple short-term storage capacity may not play a central role in recognition memory for language as measured in this paradigm. These results refine our understanding of the representations that underlie memory for language and point to promising directions for future research that will further clarify the roles of working memory, encoding strategy, and temporal dynamics in shaping what we remember from language.
