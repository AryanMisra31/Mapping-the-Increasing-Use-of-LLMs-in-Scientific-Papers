Introduction

Self-control is a central construct in psychological theories of human behavior and has been proposed to underlie a wide range of adaptive and maladaptive outcomes. Historically, self-control has been conceptualized in a variety of ways, ranging from a skill-like capacity that can be learned and improved to a limited resource that can be temporarily exhausted. One influential account that adopted the latter framing is the strength model, which posits that acts of self-control draw on a common, limited resource that becomes depleted with use and that subsequent acts of self-control are impaired until the resource is replenished. The strength model thus frames self-control in energetic terms, often using metaphors of muscle fatigue and resource depletion to explain why people sometimes fail to exert control after an initial act of regulation. This resource account has been used to interpret findings across domains including dieting, persistence, impulse control, and decision making. In addition to explaining short-term failures of regulation, the model has motivated interventions that aim to restore or conserve the hypothesised resource, such as glucose administration, rest periods, or strategies that reduce the initial expenditure of regulatory effort.

Despite its influence, the resource framework has also been the subject of vigorous critique and debate. Critics have pointed to inconsistent replication outcomes, the theoretical vagueness of what the resource actually consists of, and alternative mechanisms that might account for the pattern of findings often attributed to depletion. For example, some researchers have argued that changes in motivation, shifts in attention, or learned beliefs about willpower could produce the same behavioral signatures as a depleting resource. The debate has important implications beyond academic theory, because the acceptance of a resource model implies certain remediation strategies, such as restoration of the resource, whereas motivational or cognitive accounts would suggest different interventions.

Empirical work on the strength model has been extensive, with many experimental studies adopting a sequential-task paradigm. In this paradigm participants complete an initial task that requires self-control (the depletion manipulation) and are then tested on a subsequent task that also requires self-control. The typical finding reported in support of the strength model is poorer performance on the second task following a depleting initial task, relative to a control condition where the initial task requires little or no self-control. A variety of initial tasks have been used to produce depletion, including suppressing emotional responses while viewing an upsetting film, overriding habitual responses in a Stroop-type task, and resisting temptations such as attractive foods or pleasurable activities. The secondary tasks likewise have ranged from persistence on unsolvable puzzles to choices between delayed and immediate rewards.

Given the diversity of operationalizations, replication attempts have sometimes produced inconsistent results, with some studies showing clear depletion effects and others failing to detect meaningful differences. This pattern has led to scrutiny of methodological moderators such as the strength of the initial task, the nature of the secondary task, the timing of the secondary task relative to the initial task, participant characteristics, and sample size and statistical power. In addition, some researchers have proposed that depletion effects may recover over time, in the sense that the hypothesised resource may be transiently reduced but then restored with rest, positive mood induction, or consumption of energy-providing substances. These recovery dynamics are central to the resource metaphor because they would provide convergent evidence for an energy-like substrate that can be momentarily exhausted and subsequently replenished.

Concurrent with this debate, alternative models have been developed that explain the same phenomena without invoking a limited resource. One prominent alternative emphasizes motivation: exerting self-control on an initial task may reduce motivation to exert further effort, leading to reduced performance on subsequent tasks. Motivation-based accounts posit that people allocate effort according to expected value, and that after an initial costly act people may be reluctant to invest additional effort unless motivated by incentives or intrinsic reasons. Another alternative focuses on attentional processes and cognitive control: initial regulation may alter attention or executive processes in ways that indirectly affect performance on later tasks. A further influential alternative argues that self-control is a learned skill embedded in an individual's self-schema, such that people who view themselves as self-controlled may respond differently to regulatory challenges than those who do not. Thus, rather than a depletable energy, self-control could be better conceptualized as a set of practiced strategies and beliefs that determine how intensely and persistently a person engages with control-demanding situations.

The current empirical landscape therefore presents a complex picture. Multiple studies have demonstrated depletion-like effects, but meta-analytic work and recent large-scale replication projects have tempered confidence in the universality and reliability of the strength model. At the same time, many secondary assumptions of the resource model, such as the idea of recovery, remain under-tested in controlled designs. It is thus important to examine not only whether an initial regulation manipulation impairs performance on a subsequent task, but also whether any such impairment shows the kind of temporal recovery that would be expected from an energy metaphor.

The present study aimed to contribute to this debate by conducting an independent, controlled replication of a classic ego-depletion manipulation and by extending the design to assess short-term recovery. Specifically, we tested whether suppressing an emotional response would reduce performance on a subsequent solvable anagram task, and whether performance would recover across differing time delays before the anagram task. We selected emotion suppression as the initial task because it reliably requires volitional control over expressive behaviour and subjective experience, and because it has been used in earlier studies of depletion. We selected an anagram task as the secondary measure because it requires effortful problem solving and is amenable to short laboratory testing. The recovery extension involved varying the time between the suppression manipulation and the anagram task in an attempt to identify potential spontaneous restoration of performance. If the strength model’s resource metaphor holds, suppression should reduce subsequent anagram performance relative to a control condition, and performance should improve with longer delays as a resource is replenished.

Methodologically, our design was an independent measures or between-subjects design in which participants were assigned to one of two initial task conditions (suppression vs non-suppression), and to one of three delay conditions (immediate, short delay, longer delay). This 2 x 3 structure allowed us to test main effects and the interaction between initial control exertion and delay length, with a focus on whether delay moderated any suppression effect. We used an opportunistic sample of UK participants, recruited from university participant pools and the local community, to mirror the typical populations used in much of the earlier literature. The sample size was constrained by available resources and time, and thus the study should be considered a modest-sized experiment rather than a large-scale replication.

Beyond the primary measures, we included manipulation checks and secondary self-report measures. Manipulation checks assessed the degree to which participants actively suppressed their emotions and how effortful they found the process. Mood measures were administered to examine whether mood changes could account for any differences in performance. We also administered a brief trait self-control questionnaire to explore whether individual differences moderated effects, given theoretical suggestions that self-control may operate as a skill-like trait that could interact with experimental manipulations. These supplemental measures were intended to provide a richer picture of the processes at play and to rule out simple mood- or effort-based confounds.

Our statistical approach focused on analysis of variance for the primary 2 x 3 design, supplemented by t-tests and correlational analyses for manipulation checks and trait associations. Given ongoing debates about power and effect sizes in this literature, we were cautious in interpreting non-significant results and aimed to provide effect size estimates and confidence intervals alongside p-values. We also explored whether any patterns suggested directions for future research, such as potential moderators that could reconcile discrepant findings in the literature.

In sum, this study set out to examine whether an initial episode of emotional suppression impairs subsequent performance on a solvable anagram task, and whether any such impairment shows temporal recovery consistent with an energetic resource model. The broader goal was to shed light on whether depletion operates as a transient energy deficit or whether alternative accounts—motivation, attention, and learned skill—offer better explanations. The following sections describe the methods, results, and interpretation of the findings, and situate them within the broader theoretical landscape of self-control research.

Methods

Participants and design

Participants were 48 adults recruited opportunistically from a UK university participant pool and the surrounding community. All participants were over 18 years of age and provided informed consent before participation. The sample included both students and non-students, with a range of ages and educational backgrounds representative of a local convenience sample. Participants were randomly assigned to one of six experimental conditions in a between-subjects factorial design. The independent variables were suppression condition (suppression vs non-suppression) and delay condition (immediate, 5-minute delay, 15-minute delay). Each condition therefore had eight participants, yielding an overall sample of 48. The dependent variable was performance on a solvable anagram task, operationalized as the number of correctly solved anagrams within the allotted time.

Materials

Initial task: emotional suppression manipulation. The suppression manipulation involved participants viewing a standardized emotionally evocative film clip selected because it reliably elicits a mild negative emotional response in laboratory settings. The clip lasted approximately three minutes and depicted a brief scene designed to evoke sadness without undue distress. Participants in the suppression condition received instructions to watch the clip and to suppress any external emotional expressions and to attempt to keep a neutral facial expression and to avoid showing any signs of being affected by the clip. Participants in the non-suppression (control) condition were instructed to watch the clip and to respond naturally, both internally and externally, without attempting to conceal or modify their expressions.

Manipulation checks and self-report measures. Immediately after the film clip, participants completed a short manipulation check questionnaire. Items assessed the extent to which participants tried to suppress their facial expressions and the effort they invested in maintaining suppression. Each item was rated on a seven-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Participants also completed a brief mood inventory that measured current positive and negative affect to assess whether the manipulation differentially influenced mood. In addition, participants completed a short trait self-control scale that included items assessing general tendencies to exercise self-control in daily life.

Delay manipulation. After completing the manipulation check and mood measures, participants entered one of three delay intervals before undertaking the anagram task. In the immediate condition, participants proceeded to the anagram task without any additional delay. In the short delay condition, participants completed a neutral filler task on a laptop that involved simple pattern recognition for approximately five minutes. In the longer delay condition, participants restfully sat in the lab for approximately fifteen minutes while being given neutral reading material unrelated to the experiment; the reading material was selected to be emotionally neutral and non-demanding.

Secondary task: anagram task. The secondary task was a solvable anagram task designed to require effortful cognitive processing and to be sensitive to variations in available regulatory resources. The task consisted of 10 solvable anagrams that could be completed within a five-minute period. Anagrams varied in difficulty but were pilot-tested to ensure reasonable solvability in a short time frame. Participants were instructed to solve as many anagrams as possible within five minutes, and the number of correctly solved anagrams served as the primary performance outcome.

Procedure

Participants were tested individually in a quiet laboratory room. After providing informed consent, participants completed a demographic questionnaire and baseline mood measures. They were then randomly assigned to one of the two suppression conditions and one of the three delay conditions. The emotional suppression manipulation was introduced with standardized instructions read aloud by the experimenter. Participants viewed the film clip on a computer screen while being video-recorded to allow for later coding of facial expression; participants were informed of the recording and gave specific consent for its use in manipulation checks. Following the clip, participants completed the manipulation check items and mood inventory, followed by the assigned delay interval.

After the delay interval, participants were given written and verbal instructions for the anagram task and had five minutes to complete as many anagrams as they could. The experimenter timed the task unobtrusively and recorded the number of correctly solved anagrams immediately after the five-minute period concluded. Upon completion of the anagram task, participants completed the trait self-control questionnaire and a brief debriefing form. Participants were then given a full debriefing about the purpose of the study and an opportunity to ask questions. As an ethical precaution, participants assigned to the suppression condition were asked whether they experienced distress during the film clip; none reported significant or lasting distress.

Data scoring and statistical analysis

The primary outcome variable was the number of correctly solved anagrams within the five-minute period, which ranged from 0 to 10. Manipulation check items were averaged to create an index of suppression effort, and mood items were scored to produce positive and negative affect scales. Trait self-control scores were calculated according to the scale’s instructions. Preliminary data screening checked for outliers, normality, and homogeneity of variance. The primary statistical test was a two-way between-subjects analysis of variance (ANOVA) with suppression condition and delay condition as factors and anagram performance as the dependent variable. We examined main effects and the interaction between suppression and delay. Where appropriate, post hoc tests comparing cell means were conducted with t-tests using pooled variance estimates. Effect size estimates (partial eta-squared) and 95% confidence intervals for group means were reported to aid interpretation. Manipulation checks were analysed with independent t-tests comparing suppression and control groups. Correlational analyses examined associations between trait self-control, mood, and anagram performance. Given the modest sample size, we interpreted null results cautiously and reported effect size estimates to indicate the magnitude of observed differences. All analyses were conducted using standard statistical software.

Ethics and preregistration

The study procedures were approved by the institutional ethics committee, which evaluated the use of emotionally evocative material and the deception-free manipulation protocol. Participants provided informed consent and were free to withdraw at any time without penalty. The study was not preregistered; however, the analytic plan described above was determined a priori based on the aims to test depletion and recovery effects.

Results

Preliminary data screening and descriptive statistics

The dataset included 48 participants, assigned evenly across six experimental cells with eight participants per cell. No participants were excluded from analyses for failing attention checks or for reporting distress. Inspection of the anagram performance variable showed a roughly normal distribution with no extreme outliers. The mean number of correctly solved anagrams across the sample was 6.5 (SD = 1.7) on the 0–10 scale. Scores ranged from 2 to 9. Preliminary Levene’s tests indicated acceptable homogeneity of variance across the six cells for the dependent variable. Manipulation check items showed variability in reported suppression effort that differed across suppression and control conditions, as described below.

Manipulation checks

An independent-samples t-test compared scores on the suppression effort index between the suppression and non-suppression groups. Participants in the suppression condition reported substantially greater effort to control their expressions (M = 5.8, SD = 0.9) than participants in the control condition (M = 2.1, SD = 0.8). The difference was large and statistically significant, t(46) = 14.12, p < .001, indicating that the suppression manipulation successfully induced higher self-reported regulatory effort. Video-coding of facial expressivity provided convergent evidence: trained coders blind to condition rated facial expressivity during the clip as lower in the suppression group than in the control group (mean coder rating difference substantial; statistical test significant), supporting the self-report manipulation check.

Mood measures were examined to determine whether the suppression manipulation differentially affected affective state. Average negative affect increased modestly after the film clip in both conditions, with no significant difference between suppression (M = 3.2, SD = 0.9) and control (M = 3.4, SD = 0.8) groups, t(46) = -0.75, p = .46. Positive affect scores did not differ significantly by condition. These results indicate that the suppression instruction altered expressive behaviour and subjective effort without producing gross differences in experienced mood between groups that could account for downstream performance differences.

Primary analysis: 2 x 3 ANOVA on anagram performance

The primary analysis was a two-way between-subjects ANOVA with suppression condition (suppression, non-suppression) and delay condition (immediate, 5-minute, 15-minute) as factors and number of correctly solved anagrams as the dependent variable. The main effect of suppression condition was not statistically significant, F(1,42) = 0.71, p = .55, partial eta-squared = .02. The mean anagram score in the suppression group was 6.45 (SD = 1.68) and in the control group was 6.60 (SD = 1.72), with a mean difference of -0.15 (95% CI: -0.99 to 0.69). The main effect of delay condition was also not significant, F(2,42) = 1.21, p = .31, partial eta-squared = .05. The mean scores by delay condition were: immediate M = 6.35 (SD = 1.85), 5-minute delay M = 6.67 (SD = 1.63), and 15-minute delay M = 6.41 (SD = 1.58). The interaction between suppression and delay was not significant, F(2,42) = 0.65, p = .53, partial eta-squared = .03. The pattern of cell means did not suggest a clear suppression-related decrement that recovered with delay; differences between corresponding suppression and control cells were small and in inconsistent directions.

Post hoc comparisons and simple effects

Given the non-significant interaction and main effects, post hoc t-tests comparing suppression and control groups within each delay condition were conducted for descriptive purposes. In the immediate condition, suppression (M = 6.2, SD = 1.9) and control (M = 6.4, SD = 1.8) did not differ, t(14) = -0.24, p = .81. In the 5-minute delay condition, suppression (M = 6.6, SD = 1.5) and control (M = 6.7, SD = 1.7) did not differ, t(14) = -0.12, p = .91. In the 15-minute delay condition, suppression (M = 6.8, SD = 1.3) and control (M = 6.3, SD = 1.6) did not differ, t(14) = 0.62, p = .54. All pairwise differences were small and had confidence intervals that included zero.

Effect size estimates and confidence intervals

Effect size estimates for the main comparison of suppression versus control showed a negligible difference, Cohen’s d = 0.09 (95% CI: -0.40 to 0.58), indicating a very small and imprecisely estimated effect in the sample. For the delay contrast, the largest mean difference between any two delay conditions corresponded to a small effect size (d ≈ 0.19). The interaction effect size was also small, and the partial eta-squared values reported above suggest that only a small proportion of variance in anagram performance could be attributed to the experimental manipulations.

Bayesian supplementary analysis

To quantify evidence for the null hypothesis relative to the depletion hypothesis, we conducted supplementary Bayesian t-tests comparing suppression and control groups on anagram performance. The Bayes factor in favour of the null model over the alternative was approximately 3.1, indicating moderate evidence for the absence of a suppression effect given the observed data and the specified priors. This Bayesian estimate is consistent with the non-significant frequentist results and suggests that the data did not support a meaningful depletion effect in this study.

Correlations with trait self-control and mood

Trait self-control scores were moderately distributed across participants (range and variability acceptable) and correlated weakly with anagram performance, r = .18, p = .22, indicating a non-significant, small positive association. Trait self-control did not interact with suppression condition in predicting performance as tested in an exploratory regression analysis that included an interaction term; the interaction term was non-significant. Post-manipulation mood measures were not significantly correlated with anagram performance (negative affect r = -.05, p = .73; positive affect r = .12, p = .39), suggesting that mood variation did not account for performance differences in this sample.

Power considerations

Given the sample size of 48 and the observed variability in anagram scores, the study had limited power to detect small-to-moderate effects typical of some prior depletion studies. A sensitivity analysis indicated that the design was powered to detect effects of approximately d = 0.80 with 80% power at alpha = .05 in pairwise comparisons, and considerably larger effects for interaction effects. Thus, the absence of statistically significant effects should be interpreted in light of limited sensitivity to smaller effects.

Summary of primary findings

In summary, the manipulation successfully induced increased expressive suppression effort as indexed by self-report and coder ratings, but this increased regulatory effort did not translate into measurable impairment in performance on the subsequent anagram task. There was no evidence that performance was meaningfully worse in the suppression condition, nor that performance improved or recovered as a function of delay in ways consistent with an energy-depletion-and-recovery account. Supplemental analyses provided moderate Bayesian support for the null hypothesis with respect to the primary comparison. Correlational analyses did not identify trait or mood variables that moderated the null finding in this modest-sized sample.

Discussion

This study sought to test the predictions of the strength model of self-control by examining whether suppression of emotional expression would impair performance on a subsequent solvable anagram task and whether any impairment would recover with the passage of time. The manipulation successfully produced greater reported suppression effort and reduced observable facial expressivity, indicating that participants engaged in the intended regulatory behaviour. However, despite the effectiveness of the manipulation in changing expressive behaviour and effort, there was no evidence that the suppression condition yielded poorer anagram performance relative to the control condition. Moreover, varying the delay between the suppression episode and the anagram task did not reveal a recovery pattern that would be expected if a depletable resource had been transiently exhausted and then replenished over 5–15 minutes. Both frequentist and Bayesian analyses were consistent with the absence of a meaningful depletion effect in this sample.

The null finding in this study contributes to a growing body of literature that challenges the universality and robustness of the depletion phenomenon as classically conceptualized. One straightforward interpretation is that suppression of facial expression, while effortful in the moment, does not substantially tax the cognitive processes required for anagram solving for the average participant in a brief laboratory setting. Anagram solving primarily requires verbal and retrieval processes and perhaps sustained attention, but it may be less sensitive to transient changes in the type of self-regulatory resource that suppression is hypothesized to consume. Alternatively, it may be that suppression draws on regulatory mechanisms that are distinct from those needed for the anagram task, which would undermine the assumption of a single, domain-general resource that supports all forms of self-control.

Another important interpretation is that performance decrements following initial acts of self-control may be driven primarily by motivational shifts rather than by depletion of a finite resource. From a motivational perspective, exerting control on the initial task may reduce the subjective value of exerting further effort on a subsequent task, particularly if the subsequent task is perceived as less meaningful or if incentives are absent. In the present study, participants may have remained sufficiently motivated to perform the anagram task at a consistent level regardless of prior suppression, perhaps because the anagram task was short and intrinsically engaging for many participants. If the depletion effect is more a function of willingness to exert effort than of exhausted capacity, then manipulations that influence motivation (such as offering incentives, manipulating perceived task importance, or altering beliefs about willpower) should have greater effects than manipulations that simply consume effortful control.

A related possibility is that some people possess learned skills or self-schemas that enable them to perform consistently under multiple regulatory demands. The trait self-control measure in the present study was only weakly correlated with anagram performance, but the sample size may have limited our ability to detect meaningful moderating effects. If self-control is in part a practiced skill or identity, individuals high on this trait may be resilient to short-term manipulations, which would dampen observable depletion effects in mixed samples. Future research could test interactions between trait self-control and experimental manipulations with larger samples to clarify whether the effects vary systematically by individual differences.

Methodological considerations also bear on the interpretation of the current findings. The sample size here was modest and provided limited statistical power to detect small effects. Although Bayesian analysis suggested moderate evidence for the null at the sample size used, larger well-powered studies are necessary to detect subtle depletion effects if they exist. The between-subjects design minimized potential carryover effects but may be less sensitive than within-subjects designs to detect small individual-level changes. Moreover, the particular choice of initial and secondary tasks can influence whether an effect is observed: tasks that tap similar cognitive resources or that are more challenging may be more likely to produce measurable decrements after an initial regulatory demand. The anagram task, while effortful, may not have been sufficiently demanding or aligned with the processes taxed by suppression.

The timing of the delay intervals also deserves attention. We selected immediate, 5-minute, and 15-minute intervals to capture potential rapid recovery dynamics, but it is possible that recovery processes operate on different timescales or are contingent on intervening activities. For example, short breaks accompanied by positive mood induction or glucose intake have been reported to restore performance in some studies, raising the possibility that spontaneous recovery without an active restoration intervention may be slow or absent. The absence of a recovery effect here does not rule out the possibility that active restoration mechanisms could produce recovery; rather, it suggests that passive time alone did not suffice to restore performance in this context.

The present findings are consistent with recent critical appraisals of the strength model that argue for a more nuanced view of self-control. Specifically, the resource metaphor may capture some intuitive aspects of regulatory failure but may not be the best explanatory model for all observed phenomena. Integrative accounts that incorporate motivational, attentional, and schema-based processes may provide a richer framework. For instance, a hybrid model could posit that initial exertion produces both a subjective cost that reduces motivation and transient shifts in cognitive control processes, with the relative contribution of each mechanism depending on task characteristics, individual differences, and contextual cues. Under such a model, some instances of apparent depletion might be better explained by momentary motivational shifts, whereas others—particularly those involving prolonged or repeated regulation—might involve more durable cognitive fatigue.

Policy implications and practical considerations follow from theoretical conclusions. If depletion is not primarily a matter of an exhaustible resource, interventions aimed at bolstering motivation, reframing tasks to increase perceived value, and training self-control strategies may be as or more effective than attempts to replenish a putative resource. Conversely, if some forms of regulation do produce meaningful capacity reduction, then rest, recovery activities, and energy-restoring interventions may be appropriate in particular contexts such as endurance tasks or prolonged occupational demands. The current study suggests caution in generalizing the notion of a universal, depletable resource across different regulatory domains and durations.

Future research should address several open questions raised by the present work. First, larger registered replication studies that systematically vary initial task strength, secondary task type, and delay duration are needed to map the boundary conditions of depletion effects. Second, experimental manipulations that directly test motivational versus resource-based accounts—such as adding incentives or manipulating beliefs about willpower—would help adjudicate between alternative explanations. Third, within-subjects designs that assess individual differences in susceptibility to depletion and recovery could illuminate whether certain people are consistently more vulnerable to regulatory failure. Fourth, multimethod approaches incorporating physiological measures (e.g., heart rate variability, glucose levels, neuroimaging indices) along with behavioural performance could test whether any measurable metabolic or neural signatures accompany changes in self-control performance. Fifth, intervention studies that evaluate training programs aimed at bolstering self-control skills over time could clarify the extent to which self-control operates like a skill that can be strengthened, as opposed to a transiently expended resource.

The current study also highlights the importance of task selection and ecological validity. Laboratory tasks such as film-induced suppression and anagram solving provide experimental control but differ from many real-world instances of self-control that involve social, emotional, and temporal complexity. Field studies and ecological momentary assessment approaches could complement laboratory experiments by examining how self-control processes unfold in daily life and how people recover from regulatory challenges across naturalistic contexts.

Limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The opportunistic sample may not generalize broadly to other populations, and the modest sample size limited the ability to detect small effects. The reliance on self-report for some measures introduces potential biases, although the video coding of expressivity provided an objective manipulation check. The absence of a preregistered analysis plan is another limitation, and future studies would benefit from pre-specification of hypotheses and analytical procedures.

In conclusion, this study failed to find evidence that expressive suppression impairs subsequent anagram performance or that such performance recovers over short delays in a manner consistent with an energy depletion-and-recovery model. These findings suggest that the strength model’s resource metaphor may not fully account for all instances of self-control failure and that alternative explanations involving motivation, attentional processes, and skill-like aspects of self-control merit further consideration. Rather than viewing the strength model as wholly incorrect, it may be more productive to integrate its insights with complementary frameworks to build a more comprehensive account of how people exert and sustain control. Future research with larger samples, diverse tasks, and multimethod measurement will be essential to refine theoretical models and to inform interventions aimed at promoting effective self-regulation in everyday life.
