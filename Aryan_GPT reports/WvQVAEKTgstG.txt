Introduction

The capacity of humans to perceive and respond to social information is deeply intertwined with bodily and sensory processes. Research in social cognition increasingly suggests that abstract social states, such as feelings of inclusion or exclusion, are linked to sensory experiences and physical metaphors. One prominent strand of work proposes that social warmth and physical warmth are connected in cognition and perception. From everyday language to experimental manipulations, there is a consistent cultural tendency to equate social closeness with physical warmth and social distance with physical coldness. This association has been used to argue that social experiences can shape basic perceptual judgments about the physical environment. A notable experimental claim in this literature is that experiences of social exclusion can make people literally feel colder or perceive their environments as colder. This claim, if robust, highlights a striking example of embodied cognition in which social-affective states influence low-level perceptual judgments about temperature. The idea has theoretical appeal because it suggests that social and physical regulation systems might overlap and that social isolation could trigger thermoregulatory-like perceptions or behaviors. If people who feel excluded also experience the world as physically colder, that would provide convergent evidence that social and physical homeostasis interact. Early empirical work has offered support for this link between social exclusion and perceived cold. Some experiments asked participants to recall experiences of social exclusion and then to estimate the ambient temperature, choose between warm and cold beverages, or select warm clothing, with findings reported in support of the social-thermoregulation hypothesis. These findings captured attention because they provide a concrete, measurable outcome—temperature perception—that can be tied to an abstract social manipulation. The reported effects have been cited as evidence that exclusion produces an embodied sense of cold that extends beyond metaphor. However, the literature includes variability in methods, manipulations, and measures, and the broader set of findings has not been uniformly consistent across contexts and operationalizations. Indeed, some studies used recall manipulations while others employed more interactive exclusion paradigms, and some measured self-reports whereas others measured behavioral choices. The variability in methods raises important questions about the robustness and boundary conditions of the social exclusion–temperature effect. Another crucial consideration is the role of individual differences as potential moderators of the effect. Personality traits such as extraversion might alter the strength or direction of the relation between social exclusion and perceived temperature. Extraversion is linked to social approach tendencies, reward sensitivity, and greater baseline levels of social interaction, and these characteristics could plausibly influence how exclusion is experienced and how it translates into sensory perceptions. On the one hand, highly extraverted individuals may have greater social resources and networks that buffer them against the impact of momentary social exclusion, potentially limiting the translation of exclusion into a sense of physical cold. On the other hand, extraverts may be more attuned to social signals and more sensitive to losses of affiliation, and thus they might experience stronger embodied responses—including alterations in temperature perception—when excluded. Both patterns are logically coherent, creating ambiguity about the role of extraversion in modulating exclusion-related perceptual changes. Given the theoretical importance of demonstrating embodied links between social and physical domains, understanding whether and for whom exclusion influences temperature perception is a pressing empirical question. Replication and extension of prior effects with careful consideration of moderators are essential for clarifying the reliability and generality of the social-thermoregulation hypothesis. Experimental work that both attempts to reproduce earlier findings and examines potential personality moderators can help map the boundaries of social–physical interactions. In addition, methodological differences between studies—such as whether exclusion is delivered via a recall task or via interactive ostracism—may affect the ecological validity and strength of manipulations, and thus contribute to heterogeneity in observed effects. A replication attempt using a recall manipulation permits a direct test of the original procedural logic while also offering experimental control. At the same time, adding personality measures in the same design allows for tests of moderation that can illuminate whether certain individuals are more likely to translate social experiences into physical perceptions. Evaluating these possibilities requires a design with sufficient power, reliable measures of extraversion, and validated manipulation checks that assess subjective feelings of inclusion and exclusion. It also requires outcome measures that tap the central perceptual claim, namely that exclusion shifts ambient temperature judgments or related temperature-relevant behaviors. Prior literature also raises questions about the magnitude of the reported effects and the sensitivity of temperature self-reports to manipulation. The perceptual judgments used in earlier studies typically rely on self-report scales or single-item temperature estimates, and those measures may have ceiling or floor constraints or substantial measurement noise. Some researchers have suggested including both direct perceptual estimates and behavioral proxies such as beverage choice to triangulate the phenomenon. Another methodological consideration is the timing of measurement following the exclusion manipulation, as immediate and delayed effects may differ. Theoretical accounts propose that if exclusion taps into an evolved thermoregulatory system, then the effect on perceived temperature should be robust and possibly accompanied by behavioral attempts to obtain warmth. If instead the effect is metaphorical or relies on higher-level cognitive associations, it may be more fragile and sensitive to contextual cues. Given these theoretical distinctions, it is valuable to probe whether the effect emerges in a relatively straightforward recall-based manipulation and whether it interacts with stable personality differences such as extraversion. Taking these considerations together, the present study sought to replicate an influential finding that a recall-based social exclusion manipulation reduces perceived ambient temperature. At the same time, the present investigation expanded the original design by assessing participants' level of extraversion to test competing hypotheses about whether extraversion amplifies or attenuates the exclusion–temperature link. If extraversion buffers against the subjective cold associated with exclusion, then individuals higher in extraversion should display a weaker relation between exclusion and perceived temperature relative to those lower in extraversion. Conversely, if extraversion reflects greater social sensitivity and approach motivations that increase the distress of exclusion, then higher extraversion could correspond to a stronger exclusion-related decrease in perceived temperature. The study was preregistered with respect to primary hypotheses and analytic strategy, and the design emphasized adequate power to detect effects of the magnitude commonly reported in the earlier literature. Participants were randomly assigned to recall an experience of social inclusion or exclusion and then completed measures of perceived ambient temperature, mood, and a manipulation check assessing subjective experience of exclusion. Extraversion was assessed using a validated self-report inventory, and analyses focused on both main effects of the manipulation and the interaction between condition and extraversion. Supplementary exploratory analyses examined whether mood or demographic variables accounted for any observed associations. The goals were twofold: to provide a direct empirical test of the prior claim that exclusion lowers perceived ambient temperature and to establish whether extraversion serves as an important moderator of any effect. By reporting null findings as well as positive ones, the study aims to contribute to an accurate cumulative literature on embodied social cognition, to identify boundary conditions of the social thermoregulation hypothesis, and to guide future research on when social experiences alter sensory perception. This approach is consistent with calls within psychological science for careful replication and for the examination of individual differences that might explain variability across studies. In sum, the theoretical stakes of detecting a link between social exclusion and perceived cold are high, and the present study endeavors to assess the robustness of that link while probing for personality-based moderation that could reconcile competing theoretical accounts. The subsequent sections provide a detailed account of the methods, analytic plan, results, and interpretation of findings in light of broader theoretical and methodological issues.

Methods

Participants were recruited from a university participant pool and from an online research panel to increase diversity of the sample. A total of 240 participants completed the study and were included in analyses after passing attention checks and meeting inclusion criteria. The sample size of 240 was selected on the basis of an a priori power analysis that aimed to provide at least 80% power to detect a small-to-moderate effect size (Cohen's d = 0.35) for the primary condition effect using a two-tailed test and alpha = .05. Participants ranged in age from 18 to 45 years, with a mean age of 20.9 years and a standard deviation of 3.8 years. The sample included 142 women, 96 men, and 2 participants who selected another gender option. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the university institutional review board, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study commencement. The experiment used a between-subjects manipulation with two levels: social inclusion recall and social exclusion recall. Assignment to condition was random and implemented via the online survey platform's randomization feature. Participants completed the study in a quiet laboratory room or remotely in a private setting, depending on recruitment source, and they were asked to avoid extreme temperature environments for 15 minutes prior to starting. The key manipulation consisted of a writing task adapted from prior exclusion paradigms. In the exclusion condition, participants were instructed to write for 7 minutes about a personal experience in which they had felt intensely excluded or left out by others. Instructions prompted them to describe the event in detail and to recount how they felt at the time. In the inclusion condition, participants were instructed to write for 7 minutes about a personal experience when they felt included, accepted, and connected to others. Writing time was held constant across conditions and participants were unable to proceed until the minute requirement elapsed. Immediately after the writing task, participants completed a manipulation check that asked them to rate how excluded or included they felt when recalling the experience on a 1 to 9 scale, where higher numbers indicated greater feelings of exclusion. The manipulation check consisted of two items averaged into a single score: a direct exclusion-inclusion item and a related item assessing the sense of belonging during the remembered event. Perceived ambient temperature was assessed using two approaches to capture converging evidence. The primary measure was a self-report estimate of the current room temperature in degrees Fahrenheit, where participants provided a numeric estimate. The secondary measure was a subjective rating on a 1 to 7 scale of perceived warmth of the environment, with 1 labeled "very cold" and 7 labeled "very warm." The two temperature measures were used as co-primary indicators of perceived ambient temperature and were moderately correlated. Beyond subjective estimates, we recorded the objective ambient temperature in the laboratory rooms using a calibrated digital thermometer for in-lab participants. For remote participants, we asked whether they were indoors and requested that they report the ambient temperature if they had access to a thermometer, but these objective measures were treated as auxiliary and were not available for all participants. Extraversion was measured using the extraversion subscale of the Big Five Inventory-44 (BFI-44), which consists of eight items scored on a 1 to 5 Likert scale. Items were reverse-scored when required and averaged such that higher scores reflected higher trait extraversion. The extraversion scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency in the present sample (Cronbach's alpha = .82). Participants also completed a brief mood assessment using the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) short form to capture current affective state, given that mood could covary with perceived temperature judgments. Demographic questions and debriefing items were presented at the end of the study. The analytic plan was specified prior to data collection. Primary analyses tested the main effect of condition (exclusion vs inclusion) on the co-primary temperature measures using independent-samples t-tests and linear regression models controlling for recruitment source (lab vs online) and objective room temperature where available. Secondary analyses tested the moderation hypothesis by examining the condition × extraversion interaction in linear regression models predicting perceived temperature. Extraversion was mean-centered for interaction tests. Additional exploratory analyses included testing whether mood or gender moderated or mediated the effects, testing for correlations among variables, and computing Bayes factors to quantify evidence for the null versus alternative hypotheses. Assumptions of statistical tests were evaluated via inspection of residuals and tests of homogeneity of variance. Nonparametric tests were planned as robustness checks if assumptions were violated. Manipulation checks were analyzed with independent-samples t-tests to confirm that the recall manipulation successfully elicited differential feelings of inclusion versus exclusion. Data quality checks included attention checks embedded in the survey and minimum word-count thresholds for the writing task; participants failing these checks were excluded prior to analyses. Analyses were conducted in R and in SPSS to ensure reproducibility. The reporting of results follows conventional thresholds for statistical significance (p < .05) while also emphasizing effect sizes and confidence intervals. In addition to traditional null-hypothesis significance testing, two one-sided tests (TOST) equivalence procedures were planned to evaluate whether observed effects were statistically equivalent to a negligible effect size region defined a priori (Cohen's d between -0.30 and +0.30). The study also preregistered that any unexpected deviations from the analytic plan or additional data exclusions would be documented and justified in the methods and results sections. Overall, the methods aimed to replicate the prior experimental logic while adding a trait-level moderator and multiple measures of perceived temperature with adequate power to detect effects of practical interest.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the primary variables are presented first. The mean perceived temperature estimate in the inclusion condition was 72.6 degrees Fahrenheit with a standard deviation of 6.2 degrees, and the mean perceived temperature estimate in the exclusion condition was 71.9 degrees with a standard deviation of 6.8 degrees. On the subjective warmth rating, the inclusion condition had a mean of 4.78 (SD = 1.18) and the exclusion condition had a mean of 4.63 (SD = 1.29). The extraversion scale had a sample mean of 3.22 (SD = 0.78) on the 1–5 scale. The manipulation check indicated that participants in the exclusion condition reported substantially higher feelings of exclusion (M = 7.12, SD = 1.32) compared to those in the inclusion condition (M = 2.08, SD = 1.01), demonstrating that the recall task produced the intended subjective states. The manipulation check difference was statistically significant, t(238) = 38.2, p < .001, with a very large effect size (Cohen's d > 4), confirming that the writing task elicited the expected recall-based feelings. The main test of the primary hypothesis compared the two experimental conditions on the perceived temperature estimates. An independent-samples t-test comparing numeric temperature estimates by condition indicated no significant difference, t(238) = 0.93, p = .352, with a mean difference of 0.7 degrees Fahrenheit (95% CI = -0.76 to 2.16). The corresponding Cohen's d for the condition effect on numeric temperature estimates was 0.12, indicating a very small effect size. For the subjective warmth rating, an independent-samples t-test likewise revealed a non-significant condition effect, t(238) = 1.16, p = .247, with a mean difference of 0.15 scale points (95% CI = -0.12 to 0.42) and Cohen's d = 0.15. These results indicate that the exclusion manipulation did not produce a reliable decrease in perceived ambient temperature on either the numeric or subjective warmth measures. A regression model predicting the numeric temperature estimate included condition (coded 0 = inclusion, 1 = exclusion), recruitment source (0 = lab, 1 = online), and objective room temperature for lab participants as covariates. In this model, the effect of condition was non-significant, b = -0.68, SE = 0.73, t = -0.93, p = .353, and objective room temperature was a strong predictor as expected, b = 0.92, SE = 0.08, t = 11.5, p < .001. The second regression model predicting the subjective warmth rating again showed no significant main effect of condition, b = -0.14, SE = 0.12, t = -1.16, p = .247. The planned moderation analysis tested the interaction between condition and extraversion in predicting perceived temperature. The interaction term was not significant for the numeric temperature estimate, b = -0.22, SE = 0.32, t = -0.69, p = .491, and it was not significant for the subjective warmth rating, b = -0.03, SE = 0.05, t = -0.60, p = .549. Simple slopes analyses at ±1 SD of extraversion revealed no reliable condition differences at high extraversion nor at low extraversion on either outcome. Specifically, at high extraversion (+1 SD), inclusion had a mean subjective warmth of 4.76 and exclusion had 4.61, difference = 0.15, p = .31; at low extraversion (-1 SD), inclusion had 4.80 and exclusion had 4.64, difference = 0.16, p = .28. Correlational analyses showed that the manipulation check (self-reported exclusion) correlated weakly and non-significantly with numeric perceived temperature (r = -0.05, p = .454) and with subjective warmth rating (r = -0.06, p = .357), suggesting little linear association between felt exclusion intensity and temperature judgments across individuals. Positive and negative affect scores from the PANAS were included in supplementary models. Negative affect was slightly higher in the exclusion condition, as expected, but when included as a covariate the condition effect on perceived temperature remained non-significant, and negative affect itself was not a significant predictor of temperature judgments. Gender was examined as another potential moderator, but neither gender nor the condition × gender interaction predicted perceived temperature outcomes. Bayesian analyses were conducted to quantify evidence for the null hypothesis. For the subjective warmth rating, a Bayes factor comparing the null model to a model including condition returned BF01 = 4.55, indicating moderate evidence in favor of the null over the condition effect. For the numeric temperature estimate, BF01 = 3.89, likewise suggesting moderate evidence in support of the null. The moderation models produced Bayes factors that did not favor the inclusion of the interaction term; BF01 values were above 3 for both outcomes. To further assess the practical absence of an effect, equivalence testing using two one-sided tests (TOST) was performed with equivalence bounds set to Cohen's d = ±0.30. The TOST for the subjective warmth rating rejected the null of non-equivalence, t = 2.15, p = .016 for the lower bound and t = -2.02, p = .022 for the upper bound, indicating statistical equivalence to a negligible effect within the preset bounds. The TOST for the numeric temperature estimate yielded similar results and supported equivalence within the specified bounds. Additional exploratory analyses examined whether objective room temperature moderated any effects in the subset of lab participants with thermometer data, but no significant moderation emerged. Analyses restricted to only lab participants produced similarly null results for the main effect and interaction, suggesting that the inclusion of online participants did not obscure a lab-specific effect. Inspection of residuals, tests of homogeneity, and sensitivity analyses using nonparametric Mann-Whitney U tests did not alter the pattern of non-significant findings. Finally, the writing samples were examined for word count and emotional language as potential predictors of perceived temperature, but neither writing length nor the proportion of negative-affect words predicted temperature ratings above and beyond condition. The overall pattern of results thus indicates that while the recall manipulation successfully altered subjective feelings of exclusion, it did not produce reliable changes in perceived ambient temperature, and extraversion did not moderate the (non-existent) effect.

Discussion

The present study set out to replicate a previously reported effect that social exclusion reduces perceived ambient temperature and to test whether extraversion moderates that effect. The manipulation was successful in altering participants' subjective recollections, as those instructed to recall exclusion reported substantially greater feelings of exclusion than those instructed to recall inclusion. Despite this manipulation check success, the key outcome measures—both a numeric estimate of room temperature and a subjective warmth rating—showed no significant differences between exclusion and inclusion conditions. Equivalence testing and Bayesian analyses provided moderate evidence in support of the null hypothesis for the anticipated effect size range. Additionally, extraversion did not moderate the relation between condition and perceived temperature in any consistent way. The combination of a successful manipulation check and null findings on the primary perceptual outcomes invites careful interpretation about what these results mean for the broader claim that social exclusion induces a literal sense of cold. One interpretation is that the phenomenon, if it exists at all, may be smaller and more context-dependent than earlier reports suggested. The present study was designed to be adequately powered to detect effects in the small-to-moderate range, and our equivalence tests suggest that any true effect is likely to be smaller than the bounds we deemed theoretically meaningful. This interpretation aligns with a view that embodied metaphors linking social warmth to physical warmth may not consistently produce shifts in low-level sensory perception under all operationalizations. Instead, the social–physical warmth link might manifest more reliably in certain behavioral choices—such as reaching for a warm drink—or under more intense or interactive exclusion experiences than a recall task provides. A second interpretation is that methodological nuances matter greatly for eliciting perceptual changes. Prior studies that reported inclusion-exclusion differences in temperature perception sometimes used different manipulations, such as real-time exclusion in interactive tasks (e.g., ostracism through ball-tossing paradigms), or had participants make decisions with explicit warmth cues like beverage selection. The recall paradigm used here is psychologically potent for eliciting subjective feelings but may not robustly activate embodied homeostatic or thermoregulatory processes in the same way interactive real-time exclusion might. In other words, the cognitive reconstruction of an old exclusion experience might create a strong affective response without engaging perceptual systems tied to bodily states. It is also possible that the timing between the recall task and the temperature judgement matters; immediate physiological responses in some paradigms might dissipate quickly or may not be recruited by recall. Third, individual differences in extraversion did not serve as a moderator in the anticipated ways. Theoretical arguments could have been made for either direction of moderation: extraversion as buffer due to greater social resources or extraversion as amplifier due to higher social sensitivity. The absence of moderation suggests that within the measured range of extraversion in our sample, trait-level social approach tendencies do not substantially influence whether a recall-based exclusion experience translates into altered ambient temperature judgments. This finding narrows the scope of plausible boundary conditions and suggests that extraversion alone is unlikely to explain inconsistencies in prior work. Another important consideration is measurement specificity and reliability. Temperature judgments obtained via self-report are subject to individual differences in calibration and expectations, and numeric estimates may be influenced by participants' general knowledge about typical room temperatures rather than momentary perceptual experience. Although we included both numeric and subjective scale measures to address this concern, both are still self-report and may lack sensitivity to subtle changes in bodily temperature or thermosensory perception. Objective thermoregulatory measures, such as skin temperature, thermoregulatory behaviors tracked in controlled settings, or physiological markers, could provide a more direct test of the embodied hypothesis. Incorporating such measures in future research would help clarify whether social exclusion has somatic consequences that are not captured by conscious temperature estimates. The sample and recruitment strategy also warrant consideration. Although we included both lab and online participants and conducted analyses restricted to lab participants, it remains possible that contextual factors like the actual ambient temperature or participants' recent history of social interactions could influence susceptibility to exclusion effects. We controlled for objective room temperature where possible and found it to be a strong predictor of numeric temperature estimates, as expected, but it did not interact with condition to produce the hypothesized effect. It is also worth noting that most participants were young adults from a university context, and cultural or age-related differences in the social–physical metaphor may limit generalizability. A further implication concerns the nature of the embodied-cognition hypothesis itself. Theoretical accounts vary on whether social–physical analogies are grounded in sensorimotor processes or are primarily at the level of conceptual metaphors. If the latter, then shifts in language and conceptual associations might not necessarily translate into perceptual changes under all circumstances. Our null results could therefore be read as evidence that the metaphorical overlap between social and physical warmth does not automatically produce perceptual shifts in ambient temperature judgments following a recall task. The study also contributes to methodological discourse in psychological science about replication and effect size estimation. The present null results underscore the need for larger, well-powered replication attempts that test boundary conditions and explore mediators and moderators comprehensively. The favorable manipulation check demonstrates the value of verifying that experimental procedures indeed induce the intended psychological state, and the subsequent null effects show that successful manipulations do not guarantee corresponding effects on all hypothesized outcomes. This nuance is important for theory development and for the design of future studies that seek to link social states with perceptual or physiological processes. Several limitations temper strong conclusions from the present work. First, although our power and equivalence testing suggest that large effects are unlikely, the possibility of very small effects remains. Detecting such effects would require substantially larger sample sizes than used here, and the practical importance of extremely small effects is debatable. Second, the reliance on self-report temperature measures, while common in the literature, may not capture the full range of embodied responses; future studies should integrate objective physiological measures. Third, the recall manipulation, while ecologically valid in eliciting memories, may differ in key ways from live social exclusion; thus, direct comparisons between different exclusion paradigms would help clarify which forms of exclusion, if any, produce embodied thermal responses. Fourth, other personality or situational moderators not measured here—such as attachment style, social anxiety, baseline social connectedness, or cultural background—could shape the effect and merit investigation. Building on these results, several avenues for future research are recommended. First, a multisite replication that includes both recall and interactive exclusion paradigms and that combines self-report, behavioral choices (e.g., beverage selection), and physiological measures would provide a comprehensive test of the embodied social–temperature hypothesis. Second, pre-registered moderation analyses involving a broader set of individual differences could identify who is most susceptible to any effect and under what conditions. Third, longitudinal designs examining whether chronic loneliness or long-term social isolation influences resting thermoregulation or temperature perception would speak to the ecological and health-related implications of the theory. Fourth, experimental manipulations that vary the intensity and social relevance of exclusion—such as exclusion by close vs. unfamiliar others—could test whether social proximity affects embodiment. Fifth, neuroimaging or psychophysiological studies could investigate whether overlap in neural systems for social and thermosensory processing accounts for any observed associations. In conclusion, the current study did not replicate the reported effect that recall-based social exclusion reduces perceived ambient temperature, and extraversion did not moderate this non-effect. The results suggest that the relationship between social exclusion and temperature perception is not straightforward and may depend on specific methodological features, situational contexts, or other individual differences. Rather than refuting the broader idea that social and physical warmth are conceptually linked, these findings indicate that embodied influences of social states on basic sensory judgments are likely more circumscribed than some prior reports implied. Future work using diverse methodologies and larger samples will be necessary to map the conditions under which social experiences influence perceptions of the physical environment.
