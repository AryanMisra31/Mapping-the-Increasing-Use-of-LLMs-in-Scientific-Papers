Introduction
Attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) affects around 5% of children and adults
worldwide (Polanczyk et al., 2015; Simon et al., 2009) and is characterised by inattention,
impulsivity and hyperactivity (American Psychiatric Association, 2000). Having ADHD
incurs greater risk of learning, behavioural and emotional problems, poorer educational
outcomes, lower occupational status and relationship problems (Faraone et al., 2000).
Psychostimulants (e.g. amphetamine and methylphenidate) are the most effective treatments available for all ages and the first line treatment for adults. They reduce symptoms
in around 80% of patients (Barkley et al., 1991; Dittmann et al., 2014; Milich et al., 2001).
However, whilst they can be effective, many experience residual symptoms (Dittmann
et al., 2014) or significant side effects (Mariani et al., 2007), and there are concerns that
the drugs may be abused (Darredeau et al., 2007; Laviola et al., 1999; Schenk & Davidson, 1998). Non-stimulant treatments exist, but the response rate is lower (Dittmann et al.,
2014), and many still experience side effects (Mariani et al., 2007).
Given the limitations of the available medications, other therapeutic approaches are
being considered. Exercise has been shown to improve symptoms and cognitive functioning in children with ADHD (Chang et al., 2012; Jensen & Kenny, 2004; Piepmeier et al.,
2015) and may also be beneficial for adults (Abramovitch et al., 2013). Exercise within the
context of ADHD has been the focus of several systematic reviews and meta-analyses in
recent years, all of which have found exercise to offer some functional improvement (Cerrillo-Urbina et al., 2015; Neudecker et al., 2019; Suarez-Manzano et al., 2018; Vysniauske
et al., 2016). The exact mechanism by which exercise might alleviate ADHD symptoms
is unknown, but both exercise and psychostimulants act on dopamine and BDNF suggesting there may be shared mechanisms (Archer & Kostrzewa, 2012; Ji et al., 2014; Kim
et al., 2011; Kuczenski & Segal, 1997, 2002; Powers, 2000). As indicated above, one of
the concerns about psychostimulant medication is its risk of abuse. Indeed ADHD is often

Whilst the concept of exercise addiction is controversial and it is not classified as a disorder in the DSM or ICD, it has been argued to be an addiction (Berczik et al., 2012) which
means that it is important to consider whether it is safe and appropriate for people with
ADHD. No research to date has examined whether there is an association between exercise addiction and ADHD (Karaca et al., 2017), despite associations with other behavioural
addictions (Starcevic & Khazaal, 2017). The purpose of this study is to (1) assess whether
those with ADHD exercise differently to those without, (2) examine whether exercise patterns relate to symptoms and treatment, and (3) establish whether problematic exercise is
more common in those with ADHD. Specifically we hypothesized that (a) there would
be differences in exercise habits between those with ADHD and without, which would
be impacted by symptoms and treatment status and (b) those with ADHD would be more
likely to exhibit problematic exercise.

Methods
Participants
The study included participants with and without ADHD. The inclusion criteria for all participants were that they must be residing in the UK and aged 16–55 years old. Those with
ADHD were also required to have a formal diagnosis of ADHD from a healthcare professional and score on Adult ADHD Self- Report Scale screener (ASRS-1.1) of 13 or more,
whilst those without ADHD had to have a score of 14 or less (Kessler et al., 2005, 2007)
(see below for scale details). If participants were taking medication for ADHD, they were
required to have an adherence of 70% or more (see the “Measures” section). There were no
exclusion criteria. All participants were recruited through advertisements on social media
and the university’s website. Interested participants were directed to an online information
sheet and consent form from which, if they gave consent, they could access a fully anonymous online survey, hosted in Qualtrics, which took approximately 20 min to complete.
Those completing the survey had the option of entering an email address (stored separately
to data) to be entered into a prize draw for shopping vouchers.

Measures
The survey first assessed demographic variables. Specifically, participants were asked to
indicate (i) gender, choosing from ‘male’, ‘female’, ‘I use another term’ with text entry for
details and ‘prefer not to say’; (ii) ethnicity, choosing from 14 different options in addition to options of ‘other’ and ‘prefer not to say’; (iii) age; (iv) education level by indicating the number of years in post-compulsory education, with an example given to assist
participants, (v) employment status selecting from ‘full time’, ‘part time’, ‘education’ or
‘other’, and (vi) disability status indicating whether they have any physical, learning, mental, sensory or other disabilities excluding ADHD. Participants were then asked to indicate whether they had a formal diagnosis of ADHD. Participants reporting a diagnosis of
ADHD detailed medication and adherence by indicating the number of tablets prescribed
and taken in the last 14 days from which a percentage adherence could be calculated

(Safren et al., 2007). The remaining sections of the survey consisted of the following standardised and well-validated measures.

Adult ADHD Self‑Report Scale (ASRS v1.1)
This scale, developed with the World Health Organisation, consists of the first six items
of the ADHD ASRS Symptom Checklist. It is a well-validated 6-item measure of ADHD
traits, which has been found to be useful in identifying ADHD in adults, having a positive
predictive value of 0.94 and a negative predictive value 0.24 (Kessler et al., 2005, 2007).
Critically for the current study, which included participants without ADHD, this scale has
also been shown to be reliable in individuals without ADHD (Silverstein et al., 2018) The
six items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never, 4 = very often), such that a higher
score indicates greater ADHD traits. This scoring gives a maximum score of 24, with a
cut-off score of 12 for clinical populations and 14 for the general population. Cronbach’s
alpha revealed very good internal consistency for the full six items (α = .88), the inattention trait score (ITS, items 1–4, α = .87) and the hyperactivity-trait score (HTS items 5–6,
α = .75).

Godin Leisure‑Time Exercise Questionnaire (GL‑TEQ)
To assess typical activity levels in all participants, the Godin Leisure-Time Exercise
Questionnaire (GL-TEQ) (Godin, 2011) was used. This requires participants to indicate
how much they engaged in strenuous, moderate and mild exercise over a normal 7-day
period. Examples of each type of exercise are provided as part of the standard question
wording. Participants are required to indicate the number of 15-min periods of exercise for
each intensity level they complete within a typical 7-day period. For example, if someone
typically runs for 60 min per week, they indicate 4 in the strenuous exercise question. A
‘leisure score index’ is calculated by summing the weighted values ((9 × Strenuous) + (5
× Moderate) + (3 × Light)), such that a higher score indicates greater levels of activity
and participants are categorised as ‘active’ (LSI ≥ 24) or ‘insufficiently active’ (LSI ≤ 23)
based on this index (Godin, 2011).

Obligatory Exercise Questionnaire (OEQ)
The Obligatory Exercise Questionnaire (OEQ) was used to measure participants attitudes
and activities related to exercise (Thompson & Pasman, 1991). This scale consists of 20
items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = never, 4 = always). All items are worded such
that higher scores indicated a stronger sense of obligation except two items which are
reverse scored to achieve this. The items can be summed to give an overall score (Steffen
& Brehm, 1999) or considered in terms of three subscales, consisting of 11 items measuring fixation (e.g. ‘Sometimes, I find that my mind wanders to thoughts about exercising’),
frequency (e.g. ‘I exercise more than three days per week’) and commitment (Ackard et al.,
2002). Cronbach’s alpha revealed good internal consistency for the total OEQ (α = .87)
and two subscales (α = .70 for 5-item fixation subscale, α = .85 for 3-item frequency subscale). We did not use the 3-item commitment (e.g. “I may miss a day of exercise for no
good reason” reverse-scored) subscale due to poor internal consistency (α = .40). Scores
on the OEQ can be used to categorise obligatory exercisers, where a score of 50 or greater

is used as a cut-off for this, representing the halfway point between the minimum (20) and
maximum (100) score (Pasman & Thompson, 1988).

Exercise Dependence Scale (EDS)
Exercise addiction was measured using two scales, the first of which was the Exercise
Dependence Scale (EDS). This scale operationalises exercise dependence, aligning with
the DSM-5 criteria (Hausenblas & Downs, 2001). It consists of 21 items rated on a 6-point
Likert scale (1 = never, 6 = always). These 21 items can be summed and considered as a
whole or divided into seven subscales corresponding to tolerance, withdrawal, intention
effect (exercising more than intended), lack of control, time spent exercising, reduction in
other activities and continuance despite problems. Higher scores indicate greater exercise
dependency, and there are also established cut-off criteria to distinguish individuals who
are deemed at risk of exercise dependence, symptomatic non-dependent or asymptomatic
non-dependent. To be considered at risk individuals must score in the at-risk range (i.e.,
an average score of 5–6) on three of the seven factors. A score of 3–4 on average on a
minimum of three factors (or 5–6 on some factors but fail to meet the criteria for the at-risk
group) results in a categorisation of symptomatic non-dependent, and an average 1 to 2 on
at least three factors results in asymptomatic non-dependent (Lindwall & Palmeira, 2009).
Cronbach’s alpha showed good internal consistency for whole scale (α = .95) and all subscales (α > .75).

Exercise Addiction Inventory (EAI)
Secondly, the Exercise Addiction Inventory (EAI) was used because it has excellent concurrent validity when used alongside the OEQ and the EDS (Terry, Szabo, & Griffiths,
2004). This consists of 6 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5
= strongly agree), where a higher score indicates greater risk of addiction. With a maximum score of 30, the cut-off score for participants considered “at-risk” is a score of 24
and above, which represents individuals with scores in the top 15% of the total scale score
(Terry et al., 2004). Cronbach’s alpha revealed good internal consistency (α = .80).

Data processing and analysis
Prior to analysis, participants were excluded if they (a) did not meet the inclusion criteria
(n = 4), (b) did not complete the survey (n = 136), (c) reported no ADHD diagnosis but
had an ASRS score >12 indicative of ADHD (n = 78) or reported a diagnosis but had an
ASRS < 14 (range 0–12; n = 7) and (d) reported taking medication for ADHD but had low
(< 70%) adherence (n = 23), reported taking too much medication (n = 3), did not provide
adherence information (n = 6) or was taking an antidepressant for ADHD (n = 1). The final
sample size was 237, which was divided into three participant groups (healthy controls,
ADHD on medication, ADHD not on medication). The demographic characteristics of the
three participant groups were compared using chi-square for categorical variables (gender, ethnicity, employment, disability) and one-way ANOVA (with ηp2) with Bonferroni
adjustment for multiple comparisons for three group comparisons and independent t tests
(with Cohen’s d) for two group comparisons of continuous variables (age, education). Similarly, exercise habits were compared between groups using chi-square for the categorical

GL-TEQ LSI and one-way ANOVA for the continuous variables resulting from the OEQ.
To examine the relationship between ADHD traits, as measured by the ASRS 1.1 and exercise habits, comparisons of ASRS scores for the total ASRS 1.1, inattention trait (ITS)
and hyperactivity impulsivity trait (HTS) were made between the active and insufficiently

Results
Sample Characteristics
Participants were divided into three groups (Table 1): healthy controls (HC, n = 125),
ADHD/medicated (ADHD-M, n = 70) and ADHD/unmedicated (ADHD-U, n = 42).
Within the ADHD-M group, 90.1% reported taking stimulants, 8.5% reported taking nonstimulants and 1.4% reported taking a combination. ASRS scores differed significantly
between groups (Table 2).

Exercise in ADHD and Healthy Controls
GL-TEQ responses demonstrated that participants were primarily active, and levels of
activity did not differ between groups (HC, n = 104, 83.2% active; ADHD-M, n = 56,
81.2% active; ADHD-U, n = 33, 78.6% active; χ2 (2) = .477, p = .788). There were no differences between groups for total OEQ scores (HC, M = 42.74, SD = 9.91; ADHD-M, M
= 44.13, SD = 10.46; ADHD-U, M = 44.13, SD = 9.04; F (2, 222) = .546, p = .546, ηp2
= .005) or the frequency subscale (HC M = 8.00, SD = 2.59; ADHD-M, M = 7.86, SD =
2.91; ADHD-U M = 8.34, SD = 2.55; F (2, 232) = .426, p = .654, ηp2 = .004). However,
there was a main effect of group on exercise fixation (HC, M = 9.59, SD = 2.97; ADHDM, M = 11.22, SD = 3.03; ADHD-U, M = 10.63, SD = 2.89; F (2,230) = 7.134, p = .001,
ηp2 = .058) where ADHD-M displayed higher scores than HC (1.64, 95% CI [.563, 2.714],
p = .001). There were no other group differences.

Exercise Patterns, Symptoms, and Treatment
ADHD Traits and Exercise Levels
Healthy Controls Those rated by the GL-TEQ as insufficiently active scored significantly
higher on the ASRS V1.1 (M = 10.57, SD = 2.50) than those rated as active (M = 8.67, SD
= 3.33), t(123) = 2.475, p = .015, d = 0.65, 95% CI [.380, 3.416]. The insufficiently active
group (M = 7.42, SD = 2.46) had significantly higher inattention scores than the active
group (M = 5.74, SD = 2.31; t(123) = 3.025, p = .003, d = 0.70, 95% CI [.583, 2.793]).
There were no differences for hyperactivity (t(123) = .506, p = .614, d = 0.12, 95% CI [−
.612, 1.033]). There were no correlations between the total OEQ score, or OEQ frequency
and ADHD measures (ASRS-v1.1, ITS, HTS all p > .05). There was a positive correlation
between OEQ fixation score and ITS (r = .210, p = .021) but not HTS (r = .024, p = .794)
or ASRS-v1.1 (r = .166, p = .067).
ADHD with Medication There were no differences between insufficiently active and active
participants for ASRS V1.1 (t(67) = 1.379, p = .172, d = 0.41, 95% CI [− 2.74, .501]),
ITS (t(67) = .468, p = .641, d = 0.17, 95% CI [− 1.656, 1.027]) or HTS (t(67) = 1.770, p
= .081, d = 0.52, 95% CI [− 1.713, 103]). There were no correlations between the ASRS
V1.1 and all OEQ measures (p > .05) or ITS and OEQ scores (p > .05). However, there

Group Differences in Problematic Levels of Exercise and Exercise Addiction Risk
Group comparisons of EAI and EDS are shown in Table 2. There were no significant group
differences in the total EAI score. Based on the EAI, participants were grouped according to risk of exercise addiction as ‘low’ (HC, n = 111, 88.8%; ADHD-M, n = 62, 88.6%;
ADHD-U, n = 38, 90.5%) and ‘high’ risk (HC, n = 14, 11.2%; ADHD-M, n = 8, 11.4%;
ADHD-U, n = 4, 9.5%). Chi-square goodness-of-fit test confirmed no differences in the
risk across the groups (X 2 (2) = .112, p = .946). There were no differences between groups
for total-EDS. EDS subscales identified significant group differences on two of the seven
subscales: withdrawal and reduction. However, post hoc testing only revealed a significant
difference for withdrawal where the ADHD-M group scored higher than the HC. Categorisation of the EDS into ‘at risk of exercise dependency’, ‘symptomatic non-dependent’
and ‘asymptomatic non-dependent’ revealed that very few participants were deemed at risk
overall (HC, n = 3, 2.4%; ADHD-M, n = 2, 2.9%; ADHD-U, n = 3, 7.9%). This results in
a violation of the expected cell counts assumption of chi-square to examine the relationship
between group and EDS-calculated risk when considering all three risk categories. Given
the low numbers in the at-risk group, we opted to compare only the ‘symptomatic nondependent’ and ‘asymptomatic non-dependent’, for which chi-square revealed a significant
difference between groups (X 2 (2) = .7.714, p = .021) with those with ADHD more likely
than healthy controls to be classed as symptomatic non-dependent (HC, n = 44, 36.1%;
ADHD-M, n = 36, 52.9%; ADHD-U, n = 22, 56.4%) than asymptomatic non-dependent
(HC, n = 78, 63.9%; ADHD-M, n = 32, 47.1%; ADHD-U, n = 17, 43.6%).

Discussion
This study aimed to investigate exercise behaviours and risk of addiction in ADHD. We
found that most participants were active and that those with ADHD exercise at similar levels to controls, irrespective of medication use, according to both the LSI calculated from
the Godin Leisure-Time Exercise Questionnaire and the Obligatory Exercise Questionnaire. Mean OEQ scores were below the level typically associated with obligatory exercisers (Elbourne & Chen, 2007) and did not differ between groups. However, ADHD-M
showed higher levels of exercise fixation than HC, indicating that exercise may confer
greater preoccupation and emotionality in this group. This result is unlikely to be related
to differences in demographics because, although demographic differences did exist, they
were typically greatest between HC and ADHD-U, rather than the ADHD-M. Active HC
participants had better attention scores, as measured by the ASRSv1.1, than those who
were not active, supporting previous research showing exercise is associated with improved

cognition in healthy people (Reigal et al., 2020). Inattention was correlated with OEQ fixation suggesting those with greater levels of inattention were more fixated on exercise. Conversely, in the ADHD groups, there were no differences in ADHD traits between insufficiently active and active participants. Only the ADHD-M group showed a correlation
between hyperactivity and total OEQ and fixation, indicating that hyperactivity, rather
than inattention, could be associated with exercise in this group. These results indicate that
whilst those with ADHD exercise to similar levels and obligation to those without ADHD,
the factors associated with exercise may differ from those without ADHD. The results offer
partial support of our first hypothesis because, whilst the levels of exercise and overall obligation to exercise do not differ between groups, the constructs underlying exercise habits
do vary according to symptoms and treatment.
Our second hypothesis was that those with ADHD would be more likely to exhibit problematic exercise. Using the EAI, we found no difference between groups in terms of overall
score or risk. However, the EDS indicated some differences do exist, giving partial support
to our hypothesis. Whilst the total EDS score did not differ between groups, the ADHD-M
showed greater withdrawal than HC. When combined with the higher levels of exercise
fixation the ADHD-M group displayed, this could suggest an increased risk of exercise
dependence in this group. The exact mechanism by which medication, which was largely
stimulant-based in the current study, could increase risk of exercise addiction cannot be
confirmed from the current study. However, several studies have shown that there appears
to be a cross-sensitization between psychostimulants and gambling related measures, mediated by dopaminergic and glutamatergic changes (Ferland et al., 2019; Fugariu et al., 2020;
Mascia et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2018), which could suggest that repeated administration
with psychostimulants used to treat ADHD increases sensitivity to exercise addiction via
actions on these neurotransmitters. It should also be noted that, in contrast to the possibility of cross-sensitization, some studies have found that exercise protects against developing
stimulant abuse (Lespine & Tirelli, 2018; Rauhut & Warnick, 2020); however, no studies
have specifically looked at individuals receiving stimulants for treating ADHD to date.
When considering risk categories for the EDS, only 3.4% of the sample were identified
as at-risk of exercise dependency. This aligns with previous research which suggests that
the rate of exercise dependence is 3–12% (Orhan et al., 2019). Most of the participants
in the present study were therefore categorised as non-dependent (96.6%) across all three
groups; however, there was a significant difference within the non-dependency in terms
of whether individuals were symptomatic. Those with ADHD, irrespective of medication
status, were more likely to be symptomatic non-dependent than those without the condition. This finding, combined with the data from the EAI and OEQ, suggests that exercise
dependency in ADHD warrants further investigation because individuals with ADHD may
be more likely to fixate on exercise and experience withdrawal as well as show symptoms
of exercise dependency when contrasted with those without ADHD, meaning any exercise intervention should be approached with caution at this stage. Exactly why ADHD may
confer a greater risk of exercise addiction is not clear at this stage. However, research into
gambling and ADHD may provide some insights. For example, it has been found that emotional regulation may mediate the relationship between ADHD and problematic gambling
(Mestre-Bach et al., 2021) and that impulsivity is a common feature of both conditions
(Chamberlain et al., 2015) with some shared genetic factors potentially underpinning this
(Comings et al., 1999). Studies have yet to be conducting examining these factors for any
other behavioural addiction in ADHD, including exercise.
This study was the first to investigate exercise addiction in ADHD and presents preliminary findings to inform further work in this area. Although this makes an important

contribution, the study has several limitations, meaning the data should be interpreted with
caution. Firstly, the sample size was small and limited to adults in the UK, meaning results
may not generalise to other areas or ages. Secondly, we did not collect data on specific
comorbidities which could have impacted results, although data about other conditions collected under demographics suggests an ecologically valid sample. Furthermore, our groups

those with and without ADHD, specifically focusing on the emotionality of the experience.
Additionally, the specific details of comorbidity and a wider of treatment groups should
be considered in analysis, for example, comparing non-stimulant with stimulant treatment,
which was not possible in the present study due to the sample size. Results from this further work, combined with this study will then provide an evidence base to inform the use
of exercise intervention in ADHD.
